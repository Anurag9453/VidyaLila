# Topic 2: Ions in Water: H⁺(aq), OH⁻(aq), Dilution, and Strength

In the previous topic, we saw that whether we test hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), or the citric acid in lemon juice—**every acid** turns blue litmus red, turns methyl orange red, and leaves phenolphthalein colourless. Similarly, **every soluble base** such as NaOH, KOH, and Ca(OH)₂ turns red litmus blue and phenolphthalein pink. Now think about this: **what single feature do all these acids share in common** that makes them behave in the same way? And can an acid show its acidic properties when no water is present? Today we will unlock that mystery.

---

## The Battery Workshop and the Dry Litmus Puzzle

On a rainy evening, Maya visited a backup-battery service workshop run by her father's friend, Mr. Miller. In one corner of the shop stood large white jugs labelled **"Battery-Grade Distilled Water"**, and in another corner stood bottles labelled **"Dilute Sulphuric Acid (Battery Electrolyte)"**, marked with hazard symbols.

Maya asked, "Mr. Miller, why does a backup battery need dilute acid mixed with distilled water? Can't electricity flow through pure distilled water on its own?"

Mr. Miller smiled: "Great question! Remember the Class 9 experiment with salt water, sugar water, and an LED bulb ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md))? Pure distilled water has almost no free **ions** to carry charge, so the bulb doesn't light up. But the moment you dissolve an acid in water, the acid splits apart into **ions**, and electric current flows right through!"

Maya thought for a second: "So acids have ions hidden inside them? Wait—what if there's no water at all, just completely dry HCl gas? Will dry HCl gas still turn blue litmus paper red?"

Mr. Miller grinned: "That is the biggest puzzle of all! **Without water, dry HCl gas does not change the colour of dry blue litmus paper at all!** Water is the key that unlocks the acid." Let's trace how this key works step by step.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Hydrogen ion, H⁺(aq)** | The positively charged hydrogen ion released when an acid splits apart in water | HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) |
| **Hydronium ion, H₃O⁺** | The ion formed when a bare H⁺ ion attaches to a water molecule (H₂O) | H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ |
| **Hydroxide ion, OH⁻(aq)** | The negatively charged ion released when a base dissolves in water | NaOH(aq) → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) |
| **Alkali** | A base that **dissolves in water** (water-soluble base) | NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ (slightly), NH₄OH |
| **Ionisation / Dissociation** | The splitting of a compound into its positive (+) and negative (−) ions when dissolved in water | HNO₃(aq) → H⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq) |
| **Strong acid** | An acid that ionises **almost completely (~100%)** in water to release a large concentration of H⁺(aq) ions | HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃ |
| **Weak acid** | An acid in which **only a small fraction of molecules** split apart in water to give H⁺(aq) ions | CH₃COOH (acetic acid), H₂CO₃, citric acid |
| **Basicity of an acid** | The maximum number of replaceable H⁺ ions that **1 molecule** of an acid can release in water | HCl = 1 (monobasic), H₂SO₄ = 2 (dibasic), H₃PO₄ = 3 (tribasic) |
| **Acidity of a base** | The number of replaceable OH⁻ ions released by **1 formula unit** of a base | NaOH = 1 (monoacidic), Ca(OH)₂ = 2 (diacidic) |
| **Dilution** | Adding water to an acid or a base to decrease the concentration of ions per unit volume | Slowly adding concentrated H₂SO₄ to water |

## Part A: What Do All Acids Have in Common? H⁺(aq) and the Hydronium Ion (H₃O⁺)

If we look at the chemical formulas of different acids—HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃, CH₃COOH—every single one contains **Hydrogen (H)**. But hold on! **Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)** and **ethyl alcohol (C₂H₅OH)** also contain hydrogen atoms! Are glucose and alcohol acids too? Let's test them with a school laboratory demonstration.

> **Activity 2.1 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): Which Conducts Electricity—Acid or Glucose?**
> Just like in Class 9 ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)), the teacher sets up two graphite rods in a beaker and connects them to a **6 V battery**, a switch, and a small **bulb (or LED)** *(never use a household mains socket!)*.
> 1. First, the teacher pours **dilute HCl** and then **dilute H₂SO₄** into the beaker and turns the switch ON: **The bulb glows brightly!**
> 2. Next, after rinsing the beaker thoroughly, the teacher tests an aqueous solution of **glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)** and then **ethyl alcohol (C₂H₅OH)**: **The bulb does NOT glow at all!** (Nor do these solutions turn blue litmus red.)

```
     BEAKER 1: Dilute HCl(aq)               BEAKER 2: Glucose C₆H₁₂O₆(aq)

        +---[6V Battery]---( * )               +---[6V Battery]---(   )
        :                  BULB GLOWS!         :                  BULB OFF
     +--+--+                                +--+--+
     :  :  :                                :  :  :
     : |:  :|                               : |:  :|
     :  H⁺ Cl⁻                              :  C₆H₁₂O₆ molecules
     :  Cl⁻ H⁺                              :  (no ions formed!)
     +--------+                             +--------+
     Free ions move -> current flows        Only neutral molecules -> no current
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Testing electrical conductivity in two beakers using a 6 V battery and bulb. In the left beaker, dilute HCl ionises in water into H⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq) ions, so the bulb glows. In the right beaker, glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) dissolves in water as neutral molecules without releasing H⁺ ions, so the bulb stays off.

**Conclusion:** Merely having H in its formula does not make a substance an acid! **An acid is a substance that dissolves in water to produce Hydrogen ions, H⁺(aq):**

- HCl(aq) → H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq)
- HNO₃(aq) → H⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)
- H₂SO₄(aq) → 2H⁺(aq) + SO₄²⁻(aq)
- CH₃COOH(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + CH₃COO⁻(aq)

This **H⁺(aq) ion** is the true signature of every acid! In glucose and alcohol, the hydrogen atoms are held so tightly by covalent bonds that they do not break away as H⁺ ions in water; therefore, despite containing hydrogen, they are **not acids**.

### Why Doesn't an Acid Work Without Water? (Dry HCl vs. Wet Litmus)

Now let's return to Maya's question: will dry HCl gas turn dry blue litmus red?

> **Activity 2.2 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): Dry HCl Gas and Moist Litmus.**
> The teacher places a small amount of common salt (NaCl) in a clean, dry test tube and adds a little **concentrated H₂SO₄** using a dropper *(Safety: both concentrated H₂SO₄ and HCl gas are hazardous; this demonstration is performed only by the teacher in a fume hood or well-ventilated area wearing gloves and safety goggles)*. The reaction produces **hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas**, which the teacher passes through a guard tube packed with **anhydrous calcium chloride** to remove any moisture (recall from Class 9 [water-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part4.md) that CaCl₂ is a drying agent):
> 2NaCl(s) + H₂SO₄(conc.) → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl(g)↑
> - When the teacher holds a strip of **dry blue litmus paper** at the mouth of the tube releasing dry HCl gas: **The colour does NOT change (it stays blue)!**
> - When the teacher holds a strip of **moist (wet) blue litmus paper** at the mouth of the tube: **It immediately turns RED!**

**Why?** Because dry HCl gas consists of neutral covalent molecules (H–Cl) in which hydrogen and chlorine share electrons; there are no free H⁺ ions present. The moment HCl gas meets moist litmus (that is, **water**), water molecules pull HCl apart into **H⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq) ions**, and only then does the litmus paper turn red!

### What Is a Hydronium Ion (H₃O⁺)?

Here is one more important chemical detail: inside water, a bare **H⁺ ion** (which is nothing but an exposed proton, since a hydrogen atom has lost its single electron!) cannot float around on its own. It immediately latches onto a water molecule (H₂O) to form a **Hydronium ion (H₃O⁺)**:

> **H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ (Hydronium ion)**
> Therefore, we can write the reaction of HCl with water more completely as:
> **HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻**

In practice, whenever chemists write **H⁺(aq)**, it is simply shorthand for **H₃O⁺**. Both notations are completely acceptable! *(We will examine the special 'coordinate bond' between H₂O and H⁺ in Chapter 7.)*

## Part B: What Do Bases Release in Water? OH⁻(aq) and Alkalis

Just as acids produce H⁺(aq) (or H₃O⁺) in water, when a **base** dissolves in water, it produces **Hydroxide ions, OH⁻(aq)**:

- NaOH(s) + water → Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
- KOH(s) + water → K⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
- Mg(OH)₂(s) + water ⇌ Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq)

### What Is the Difference Between a Base and an Alkali?

This is a classic point where students often slip up: **Not all bases dissolve in water!**
- Many bases—such as copper(II) hydroxide Cu(OH)₂, iron(III) hydroxide Fe(OH)₃, zinc oxide ZnO, and copper(II) oxide CuO—are **insoluble** in water. However, because they react with acids to form salt and water, they are still classified as **bases**.
- Bases that **dissolve in water** to release OH⁻(aq) ions in solution are given a special name: **Alkalis**. The common alkalis are **NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂** (slightly soluble, forming limewater), and **NH₄OH**.

```
   +--------------------------------------------------------+
   : ALL BASES (react with acids to form salt + water)      :
   : CuO, ZnO, Fe(OH)₃, Cu(OH)₂  (insoluble in water)       :
   :                                                        :
   : +---------------------------------------------+        :
   : :  ALKALIS (water-soluble bases)              :        :
   : :  NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH                  :        :
   : +---------------------------------------------+        :
   +--------------------------------------------------------+
```

> **Figure 2.2** – The relationship between Bases and Alkalis: Alkalis are the smaller subset within the larger family of Bases that dissolve in water. Therefore: **"All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis!"**

## Part C: Basicity of Acids and Acidity of Bases

The names sound slightly backwards at first, so read them carefully:
1. **Basicity of an Acid:** The maximum number of **H⁺ ions** produced when **one molecule** of an acid ionises in water is called the **basicity** of that acid:
   - **Monobasic acid (Basicity = 1):** 1 molecule yields **1 H⁺**. Examples: HCl, HNO₃, CH₃COOH. *(Watch out: CH₃COOH has 4 H atoms in its formula, but only the **1 H** attached to the oxygen in the –COOH group breaks off as H⁺ in water! The 3 H atoms bonded to carbon do not ionise. Therefore, the basicity of CH₃COOH is **1**, not 4!)*
   - **Dibasic acid (Basicity = 2):** 1 molecule yields **2 H⁺**. Examples: H₂SO₄ (2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻), H₂CO₃ (2H⁺ + CO₃²⁻).
   - **Tribasic acid (Basicity = 3):** 1 molecule yields **3 H⁺**. Example: Phosphoric acid, H₃PO₄ (3H⁺ + PO₄³⁻).

2. **Acidity of a Base:** The number of **OH⁻ ions** released by **one formula unit** of a base:
   - **Monoacidic base (Acidity = 1):** NaOH, KOH, NH₄OH (1 OH⁻).
   - **Diacidic base (Acidity = 2):** Ca(OH)₂, Mg(OH)₂, Cu(OH)₂ (2 OH⁻).
   - **Triacidic base (Acidity = 3):** Al(OH)₃, Fe(OH)₃ (3 OH⁻).

## Part D: Strong vs. Weak Compared with Concentrated vs. Dilute

Students frequently confuse a **"strong acid"** with a **"concentrated acid"**, but in chemistry these two terms mean completely different things!

### 1. Strong vs. Weak: What Percentage of Molecules Ionise?

- **Strong Acid:** When dissolved in water, **almost all (~100%) of its molecules split apart** to produce H⁺(aq) ions. Examples: **Hydrochloric acid (HCl), Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), Nitric acid (HNO₃)**. (We write a single forward arrow → in the equation.)
- **Weak Acid:** When dissolved in water, **only a tiny fraction (say, 1 or 2 out of every 100) of its molecules split apart** to produce H⁺(aq) ions; the remaining 98–99 molecules float around intact as unionised molecules! Examples: **Acetic acid (CH₃COOH), Citric acid, Carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), Oxalic acid, Methanoic acid**. (We write a reversible double arrow ⇌ in the equation.)
- In the exact same way:
  - **Strong Base (Alkali):** Dissociates ~100% in water to give OH⁻ ions—**NaOH, KOH**.
  - **Weak Base:** Only a small fraction produces OH⁻ ions—**NH₄OH**. *(Note: Ca(OH)₂ and Mg(OH)₂ dissolve only slightly in water, so their solutions contain a low concentration of OH⁻ ions; however, whatever small portion does dissolve dissociates almost completely. They are better described as sparingly soluble bases rather than weak bases.)*

```
   STRONG ACID (e.g., HCl in water)       WEAK ACID (e.g., CH₃COOH in water)
   (10 molecules added -> all 10 split!)  (10 molecules added -> only 1 splits)

    H⁺   Cl⁻    H⁺   Cl⁻    H⁺             CH₃COOH    CH₃COOH    CH₃COOH
    Cl⁻  H⁺     Cl⁻  H⁺     Cl⁻            CH₃COOH    H⁺         CH₃COO⁻
    H⁺   Cl⁻    H⁺   Cl⁻    H⁺             CH₃COOH    CH₃COOH    CH₃COOH
    Cl⁻  H⁺     Cl⁻  H⁺     Cl⁻            CH₃COOH    CH₃COOH

    Count: 10 H⁺ & 10 Cl⁻ (0 intact HCl)   Count: 9 intact CH₃COOH, 1 H⁺, 1 CH₃COO⁻
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Particle sketch comparing a strong acid and a weak acid (using 10 molecules). In the left box, all 10 HCl molecules split completely in water to form 10 H⁺ and 10 Cl⁻ ions. In the right box, out of 10 CH₃COOH molecules, 9 stay intact and only 1 splits into 1 H⁺ and 1 CH₃COO⁻. (We show 1 out of 10 for clarity; in real dilute vinegar, roughly 1 out of 100 molecules ionises.)

### 2. Concentrated vs. Dilute: How Much Water Is Present?

- **Concentrated:** The solution contains a **large amount** of acid (or base) dissolved in a **small amount** of water.
- **Dilute:** The solution contains a **large amount** of water and only a **small amount** of acid (or base).

**The Golden Distinction:**
- If you add plenty of water to **HCl**, it becomes **Dilute HCl**, but it is still a **Strong Acid** (because 100% of the HCl molecules present in it are ionised!).
- If you have **Acetic acid (CH₃COOH)** with very little water, it is **Concentrated Acetic Acid**, but it is still a **Weak Acid** (because only a tiny fraction of its molecules ionise)!

## Part E: Dilution and Safety: Acid into Water, or Water into Acid?

When a concentrated acid (especially concentrated H₂SO₄ or HNO₃) mixes with water, **a huge amount of heat is released**—it is a **highly exothermic** process ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part4.md)).

> **Life-Saving Lab Rule: Always add ACID slowly into WATER with constant stirring; NEVER pour water into concentrated acid!**

```
   CORRECT METHOD (Safe)                  WRONG METHOD (Dangerous!)

       Acid (drop by drop)                    Water (poured on top)
            :                                      :
            v   (stir with glass rod)              v   (flashes into steam!)
        +-------+                              +-------+
        :       :                              : \ ! / :  <-- Acid splashes onto
        :~~~~~~~:                              :~~~~~~~:      face and hands!
        : WATER :                              : CONC. :
        : (large:                              : ACID  :
        :  bulk):                              :       :
        +-------+                              +-------+
   Water absorbs the heat safely          Water boils instantly & spatters acid
```

> **Figure 2.4** – The safe versus dangerous way to dilute a concentrated acid. Left: adding acid drop by drop into a large volume of water while stirring allows the water to absorb the heat safely (recall from Class 9 Physics [heat-and-temperature-part3.md](../../../class9/physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part3.md) that water has a very high specific heat capacity). Right: pouring water into concentrated acid causes the water at the surface to boil violently into steam and spatter scalding acid out of the beaker, which can also crack the glass container.

**How does dilution affect ion concentration?**
When we add more water to an acid or a base (**dilution**), the **concentration of H₃O⁺ (or OH⁻) ions per unit volume (per mL or per L) DECREASES**. The solution becomes less concentrated.

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. How Dilution Decreases Ion Concentration

**Problem:** A beaker contains **50 mL** of dilute acid in which each 1 mL contains **4 × 10¹⁸** H₃O⁺ ions. The teacher adds **150 mL** of pure distilled water to the beaker.
(a) What is the new total volume in the beaker?
(b) Did the total number of H₃O⁺ ions in the beaker change?
(c) How many H₃O⁺ ions are now present per 1 mL of solution?

**Solution:**
- (a) New total volume = 50 mL + 150 mL = **200 mL**.
- (b) Initial total H₃O⁺ ions = 50 × (4 × 10¹⁸) = **2 × 10²⁰** ions. Because the strong acid was already completely ionised, adding water keeps the total number of H₃O⁺ ions essentially unchanged at 2 × 10²⁰; only the volume increased 4-fold.
- (c) New number of H₃O⁺ ions per 1 mL = (2 × 10²⁰) ÷ 200 mL = **1 × 10¹⁸** ions per mL.

**Check:** Since the total volume increased from 50 mL to 200 mL (**4 times**), the number of ions per mL dropped to one-fourth of 4 × 10¹⁸, which is 1 × 10¹⁸ ✅.

---

### Example 2. Basicity and Total Moles of H⁺ Ions

**Problem:** If **0.2 mole of H₂SO₄** and **0.2 mole of HCl** are dissolved separately in water and ionise completely, which solution will contain more H⁺(aq) ions, and how many moles of H⁺(aq) will each contain?

**Solution:**
- HCl is a **monobasic** acid: HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ (1 mole of HCl gives 1 mole of H⁺).
  Therefore, 0.2 mole of HCl gives = **0.2 mole of H⁺(aq) ions**.
- H₂SO₄ is a **dibasic** acid: H₂SO₄ → 2H⁺ + SO₄²⁻ (1 mole of H₂SO₄ gives 2 moles of H⁺).
  Therefore, 0.2 mole of H₂SO₄ gives = 2 × 0.2 = **0.4 mole of H⁺(aq) ions**!
- Conclusion: **The H₂SO₄ solution contains twice as many (0.4 mole) H⁺(aq) ions.**

**Check:** 0.4 mole ÷ 0.2 mole = 2, which matches the ratio of the basicities of H₂SO₄ and HCl (2 : 1) ✅.

---

### Example 3. Identifying Bases vs. Alkalis

**Problem:** From the list of compounds below, first select all the **Bases**, and then pick out the **Alkalis** from among them:
NaOH, CuO, H₂SO₄, KOH, Fe(OH)₃, C₂H₅OH, NH₄OH.

**Solution:**
- H₂SO₄ is an acid, and C₂H₅OH (ethanol) is a neutral covalent compound (neither an acid nor a base—its –OH group does not split off as OH⁻ ions in water!).
- **Bases (5 in total):** NaOH, CuO, KOH, Fe(OH)₃, NH₄OH.
- **Alkalis among them (water-soluble bases, 3 in total):** **NaOH, KOH, NH₄OH**. (CuO and Fe(OH)₃ do not dissolve in water, so they are bases but not alkalis.)

**Check:** All 3 alkalis (NaOH, KOH, NH₄OH) are a subset of the 5 bases listed ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Mistaking C₂H₅OH (alcohol) for a base because of its OH group, or C₆H₁₂O₆ (glucose) for an acid because of its H atoms.**
   Alcohol and glucose are covalent compounds; they do **not** split into OH⁻ or H⁺ ions in water, and their aqueous solutions do not conduct electricity.
2. **Mistake 2: Writing that the basicity of CH₃COOH (acetic acid) is 4 because it has 4 hydrogen atoms.**
   In CH₃COOH, only the **1 hydrogen** in the –COOH group ionises as H⁺ in water (CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺). Therefore, acetic acid is **monobasic (basicity = 1)**!
3. **Mistake 3: Saying "Dilute HCl is a weak acid."**
   Completely wrong! HCl is always a **strong acid** (whether concentrated or dilute) because it ionises ~100% in water. Adding water makes it **dilute**, not **weak**!
4. **Mistake 4: Pouring water into concentrated acid.**
   Never! Always add acid slowly to water with constant stirring; otherwise the intense exothermic heat can boil the water instantly and spatter acid out of the container.
5. **Mistake 5: Expecting dry HCl gas to turn dry blue litmus red.**
   Without water, HCl gas cannot form H⁺(aq) ions, so dry litmus paper shows no colour change.

## Quick Recap

- All **acids** dissolve in water to produce **H⁺(aq)** or **hydronium ions (H₃O⁺)** (H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺).
- **Without water**, an acid cannot form ions: dry HCl gas does not change the colour of dry blue litmus, but immediately turns moist blue litmus red.
- **Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)** and **ethyl alcohol (C₂H₅OH)** contain hydrogen, but they do not release H⁺ ions in water, so their solutions do not light up the conductivity bulb and they are not acids.
- Soluble **bases** release **OH⁻(aq) ions** in water. Water-soluble bases are called **alkalis** (NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂, NH₄OH). **"All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis."**
- **Strong acids** (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) ionise ~100% in water; **weak acids** (CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, citric acid) ionise only slightly.
- **Dilution** (Acid → Water, with constant stirring) is a highly **exothermic** process and decreases the concentration of H₃O⁺ or OH⁻ ions per unit volume (per mL).

3. Write the balanced equation showing how a hydrogen ion (H⁺) forms a hydronium ion in water. Why can a bare H⁺ ion not exist freely in aqueous solution?

   (b) Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)

   (d) Acetic acid (CH₃COOH)

6. While diluting a concentrated acid in the laboratory, why is it recommended that the acid should be added slowly to water with constant stirring, and NOT water to the acid?
7. How does the concentration of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) per millilitre change when a solution of an acid is diluted with water?

9. Equal lengths of magnesium ribbon are dropped into two test tubes: Tube A contains dilute HCl and Tube B contains dilute CH₃COOH (both at the same concentration and temperature). In which test tube will fizzing (bubbling) occur more vigorously, and why?
10. A beaker contains **40 mL** of potassium hydroxide (KOH) solution containing **6 × 10¹⁹** OH⁻ ions per mL. If **160 mL** of distilled water is added to the beaker, find:
    (a) the final volume of the solution,
    (b) the total number of OH⁻ ions in the beaker, and
    (c) the new number of OH⁻ ions per mL after dilution.

### Answers

1. Electric current flows through a solution via the movement of **free ions**. HCl and HNO₃ ionise in water to form H⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq) / NO₃⁻(aq) ions, so their solutions conduct electricity. Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) and ethyl alcohol (C₂H₅OH) remain as neutral covalent molecules when dissolved in water and do not split into H⁺ ions, so their solutions do not conduct electricity.

2. A base exhibits its basic properties (releasing OH⁻ ions) **only in the presence of water**. Dry NH₃ gas contains no OH⁻ ions, so dry red litmus paper does not change colour. When dissolved in water, however, ammonia reacts with water to form ammonium ions and hydroxide ions (NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻), and these OH⁻(aq) ions turn red litmus blue.

3. Equation: **H⁺ + H₂O → H₃O⁺** (and for HCl: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻).
   A bare H⁺ ion is simply a tiny, positively charged atomic nucleus (a single proton) with no electrons left around it. Because of its intense positive charge density, it cannot exist alone in water and immediately bonds to a water molecule (H₂O) to form a **hydronium ion (H₃O⁺)**.

4. (a) **HNO₃:** Basicity = **1 (Monobasic)**; Ions: H⁺(aq) and NO₃⁻(aq).
   (b) **H₂SO₄:** Basicity = **2 (Dibasic)**; Ions: 2H⁺(aq) and SO₄²⁻(aq).
   (c) **H₃PO₄:** Basicity = **3 (Tribasic)**; Ions: 3H⁺(aq) and PO₄³⁻(aq).
   (d) **CH₃COOH:** Basicity = **1 (Monobasic)** (despite having 4 H atoms, only the 1 H in the –COOH group ionises as H⁺); Ions: H⁺(aq) and CH₃COO⁻(aq).

5. A **base** is any substance that reacts with an acid to form salt and water. Among all bases, **only those that are soluble in water** are called **alkalis**.
   - Two bases that **are alkalis** (water-soluble): **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** and **Potassium hydroxide (KOH)**.
   - Two bases that **are not alkalis** (water-insoluble): **Copper(II) hydroxide (Cu(OH)₂)** and **Iron(III) hydroxide (Fe(OH)₃)** (or CuO, ZnO).

6. Mixing a concentrated acid with water is a **highly exothermic** (heat-releasing) process. If water is poured into concentrated acid, the sudden heat released boils the water at the surface into steam instantly, causing scalding acid to spatter out onto the face or hands and potentially cracking the glass beaker. Adding acid drop by drop into a large volume of water while stirring allows the bulk water to absorb the heat safely.

7. When an acid is diluted by adding water, the total volume increases, so the **concentration of hydronium ions (H₃O⁺) per millilitre (per unit volume) DECREASES**.

8. The student is **wrong**!
   - **Strong vs. Weak acid** depends on the **percentage of acid molecules that ionise in water**: HCl ionises ~100% in water, so it is always a **strong acid**; CH₃COOH ionises only slightly, so it is a **weak acid**.
   - **Concentrated vs. Dilute acid** depends on **how much water** is mixed with the acid. Adding plenty of water to concentrated HCl produces a **dilute strong acid**, not a weak acid!

9. Fizzing (bubbling) will occur **much more vigorously in Tube A (dilute HCl)**! Because HCl is a **strong acid**, it ionises completely in water and provides a much higher concentration of H⁺(aq) ions than CH₃COOH (a weak acid) at the same molar concentration. The higher concentration of H⁺(aq) ions causes hydrogen gas (H₂) to be produced much faster with magnesium in Tube A.

10. (a) Final total volume = 40 mL + 160 mL = **200 mL**.
    (b) Total number of OH⁻ ions = 40 mL × (6 × 10¹⁹ ions/mL) = **2.4 × 10²¹** ions.
