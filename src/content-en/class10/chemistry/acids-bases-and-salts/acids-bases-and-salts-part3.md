# Topic 3: Chemical Reactions of Acids and Bases

In the previous topic, we saw that the moment an acid enters water, it releases active **H⁺(aq) ions**, while a base releases **OH⁻(aq) ions**. Now imagine what happens when these energetic ions collide with a **metal**, **baking soda**, **limestone**, or **each other**! In Class 9 ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)), we caught our first glimpse of the reactivity ladder and neutralisation. Today, rather than simply repeating those basics, we will build on them to master **five major families of chemical reactions**, the laboratory tests for the gases evolved, and the stoichiometry behind them.

---

## The Brass Bowl Warning and Fizzy Lemon Soda

On a warm afternoon, Liam was in the kitchen helping his aunt, Mrs. Evans, prepare homemade yoghurt. Liam reached into the cupboard, pulled out a gleaming golden **brass bowl**, and was just about to pour sour yoghurt starter into it when Mrs. Evans stopped his hand!

"Hold on, Liam! We **never** store yoghurt, buttermilk, lemon juice, or sour pickles in an unlined brass or copper vessel!"

Liam looked surprised: "Why not, Aunt Sarah? This brass bowl looks so sturdy and shiny!"

Mrs. Evans set the brass bowl aside and explained, "It is shiny, but yoghurt and lemon juice contain **acids**. If you store sour foods in an unlined brass or copper container, the acid reacts with the metal surface to form **bluish-green, bitter-tasting, toxic copper salts** that spoil the food and can make you sick. That's why we always use glass, ceramic, glazed earthenware, or stainless-steel bowls for sour foods."

Then Mrs. Evans poured fresh lemon juice and cold water into a tall glass, and the moment she stirred in a pinch of **baking soda**—*fzzzzzz!*—a lively cloud of foam and bubbles surged to the top of the glass!

Liam laughed: "Aunt Sarah, what gas is making all those bubbles? Is it the same gas that forms when an acid reacts with a metal, or a completely different one?"

Mrs. Evans smiled: "A completely different gas! With active metals we get **Hydrogen (H₂)**, and with baking soda we get **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)**. Let's look at how we test for both gases and explore all five reaction families one by one."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Pop-sound test** | The test for Hydrogen (H₂) gas: it burns with a characteristic 'pop' sound when a burning splint is brought near soap bubbles filled with the gas (teacher demo only) | Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑ |
| **Sodium zincate, Na₂ZnO₂** | The salt formed when zinc metal reacts with warm sodium hydroxide (NaOH) base | 2NaOH + Zn → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂↑ |
| **Effervescence** | Rapid bubbling and fizzing in a liquid caused by the escape of a gas produced in a reaction | CO₂ bubbles rising when lemon juice hits baking soda |
| **Limewater test** | The test for Carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas: clear limewater, Ca(OH)₂(aq), turns milky due to insoluble CaCO₃↓ | Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O |
| **Basic oxide** | A metallic oxide that reacts with an acid to form salt and water | CuO, MgO, CaO, Na₂O |
| **Acidic oxide** | A non-metallic oxide that reacts with a base to form salt and water | CO₂, SO₂, SO₃ |
| **Ionic neutralisation** | The net ionic heart of neutralisation, where H⁺(aq) from the acid and OH⁻(aq) from the base combine to form water (H₂O) | H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) |

## Part A: Reaction 1 — Reactions of Acids and Bases with Metals

### 1. Active Metal + Dilute Acid → Salt + Hydrogen Gas (H₂↑)

You have already met this rule twice in Class 9: on the reactivity ladder ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)) and in the laboratory preparation of hydrogen ([hydrogen-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/hydrogen/hydrogen-part2.md)), where we wrote balanced equations for Zn, Mg, and Fe reacting with dilute HCl and dilute H₂SO₄. In one sentence: **any metal above H on the reactivity ladder + dilute acid → salt + H₂↑**. Here is one new example—aluminium—which has slightly larger balancing coefficients:

   2Al(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂(g)↑
   *(Check: Al 2=2, H 6=6, Cl 6=6 ✅)*

Looking deeper through the lens of Topic 2, the real action involves **H⁺(aq) ions**: metal atoms transfer electrons to H⁺ ions, turning them into H₂ gas molecules while the metal ions (such as Zn²⁺ or Al³⁺) enter the solution. Consequently, a solution with a higher concentration of H⁺(aq) (a strong acid) produces much more vigorous fizzing.

*(Remember: Copper Cu, Silver Ag, and Gold Au lie below Hydrogen on the reactivity ladder, so they do **not** displace hydrogen gas from dilute HCl or dilute H₂SO₄! Then why does yoghurt spoil in Liam's brass or copper bowl? Because in the presence of atmospheric **oxygen (O₂)**, the organic acids in yoghurt or lemon juice slowly react with the thin copper oxide layer on the metal surface to form soluble, toxic bluish-green copper salts!)*

> **Activity 3.1 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): The Soap-Bubble Pop Test for H₂.**
> The teacher places **zinc granules (Zn)** and **dilute H₂SO₄** in a test tube clamped to a stand. The evolving gas is led through a delivery tube into a trough filled with **soap solution**:
> - **Soap bubbles filled with H₂** detach and float upward into the air (because H₂ is much lighter than air!).
> - Standing well away from the test tube, the teacher touches a burning candle mounted on a long rod to a rising soap bubble: the bubble burns with a sharp **"POP!"** sound!
> *(Safety: Hydrogen gas is highly flammable. Never bring a naked flame directly to the mouth of the reaction test tube; the teacher demonstrates the pop test only on rising soap bubbles at a safe distance.)*

```
      Stand        Delivery tube
        :          +-------------+
        :          :             :
       +:+         :             :        ( H₂ )  <-- Burning candle brought near
       : :         :             :      (H₂)          gives a sharp "POP!" sound!
       : : Dilute  :             v    (H₂)
       : : H₂SO₄   :         +--------------+
       :o: + Zn    :         :~~~~~~~~~~~~~~:
       +_+ granules          : Soap solution:
                             +--------------+
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Teacher demonstration: Passing H₂ gas (from zinc and dilute H₂SO₄) through soap solution produces lightweight H₂-filled soap bubbles that float upward and burn with a 'pop' sound when touched with a burning candle.

### 2. Do Bases Also React with Metals to Give Hydrogen? (The Special Case of Zn and Al)

Most metals (such as Fe, Cu, and Mg) do **not** react with alkalis like NaOH. However, a few special metals—most notably **Zinc (Zn)** and **Aluminium (Al)**—react with warm **Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH)** solution to liberate **Hydrogen gas (H₂↑)**:

> **2NaOH(aq) + Zn(s) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) (Sodium zincate) + H₂(g)↑**
> *(Check: Na 2=2, O 2=2, H 2=2, Zn 1=1 ✅. Here ZnO₂²⁻ is the zincate radical with a valency of 2, giving the formula Na₂ZnO₂!)*

## Part B: Reaction 2 — Metal Carbonates (CO₃²⁻) and Hydrogencarbonates (HCO₃⁻) + Acid

Now let's return to the fizzing bubbles in Liam's lemon soda! Whenever any **acid** is added to a **metal carbonate** (such as washing soda Na₂CO₃ or limestone CaCO₃) or a **metal hydrogencarbonate / bicarbonate** (such as baking soda NaHCO₃), rapid bubbling (**brisk effervescence**) occurs as **Carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas** escapes:

> **General Word Equation:**
> Metal Carbonate / Hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + Water (H₂O) + Carbon dioxide (CO₂↑)

Three essential balanced equations:

1. **Sodium carbonate (Washing soda) + Dilute HCl:**
   Na₂CO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
   *(Check: Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 3=1+2=3, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅)*
2. **Sodium hydrogencarbonate (Baking soda) + Dilute HCl:**
   NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
   *(Check: Na 1=1, H 1+1=2, C 1=1, O 3=1+2=3, Cl 1=1 ✅)*
3. **Calcium carbonate (Limestone / marble / crushed eggshells) + Dilute HCl:**
   CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
   *(Check: Ca 1=1, C 1=1, O 3=1+2=3, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅)*

### The Two-Stage Limewater Test for Carbon Dioxide (CO₂)!

To confirm that the escaping gas is indeed CO₂, we bubble it through **freshly prepared clear limewater, Ca(OH)₂(aq)**:

- **Stage 1 (Passing CO₂ briefly — Turns Milky):**
  When CO₂ gas is bubbled through clear limewater for a short time, an insoluble **white precipitate of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃)** forms, turning the limewater **milky (cloudy white)**:
  **Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)↓ (White milky precipitate) + H₂O(l)**
  *(Check: Ca 1=1, O 2+2=3+1=4, H 2=2, C 1=1 ✅)*

- **Stage 2 (Passing Excess CO₂ — Milkiness DISAPPEARS!):**
  If we continue bubbling **excess CO₂ gas** through that same milky suspension, the white CaCO₃ reacts with water and the extra CO₂ to form **Calcium hydrogencarbonate, Ca(HCO₃)₂**. As we learned in Class 9 ([water-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part3.md)), Ca(HCO₃)₂ is **soluble in water**! Consequently, **the milkiness vanishes and the liquid becomes clear and colourless again**:
  **CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq) (Water-soluble — clear solution)**
  *(Check: Ca 1=1, C 1+1=2, O 3+1+2=6, H 2=2 ✅)*

```
   Clear Limewater        Brief CO₂ bubbled         Excess CO₂ bubbled
   Ca(OH)₂(aq)      --->  CaCO₃(s)↓ + H₂O     --->  Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq)
   (Colourless/Clear)     (MILKY / Cloudy!)         (CLEAR / Colourless again!)
                          [insoluble solid]         [soluble in water]
```

> **Figure 3.2** – The two stages of the limewater test: first, CO₂ forms insoluble CaCO₃, making the liquid milky; second, excess CO₂ converts CaCO₃ into water-soluble Ca(HCO₃)₂, causing the milkiness to disappear.

*(Fun fact: Limestone, chalk, and marble look very different in appearance, yet chemically all three are different physical forms of the exact same compound: **Calcium carbonate, CaCO₃**!)*

## Part C: Reaction 3 — Acid + Base (The Ionic Form of Neutralisation)

In Class 9 ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)), we saw that HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) is a double-displacement reaction. Now, using the ion picture from Topic 2, let's see **what actually changes** inside the beaker:

- Before mixing: H⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + Na⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq)
- After mixing: Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) + H₂O(l)

Look closely! Na⁺(aq) and Cl⁻(aq) were floating separately in water before the reaction, and they are still floating separately in water afterwards (they are **spectator ions**)! The real chemical reaction happened solely between **H⁺(aq) and OH⁻(aq)**:

> **Net Ionic Equation for Neutralisation:**
> **H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l) + Heat (Exothermic)**

> **Activity 3.2 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): Phenolphthalein's Pink-to-Colourless Dance.**
> 1. The teacher pours 10 mL of dilute NaOH into a conical flask and adds 2 drops of **phenolphthalein**: the solution turns **bright pink** (due to OH⁻ ions).
> 2. Next, the teacher adds **dilute HCl** drop by drop from a dropper while swirling the flask. A single drop arrives at which every OH⁻ ion has combined with an H⁺ ion to form H₂O—and the solution suddenly turns **completely colourless**!
> 3. If the teacher adds just **1 drop of NaOH** back in, the solution turns **pink** again! Add 1 drop of HCl, and it goes back to **colourless**!

## Part D: Reactions 4 and 5 — The Nature of Metal Oxides and Non-Metal Oxides

### 1. Metal Oxide + Acid → Salt + Water (Metal Oxides Are BASIC!)

*(Observation only — teacher demonstration.)* The teacher places a small amount of **black Copper(II) oxide (CuO) powder** in a beaker and slowly adds **dilute HCl** while stirring gently. Before your eyes, the black powder dissolves and the liquid turns a striking **bluish-green**!

**Why?** Because soluble **Copper(II) chloride (CuCl₂)** has formed:

> **CuO(s) (Black) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) (Bluish-green) + H₂O(l)**
> *(Check: Cu 1=1, O 1=1, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅)*

Compare this equation with Base + Acid → Salt + Water! CuO behaved just like a **base**, neutralising the acid (HCl) to produce a salt (CuCl₂) and water (H₂O). This gives us a fundamental rule:

> **Metallic oxides (such as CuO, MgO, CaO, and Na₂O) are BASIC in nature!**

### 2. Non-Metal Oxide + Base → Salt + Water (Non-Metal Oxides Are ACIDIC!)

Now look again at the limewater reaction from Part B:
**CO₂(g) (Non-metal oxide) + Ca(OH)₂(aq) (Base) → CaCO₃(s) (Salt) + H₂O(l) (Water)**

Here, CO₂—the oxide of the non-metal carbon—reacts with the base Ca(OH)₂ to produce a salt (CaCO₃) and water (H₂O), behaving just like an **acid**! Similarly, sulphur dioxide (SO₂) reacts with NaOH to form Na₂SO₃ + H₂O. Therefore:

> **Non-metallic oxides (such as CO₂, SO₂, and SO₃) are ACIDIC in nature!**

## Part E: Solved Examples

### Example 1. How Much CO₂ Gas Is Produced from Marble (CaCO₃)?

**Problem:** If **10 g of pure marble (CaCO₃)** reacts completely with dilute HCl according to the equation CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂, how many grams of **CO₂ gas** and how many grams of **CaCl₂** are formed? *(Atomic masses: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16, H = 1, Cl = 35.5)*

**Solution:**
- First calculate all molar masses:
  - CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + (3 × 16) = **100 g/mol**.
  - 2HCl = 2 × (1 + 35.5) = **73 g**.
  - CaCl₂ = 40 + (2 × 35.5) = **111 g/mol**.
  - H₂O = 2 + 16 = **18 g/mol**.
  - CO₂ = 12 + (2 × 16) = **44 g/mol**.
- 100 g of CaCO₃ produces 44 g of CO₂ and 111 g of CaCl₂.
- Therefore, **10 g of CaCO₃** (which is one-tenth of 100 g, or 0.1 mol) produces:
  - Mass of CO₂ = 0.1 × 44 = **4.4 g**
  - Mass of CaCl₂ = 0.1 × 111 = **11.1 g**
  - (Also, H₂O formed = 0.1 × 18 = 1.8 g, and HCl consumed = 0.1 × 73 = 7.3 g.)

**Check (Law of Conservation of Mass):**
Total mass of reactants = 10 g (CaCO₃) + 7.3 g (HCl) = **17.3 g**.
Total mass of products = 11.1 g (CaCl₂) + 1.8 g (H₂O) + 4.4 g (CO₂) = **17.3 g** ✅!

### Example 2. Identifying Two Unknown Gases X and Y

**Problem:** Adding dilute H₂SO₄ to zinc granules releases gas **X**, whereas adding dilute H₂SO₄ to baking soda (NaHCO₃) releases gas **Y**. Name gases X and Y, write the balanced chemical equations, and give one confirmatory test for each gas.

**Solution:**
- **Gas X = Hydrogen (H₂):**
  Equation: Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑ *(Check: Zn 1, H 2, S 1, O 4 ✅)*
  Test: When bubbled through soap solution, the rising gas-filled bubbles burn with a sharp **'pop' sound** when touched with a burning candle.
- **Gas Y = Carbon dioxide (CO₂):**
  Equation: 2NaHCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) + 2CO₂(g)↑ *(Check: Na 2=2, H 2+2=4, C 2=2, O 6+4=4+2+4=10, S 1=1 ✅)*
  Test: When bubbled through freshly prepared clear limewater, Ca(OH)₂(aq), it turns the limewater **milky** (and extinguishes a burning splint).

**Check:** In both equations, the number of atoms of every element is identical on the left and right sides (Zn=1, H=2, S=1, O=4 and Na=2, H=4, C=2, O=10, S=1) ✅.

### Example 3. Black Powder P and Bluish-Green Solution Q

**Problem:** A black metallic oxide **P** dissolves in dilute hydrochloric acid to form a bluish-green solution **Q** and water. Identify P and Q by name and formula, and state whether P is acidic or basic in nature.

**Solution:**
- Black metallic oxide **P = Copper(II) oxide (CuO)**.
- Bluish-green solution **Q = Copper(II) chloride, CuCl₂(aq)**.
- Equation: CuO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CuCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l).
- Because CuO is a metallic oxide that neutralises an acid (HCl) to form a salt and water, **P is Basic in nature**.

**Check:** In CuO + 2HCl → CuCl₂ + H₂O, Cu = 1, O = 1, H = 2, and Cl = 2 are completely balanced on both sides ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Writing the product of NaOH + Zn as NaZnO₂.**
   The zincate ion carries a **−2 charge (ZnO₂²⁻)** while the sodium ion carries **+1 (Na⁺)**. By the criss-cross rule, the correct formula is **Na₂ZnO₂ (Sodium zincate)**, not NaZnO₂!
2. **Mistake 2: Assuming every metal reacts with NaOH to release H₂ gas.**
   Only a few special metals such as **Zinc (Zn)** and **Aluminium (Al)** react with NaOH to evolve H₂; common metals like iron (Fe), copper (Cu), and magnesium (Mg) do not react with NaOH.
3. **Mistake 3: Writing that passing excess CO₂ makes limewater "even milkier".**
   A small amount of CO₂ turns limewater milky (CaCO₃↓), but passing **excess CO₂** forms water-soluble **Ca(HCO₃)₂**, causing the **milkiness to disappear (become clear and colourless)**!
4. **Mistake 4: Writing H₂ gas as a product of Metal Carbonate + Acid.**
   Active metal (Zn, Mg) + Acid gives **H₂**; Carbonate or Hydrogencarbonate (Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃, CaCO₃) + Acid always gives **CO₂ + H₂O**!

## Quick Recap

- **Active Metal + Dilute Acid → Salt + H₂(g)↑** (e.g., Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂↑). H₂ gas in soap bubbles burns with a **'pop' sound**.
- **Yoghurt, lemon juice, and sour pickles** must not be stored in unlined brass or copper vessels because their acids react with the metal surface to form **toxic copper salts**.
- **Zinc (Zn) + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ (Sodium zincate) + H₂(g)↑**.
- **Metal Carbonate / Hydrogencarbonate + Acid → Salt + H₂O + CO₂(g)↑**.
- **Limewater test:** Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)↓ (milky) + H₂O(l); with excess CO₂: CaCO₃(s) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g) → Ca(HCO₃)₂(aq) (colourless, soluble).
- **Net ionic equation for neutralisation:** H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
- **Metallic oxides (CuO, MgO, CaO) are BASIC** (Metal oxide + Acid → Salt + H₂O); **Non-metallic oxides (CO₂, SO₂) are ACIDIC** (Non-metal oxide + Base → Salt + H₂O).

   The remaining ions (such as Na⁺ and Cl⁻) stay unchanged in solution before and after the reaction without taking part in bond formation, so they are called **spectator ions**.

6. **Limewater test (or Pop test):** Bubble each gas through clear limewater, Ca(OH)₂(aq): **CO₂ turns limewater milky**, whereas **H₂ produces no change in limewater**. (Alternatively, soap bubbles filled with H₂ burn with a 'pop' sound when touched with a flame, whereas CO₂ extinguishes a flame.)

7. CO₂ and SO₂ are oxides of non-metals, which are **acidic** in nature. Therefore, they undergo neutralisation-like reactions with bases/alkalis to form salt and water.
   Balanced equation: **SO₂(g) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂SO₃(aq) + H₂O(l)** *(Check: S 1=1, O 2+2=3+1=4, Na 2=2, H 2=2 ✅)*.

8. (a) Initially, because of the base KOH, phenolphthalein is **pink**.
   (b) As dilute H₂SO₄ is added drop by drop, H⁺ ions from the acid neutralise all the OH⁻ ions from the base to form water (H₂O). Once the solution becomes neutral (or slightly acidic), phenolphthalein turns **colourless**.
   (c) Without adding any more indicator, simply add **a few drops of KOH (base)** back to the flask: as soon as the solution becomes basic again, the **pink** colour returns!

9. (a) **False** ❌. Copper (Cu) lies below Hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it does **not** react with dilute HCl.
   (b) **True** ✅. Chalk, marble, and limestone all have the same chemical formula, **CaCO₃**.
   (c) **False** ❌. CaCO₃ is insoluble in water, but **Ca(HCO₃)₂ is soluble in water**.
   (d) **True** ✅. Metallic oxides are generally basic and non-metallic oxides are acidic.

10. Molar masses:
    - NaHCO₃ = 23 + 1 + 12 + (3 × 16) = **84 g/mol**.
    - NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = **58.5 g/mol**.
    - CO₂ = 12 + 32 = **44 g/mol**.
    - (HCl = 36.5 g/mol; H₂O = 18 g/mol.)
    (a) Moles of NaHCO₃ = 16.8 g ÷ 84 g/mol = **0.2 mol**.
    (b) In the equation NaHCO₃ + HCl → NaCl + H₂O + CO₂, the mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
        Therefore, 0.2 mol of NaHCO₃ produces **0.2 mol of NaCl** → Mass = 0.2 × 58.5 = **11.7 g**.
    (c) **0.2 mol of CO₂** is released → Mass = 0.2 × 44 = **8.8 g**.
    *(Check: HCl consumed = 0.2 × 36.5 = 7.3 g; H₂O formed = 0.2 × 18 = 3.6 g. Reactants = 16.8 + 7.3 = 24.1 g; Products = 11.7 + 3.6 + 8.8 = 24.1 g ✅!)*
