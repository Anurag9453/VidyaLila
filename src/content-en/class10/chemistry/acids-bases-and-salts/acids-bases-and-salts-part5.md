# Topic 5: Families of Salts, Their Types, and Methods of Preparation

In [Part 3](acids-bases-and-salts-part3.md), we saw that when an acid and a base react with each other, neutralisation takes place to form a **salt** and **water**. And in [Part 4](acids-bases-and-salts-part4.md), we mastered the **pH scale** from 0 to 14. Now an intriguing question arises: **is the aqueous solution of every salt strictly neutral (pH = 7)?** Furthermore, in the laboratory, why do some salts dissolve completely in water (like NaCl) while others settle immediately to the bottom as a white solid precipitate (like BaSO₄)? How many families and types of salts exist, and how do chemists prepare them in the laboratory? Today we will open up the entire world of salts.

---

## The School Lab's 'Salt Cabinet' and the Three-Colour Surprise

On Saturday morning, **Noah** was helping his chemistry teacher, **Mr. Harrison**, organise the reagent shelves in the school laboratory. Inside the wooden cabinet stood dozens of glass jars filled with white and blue crystals—labelled Sodium chloride, Sodium sulphate, Ammonium chloride, Sodium carbonate, and more.

As Noah arranged the jars, he remarked, "Mr. Harrison, all of these compounds are **salts**, right? And since a salt is formed by the neutralisation of an acid and a base, every single one of them should give a **neutral solution (pH = 7)** in water!"

Mr. Harrison smiled: "Is that so? Let's put your hypothesis to the test right now!" He poured equal volumes of distilled water into three clean test tubes. In the first tube he dissolved **Sodium chloride (NaCl)**, in the second **Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl)**, and in the third **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)**—all three solutions looked crystal-clear and colourless like plain water. Then he asked Noah to add 2 drops of **Universal Indicator** to each tube.

The moment the drops fell in, Noah stared in amazement:
- The NaCl tube turned **green (pH = 7, neutral)**,
- The NH₄Cl tube turned **orange-red (pH ≈ 5, acidic)**, and
- The Na₂CO₃ tube turned **deep blue (pH ≈ 10.5, basic)**!

"How did that happen?!" Noah asked in astonishment. "All three are salts—why did one turn out neutral, one acidic, and one basic?"

Mr. Harrison explained, "Noah, just as children inherit traits from their parents, every salt carries the chemical imprint of its **parent acid** and **parent base**! Whichever parent is stronger dominates when the salt dissolves in water." Let's join Noah and understand the families, nature, and preparation of salts step by step.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Salt** | An ionic compound formed when the replaceable H⁺ ion(s) of an acid are replaced by a metal ion or an ammonium ion (NH₄⁺) | NaCl, KNO₃, CuSO₄, NH₄Cl |
| **Basic radical (cation)** | The positively charged part of a salt that comes from the **parent base** | Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, NH₄⁺ |
| **Acidic radical (anion)** | The negatively charged part of a salt that comes from the **parent acid** | Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, CO₃²⁻, CH₃COO⁻ |
| **Family of salts** | A group of salts that share either the same positive ion (cation) or the same negative ion (anion) | Sodium salts (NaCl, Na₂SO₄), Chloride salts (NaCl, KCl) |
| **Normal salt** | A salt formed by the complete replacement of all replaceable H⁺ ions of an acid | NaCl, Na₂SO₄, Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONa |
| **Acid salt** | A salt formed by the partial (incomplete) replacement of the H⁺ ions of a polybasic acid, still containing replaceable H⁺ | NaHSO₄, NaHCO₃, NaH₂PO₄ |
| **Basic salt** | A salt formed by the partial replacement of the OH⁻ ions of a polyacidic base, still containing OH⁻ | Pb(OH)Cl, Cu(OH)NO₃ |
| **Precipitation** | The formation of an insoluble solid salt (↓) when two solutions of soluble salts are mixed together | BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq) |

## Part A: What Are Salts, and What Are Families of Salts?

When the ionisable hydrogen ion (H⁺) of an acid is replaced by a metallic cation (such as Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, or Zn²⁺) or an ammonium ion (NH₄⁺), the resulting ionic compound is called a **Salt**.

Every salt consists of two parts (refresh your memory from Class 9 Chapter 3, [language-of-chemistry-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/language-of-chemistry/language-of-chemistry-part4.md)):
1. **Basic Radical (Cation — Positive ion):** Derived from the **parent base** (or metal)—for example, Na⁺ from NaOH, K⁺ from KOH, Cu²⁺ from Cu(OH)₂, and NH₄⁺ from NH₄OH.
2. **Acidic Radical (Anion — Negative ion):** Derived from the **parent acid**—for example, Cl⁻ (chloride) from HCl, SO₄²⁻ (sulphate) from H₂SO₄, NO₃⁻ (nitrate) from HNO₃, CO₃²⁻ (carbonate) from H₂CO₃, and CH₃COO⁻ (acetate/ethanoate) from CH₃COOH.

```
   THE TWO PARTS OF A SALT: IDENTIFYING THE PARENT BASE AND PARENT ACID

      Parent Base: NaOH                   Parent Acid: H₂SO₄
                   │                                   │
                   ▼                                   ▼
                [ Na⁺ ]          +                 [ SO₄²⁻ ]
             (Basic Radical)                    (Acidic Radical)
                   │                                   │
                   └───────────────┬───────────────────┘
                                   ▼
                             Salt: Na₂SO₄
                          (Sodium sulphate)
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Every salt gets its positive ion (basic radical) from its parent base and its negative ion (acidic radical) from its parent acid.

### Families of Salts

Salts that share **the same positive ion (cation)** or **the same negative ion (anion)** are said to belong to the same **Family of Salts**:

| Family Name | Common Ion | Member Salts (Examples) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium salts family** | Na⁺ | NaCl, Na₂SO₄, NaNO₃, Na₂CO₃, CH₃COONa |
| **Potassium salts family** | K⁺ | KCl, K₂SO₄, KNO₃, K₂CO₃ |
| **Magnesium salts family** | Mg²⁺ | MgCl₂, MgSO₄, Mg(NO₃)₂ |
| **Chloride salts family** | Cl⁻ | NaCl, KCl, NH₄Cl, MgCl₂, CaCl₂, ZnCl₂ |
| **Sulphate salts family** | SO₄²⁻ | Na₂SO₄, K₂SO₄, MgSO₄, CuSO₄, CaSO₄, (NH₄)₂SO₄ |
| **Nitrate salts family** | NO₃⁻ | NaNO₃, KNO₃, Ca(NO₃)₂, Pb(NO₃)₂ |
| **Carbonate salts family** | CO₃²⁻ | Na₂CO₃, K₂CO₃, CaCO₃, ZnCO₃ |

> **Important Note:** The same salt can belong to two different families at once! For instance, **Na₂SO₄** belongs to the **Sodium salts family** because it contains Na⁺, and it also belongs to the **Sulphate salts family** because it contains SO₄²⁻.

## Part B: pH of Salt Solutions: Is Every Salt Neutral?

Here lies the secret behind Noah's three test tubes. When a salt dissolves in water, the pH of its solution depends on whether its **parent acid** and **parent base** are strong or weak (recall the strong/weak list from [Part 2](acids-bases-and-salts-part2.md)):

- **Strong Acids:** HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃
- **Weak Acids:** H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid), CH₃COOH (acetic acid), H₂SO₃
- **Strong Bases (Alkalis):** NaOH, KOH
- **Weak Bases:** NH₄OH, Cu(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, Zn(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃

This gives us **3 reliable rules** for predicting the pH of salt solutions:

1. **Strong Acid + Strong Base → Neutral Salt Solution (pH = 7):**
   When both parents are equally strong, neither dominates in water and the solution stays strictly **neutral (pH = 7)**. Universal indicator turns **green** and litmus paper shows no change.
   - **Examples:** NaCl (HCl + NaOH), KNO₃ (HNO₃ + KOH), K₂SO₄ (H₂SO₄ + KOH), Na₂SO₄ (H₂SO₄ + NaOH).
2. **Strong Acid + Weak Base → Acidic Salt Solution (pH < 7):**
   When the parent acid is **strong** and the parent base is **weak**, the strong acid's character dominates when the salt interacts with water (a process called *salt hydrolysis*). Free H⁺(aq) ions outnumber OH⁻(aq) ions in solution, so **pH < 7 (acidic)** (blue litmus → red).
   - **Examples:** NH₄Cl (HCl + NH₄OH), CuSO₄ (H₂SO₄ + Cu(OH)₂), AlCl₃ (HCl + Al(OH)₃), ZnSO₄ (H₂SO₄ + Zn(OH)₂).
3. **Weak Acid + Strong Base → Basic Salt Solution (pH > 7):**
   When the parent acid is **weak** and the parent base is **strong**, the strong base's character dominates in water. Free OH⁻(aq) ions outnumber H⁺(aq) ions in solution, so **pH > 7 (basic/alkaline)** (red litmus → blue).
   - **Examples:** Na₂CO₃ (H₂CO₃ + NaOH), NaHCO₃ (H₂CO₃ + NaOH), CH₃COONa (CH₃COOH + NaOH), K₂CO₃ (H₂CO₃ + KOH).

| Salt Name and Formula | Parent Acid (Strength) | Parent Base (Strength) | Nature of Aqueous Solution | pH Value Range | Effect on Litmus / Universal Indicator |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Sodium chloride (NaCl)** | HCl (Strong acid) | NaOH (Strong base) | **Neutral** | pH = 7 | No change / Green |
| **Potassium nitrate (KNO₃)** | HNO₃ (Strong acid) | KOH (Strong base) | **Neutral** | pH = 7 | No change / Green |
| **Sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄)** | H₂SO₄ (Strong acid) | NaOH (Strong base) | **Neutral** | pH = 7 | No change / Green |
| **Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl)** | HCl (Strong acid) | NH₄OH (Weak base) | **Acidic** | pH < 7 | Blue litmus → Red / Orange-red |
| **Copper(II) sulphate (CuSO₄)** | H₂SO₄ (Strong acid) | Cu(OH)₂ (Weak base) | **Acidic** | pH < 7 | Blue litmus → Red / Orange |
| **Aluminium chloride (AlCl₃)** | HCl (Strong acid) | Al(OH)₃ (Weak base) | **Acidic** | pH < 7 | Blue litmus → Red / Orange-red |
| **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)** | H₂CO₃ (Weak acid) | NaOH (Strong base) | **Basic** | pH > 7 | Red litmus → Blue / Blue |
| **Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃)** | H₂CO₃ (Weak acid) | NaOH (Strong base) | **Mildly Basic** | pH > 7 (~8.3) | Red litmus → Blue / Bluish-green |
| **Sodium acetate (CH₃COONa)** | CH₃COOH (Weak acid) | NaOH (Strong base) | **Basic** | pH > 7 | Red litmus → Blue / Blue |

> **Activity 5.1 (In the school lab with a teacher).** Dissolve small samples of NaCl, KNO₃, AlCl₃, ZnSO₄, CuSO₄, and CH₃COONa in distilled water in six separate test tubes. Test each solution with pH paper: NaCl and KNO₃ give pH 7 (green); AlCl₃, ZnSO₄, and CuSO₄ give pH below 7 (acidic); whereas CH₃COONa gives pH above 7 (basic)!

## Part C: Types of Salts (Normal, Acid, Basic) and 6 Laboratory Preparation Methods

### 1. Three Types of Salts Based on Their Formula

Depending on whether the replaceable H⁺ ions of an acid or the OH⁻ ions of a base have been completely or partially replaced, salts are classified into **3 types**:

1. **Normal Salts:**
   When **all the replaceable H⁺ ions** of an acid are **completely** replaced by a metal ion or NH₄⁺ ion, a **Normal salt** is formed. Its formula contains no replaceable H⁺ or OH⁻ ions.
   - HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O
   - H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O (both H⁺ ions of H₂SO₄ are replaced here)
   - **Examples:** NaCl, KNO₃, Na₂SO₄, Na₂CO₃, CaCO₃, Na₃PO₄, CH₃COONa.
   - *(Watch out: In CH₃COONa, the 3 hydrogen atoms in the CH₃– group are bonded covalently to carbon and are non-ionisable; therefore, CH₃COONa is a **Normal salt**!)*

2. **Acid Salts:**
   When the replaceable H⁺ ions of a polybasic acid (such as H₂SO₄, H₂CO₃, or H₃PO₄) undergo **only partial replacement**—typically when an insufficient amount of alkali is added—one or two replaceable H⁺ ions remain inside the salt's formula. Such a salt is called an **Acid salt**.
   - H₂SO₄ + NaOH (insufficient) → NaHSO₄ (Sodium hydrogensulphate) + H₂O
   - H₂CO₃ + NaOH → NaHCO₃ (Sodium hydrogencarbonate) + H₂O
   - H₃PO₄ + NaOH → NaH₂PO₄ (Sodium dihydrogenphosphate) + H₂O
   - When an acid salt (such as NaHSO₄) reacts with more alkali (NaOH), the remaining replaceable H⁺ ion is also neutralised to form a normal salt (Na₂SO₄):
     NaHSO₄(aq) + NaOH(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)

3. **Basic Salts:**
   When the OH⁻ ions of a polyacidic base (such as Pb(OH)₂, Cu(OH)₂, or Mg(OH)₂) undergo **partial replacement** by an acid, one or more OH⁻ groups remain in the salt. Such a salt is called a **Basic salt**.
   - Pb(OH)₂ + HCl (insufficient) → Pb(OH)Cl (Basic lead(II) chloride) + H₂O
   - Cu(OH)₂ + HNO₃ (insufficient) → Cu(OH)NO₃ (Basic copper(II) nitrate) + H₂O

### 2. Soluble vs. Insoluble Salts and the 6 Laboratory Preparation Methods

Choosing the right laboratory method to prepare a salt depends first and foremost on whether the target salt is **soluble** or **insoluble** in water:

| Salt Category | Soluble in Water | Insoluble in Water (Forms a Precipitate) |
|---|---|---|
| **Sodium (Na⁺), Potassium (K⁺), and Ammonium (NH₄⁺) salts** | **All are soluble** (without exception!) | None |
| **Nitrates (NO₃⁻) and Bicarbonates (HCO₃⁻)** | **All nitrates are soluble** (KNO₃, Pb(NO₃)₂, AgNO₃) | No common nitrate is insoluble |
| **Chlorides (Cl⁻)** | Almost all are soluble (NaCl, MgCl₂, ZnCl₂, CuCl₂) | **Exceptions:** AgCl (white ppt) and PbCl₂ (insoluble in cold water, soluble in hot water) |
| **Sulphates (SO₄²⁻)** | Most are soluble (Na₂SO₄, MgSO₄, CuSO₄, ZnSO₄, FeSO₄) | **Exceptions:** BaSO₄ (white ppt), PbSO₄ (white ppt), CaSO₄ (sparingly soluble) |
| **Carbonates (CO₃²⁻), Sulphites (SO₃²⁻), Sulphides (S²⁻)** | **Only** the Na⁺, K⁺, and NH₄⁺ salts are soluble (Na₂CO₃, K₂CO₃) | **All others are insoluble** (CaCO₃, ZnCO₃, CuCO₃, CuS, PbS) |

#### The 6 Major Laboratory Methods for Preparing Salts:

1. **Method 1 – Direct Combination (Synthesis: Metal + Non-metal):**
   Heating a metal and a non-metal directly together:
   2Na(s) + Cl₂(g) → 2NaCl(s)
   2Fe(s) + 3Cl₂(g) (dry) → 2FeCl₃(s) (Iron(III) chloride)
   Fe(s) + S(s) --(Heat)--> FeS(s) (Iron(II) sulphide)
2. **Method 2 – Active Metal + Dilute Acid (Displacement — for Soluble Salts):**
   Active metals (Mg, Zn, Fe) displace H₂ gas from a dilute acid to leave behind a soluble salt solution:
   Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
   Fe(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → FeSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
3. **Method 3 – Insoluble Metal Oxide / Hydroxide + Dilute Acid (Neutralisation):**
   Copper (Cu) does not react directly with dilute H₂SO₄ (since Cu lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series). Therefore, to prepare CuSO₄, we react black **Copper(II) oxide (CuO)** with warm dilute H₂SO₄:
   CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
   PbO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
4. **Method 4 – Metal Carbonate / Hydrogencarbonate + Dilute Acid:**
   Adding dilute acid to an insoluble carbonate produces brisk effervescence of CO₂ and a soluble salt in solution:
   ZnCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
   CaCO₃(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
5. **Method 5 – Titration (Soluble Alkali + Dilute Acid — for Na⁺, K⁺, and NH₄⁺ Salts):**
   Because NaOH, KOH, and NH₄OH are completely soluble in water, any unreacted excess cannot simply be filtered off. We therefore use an acid-base **titration** with an indicator to find the exact neutralisation point:
   NaOH(aq) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
   2KOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)
6. **Method 6 – Precipitation / Double Decomposition (for Insoluble Salts):**
   To prepare an **insoluble salt** (such as BaSO₄, AgCl, PbSO₄, or CaCO₃), we mix **aqueous solutions of two soluble salts**. The ions swap partners ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)) and the insoluble salt immediately settles out as a solid **precipitate (↓)**:
   BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ (White ppt) + 2NaCl(aq)
   AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s)↓ (Curdy white ppt) + NaNO₃(aq)
   Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → PbSO₄(s)↓ (White ppt) + 2NaNO₃(aq)

## Part D: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Identifying Parent Acid, Parent Base, Family, and Aqueous pH

**Problem:** For each of the four salts below, identify its (a) Parent Acid, (b) Parent Base, (c) Common Family, and (d) the nature of its aqueous solution (pH = 7, < 7, or > 7):
1. Potassium sulphate (K₂SO₄)
2. Ammonium sulphate ((NH₄)₂SO₄)
3. Copper(II) sulphate (CuSO₄)
4. Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)

**Solution:**
1. **K₂SO₄:** Cation K⁺ → Parent Base = **KOH (Strong base)**; Anion SO₄²⁻ → Parent Acid = **H₂SO₄ (Strong acid)**. Strong acid + Strong base → **Neutral solution (pH = 7)**.
2. **(NH₄)₂SO₄:** Cation NH₄⁺ → Parent Base = **NH₄OH (Weak base)**; Anion SO₄²⁻ → Parent Acid = **H₂SO₄ (Strong acid)**. Strong acid + Weak base → **Acidic solution (pH < 7)**.
3. **CuSO₄:** Cation Cu²⁺ → Parent Base = **Cu(OH)₂ (Weak base)**; Anion SO₄²⁻ → Parent Acid = **H₂SO₄ (Strong acid)**. Strong acid + Weak base → **Acidic solution (pH < 7)**.
4. **Na₂CO₃:** Cation Na⁺ → Parent Base = **NaOH (Strong base)**; Anion CO₃²⁻ → Parent Acid = **H₂CO₃ (Weak acid)**. Weak acid + Strong base → **Basic solution (pH > 7)**.
- **Common Family:** The first three salts (K₂SO₄, (NH₄)₂SO₄, and CuSO₄) all share the SO₄²⁻ ion, so they belong to the **Sulphate salts family**.

**Check:** Every formula is electrically neutral (K₂SO₄: 2(+1)+(-2)=0; CuSO₄: (+2)+(-2)=0), and the stronger parent determines the solution's pH ✅.

### Example 2. Calculating the Mass of an Insoluble Salt Formed by Precipitation

**Problem:** In a teacher demonstration (since barium compounds are toxic, students do not handle them directly), Mr. Harrison dissolved **4.16 g of Barium chloride (BaCl₂)** in water and added excess Sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄) solution.
1. Write the balanced chemical equation for this reaction and name the salt preparation method used.
2. Calculate how many grams of white precipitate (BaSO₄) are formed, and how many grams of Sodium chloride (NaCl) remain dissolved in solution.
*(Atomic masses: Ba = 137 u, Cl = 35.5 u, Na = 23 u, S = 32 u, O = 16 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Balanced Chemical Equation and Method):**
  BaCl₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → BaSO₄(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)
  This is the **Precipitation (Double Decomposition)** method because two soluble salts react in solution to form a white precipitate of an insoluble salt (BaSO₄).
- **Step 2 (Formula Unit Masses):**
  - BaCl₂ = 137 + 2(35.5) = 137 + 71 = 208 u
  - Na₂SO₄ = 2(23) + 32 + 4(16) = 46 + 32 + 64 = 142 u
  - BaSO₄ = 137 + 32 + 4(16) = 137 + 32 + 64 = 233 u
  - 2NaCl = 2 × (23 + 35.5) = 2 × 58.5 = 117 u
- **Step 3 (Mass Calculation):**
  - Moles of BaCl₂ = 4.16 g ÷ 208 g/mol = 0.02 mol.
  - Mass of BaSO₄ precipitated = 0.02 × 233 = **4.66 g**.
  - Mass of NaCl formed = 0.02 × 117 = **2.34 g**.

**Check:** Na₂SO₄ reacted = 0.02 × 142 = 2.84 g. Total reactants = 4.16 g + 2.84 g = 7.00 g; Total products = 4.66 g + 2.34 g = 7.00 g (Law of Conservation of Mass holds) ✅.

### Example 3. Preparing a Soluble Salt from an Insoluble Metal Oxide

**Problem:** To prepare pure Copper(II) sulphate (CuSO₄) in the lab, **3.975 g of black Copper(II) oxide (CuO)** is warmed with the exact stoichiometric amount of dilute Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).
1. Write the balanced equation and explain why CuSO₄ is not prepared by adding copper metal directly to dilute H₂SO₄.
2. Calculate how many grams of anhydrous CuSO₄ and how many grams of water (H₂O) are produced.
*(Atomic masses: Cu = 63.5 u, O = 16 u, H = 1 u, S = 32 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Equation and Reason):**
  CuO(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → CuSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l)
  Copper (Cu) lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace H₂ gas from dilute H₂SO₄. We therefore react its insoluble basic oxide (CuO) with dilute H₂SO₄.
- **Step 2 (Formula Unit Masses):**
  - CuO = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5 u
  - H₂SO₄ = 2(1) + 32 + 4(16) = 98 u
  - CuSO₄ = 63.5 + 32 + 4(16) = 159.5 u
  - H₂O = 2(1) + 16 = 18 u
- **Step 3 (Mass Calculation):**
  - Moles of CuO = 3.975 ÷ 79.5 = 0.05 mol.
  - Mass of CuSO₄ formed = 0.05 × 159.5 = **7.975 g**.
  - Mass of H₂O formed = 0.05 × 18 = **0.90 g**.

**Check:** H₂SO₄ reacted = 0.05 × 98 = 4.90 g. Total mass of reactants = 3.975 + 4.90 = 8.875 g; Total mass of products = 7.975 + 0.90 = 8.875 g ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming every salt solution is neutral (pH = 7).**
   Only salts of a **Strong Acid + Strong Base** (such as NaCl, KNO₃, and Na₂SO₄) give neutral solutions! Aqueous solutions of NH₄Cl and CuSO₄ are **acidic (pH < 7)**, whereas solutions of Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃, and CH₃COONa are **basic (pH > 7)**.
2. **Mistake 2: Confusing an "Acid salt" (such as NaHCO₃) with a "Salt that forms an acidic solution" (such as NH₄Cl).**
   - **NaHCO₃** is classified as an **Acid salt** by formula (because it still holds one replaceable H from H₂CO₃), yet its solution in water is **mildly basic (pH ≈ 8.3)**!
   - **NH₄Cl** and **CuSO₄** are **Normal salts** by formula (no replaceable H⁺ left), yet their solutions in water are **acidic (pH < 7)**!
3. **Mistake 3: Calling CH₃COONa an acid salt because it contains 3 hydrogen atoms.**
   In CH₃COOH, only the 1 hydrogen in the –COOH group was replaceable, and it has been replaced by Na⁺; the 3 hydrogens in the CH₃– group are non-ionisable. Therefore, CH₃COONa is a **Normal salt**!
4. **Mistake 4: Writing Cu + dilute H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + H₂.**
   Copper does not displace H₂ from dilute HCl or dilute H₂SO₄! To prepare CuSO₄, use **CuO + dilute H₂SO₄** or **CuCO₃ + dilute H₂SO₄**.
5. **Mistake 5: Trying to prepare PbSO₄ by adding dilute H₂SO₄ directly to insoluble PbCO₃ or PbO.**
   Doing so forms an insoluble crust of PbSO₄ on the surface of the solid PbCO₃ or PbO, stopping the reaction before completion! The correct route is a 2-step process: first dissolve PbO/PbCO₃ in dilute HNO₃ to make soluble Pb(NO₃)₂, and then add Na₂SO₄(aq) to precipitate pure PbSO₄↓.

## Quick Recap

- In a **Salt**, the **Basic radical (Cation)** comes from the parent base and the **Acidic radical (Anion)** comes from the parent acid.
- **Family of Salts:** Salts sharing a common positive or negative ion belong to the same family (e.g., Sodium salts, Sulphate salts, Chloride salts).
- **pH of Salt Solutions:**
  - Strong Acid + Strong Base → Neutral (pH = 7) (NaCl, KNO₃, Na₂SO₄).
  - Strong Acid + Weak Base → Acidic (pH < 7) (NH₄Cl, CuSO₄, AlCl₃, ZnSO₄).
  - Weak Acid + Strong Base → Basic (pH > 7) (Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃, CH₃COONa).
- **Types of Salts:** **Normal salt** (complete replacement of H⁺, e.g., NaCl, Na₂SO₄), **Acid salt** (partial replacement of H⁺, e.g., NaHSO₄, NaHCO₃), and **Basic salt** (partial replacement of OH⁻, e.g., Pb(OH)Cl).
- **Preparation of Salts:** Soluble salts are prepared by reacting an Active Metal / Insoluble Oxide / Carbonate with Dilute Acid, or by Alkali + Acid **Titration**; Insoluble salts (BaSO₄, AgCl, PbSO₄) are always prepared by **Precipitation (Double Displacement)** from two soluble salt solutions.

### Answers

1. Salts that have the same positive radical (cation) or the same negative radical (anion) in common are said to belong to a **Family of Salts**.
   - **Sodium salts family** (common Na⁺ ion): NaCl, Na₂SO₄, NaNO₃.
   - **Sulphate salts family** (common SO₄²⁻ ion): Na₂SO₄, K₂SO₄, CaSO₄, MgSO₄. *(Note: Na₂SO₄ belongs to both families!)*

2. (a) **NH₄NO₃:** Parent Acid = HNO₃ (Strong acid), Parent Base = NH₄OH (Weak base). Because it is formed from a strong acid and a weak base, its aqueous solution is **Acidic (pH < 7)**.
   (b) **K₂CO₃:** Parent Acid = H₂CO₃ (Weak acid), Parent Base = KOH (Strong base). Because it is formed from a weak acid and a strong base, its aqueous solution is **Basic (pH > 7)**.
   (c) **NaNO₃:** Parent Acid = HNO₃ (Strong acid), Parent Base = NaOH (Strong base). Because both parents are strong, its aqueous solution is **Neutral (pH = 7)**.

3. - **Acid Salt:** Formed by the **partial (incomplete) replacement** of the replaceable H⁺ ions of a polybasic acid (such as H₂SO₄):
     H₂SO₄(aq) + NaOH(aq) (insufficient) → NaHSO₄(aq) (Sodium hydrogensulphate — Acid salt) + H₂O(l)
   - **Normal Salt:** Formed by the **complete replacement** of all replaceable H⁺ ions of an acid:
     H₂SO₄(aq) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) (Sodium sulphate — Normal salt) + 2H₂O(l)

4. Both parts of the student's claim are incorrect:
   - **Not every acid salt forms an acidic solution:** Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) is an **Acid salt** by formula (retaining one replaceable H⁺ from H₂CO₃), yet its aqueous solution is **mildly basic (pH ≈ 8.3)** because its parent base (NaOH) is strong while its parent acid (H₂CO₃) is weak.
   - **Not every normal salt forms a neutral solution:** Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) and Copper(II) sulphate (CuSO₄) are **Normal salts**, yet their aqueous solutions are **acidic (pH < 7)**. Similarly, Na₂CO₃ is a **Normal salt**, yet its aqueous solution is **basic (pH > 7)**.

5. Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) is a monobasic acid containing **only one replaceable H⁺ ion** (in the –COOH group). The other three hydrogen atoms (in the CH₃– group) are covalently bonded to carbon and do not ionise in water. When that single replaceable H⁺ ion is replaced by Na⁺, complete replacement has occurred, so CH₃COONa is classified as a **Normal salt**.
   Because it is derived from a weak acid (CH₃COOH) and a strong base (NaOH), its aqueous solution is **basic (pH > 7)**—it turns **red litmus blue** and has no effect on blue litmus.

6. (a) **ZnSO₄ (Soluble salt):** **Active Metal + Dilute Acid (Displacement)** method:
   Zn(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂(g)↑
   (b) **AgCl (Insoluble salt):** **Precipitation (Double Displacement)** method (mixing two soluble salt solutions):
   AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s)↓ (White ppt) + NaNO₃(aq)
   (c) **K₂SO₄ (Soluble alkali salt):** **Acid-Base Titration (Neutralisation)** method:
   2KOH(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → K₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l)

7. If dilute H₂SO₄ is added directly to insoluble PbO, an insoluble coating of PbSO₄ forms right on the surface of the solid PbO particles, preventing the inner PbO from reacting further and leaving an impure, incomplete product.
   **Correct 2-Step Method:**
   - **Step 1:** Dissolve insoluble PbO in dilute Nitric acid (HNO₃) to obtain **soluble Lead(II) nitrate, Pb(NO₃)₂**:
     PbO(s) + 2HNO₃(aq) → Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + H₂O(l)
   - **Step 2:** Mix this soluble Pb(NO₃)₂ solution with Sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄) solution (or dilute H₂SO₄) to precipitate pure white PbSO₄:
     Pb(NO₃)₂(aq) + Na₂SO₄(aq) → PbSO₄(s)↓ (White ppt) + 2NaNO₃(aq)

8. Balanced Equation: AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s)↓ + NaNO₃(aq)
   - Formula Unit Masses: AgNO₃ = 108 + 14 + 48 = 170 u; NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u; AgCl = 108 + 35.5 = 143.5 u; NaNO₃ = 23 + 14 + 48 = 85 u.
   - Moles of AgNO₃ = 3.40 ÷ 170 = 0.02 mol.
   - Mass of AgCl precipitated = 0.02 × 143.5 = **2.87 g**.
   - Mass of NaNO₃ formed = 0.02 × 85 = **1.70 g** ✅ (Check: Reacted NaCl = 0.02 × 58.5 = 1.17 g; Reactants 3.40 + 1.17 = 4.57 g = Products 2.87 + 1.70 = 4.57 g ✅).

9. Balanced Equation: ZnCO₃(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) → ZnSO₄(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
   - Formula Unit Masses: ZnCO₃ = 65 + 12 + 48 = 125 u; ZnSO₄ = 65 + 32 + 64 = 161 u; CO₂ = 12 + 32 = 44 u.
   - Moles of ZnSO₄ desired = 6.44 ÷ 161 = 0.04 mol.
   - Mass of ZnCO₃ required = 0.04 × 125 = **5.00 g**.
   - Mass of CO₂ gas evolved = 0.04 × 44 = **1.76 g** ✅ (Check: Reacted H₂SO₄ = 0.04 × 98 = 3.92 g, H₂O = 0.04 × 18 = 0.72 g; Reactants 5.00 + 3.92 = 8.92 g = Products 6.44 + 0.72 + 1.76 = 8.92 g ✅).

10. - **Tube X (pH ≈ 10.5, Blue — Basic):** Contains **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)**, the salt of a weak acid (H₂CO₃) and a strong base (NaOH).
    - **Tube Y (pH = 7.0, Green — Neutral):** Contains **Sodium chloride (NaCl)**, the salt of a strong acid (HCl) and a strong base (NaOH).
    - **Tube Z (pH ≈ 4.5, Orange — Acidic):** Contains **Zinc sulphate (ZnSO₄)**, the salt of a strong acid (H₂SO₄) and a weak base (Zn(OH)₂).
    - **When dilute HCl is added to Tube X (Na₂CO₃):** Brisk effervescence of CO₂ gas is produced:
      Na₂CO₃(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑
