# Topic 6: Treasures from Common Salt 1: Common Salt, Caustic Soda, Bleaching Powder, and Baking Soda

In [Part 5](acids-bases-and-salts-part5.md), we saw that the neutral salt **Sodium chloride (NaCl)**—formed when hydrochloric acid (HCl) and sodium hydroxide (NaOH) neutralise each other—is the familiar **Common Salt (table salt)** we use in our food every day. Yet the story of NaCl does not end in the kitchen salt shaker—that is where industrial chemistry truly begins! In today's topic, we will discover how ordinary common salt is transformed into **Caustic Soda (NaOH)**, **Bleaching Powder (CaOCl₂)**, and **Baking Soda (NaHCO₃)**.

## Coastal Salt Pans and the Secret of Fluffy Cakes

During summer vacation, **Chloe** visited a coastal town with her uncle, **Mr. Bennett**, a senior chemical plant engineer. Along the sunny shoreline stretched rows of shallow rectangular ponds (**salt pans**) where seawater evaporated in the warm breeze, leaving behind glistening white mounds of salt.

Mr. Bennett scooped up a handful of white salt crystals and said, "Chloe, this ordinary salt (NaCl) that costs less than \$1 a kilogram in the grocery store is the 'King Raw Material' of the chemical industry! From the bath soap you use every morning to the bleaching powder that disinfects drinking water and the baking soda that makes cakes rise—all of them are children of common salt."

That evening back at the house, Chloe's mother was baking a vanilla sponge cake and frying crispy onion fritters. Chloe noticed that her mother measured **Baking Powder** into the cake batter, and the cake baked up light, golden, and airy. Chloe remembered that the previous month, when she had accidentally used a full spoonful of plain **Baking Soda** instead, her cupcakes had turned out **bitter, soapy-tasting**, and yellowish!

Chloe immediately asked, "Uncle Ben, how do factories turn seawater salt into baking soda? And what is the difference between baking soda and baking powder that makes one cake taste bitter and the other delicious?"

Mr. Bennett smiled: "Fantastic question! Let's open up the treasure chest of common salt and examine each of these industrial gems one by one."

## Part A: Common Salt (NaCl) and the Chlor-Alkali Process (NaOH)

### 1. Where Do We Get Common Salt (NaCl)?

1. **From Seawater:** Seawater contains many dissolved salts, predominantly sodium chloride. Seawater is trapped in wide, shallow coastal **salt pans** where sunlight and wind evaporate the water, leaving behind crude solid salt that is then purified to give pure NaCl.
2. **From Underground Rock Salt (Halite):** Millions of years ago, ancient inland seas dried up and their thick salt beds were buried beneath layers of rock. These underground deposits are called **Rock Salt**. Because of mineral impurities, rock salt is often **brownish** in colour and is mined from underground deposits much like coal.

### 2. Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH — Caustic Soda): The Chlor-Alkali Process

When an electric current is passed through a concentrated aqueous solution of sodium chloride (called **Brine**), it decomposes by electrolysis to produce **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)**, **Chlorine gas (Cl₂)**, and **Hydrogen gas (H₂)**.

- **Why is it called the "Chlor-Alkali Process"?**
  Because its two main industrial products are **Chlor** (standing for **Chlorine, Cl₂**) and **Alkali** (standing for **Sodium hydroxide, NaOH**)!
- **Balanced Chemical Equation:**
  2NaCl(aq) [Brine] + 2H₂O(l) --(Electricity / Electrolysis)--> 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂(g)↑

```
   THE CHLOR-ALKALI CELL: WHERE EACH PRODUCT FORMS

              (+) ANODE                         (–) CATHODE
           [ Cl₂(g) Gas ↑ ]                  [ H₂(g) Gas ↑ ]
                  ▲                                 ▲
        ┌─────────┴───────────────┬─────────────────┴─────────┐
        │   (+)                   │                     (–)   │
        │  Anode                  │                   Cathode │
        │  ║║║                    │                     ║║║   │
        │  ║║║    Brine In ──►    │     ──► NaOH(aq)    ║║║   │
        │  ║║║   (NaCl + H₂O)     │    (Near Cathode)   ║║║   │
        │                         │                           │
        │        Cl⁻ ions         │      Na⁺ and H⁺ ions      │
        │     move to Anode       │      move to Cathode      │
        └─────────────────────────┴───────────────────────────┘
                           (Membrane Cell)
```

> **Figure 6.2** – During the electrolysis of brine, Cl₂ gas is liberated at the Anode (+), H₂ gas is liberated at the Cathode (–), and NaOH solution forms near the Cathode.

#### Where All Three Products Form and Their Major Uses:

| Product | Where It Forms in the Cell | Major Industrial & Everyday Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Chlorine gas (Cl₂)** | Liberated at the **Anode (+ electrode)** | Disinfecting/sterilising drinking water and swimming pools; manufacturing **PVC** (polyvinyl chloride), **Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂)**, pesticides, and solvents. |
| **2. Hydrogen gas (H₂)** | Liberated at the **Cathode (– electrode)** | Clean rocket fuel, hydrogenation of vegetable oils to make **margarine**, and manufacturing **Ammonia (NH₃)** for fertilisers. |
| **3. Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** | Formed in solution **near the Cathode** | **Degreasing metals**, making **soaps and detergents**, **paper manufacturing**, and producing artificial textile fibres (such as **rayon**). |
| **Bonus: HCl and Household Bleach** | H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl; Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaOCl + H₂O | Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is used for cleaning steel (pickling), medicines, and cosmetics; NaOCl is used in household liquid bleach. |

## Part B: Bleaching Powder (CaOCl₂ — Calcium Oxychloride)

When **Chlorine gas (Cl₂)** obtained from the anode of the chlor-alkali process is passed over **dry slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂**, **Bleaching Powder** is produced.

- **Chemical Name:** Calcium oxychloride
- **Chemical Formula:** **CaOCl₂** (Formula unit mass = 40 + 16 + 2(35.5) = 127 u)
- **Preparation (Balanced Equation):**
  Ca(OH)₂(s) [Dry slaked lime] + Cl₂(g) → CaOCl₂(s) [Bleaching powder] + H₂O(l)

### Key Properties and Uses of Bleaching Powder:

1. **Yellowish-White Powder with a Sharp Chlorine Smell:** It is a pale yellowish-white powder that smells strongly of chlorine because it slowly reacts with atmospheric CO₂ (or rapidly with dilute acids) to release free chlorine gas:
   CaOCl₂(s) + H₂SO₄(aq) (dilute) → CaSO₄(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂O(l)
2. **Bleaching in Textile and Paper Industries:** Used for bleaching **cotton and linen** fabrics in the textile industry, bleaching **wood pulp** in paper mills, and bleaching washed clothes in commercial laundries.
3. **Oxidising Agent:** Used extensively as an **oxidising agent** in many chemical manufacturing processes.
4. **Disinfecting Drinking Water:** Used as a disinfectant to make drinking water **germ-free**.

> **Important Safety Rule:** **Never** mix bleaching powder or household liquid bleach with any other cleaning product—such as acidic toilet-bowl cleaners or ammonia-based glass cleaners. Mixing them immediately releases toxic **chlorine** (or chloramine) gas, which can severely injure the airways and lungs. Bleaching powder should only be handled by adults wearing gloves in a well-ventilated area.

## Part C: Baking Soda (NaHCO₃ — Sodium Hydrogencarbonate)

Now let's answer Chloe's kitchen question! The white powder used to make crispy batters rise or to tenderise beans quickly while cooking is **Baking Soda**.

- **Chemical Name:** Sodium hydrogencarbonate (or Sodium bicarbonate)
- **Chemical Formula:** **NaHCO₃** (Formula unit mass = 23 + 1 + 12 + 3(16) = 84 u)
- **Industrial Preparation (Solvay / Ammonia-Soda Process):**
  When **Carbon dioxide (CO₂)** and **Ammonia (NH₃)** gases are bubbled through cold, concentrated brine (NaCl + H₂O), sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃)—which is only **sparingly soluble** in cold water—precipitates out as fine white crystals:
  NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl (Ammonium chloride) + NaHCO₃↓ (Baking soda)

### Properties of Baking Soda and Its 3 Biggest Uses:

#### 1. What Happens When Baking Soda Is Heated During Cooking? (Action of Heat)
Baking soda is a **mild, non-corrosive basic salt** (pH ≈ 8.3). When heated during cooking or baking, it undergoes thermal decomposition to form **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)**, steam (H₂O), and **Carbon dioxide gas (CO₂)**:
2NaHCO₃(s) --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(g) + CO₂(g)↑

#### 2. Use 1 – Making Baking Powder (The Secret of Baking Soda vs. Baking Powder!)
If we bake a cake or loaf of bread using **only Baking Soda (NaHCO₃)**, the escaping CO₂ gas will make the batter rise, but the **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)** left behind is strongly alkaline and gives the cake a **bitter, soapy taste** and a **yellowish colour** (just as Chloe experienced)!
- To solve this problem, we use **Baking Powder**, which is a dry mixture of **Baking Soda (NaHCO₃)** and a **mild edible solid acid (such as Tartaric acid)**.
- As soon as baking powder is moistened with water or heated in the oven, H⁺ ions from the tartaric acid react with NaHCO₃ (and neutralise any alkaline carbonate) to release CO₂ gas, water, and a harmless, neutral-tasting sodium salt of the acid:
  NaHCO₃ + H⁺ (from edible tartaric acid) → CO₂(g)↑ + H₂O(l) + Sodium salt of acid (Neutral, not bitter!)
- The rising bubbles of **CO₂ gas** get trapped inside the dough or batter, making cakes and bread **soft, light, and spongy**, with zero bitter aftertaste from Na₂CO₃!

> **Activity 6.1 (At home with an adult).** Mix equal amounts of flour and water in two small ramekins. Into Ramekin A stir ¼ teaspoon of **plain baking soda**, and into Ramekin B stir ¼ teaspoon of **baking powder** (or baking soda + 2 drops of lemon juice). Have an adult steam or bake both ramekins (adults should handle the hot oven/steamer). Once cooled, cut both open and **compare their appearance and texture** (no need to taste): Ramekin B will be fluffier, lighter, and evenly spongy, whereas Ramekin A will look slightly yellowish and may have a soapy soda smell (caused by Na₂CO₃).

#### 3. Use 2 – As an Ingredient in Antacids (Relieving Stomach Acidity)
Because NaHCO₃ is mildly alkaline, it neutralises excess Hydrochloric acid (HCl) in the stomach and provides rapid relief from heartburn and indigestion:
NaHCO₃(s) + HCl(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l) + CO₂(g)↑

#### 4. Use 3 – In Soda-Acid Fire Extinguishers
Inside a traditional soda-acid fire extinguisher, the metal cylinder holds an aqueous solution of NaHCO₃ while a small glass bottle near the top holds Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄). When the plunger knob is struck, the acid bottle breaks and H₂SO₄ reacts rapidly with NaHCO₃ to eject a powerful stream of **CO₂ gas** and liquid:
2NaHCO₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) + 2CO₂(g)↑
- Because CO₂ gas is denser than air and neither burns nor supports combustion, it settles over the flames like a heavy **blanket**, cutting off the **supply of oxygen (O₂)** and extinguishing the fire!

## Part D: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Calculating Masses and Products in the Chlor-Alkali Process

**Problem:** In an electrolysis cell at a chlor-alkali plant, **234 g of pure Sodium chloride (NaCl)** is completely electrolysed in aqueous solution.
1. Write the balanced chemical equation and state which gases are released at the anode and cathode.
2. Calculate how many grams of Caustic soda (NaOH), Chlorine gas (Cl₂), and Hydrogen gas (H₂) are produced.
*(Atomic masses: Na = 23 u, Cl = 35.5 u, O = 16 u, H = 1 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Equation and Electrodes):**
  2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) --(Electricity)--> 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂(g)↑
  - **At the Anode (+):** Chlorine gas (Cl₂)
  - **At the Cathode (–):** Hydrogen gas (H₂)
- **Step 2 (Equation Masses):**
  - 2NaCl = 2 × (23 + 35.5) = 2 × 58.5 = 117 u
  - 2H₂O = 2 × 18 = 36 u
  - 2NaOH = 2 × (23 + 16 + 1) = 2 × 40 = 80 u
  - Cl₂ = 2 × 35.5 = 71 u
  - H₂ = 2 × 1 = 2 u
- **Step 3 (Mass Calculation):**
  - 117 g of NaCl produces 80 g of NaOH, 71 g of Cl₂, and 2 g of H₂.
  - We have 234 g of NaCl, which is exactly **234 ÷ 117 = 2 times** the equation mass!
  - Therefore:
    - Mass of NaOH formed = 80 × 2 = **160 g**
    - Mass of Cl₂ gas at the anode = 71 × 2 = **142 g**
    - Mass of H₂ gas at the cathode = 2 × 2 = **4 g**

**Check:** H₂O reacted = 36 × 2 = 72 g. Total reactants = 234 + 72 = 306 g; Total products = 160 + 142 + 4 = 306 g ✅.

### Example 2. Mass Loss and CO₂ Evolved on Heating Baking Soda

**Problem:** In a laboratory demonstration, the teacher heated **8.40 g of pure Baking soda (NaHCO₃)** in an open test tube until thermal decomposition was complete.
1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
2. Calculate how many grams of solid Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) remain in the test tube, and how many grams of CO₂ gas escape.
*(Atomic masses: Na = 23 u, H = 1 u, C = 12 u, O = 16 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Balanced Chemical Equation):**
  2NaHCO₃(s) --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(g)↑ + CO₂(g)↑
- **Step 2 (Formula Unit Masses):**
  - 2NaHCO₃ = 2 × (23 + 1 + 12 + 48) = 2 × 84 = 168 u
  - Na₂CO₃ = 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) = 106 u
  - H₂O = 18 u; CO₂ = 44 u
- **Step 3 (Mass Calculation):**
  - Scaling factor = 8.40 ÷ 168 = 0.05.
  - Solid Na₂CO₃ left in the test tube = 106 × 0.05 = **5.30 g**.
  - Mass of CO₂ gas evolved = 44 × 0.05 = **2.20 g**.
  - Mass of H₂O vapour evolved = 18 × 0.05 = **0.90 g**.

**Check:** Total mass of products = 5.30 + 2.20 + 0.90 = 8.40 g, matching the initial mass of NaHCO₃ (8.40 g) ✅.

### Example 3. Manufacturing Bleaching Powder from Slaked Lime

**Problem:** In a bleaching powder unit, excess Chlorine gas (Cl₂) is passed over **18.50 g of dry slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂**.
1. How many grams of Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) are formed?
2. How many grams of Chlorine gas (Cl₂) are consumed in this reaction?
*(Atomic masses: Ca = 40 u, O = 16 u, H = 1 u, Cl = 35.5 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Balanced Chemical Equation):**
  Ca(OH)₂(s) + Cl₂(g) → CaOCl₂(s) + H₂O(l)
- **Step 2 (Formula Unit Masses):**
  - Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2(16 + 1) = 74 u
  - Cl₂ = 2(35.5) = 71 u
  - CaOCl₂ = 40 + 16 + 71 = 127 u
  - H₂O = 18 u
- **Step 3 (Mass Calculation):**
  - Moles of Ca(OH)₂ = 18.50 ÷ 74 = 0.25 mol.
  - Mass of Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) formed = 0.25 × 127 = **31.75 g**.
  - Mass of Cl₂ gas consumed = 0.25 × 71 = **17.75 g**.

**Check:** Mass of H₂O formed = 0.25 × 18 = 4.50 g. Reactants = 18.50 + 17.75 = 36.25 g; Products = 31.75 + 4.50 = 36.25 g ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Swapping the gases liberated at the Anode and Cathode in the chlor-alkali process.**
   Remember: Chloride ions (Cl⁻) are negatively charged, so they migrate to the positive electrode—the **Anode (+)**—to release **Cl₂ gas**! **H₂ gas** is liberated at the negative electrode—the **Cathode (–)**.
2. **Mistake 2: Confusing Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) with Calcium chloride (CaCl₂).**
   Bleaching powder is **Calcium oxychloride (CaOCl₂)**, made from dry Ca(OH)₂ + Cl₂; ordinary CaCl₂ has no bleaching properties!
3. **Mistake 3: Treating Baking Soda (NaHCO₃) and Baking Powder as identical.**
   **Baking soda** is pure NaHCO₃ alone, whereas **Baking powder** is a mixture of NaHCO₃ + a mild edible solid acid (such as tartaric acid) designed to neutralise the bitter Na₂CO₃ formed on heating.
4. **Mistake 4: Writing NaOH as the product when baking soda is heated.**
   Heating NaHCO₃ produces **Na₂CO₃ (Sodium carbonate)**, H₂O, and CO₂ (2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂), never NaOH!
5. **Mistake 5: Defining "Brine" as molten (fused) NaCl.**
   **Brine** is a **concentrated aqueous solution of NaCl in water**. Electrolysing molten NaCl (with no water present) cannot produce NaOH or H₂ (it yields only metallic Na and Cl₂ gas)!

## Quick Recap

- **Common Salt (NaCl):** Obtained by evaporating seawater and mining underground **Rock Salt**. It is the primary raw material for manufacturing NaOH, CaOCl₂, NaHCO₃, and Na₂CO₃·10H₂O.
- **Chlor-Alkali Process:** Electrolysis of brine, NaCl(aq), yields three key products:
  2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) --(Electricity)--> 2NaOH(aq) (near cathode) + Cl₂(g) (at anode) + H₂(g) (at cathode)
- **Bleaching Powder (CaOCl₂ — Calcium oxychloride):**
  Ca(OH)₂ (dry slaked lime) + Cl₂ → CaOCl₂ + H₂O. Used for bleaching textiles and wood pulp, as an oxidising agent, and for disinfecting drinking water.
- **Baking Soda (NaHCO₃ — Sodium hydrogencarbonate):**
  - Preparation: NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃
  - Heating: 2NaHCO₃ --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑
  - **Baking Powder** = NaHCO₃ + Mild edible acid (Tartaric acid) so that CO₂ makes cakes spongy while neutralising bitter Na₂CO₃.
  - Also used as an antacid and in **Soda-Acid Fire Extinguishers**.

### Answers

1. A concentrated aqueous solution of sodium chloride (NaCl) in water is called **Brine**.
   Its electrolysis is called the **Chlor-alkali process** because its two principal products are **Chlor** (Chlorine, Cl₂) and **Alkali** (Sodium hydroxide, NaOH):
   2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) --(Electricity)--> 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂(g)↑

2. (a) **At the Anode (+):** **Chlorine gas (Cl₂)** — Uses: (i) Disinfecting drinking water and swimming pools, (ii) Manufacturing bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) and PVC.
   (b) **At the Cathode (–):** **Hydrogen gas (H₂)** — Uses: (i) Clean rocket fuel, (ii) Manufacturing ammonia (NH₃) for fertilisers and hydrogenating vegetable oils (margarine).
   (c) **Near the Cathode:** **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** — Uses: (i) Manufacturing soaps and detergents, (ii) Paper manufacturing and making artificial fibres (rayon).

3. - **Chemical Name:** Calcium oxychloride; **Formula:** CaOCl₂.
   - **Preparation:** By passing Chlorine gas (Cl₂) over dry slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂:
     Ca(OH)₂(s) + Cl₂(g) → CaOCl₂(s) + H₂O(l)
   - **Uses:** (i) Bleaching cotton and linen in the textile industry, (ii) Bleaching wood pulp in paper mills, (iii) Disinfecting drinking water to make it germ-free and serving as an oxidising agent in chemical industries.

4. Carbon dioxide (CO₂) and moisture present in open air react slowly with Bleaching powder (CaOCl₂) to release free **Chlorine gas (Cl₂)** (CaOCl₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + Cl₂↑). This escaping Cl₂ gas gives it a strong chlorine smell. If it is not stored in an airtight container, it gradually loses its active chlorine and its bleaching power.

5. - **Balanced Equation:** NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃↓
   - **Reason:** In cold aqueous solution, Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) is **sparingly soluble**, whereas Ammonium chloride (NH₄Cl) is highly soluble. Therefore, NaHCO₃ crystallises out first as a white solid and can be separated by filtration.

6. - **Baking Soda** is a single pure compound — **Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃)**.
   - **Baking Powder** is a dry mixture — **Baking Soda (NaHCO₃) + a mild edible solid acid (such as Tartaric acid)**.
   - If a baker adds **only Baking Soda** to a cake batter, heating decomposes it as follows:
     2NaHCO₃(s) --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) + H₂O(g) + CO₂(g)↑
     The **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃)** left behind makes the cake taste **bitter (soapy)** and gives it a yellowish tinge!
   - When **Baking Powder** is used, H⁺ ions from the tartaric acid neutralise the alkaline carbonate (NaHCO₃ + H⁺ → CO₂↑ + H₂O + neutral sodium salt of the acid), producing a soft, spongy cake with no bitter taste.

7. Inside a soda-acid fire extinguisher, Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃) solution and Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) are kept in separate compartments. Pressing the knob mixes them together, rapidly generating a large volume of **CO₂ gas**:
   2NaHCO₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) + 2CO₂(g)↑
   The heavy CO₂ gas forms a non-combustible blanket over the burning material, **cutting off the supply of air (oxygen)** and extinguishing the fire.

8. Balanced Equation: 2NaCl(aq) + 2H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + Cl₂(g)↑ + H₂(g)↑
   - Masses in Equation: 2NaCl = 117 u, 2NaOH = 80 u, Cl₂ = 71 u, H₂ = 2 u.
   - Scaling factor = 35.10 ÷ 117 = 0.30.
   - (a) Mass of NaOH produced = 80 × 0.30 = **24.00 g**.
   - (b) Mass of Cl₂ liberated at the anode = 71 × 0.30 = **21.30 g** ✅ (Check: Reacted H₂O = 36 × 0.30 = 10.80 g, H₂ = 2 × 0.30 = 0.60 g; Reactants 35.10 + 10.80 = 45.90 g = Products 24.00 + 21.30 + 0.60 = 45.90 g ✅).

9. Balanced Equation: Ca(OH)₂(s) + Cl₂(g) → CaOCl₂(s) + H₂O(l)
   - Formula Unit Masses: Ca(OH)₂ = 74 u, Cl₂ = 71 u, CaOCl₂ = 127 u, H₂O = 18 u.
   - Moles of CaOCl₂ to be manufactured = 50.80 ÷ 127 = 0.40 mol.
   - Mass of Ca(OH)₂ required = 0.40 × 74 = **29.60 g**.
   - Mass of H₂O formed = 0.40 × 18 = **7.20 g** ✅ (Check: Cl₂ used = 0.40 × 71 = 28.40 g; Reactants 29.60 + 28.40 = 58.00 g = Products 50.80 + 7.20 = 58.00 g ✅).

10. Balanced Equation: 2NaHCO₃(aq) + H₂SO₄(aq) → Na₂SO₄(aq) + 2H₂O(l) + 2CO₂(g)↑
    - Equation Masses: 2NaHCO₃ = 168 u, H₂SO₄ = 98 u, Na₂SO₄ = 142 u, 2H₂O = 36 u, 2CO₂ = 88 u.
    - Scaling factor = 25.20 ÷ 168 = 0.15.
    - Mass of CO₂ gas released = 88 × 0.15 = **13.20 g**.
    - Mass of Na₂SO₄ formed = 142 × 0.15 = **21.30 g** ✅ (Check: Reacted H₂SO₄ = 98 × 0.15 = 14.70 g, 2H₂O = 36 × 0.15 = 5.40 g; Reactants 25.20 + 14.70 = 39.90 g = Products 21.30 + 5.40 + 13.20 = 39.90 g ✅).
