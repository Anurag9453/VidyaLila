# Topic 7: Treasures from Common Salt 2: Washing Soda, Plaster of Paris, and Chapter Summary

In the Class 9 Water chapter ([water-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part4.md)), we learned that many sparkling salt crystals—such as blue CuSO₄·5H₂O and green FeSO₄·7H₂O—feel completely dry to the touch, yet every single formula unit contains a fixed number of chemically bound water molecules (**Water of Crystallisation**). In our concluding topic today, we will explore the full story behind two of the most famous industrial stars of Water of Crystallisation: **Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)**, which cleans clothes and softens hard water, and **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O)**, which immobilises fractured bones so they heal straight.

---

## Dr. Foster's Cast Room and the Bandage That Turns to Stone in 15 Minutes

After taking a tumble during a school football match, **Sam** suffered a hairline fracture in his right wrist. In the cast room of the orthopaedic clinic, **Dr. Foster** opened a tightly sealed, moisture-proof metal tin, took out a roll of gauze bandage coated with a fine white powder, dipped it into a bowl of lukewarm water for just 5 seconds, and wrapped it neatly around Sam's wrist.

To Sam's amazement, within 10 to 15 minutes the soft, wet bandage grew slightly warm and hardened into a **rock-solid white cast**!

Sam asked curiously, "Dr. Foster, water usually makes paper and flour soft and soggy—how did this white powder turn hard as stone the moment it got wet? And why did you keep the bandage roll locked inside an airtight tin?"

Dr. Foster smiled: "Sam, this white powder is called **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O)**. You can think of it as thirsty **Gypsum**! When gypsum rock was heated in a kiln, most of its water was driven off. The moment I dipped the bandage in water, the powder drank back its lost **Water of Crystallisation** and locked together into a rigid network of **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)** crystals! If I left the roll exposed to open air, it would absorb moisture straight from the air and turn to stone right inside the tin." Let's explore the chemistry behind Sam's cast and Washing Soda.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Water of crystallisation** | The fixed number of water molecules chemically bound within **one formula unit** of a crystalline salt | 5 in CuSO₄·5H₂O, 10 in Na₂CO₃·10H₂O |
| **Anhydrous salt** | A salt that has lost all its water of crystallisation (usually upon heating) | White CuSO₄, Soda ash (Na₂CO₃) |
| **Soda ash** | Anhydrous sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) — a dry white powder with no water of crystallisation | Obtained by heating baking soda |
| **Washing soda** | Sodium carbonate decahydrate (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) — a basic salt containing 10 molecules of water of crystallisation | Used in laundry and for softening hard water |
| **Gypsum** | Naturally occurring hydrated calcium sulphate (CaSO₄·2H₂O — 2 water molecules per formula unit) | Raw material used to make Plaster of Paris |
| **Plaster of Paris (PoP)** | Calcium sulphate hemihydrate (CaSO₄·½H₂O or (CaSO₄)₂·H₂O) | Used for orthopaedic casts, statues, and decorative ceilings |
| **Dead-burnt plaster** | Anhydrous calcium sulphate (CaSO₄) formed when gypsum is heated above 373 K (100 °C); it does not set with water | Overheated, unusable plaster |

## Part A: Water of Crystallisation (Quick Bridge from Class 9) and Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)

### 1. Water of Crystallisation: A Quick Refresher
In [Class 9 Water Topic 4](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part4.md), we saw that when **blue CuSO₄·5H₂O crystals** are heated in a dry boiling tube, tiny water droplets condense near the cooler upper wall of the tube and the blue crystals turn into a **white powder of anhydrous CuSO₄**. Adding just 2–3 drops of water back onto the white powder immediately restores its vibrant **blue** colour:
CuSO₄·5H₂O(s) [Blue] ⇌ CuSO₄(s) [White] + 5H₂O

These fixed water molecules (5 in CuSO₄·5H₂O, 7 in FeSO₄·7H₂O, 10 in Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, and 2 in CaSO₄·2H₂O) give hydrated crystals their geometric shape and, in many cases, their characteristic colour.

---

### 2. Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O — Sodium Carbonate Decahydrate)

In [Part 6](acids-bases-and-salts-part6.md), we learned that heating Baking soda (NaHCO₃) produces anhydrous **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃ — Soda Ash)**. When this Soda Ash is dissolved in water and allowed to crystallise (**recrystallisation**), we obtain crystals of **Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)**!

- **Chemical Name:** Sodium carbonate decahydrate (*deca* = 10)
- **Chemical Formula:** **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O**
- **Formula Unit Mass:**
  - Na₂CO₃ part = 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106 u
  - 10H₂O part = 10 × (2 × 1 + 16) = 10 × 18 = 180 u
  - **Total Formula Unit Mass of Na₂CO₃·10H₂O** = 106 + 180 = **286 u**

#### The 3-Step Route from Common Salt (NaCl) to Washing Soda:
1. **Step 1 (Brine to Baking Soda):**
   NaCl + H₂O + CO₂ + NH₃ → NH₄Cl + NaHCO₃↓
2. **Step 2 (Heating Baking Soda to Make Soda Ash):**
   2NaHCO₃(s) --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) [Anhydrous Soda Ash] + H₂O(g)↑ + CO₂(g)↑
3. **Step 3 (Recrystallising Soda Ash to Make Washing Soda):**
   Na₂CO₃(s) + 10H₂O(l) → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O(s) [Washing Soda]

#### Four Major Uses of Washing Soda:
1. **Glass, Soap, and Paper Industries:** Used in large quantities in manufacturing glass, soap, and paper.
2. **Manufacturing Sodium Compounds:** Used to manufacture important sodium compounds such as **Borax (Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O)**.
3. **Domestic Cleaning Agent:** Used for household laundry and removing stubborn grease and oil stains.
4. **Removing Permanent Hardness of Water:** As we learned in [Class 9 Water Topic 3](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part3.md), washing soda reacts with the dissolved chlorides and sulphates of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ in hard water, precipitating them out as insoluble carbonates (CaCO₃↓, MgCO₃↓) and softening the water:
   CaCl₂(aq) [Hard water] + Na₂CO₃(aq) [Washing soda] → CaCO₃(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)

## Part B: Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O — Calcium Sulphate Hemihydrate)

Now let's examine the complete chemistry of the white powder in Dr. Foster's cast room:

- **Chemical Name:** Calcium sulphate hemihydrate (*hemi* = half, *hydrate* = water)
- **Chemical Formula:** **CaSO₄·½H₂O** (or **(CaSO₄)₂·H₂O**)
- **Formula Unit Masses:**
  - **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O):** 40 + 32 + 4(16) + 2(18) = 136 + 36 = **172 u**
  - **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O):** 40 + 32 + 4(16) + 0.5(18) = 136 + 9 = **145 u** (or (CaSO₄)₂·H₂O = 290 u)

### 1. How Is Plaster of Paris Prepared? (The 373 K Temperature Rule!)

When **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)** is heated in a kiln at a carefully controlled temperature of **373 K (100 °C)**, it loses **1½ (3/2) of its 2 water molecules** and turns into the fine white powder called **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O)**:

CaSO₄·2H₂O(s) [Gypsum] --(Heat carefully at 373 K / 100 °C)--> CaSO₄·½H₂O(s) [Plaster of Paris] + 1½H₂O(g)↑

*(Or written with whole-number coefficients: 2(CaSO₄·2H₂O) --(373 K)--> (CaSO₄)₂·H₂O + 3H₂O↑)*

> **Crucial Question — What Happens If Gypsum Is Heated Above 373 K (100 °C)?**
> If the temperature rises significantly above 373 K (100 °C), **all of the water of crystallisation** is driven off, leaving behind completely **anhydrous Calcium sulphate (CaSO₄)**, known as **"Dead-Burnt Plaster"**. Dead-burnt plaster loses its ability to set into a hard mass when mixed with water!

---

### 2. How Can a Formula Contain "Half a Molecule of Water" (½H₂O)?

Can half a water molecule (½H₂O) exist on its own, chopped in two? **Of course not!**
Writing CaSO₄·½H₂O simply means that inside the crystal lattice, **two (2) formula units of CaSO₄ share one (1) single molecule of water (H₂O)** between them:

```
   WHAT CaSO₄·½H₂O REALLY MEANS: 2 CaSO₄ UNITS SHARE 1 H₂O MOLECULE!

         [ CaSO₄ ] ───────┐
                          ├───► [ H — O — H ]   (1 Water Molecule Shared)
         [ CaSO₄ ] ───────┘

   2 Formula Units of CaSO₄  +  1 Molecule of H₂O  =  (CaSO₄)₂·H₂O
   Therefore, PER SINGLE (1) CaSO₄ unit, the share =  CaSO₄·½H₂O !
```

> **Figure 7.1** – Because two CaSO₄ formula units share a single H₂O molecule between them, the ratio per CaSO₄ formula unit is written as ½H₂O.

---

### 3. Why Does Plaster of Paris Harden When Mixed with Water? (Setting of PoP)

When water is added to Plaster of Paris powder, it reabsorbs 1½ molecules of water per formula unit and within **10 to 15 minutes** recrystallises into a dense, interlocking network of needle-like crystals of **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)**, forming a **hard solid mass**:

CaSO₄·½H₂O(s) [Plaster of Paris] + 1½H₂O(l) → CaSO₄·2H₂O(s) [Gypsum — Hard solid]

- **Why must it be stored in a Moisture-Proof Container?** Because if Plaster of Paris is left exposed to damp air or moisture, it slowly absorbs water vapour from the air and turns into hard **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)** right inside its package, rendering it useless for future moulding or casts!
- **Major Uses:**
  1. Used by orthopaedic doctors to make **plaster casts** that hold **fractured bones** immobile in the right position while they heal.
  2. Used for making **toys**, **statues**, dental and ornamental casts, and decorative pieces.
  3. Used for making the surfaces of walls and **ceilings smooth** before painting, and for moulding ornamental ceiling designs.
  4. Used in making blackboard chalk and **fire-proofing** building panels.

## Part D: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Percentage of Water of Crystallisation and Mass Loss in Washing Soda

**Problem:**
1. In Class 9 we calculated the mass percentage of water in washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O). Using that result, estimate how many grams of water are present in 14.30 g of pure washing soda crystals.
2. If **14.30 g of pure Washing soda crystals** are heated strongly until they turn completely into anhydrous Soda ash (Na₂CO₃), how many grams of anhydrous Na₂CO₃ remain behind and how many grams of water escape as steam?
*(Atomic masses: Na = 23 u, C = 12 u, O = 16 u, H = 1 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Recall the Class 9 result):** Na₂CO₃·10H₂O = 106 + 180 = 286 u, and water accounts for **62.94%** by mass ([water-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part4.md), Example 2). So 14.30 g of crystals should contain 14.30 × 0.6294 = **9.00 g** of water. Now let's verify this directly from the chemical equation:
- **Step 2 (Heating 14.30 g of Washing Soda):**
  - Equation: Na₂CO₃·10H₂O(s) --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) + 10H₂O(g)↑
  - Moles of Washing soda = 14.30 ÷ 286 = 0.05 mol.
  - Mass of anhydrous Na₂CO₃ left = 0.05 × 106 = **5.30 g**.
  - Mass of water vapour lost = 0.05 × 180 = **9.00 g**.

**Check:** 5.30 g (Na₂CO₃) + 9.00 g (H₂O) = 14.30 g (initial mass of Washing soda!), and (9.00 ÷ 14.30) × 100 = 62.94% ✅.

### Example 2. Preparing Plaster of Paris from Gypsum

**Problem:** In a factory kiln, **34.40 g of pure Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)** is heated carefully at 373 K (100 °C) to convert it into Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O).
1. Write the balanced chemical equation.
2. Calculate how many grams of Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) are formed, and how many grams of water escape as steam.
*(Atomic masses: Ca = 40 u, S = 32 u, O = 16 u, H = 1 u)*

**Solution:**
- **Step 1 (Balanced Chemical Equation):**
  CaSO₄·2H₂O(s) --(373 K)--> CaSO₄·½H₂O(s) + 1½H₂O(g)↑
- **Step 2 (Formula Unit Masses):**
  - Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) = 40 + 32 + 64 + 2(18) = 136 + 36 = 172 u
  - Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) = 136 + 0.5(18) = 136 + 9 = 145 u
  - Water lost (1.5 H₂O) = 1.5 × 18 = 27 u
- **Step 3 (Mass Calculation):**
  - Moles of Gypsum = 34.40 ÷ 172 = 0.20 mol.
  - Mass of Plaster of Paris formed = 0.20 × 145 = **29.00 g**.
  - Mass of water vapour lost = 0.20 × 27 = **5.40 g**.

**Check:** 29.00 g (Plaster of Paris) + 5.40 g (Water lost) = 34.40 g (Initial Gypsum) ✅.

### Example 3. "Who Am I?" — Compound Identification Puzzle

**Problem:** A white sodium compound **'A'** (Formula mass = 84 u) is used for making cakes fluffy and as an antacid. When **'A'** is heated, it forms a new solid compound **'B'** (Formula mass = 106 u) and releases a gas **'C'** that turns limewater milky. When **'B'** is recrystallised from water, it yields a hydrated salt **'D'** (Formula mass = 286 u) used for laundry and for removing the permanent hardness of water.
Identify **'A'**, **'B'**, **'C'**, and **'D'**, and write balanced chemical equations for all the conversions.

**Solution:**
1. **Compound 'A' (84 u, antacid & baking):** **Baking Soda — Sodium hydrogencarbonate (NaHCO₃)** (23 + 1 + 12 + 48 = 84 u).
2. **Compound 'B' (106 u) and Gas 'C':** Heating NaHCO₃ gives:
   2NaHCO₃(s) [A] --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) [B] + H₂O(g) + CO₂(g)↑ [C]
   - **'B'** = Anhydrous **Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃, Soda ash)** (46 + 12 + 48 = 106 u).
   - **'C'** = **Carbon dioxide gas (CO₂)**, which turns limewater milky:
     Ca(OH)₂(aq) + CO₂(g) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + H₂O(l)
3. **Compound 'D' (286 u, removes permanent hardness):** Recrystallisation of Na₂CO₃ gives:
   Na₂CO₃(s) [B] + 10H₂O(l) → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O(s) [D]
   - **'D'** = **Washing Soda — Sodium carbonate decahydrate (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)** (106 + 180 = 286 u).

**Check:** Formula unit masses: A (NaHCO₃) = 84 u, B (Na₂CO₃) = 106 u, C (CO₂) = 44 u, D (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) = 286 u — every clue matches 100% ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Writing the formula of Washing Soda as just Na₂CO₃.**
   Plain Na₂CO₃ is **Soda Ash** (anhydrous sodium carbonate)! **Washing Soda** contains 10 molecules of water of crystallisation: **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O**.
2. **Mistake 2: Swapping the formulas of Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) and Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O).**
   Remember: **Gypsum** is the natural mineral rock containing **a full 2H₂O**; heating it at 373 K drives off water to leave **half a water molecule (½H₂O)** in **Plaster of Paris**!
3. **Mistake 3: Writing the heating temperature of Gypsum as 373 °C instead of 373 K.**
   The temperature is **373 K**, which equals **100 °C** (373 − 273 = 100 °C)! Heating gypsum to 373 °C would drive off all its water and ruin it as **dead-burnt plaster (CaSO₄)**!
4. **Mistake 4: Writing CaSO₄·½H₂O + ½H₂O → CaSO₄·H₂O.**
   Gypsum has 2H₂O! Turning ½H₂O into 2H₂O requires adding **1½H₂O (3/2 H₂O)**: CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O.
5. **Mistake 5: Thinking that Water of Crystallisation makes a salt feel wet.**
   Not at all! Water of crystallisation is locked inside the crystal lattice; unless heated, hydrated crystals feel completely **dry** to the touch.

## Quick Recap

- **Water of Crystallisation:** The fixed number of water molecules bound within one formula unit of a salt crystal (5 in CuSO₄·5H₂O, 7 in FeSO₄·7H₂O, 10 in Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, 2 in CaSO₄·2H₂O).
- **Washing Soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O, 286 u):** Prepared by heating baking soda to get Na₂CO₃ and then recrystallising it from water (Na₂CO₃ + 10H₂O → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O); used in the glass, soap, and paper industries, borax manufacture, and removing permanent hardness of water.
- **Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O, 145 u):** Prepared by heating Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O, 172 u) at 373 K (100 °C); sets into a hard mass of Gypsum within 15 minutes when mixed with water (CaSO₄·½H₂O + 1½H₂O → CaSO₄·2H₂O).

### Answers

1. All three are forms of calcium sulphate differing only in their water of crystallisation:
   - **Gypsum:** CaSO₄·2H₂O — 2 H₂O molecules per CaSO₄ unit; since it is already fully hydrated and set, it does not set again when mixed with water.
   - **Plaster of Paris:** CaSO₄·½H₂O — ½ H₂O molecule per CaSO₄ unit; when mixed with water, it absorbs 1½H₂O to form gypsum and **sets into a hard mass**.
   - **Dead-burnt plaster:** CaSO₄ — 0 water molecules; it **does not set** when mixed with water.

2. Washing soda is obtained from Baking soda in two steps:
   - **Step 1 (Thermal decomposition of Baking soda):**
     2NaHCO₃(s) --(Heat)--> Na₂CO₃(s) [Soda ash] + H₂O(g)↑ + CO₂(g)↑
   - **Step 2 (Recrystallisation of Soda ash):**
     Na₂CO₃(s) + 10H₂O(l) → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O(s) [Washing soda]
   - **Four Uses:** (i) Used in the glass, soap, and paper industries, (ii) Used in manufacturing sodium compounds such as **borax**, (iii) Used as a domestic cleaning agent, (iv) Used for removing the **permanent hardness** of water.

3. - **Chemical Name:** Calcium sulphate hemihydrate (CaSO₄·½H₂O).
   - **Preparation:** By heating Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) carefully at 373 K (100 °C):
     CaSO₄·2H₂O(s) [Gypsum] --(373 K / 100 °C)--> CaSO₄·½H₂O(s) [Plaster of Paris] + 1½H₂O(g)↑
   - **Why Temperature Control at 373 K Is Essential:** If Gypsum is heated above 373 K (100 °C), all of its water of crystallisation is driven off, forming anhydrous CaSO₄ (**Dead-burnt plaster**), which will not set into a hard mass when mixed with water.

4. Half a water molecule cannot exist by itself; writing CaSO₄·½H₂O means that in the crystal structure, **two (2) formula units of CaSO₄ share one (1) molecule of water (H₂O)** between them, giving the formula (CaSO₄)₂·H₂O. Therefore, the share per single (1) CaSO₄ formula unit is written as ½H₂O.

5. Plaster of Paris (CaSO₄·½H₂O) readily absorbs moisture upon contact with damp air and turns into hard, solid **Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O)**:
   CaSO₄·½H₂O(s) + 1½H₂O(l) → CaSO₄·2H₂O(s) [Gypsum — Hard solid mass]
   If left exposed to moisture, it sets prematurely in its container and becomes useless for making casts or moulds later.

6. Washing soda (Na₂CO₃) reacts with the dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions in hard water by double displacement, precipitating them out as **insoluble carbonates (CaCO₃↓ or MgCO₃↓)**:
   CaSO₄(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + Na₂SO₄(aq)
   MgCl₂(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → MgCO₃(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)
   Once the free Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions are removed, the water becomes soft and lathers easily with soap.

7. (a) **CaSO₄·½H₂O:** Formula unit mass = 136 + 9 = 145 u; mass of water = 9 u.
   **% of Water** = (9 ÷ 145) × 100 = **6.21%** (approx.). In gypsum it was 20.93% (Class 9), showing that PoP retains much less water.
   (b) Every 172 g of gypsum loses 1½H₂O = 27 g of water, so the **percentage decrease in mass** = (27 ÷ 172) × 100 = **15.70%** (approx.) ✅.
   (Check: 172 g − 27 g = 145 g, which is the formula unit mass of PoP ✅.)

8. Balanced Equation: CaSO₄·½H₂O(s) + 1½H₂O(l) → CaSO₄·2H₂O(s)
   - Formula Unit Masses: CaSO₄·½H₂O = 145 u, 1.5 H₂O = 27 u, CaSO₄·2H₂O = 172 u.
   - Moles of Plaster of Paris = 58.00 ÷ 145 = 0.40 mol.
   - (a) Mass of water absorbed = 0.40 × 27 = **10.80 g**.
   - (b) Total mass of hardened Gypsum cast = 0.40 × 172 = **68.80 g** ✅ (Check: 58.00 g + 10.80 g = 68.80 g ✅).

9. Balanced Equation: Na₂CO₃(s) + 10H₂O(l) → Na₂CO₃·10H₂O(s)
   - Formula Unit Masses: Na₂CO₃ = 106 u, 10H₂O = 180 u, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O = 286 u.
   - Moles of Washing soda to be produced = 71.50 ÷ 286 = 0.25 mol.
   - Mass of anhydrous Na₂CO₃ required = 0.25 × 106 = **26.50 g**.
   - Mass of water incorporated = 0.25 × 180 = **45.00 g** ✅ (Check: 26.50 g + 45.00 g = 71.50 g ✅).

10. Overheating drives off all the water from gypsum: CaSO₄·2H₂O(s) --(well above 373 K)--> CaSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(g)↑
    - Formula Unit Masses: CaSO₄·2H₂O = 172 u, CaSO₄ = 136 u, 2H₂O = 36 u.
    - Moles of gypsum = 8.60 ÷ 172 = 0.05 mol.
    - Mass of dead-burnt plaster (CaSO₄) = 0.05 × 136 = **6.80 g**.
    - Mass of water lost = 0.05 × 36 = **1.80 g** ✅ (Check: 6.80 g + 1.80 g = 8.60 g ✅). This anhydrous CaSO₄ will not set when mixed with water.
