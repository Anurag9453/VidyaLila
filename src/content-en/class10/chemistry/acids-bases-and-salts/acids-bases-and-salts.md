# Topic 1: Acids, Bases, and Indicators: How We Identify Them

In Class 9, we saw that when an **acid** (such as HCl) and a **base** (such as NaOH) meet, they swap partners to form a **salt** (NaCl) and **water** (H₂O) ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)). However, one big question remained unanswered: **what exactly are acids and bases**, and how can we tell whether an unknown liquid is an acid or a base without ever tasting or touching it? In our opening topic today, we will uncover the secrets of chemical identification—from the kitchen table to the school laboratory.

---

## The Mystery of the Turmeric Stain on a White Napkin

It was Sunday afternoon lunch. Leo was sitting beside his aunt, Dr. Clara, enjoying a warm bowl of yellow curry and rice. While chatting, his spoon slipped and a drop of golden-yellow curry landed squarely on his crisp white cotton napkin, leaving a **bright yellow stain**.

Leo hurried over to the sink and rubbed a bar of **hand soap** directly onto the spot. The moment the soapy lather touched the turmeric stain, Leo's eyes widened in disbelief: the bright yellow spot suddenly turned a **deep reddish-brown**!

"Aunt Clara, look! The moment I rubbed soap on it, the yellow turmeric stain turned reddish-brown! Did I ruin the napkin forever?" Leo asked nervously.

Dr. Clara, who worked as a quality-control chemist at a pharmaceutical lab, walked over to the sink with a smile. First, she had Leo rinse the napkin thoroughly with plenty of running water, and then she brought a **small wedge of fresh lemon** from the kitchen and squeezed two drops of juice onto the reddish-brown spot. In the blink of an eye, the reddish-brown colour vanished and the stain turned back to **yellow**!

Leo was amazed: "Reddish-brown with soap, and back to yellow with lemon juice? Does turmeric have a built-in colour switch inside it?"

Dr. Clara chuckled, "You can think of it just like a switch, Leo! In chemistry, substances like this are called **indicators**. Soap contains a **base** and lemon juice contains an **acid**, and turmeric changes its colour to announce which one it has just met." Let's start with Leo's napkin and understand how this entire detective game works.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Acid** | A chemical substance that turns blue litmus red (and edible, safe acids have a sour taste) | Citric acid in lemons, acetic acid in vinegar, HCl in the lab |
| **Base** | A chemical substance that turns red litmus blue and neutralises the effect of an acid | Base in soap, baking soda, Ca(OH)₂, NaOH |
| **Organic acid** | A mild, naturally occurring acid found in plants or animals | Citric acid (lemons), lactic acid (yoghurt/curd), tartaric acid (tamarind/grapes) |
| **Mineral acid** | A strong laboratory acid prepared from minerals found in the Earth's crust | Hydrochloric acid (HCl), sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), nitric acid (HNO₃) |
| **Indicator** | A substance that changes its **colour** or **odour (smell)** to show whether a solution is acidic or basic | Litmus, turmeric, phenolphthalein, methyl orange |
| **Litmus** | A natural indicator (a purple dye) extracted from lichens (organisms formed by a partnership between a fungus and an alga) | Red in acid, blue in base |
| **Synthetic indicator** | A human-made indicator synthesised in a laboratory or factory | Phenolphthalein, methyl orange |
| **Olfactory indicator** | An indicator whose **odour (smell)**—rather than its colour—changes differently in acidic and basic solutions | Onion, vanilla extract, clove oil |

## Part A: Kitchen Acids and Bases vs. Laboratory Chemicals

Historically, people first recognised acids and bases by their **taste** and **feel**:

1. **Acids:** The word *acid* comes from the Latin word *acidus*, meaning **sour**. Lemons, oranges, tamarind, unripe green apples, plain yoghurt (curd), and vinegar all owe their tangy sourness to natural (organic) acids.
2. **Bases:** If you rub a pinch of baking soda between wet fingers, it feels **soapy or slippery**, and safe household bases taste **bitter**.

> **The Golden Safety Rule:** Tasting lemon juice or yoghurt in the kitchen is completely normal, but **you must NEVER taste or touch any chemical from an unknown bottle or in a science laboratory with bare hands!** Laboratory mineral acids (such as HCl, H₂SO₄, and HNO₃) and strong bases (such as NaOH and KOH) burn skin and tissue severely—they are **corrosive**. That is why chemists rely on **indicators**, never on their tongue or fingers, to identify chemicals.

### Which Natural Acid Is Found Where?

This reference table is worth memorising because it links everyday foods and stings to the specific organic acids inside them:

| Natural Source | Acid Present | Notable Fact |
|---|---|---|
| Vinegar | Acetic acid (CH₃COOH) | Gives pickles and salad dressings their sharp tang |
| Lemons and Oranges (Citrus fruits) | Citric acid | Provides a sour taste alongside Vitamin C |
| Tamarind and Grapes | Tartaric acid | Also used as the dry acid component in baking powder |
| Tomatoes and Spinach | Oxalic acid | Useful for removing stubborn rust stains from fabrics |
| Sour milk or Yoghurt (Curd) | Lactic acid | Produced by bacteria fermenting milk sugar (lactose) |
| Apples | Malic acid | Present in higher amounts in crisp, unripe apples |
| Red ant sting and Stinging nettle leaf | Methanoic acid (Formic acid) | Causes the sharp burning pain and swelling after a sting |

And here are some familiar **bases** along with their chemical formulas (built using the Class 9 Chapter 3 criss-cross rule, [language-of-chemistry-part5.md](../../../class9/chemistry/language-of-chemistry/language-of-chemistry-part5.md)):

- **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** and **Potassium hydroxide (KOH):** used in making soap and paper (extremely strong and corrosive).
- **Calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂ (Slaked lime):** used for whitewashing walls and neutralising acidic soil.
- **Magnesium hydroxide, Mg(OH)₂ (Milk of Magnesia):** a gentle white suspension taken as an antacid to relieve stomach acidity.
- **Ammonium hydroxide, NH₄OH:** used in many household glass-cleaning sprays (has a sharp, pungent smell; keep away from eyes).

## Part B: Colour-Changing Detectives: Natural and Synthetic Indicators

When we cannot taste a liquid, we add a drop of an **indicator** to the solution—or dip a strip of indicator paper into a drop of it. Indicators fall into two broad categories: **natural indicators** extracted from plants, and **synthetic indicators** manufactured in laboratories.

### 1. Litmus: The Most Famous Natural Indicator

Litmus is a **purple** dye extracted from **lichens** (small composite organisms formed by a partnership between a fungus and an alga, growing on rocks and tree bark). When a solution is neither acidic nor basic (such as pure distilled water), litmus keeps its natural **purple** colour. For convenience in the lab, litmus is supplied as paper strips pre-dyed in two colours: **Blue litmus paper** and **Red litmus paper**.

- An **acid** turns **blue** litmus **red**. *(Easy memory trick: **A**cid → **B**lue to **R**ed, or **ABR**—red is the colour of danger!)*
- A **base** turns **red** litmus **blue**. *(Trick: **B**ase → **B**lue!)*
- In a **neutral** solution (such as pure water or common salt solution), neither blue litmus nor red litmus changes colour.

### 2. Turmeric, Hibiscus (China Rose), and Red Cabbage

- **Turmeric:** Its natural colour is **yellow**. In both acidic and neutral solutions it **remains yellow**, but the moment it meets a **base**, it turns **reddish-brown**! That is exactly what happened on Leo's napkin: soap (a base) turned the turmeric stain reddish-brown, and lemon juice (an acid) neutralised the soap's base and restored the yellow colour.
- **Hibiscus (China rose) extract:** Steeping red hibiscus petals in warm water produces a pale pink extract. It turns **dark pink (magenta)** in an **acid** and **green** in a **base**!
- **Red cabbage extract:** Naturally purplish-red. It turns **red** in an **acid** and **greenish-yellow** in a **base**.

> **Activity 1.1 (At home with an adult): Make a Turmeric Greeting Card.**
> Mix a spoonful of turmeric powder with a little water to make a thick yellow paste. Spread it evenly over a sheet of white card stock and let it dry—your **turmeric indicator paper** is ready! Now dissolve a little **laundry soap** in a small bowl of water (do not drink it or bring it near your eyes). Dip a cotton swab into the soapy water and paint a flower on the yellow card: wherever the soap touches, a **reddish-brown** flower blooms! Next, dip a fresh cotton swab in **lemon juice** and touch the centre of the reddish-brown flower: that spot immediately turns back to **yellow**. Wash your hands when finished.

### 3. Two Essential Lab Synthetic Indicators: Phenolphthalein and Methyl Orange

In school chemistry laboratories, these two synthetic indicators are used all the time:

- **Phenolphthalein:** Its solution is **colourless** like water. In an **acidic** or **neutral** solution it **remains colourless**, but as soon as it meets a **base**, it turns a vibrant **pink**!
- **Methyl orange:** Its neutral solution is **orange**. In an **acid**, it turns **red (or pinkish-red)**, and in a **base**, it turns **yellow**!

Here is a complete summary chart of all 5 major indicators at a glance:

```
   INDICATOR COLOUR WHEEL (ACID vs. NEUTRAL vs. BASE)

   Indicator          In Acid            In Neutral         In Base
   --------------------------------------------------------------------------
   Litmus             Red                Purple             Blue
   Turmeric           Yellow             Yellow             Reddish-brown
   Hibiscus           Dark Pink/Magenta  Pale Pink          Green
   Phenolphthalein    Colourless         Colourless         Pink
   Methyl Orange      Red                Orange             Yellow
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Colours of the five main indicators in acidic, neutral, and basic media. Notice that both turmeric and phenolphthalein show the same colour in acidic and neutral solutions (yellow and colourless, respectively), so telling an acid apart from a neutral liquid using only one of them requires a clever two-step trick (which we will see in Part D).

## Part C: Identifying by Smell: Olfactory Indicators

Suppose a student is visually impaired. Can they still identify acids and bases independently in the chemistry lab? Absolutely! Certain substances have a **characteristic odour (smell)** that remains intact in an acid, but **vanishes completely when mixed with a base**. Such substances are called **olfactory indicators** (*olfactory* = relating to the sense of smell).

The three best-known olfactory indicators are:

1. **Finely chopped onion-scented cloth strips** (testing with lab acid and base is for **observation only — teacher demonstration**): Place clean cotton cloth strips in a sealed plastic bag with finely chopped onions overnight in the refrigerator (this preparation step can be done **at home with an adult**). By morning, the cloth strips absorb a strong onion smell.
   - Put a few drops of a **dilute acid** (such as lemon juice or dilute HCl) on one strip: the sharp onion **smell remains**.
   - Put a few drops of a **base** (such as soapy water, or dilute NaOH handled by the teacher in the lab) on another strip and rinse lightly with water: the onion **smell disappears completely**!
2. **Vanilla extract** (the flavouring used in cakes and ice cream):
   - When shaken with an **acid**, the sweet vanilla **aroma stays unchanged**.
   - When shaken with a **base (NaOH)**, the sweet vanilla **aroma vanishes**.
3. **Clove oil:**
   - In an **acid**, the warm, spicy smell of clove **remains intact**.
   - In a **base (NaOH)**, the smell of clove **disappears (or becomes undetectable)**.

**One-line rule:** For onion, vanilla extract, and clove oil alike—their smell **STAYS in an Acid**, and **VANISHES in a Base**!

> **Activity 1.2 (Observation only — teacher demonstration).** The teacher takes small amounts of dilute HCl and dilute NaOH in two separate test tubes, adds 2 drops of **vanilla extract** to each, and shakes them gently. When wafting the air gently toward the nose from the HCl tube, the sweet scent of vanilla is clearly detected, whereas the NaOH tube has lost its vanilla scent completely. *(Safety note: Never place your nose directly over the mouth of a laboratory test tube; always fan a little vapour gently toward your nose with your hand—a technique called **wafting**.)*

## Part D: Solved Examples: Detective Puzzles

### Example 1. Three Unlabelled Test Tubes and Only Red Litmus Paper!

**Problem:** You are given three unlabelled test tubes—**A, B, and C**. One contains **dilute HCl (an acid)**, one contains **NaOH solution (a base)**, and one contains **pure distilled water (neutral)**. You are given **only a single strip of red litmus paper** (and no other indicator!). How will you identify all three liquids?

**Solution (Step-by-step):**
1. **Step 1 (Find the Base):** Tear the red litmus strip into three small pieces (or use three separate corners of the strip). Place one drop from tubes A, B, and C onto the three separate pieces. Whichever drop turns the **red litmus blue** must be the **base (NaOH)**! Suppose **Tube B** turns it blue. Then **B = Base (NaOH)**.
2. **Step 2 (You now have Blue Litmus paper!):** The piece of paper that just turned **blue** can now act as your **blue litmus paper**!
3. **Step 3 (Distinguish the Acid from Water):** Tear this newly blue piece into two halves and place a drop from each of the remaining tubes, **A and C**, onto them. Whichever tube turns the blue piece back to **red** is the **acid (HCl)**! And whichever tube leaves the blue piece unchanged is **pure distilled water (neutral)**!

**Check:** Did we need any outside indicator? No! First Red → Blue identified the Base, then that same Blue → Red identified the Acid, and no change identified the Neutral liquid ✅.

---

### Example 2. Identifying All Three Tubes (Acid, Base, Water) Using Only Turmeric Paper

**Problem:** Once again you have three unlabelled test tubes—**X, Y, and Z** (one containing an acid, one a base, and one pure water)—and this time you have **only yellow turmeric paper**. How will you identify all three?

**Solution (Step-by-step):**
1. **Step 1:** Place one drop from each tube (X, Y, and Z) onto three separate spots on the yellow turmeric paper. In both acid and water, turmeric **stays yellow**, but in a **base**, turmeric turns **reddish-brown**. Suppose **Tube Z** turns the turmeric spot reddish-brown. That proves **Z = Base**!
2. **Step 2:** Now use two reddish-brown spots (made with drops from Tube Z). Add a few drops of **Tube X** to the first reddish-brown spot, and a few drops of **Tube Y** to the second.
   - The **acid** will neutralise the base on the spot and turn the reddish-brown colour right back to **yellow**!
   - **Pure water** will not turn the reddish-brown spot back to yellow (it may only wet the paper slightly).

**Check:** Turmeric (yellow) + Base (Z) → Reddish-brown; Reddish-brown + Acid → Back to Yellow (just like Leo's napkin and the lemon juice!) ✅.

---

### Example 3. Matching Phenolphthalein, Methyl Orange, and Clove Oil Clues

**Problem:** When phenolphthalein is added to an unknown solution **P**, it remains **colourless**, and when methyl orange is added to **P**, it turns **red**. In another unknown solution **Q**, phenolphthalein turns **pink**, and the smell of clove oil **disappears**. Identify the chemical nature of **P** and **Q**.

**Solution:**
- **Solution P:** Phenolphthalein stays colourless (meaning P is not a base; it could be acidic or neutral). Methyl orange turns **red**—and methyl orange turns red **only in an acid** (it stays orange in a neutral liquid). Therefore, **Solution P = Acidic**.
- **Solution Q:** Phenolphthalein turns **pink** and the smell of clove oil **disappears**—both observations are unmistakable signatures of a **base**. Therefore, **Solution Q = Basic**.

**Check:** Both identifications match Figure 1.1 and the rules in Part C 100% ✅.

### Example 4. Calculating Indicator Testing Volumes for a Class

**Problem:** In a school laboratory, a class of 40 students has to test 5 different household liquids (lemon juice, vinegar, soapy water, limewater, and salt water) using **4 indicators** (blue litmus solution, red litmus solution, phenolphthalein, and methyl orange). If students work in pairs (2 students per group) and each test requires **2 mL** of the liquid in a separate test tube, what total volume (in mL) of each household liquid must be prepared for the entire class?

**Solution:**
- Total students = 40; students per group = 2 → Total groups = 40 ÷ 2 = **20 groups**.
- Each group tests a liquid with 4 indicators in separate test tubes → 1 group needs **4 samples** of each liquid.
- Each sample uses 2 mL → Volume of each liquid needed per group = 4 × 2 mL = **8 mL**.
- Total volume of each liquid needed for all 20 groups = 20 × 8 mL = **160 mL**.

**Check:** 160 mL ÷ 2 mL = 80 test tubes (per liquid); 80 ÷ 4 indicators = 20 groups; 20 × 2 = 40 students ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming "Phenolphthalein is colourless, so the liquid must be an acid."**
   Phenolphthalein remains colourless in **both acidic and neutral** solutions! A colourless result only proves that the liquid is **not a base**; you still need blue litmus or methyl orange to distinguish an acid from a neutral liquid.
2. **Mistake 2: Reversing the colour change of turmeric.**
   Turmeric turns reddish-brown in a **base**, not in an acid! In an acid, turmeric keeps its natural **yellow** colour.
3. **Mistake 3: Writing that olfactory indicators keep their smell in a base.**
   It is the exact opposite! Onion, vanilla extract, and clove oil **keep their characteristic smell in an acid** and **lose their smell in a base** (such as NaOH).
4. **Mistake 4: Thinking the natural (neutral) colour of litmus is blue or red.**
   The raw neutral dye extracted from lichens is **purple**. Blue and red litmus papers are pre-treated in the factory for laboratory convenience.
5. **Mistake 5: Tasting or dipping bare fingers into laboratory liquids.**
   Laboratory acids and bases are strongly corrosive; never taste or touch them with bare hands.

## Quick Recap

- Edible, safe **organic acids** (such as citric acid in lemons, lactic acid in yoghurt, tartaric acid in tamarind, and acetic acid in vinegar) taste sour; **bases** (such as baking soda and soap) taste bitter and feel soapy or slippery.
- Laboratory **mineral acids** (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃) and strong bases (NaOH, KOH) burn skin severely (**corrosive**), so they must never be tasted or touched.
- **Litmus** (from lichens): Acid turns Blue litmus **Red**; Base turns Red litmus **Blue**; Neutral shows no change (natural extract is **purple**).
- **Turmeric:** **Yellow** in Acid and Neutral; **Reddish-brown** in Base.
- **Hibiscus (China rose):** **Dark Pink (Magenta)** in Acid; **Green** in Base.
- **Phenolphthalein:** **Colourless** in Acid and Neutral; **Pink** in Base.
- **Methyl Orange:** **Red** in Acid; **Orange** in Neutral; **Yellow** in Base.
- **Olfactory indicators** (Onion, Vanilla extract, Clove oil): Smell **stays intact** in Acid; smell **vanishes** in Base.
