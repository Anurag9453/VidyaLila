# Topic 2: The Magic of Sodium Hydroxide: Which Color, Which Dissolves?

In Topic 1 we learned that qualitative analysis means finding out "what is hiding inside": add a reagent, observe the visible change (color, precipitate, or gas), and deduce which ion is present. We also saw that metal cations combine with OH⁻ ions to form insoluble hydroxide precipitates while spectator ions simply watch from the sidelines. Now comes the big question: **do all metal hydroxide precipitates look and behave the same?** Not at all! In fact, a single bottle of sodium hydroxide solution can tell apart several different metal ions. Today we uncover the chemistry behind that "magic show."

---

## The Science Assembly Magic Show

Uncle Julian had taken his niece Chloe to her school's annual Science Night assembly. Up on stage, the chemistry teacher, Ms. Harper, was presenting an act called *"One Dropper, Three Mysteries."* Three clear glass beakers stood on the demonstration table. Ms. Harper was wearing safety goggles and lab gloves, and a velvet rope kept the audience safely back from the stage table. (This was a teacher demonstration—strictly for students to watch.)

From a single dropper bottle, Ms. Harper added a few drops into the first beaker: a **pale blue** cloudy solid formed and settled toward the bottom. She added a few drops from the exact same bottle into the second beaker: a **white** precipitate appeared. Then she squeezed in a generous squirt of extra drops—and right before the audience's eyes, **the white precipitate dissolved completely**, leaving the liquid crystal clear again! Finally, she added drops to the third beaker: a **reddish-brown** precipitate formed, and no matter how many extra drops she added, that reddish-brown solid refused to budge.

Chloe tugged Uncle Julian's sleeve. "Uncle Julian, she used the exact same dropper bottle for all three beakers, yet she got three completely different results! And how did the solid in the middle beaker vanish when she added *more* of the same liquid?" Uncle Julian smiled. "It isn't stage magic, Chloe—it's chemistry. Ms. Harper put three different metal salt solutions in those beakers, and her dropper bottle held **sodium hydroxide (NaOH)**. Every metal ion has its own personality: some form distinctly colored precipitates, while others form precipitates that dissolve when you add **excess** NaOH. Those behaviors act like a **detective's fingerprint** for each metal ion."

"So if we know each ion's fingerprint," said Chloe, "we can identify what's in the beaker without reading the label?" "Spot on," said Uncle Julian. "Let's meet all seven metal ions one by one."

## Safety First, Magic Second

Every test in this topic is **strictly for study** or performed as a **teacher demonstration in a school lab**. Students never perform these tests alone or at home.

- **Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution is corrosive**: even when dilute, it can severely damage eyes and burn skin. Safety goggles and gloves are mandatory, and the teacher handles the dropper bottle.
- **All lead compounds are toxic.** A salt such as lead(II) nitrate, Pb(NO₃)₂, must never be touched with bare hands or brought near the mouth.
- **Copper salts** (such as copper(II) sulfate) are harmful if swallowed.
- Never taste or sniff any chemical. Leftover solutions containing heavy metals (lead, copper, zinc) are **never poured down the sink**—the teacher collects them in a dedicated heavy-metal waste container.

## Part A: How NaOH Forms a Precipitate

Recall from Chapter 1 (Acids, Bases, and Salts) that while NaOH, KOH, and NH₄OH are **soluble bases (alkalis)**, most other metal hydroxides—such as Cu(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃, Zn(OH)₂, and Al(OH)₃—are **practically insoluble in water**. Because NaOH is a **strong alkali** (a strong electrolyte from Chapter 4), it dissociates completely in water into Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions, providing plenty of free OH⁻ ions. Whenever a metal cation Mⁿ⁺ meets these OH⁻ ions and its hydroxide is insoluble, that hydroxide immediately precipitates out as a solid:

Mⁿ⁺ + nOH⁻ → M(OH)ₙ↓

### Laboratory Procedure (With the Teacher)

1. Place a small volume (about 2 mL) of the dilute salt solution in a clean test tube.
2. Using a dropper, add **a few drops** of dilute NaOH solution, shaking gently after each drop: observe the color of any precipitate.
3. Now add **excess** NaOH solution: observe whether the precipitate stays intact or dissolves to form a clear solution.
4. Record your findings using our three questions from Topic 1: What was added? What was seen? What does it mean?

### Example 1. CuSO₄ + NaOH (Recalling Topic 1, Now With Excess)

**Observation:** A **pale blue** precipitate forms.
**Net ionic equation:** Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓
**Molecular equation:** CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄
Spectator ions: Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻. In **excess NaOH**, the precipitate **does not dissolve**—it remains pale blue at the bottom.

**Check:** Atom count: Cu 1 = 1, S 1 = 1, O: 4 + 2 = 6 = 2 + 4, H 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2 ✅. Ionic charge: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 = charge of neutral Cu(OH)₂ ✅.

### Example 2. FeCl₃ + NaOH (Recalling Topic 1, Now With Excess)

**Observation:** A **reddish-brown** precipitate forms.
**Net ionic equation:** Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓
**Molecular equation:** FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl
In **excess NaOH**, the precipitate **does not dissolve**.

**Check:** Fe 1 = 1, Cl 3 = 3, Na 3 = 3, O 3 = 3, H 3 = 3 ✅. Charge: (+3) + 3 × (−1) = 0 ✅.

### Example 3. FeSO₄ + NaOH (Two Different Colors from the Same Metal: Iron!)

Iron forms two common cations: **Fe²⁺** (iron(II) or ferrous) and **Fe³⁺** (iron(III) or ferric). Both form precipitates with NaOH, but their colors are unmistakably different!

**Observation:** Fe²⁺ gives a **dirty green** precipitate.
**Net ionic equation:** Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂↓
**Molecular equation:** FeSO₄ + 2NaOH → Fe(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄
In **excess NaOH**, the precipitate **does not dissolve**. One neat detail: if you leave this dirty green precipitate exposed to the air for a few minutes, its top edge slowly turns **reddish-brown** as oxygen in the air oxidizes Fe(OH)₂ into Fe(OH)₃-like compounds (a one-line preview of a higher-grade reaction).

**Check:** Fe 1 = 1, S 1 = 1, O: 4 + 2 = 6 = 2 + 4, H 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2 ✅. Charge: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.

**Takeaway from Part A:** For our first three cations—**Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, and Fe³⁺**—the hydroxide precipitate **stays undissolved** even in excess NaOH. We tell all three apart immediately by their colors: **pale blue, dirty green, and reddish-brown**.

## Part C: Why Those Three Precipitates Dissolve: Amphoteric Hydroxides

At first sight, the middle beaker at the Science Night assembly looks baffling: adding a few drops of NaOH creates a solid precipitate, and then adding *more* of the exact same NaOH makes the precipitate vanish! The secret is that once the metal hydroxide precipitate forms, adding extra NaOH triggers a **second chemical reaction**.

Think of **Zn(OH)₂, Pb(OH)₂, and Al(OH)₃** as the hydroxide cousins of the **amphoteric oxides** (Al₂O₃ and ZnO) we met in Chapter 2 (Metals and Non-metals, Part 2). Just as those oxides reacted both with acids and with strong alkalis, these three hydroxides react **both with acids and with strong alkalis**. That is why we call them **amphoteric hydroxides**: toward an acid they act like a **base**, and toward a strong alkali (excess NaOH) they act like an **acid**, reacting to form a soluble salt and water!

### Example 5. Zinc: First a Precipitate, Then a Clear Solution

**Stage 1 (a few drops of NaOH):** ZnSO₄ + 2NaOH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄. Net ionic: Zn²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Zn(OH)₂↓ (white precipitate).
**Stage 2 (excess NaOH):** Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O. Sodium zincate (Na₂ZnO₂) is soluble in water, giving a clear, colorless solution.
**With an acid (teacher demonstration, dilute acid):** Zn(OH)₂ + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + 2H₂O.

**Check:** Stage 1: Zn 1 = 1, S 1 = 1, O: 4 + 2 = 6 = 2 + 4, H 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2 ✅. Stage 2: Zn 1 = 1, Na 2 = 2, O: 2 + 2 = 4 = 2 + 2, H: 2 + 2 = 4 = 4 ✅. With acid: Zn 1, S 1, O: 2 + 4 = 6 = 4 + 2, H: 2 + 2 = 4 = 4 ✅.

### Example 6. Lead: First a Precipitate, Then a Clear Solution (Toxic!)

(All lead compounds are toxic—for study or teacher demonstration only.)
**With a few drops of NaOH:** Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Pb(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃. Net ionic: Pb²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Pb(OH)₂↓ (white precipitate).
**With excess NaOH:** Pb(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂PbO₂ + 2H₂O. Sodium plumbite (Na₂PbO₂), a clear, colorless solution.
**With an acid:** Pb(OH)₂ + 2HNO₃ → Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O. (We use nitric acid here because sulfuric acid or hydrochloric acid would coat the solid with insoluble PbSO₄ or PbCl₂, as we learned in Chapter 1.)

**Check:** Stage 1: Pb 1 = 1, N 2 = 2, O: 6 + 2 = 8 = 2 + 6, H 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2 ✅. Excess: Pb 1, Na 2, O: 2 + 2 = 4 = 2 + 2, H 4 = 4 ✅. With acid: Pb 1, N 2, O: 2 + 6 = 8 = 6 + 2, H: 2 + 2 = 4 = 4 ✅.

### Example 7. Aluminum: A Jelly-Like White Precipitate

**With a few drops of NaOH:** Al³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Al(OH)₃↓. Molecular: Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6NaOH → 2Al(OH)₃↓ + 3Na₂SO₄. Forms a white, jelly-like (**gelatinous**) precipitate.
**With excess NaOH:** Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O. Forms soluble sodium aluminate (NaAlO₂), a clear, colorless solution.
**With an acid:** 2Al(OH)₃ + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6H₂O.

**Check:** Stage 1: Al 2 = 2, S 3 = 3, O: 12 + 6 = 18 = 6 + 12, H 6 = 6, Na 6 = 6 ✅. Excess: Al 1, Na 1, O: 3 + 1 = 4 = 2 + 2, H: 3 + 1 = 4 = 4 ✅. With acid: Al 2 = 2, S 3 = 3, H: 6 + 6 = 12 = 12, O: 6 + 12 = 18 = 12 + 6 ✅.

(In aqueous solution, zincate, plumbite, and aluminate actually exist as hydrated **complex ions**—a detail you will explore in higher grades.)

```
   Zn²⁺ solution
       :
       : add a few drops of NaOH
       v
   Zn(OH)₂ precipitate (white)         <-- solid precipitate forms
       :
       : add more NaOH (excess)
       v
   Na₂ZnO₂ solution (colorless)        <-- precipitate reacts and dissolves
```

> **Figure 2.3** – The two-stage path of Zn²⁺ with NaOH: first a white precipitate forms, and then in excess NaOH it reacts to form a clear, colorless solution. Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ follow the exact same two-stage pattern (forming plumbite and aluminate).

### Example 8. Stoichiometry Check: How Much Precipitate Forms?

**Question:** Suppose 8.125 g of FeCl₃ reacts completely with the exact required amount of NaOH. What mass of NaOH is needed, and what masses of Fe(OH)₃ and NaCl are formed? (Fe = 56, Cl = 35.5, Na = 23, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Solution:** Molar mass of FeCl₃ = 56 + 3 × 35.5 = **162.5 g/mol**, so moles of FeCl₃ = 8.125 ÷ 162.5 = **0.05 mol**.
From FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl: 0.05 mol of FeCl₃ requires 0.15 mol of NaOH and produces 0.05 mol of Fe(OH)₃ and 0.15 mol of NaCl.
Mass of NaOH = 0.15 × 40 = **6.0 g**. Molar mass of Fe(OH)₃ = 56 + 51 = 107 g/mol, so mass = 0.05 × 107 = **5.35 g**. Mass of NaCl = 0.15 × 58.5 = **8.775 g**.

**Check:** Total mass before = 8.125 + 6.0 = 14.125 g; total mass after = 5.35 + 8.775 = 14.125 g ✅.

## Part D: Calcium's Different Path

When NaOH is added to a Ca(NO₃)₂ solution, a **white precipitate** also forms, though it is **slight** (less dense than Zn, Pb, or Al):

Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂↓
Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Ca(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃

It is slight because Ca(OH)₂ (the solute in limewater from Chapter 1) is sparingly soluble in water, so a little bit stays dissolved before the rest precipitates out. Most importantly: **in excess NaOH, this white Ca(OH)₂ precipitate does NOT dissolve.** Calcium hydroxide is strictly a basic hydroxide, not amphoteric!

**Check:** Ca 1 = 1, N 2 = 2, O: 6 + 2 = 8 = 2 + 6, H 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2 ✅. Charge: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.

### Example 9. Identifying Five Mystery Tubes

A teacher hands out five test tubes (A through E), each containing one of our seven cations. Here are the results with NaOH:

| Tube | With a Few Drops of NaOH | With Excess NaOH |
|---|---|---|

D = **Fe³⁺** (reddish-brown). E = **Cu²⁺** (pale blue).

**Check:** Every tube's result matches a unique row (or the 3-row amphoteric group) in the Part B table ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "All white hydroxide precipitates dissolve in excess NaOH."**
This trap is easy to fall into because Zn(OH)₂, Pb(OH)₂, and Al(OH)₃ are all white and all dissolve in excess NaOH. However, **Ca(OH)₂ is also white, and it does NOT dissolve in excess NaOH!** Let's see the contrast in three steps:

1. **Take three tubes that give white precipitates:** Tube P (Ca²⁺), Tube Q (Zn²⁺), and Tube R (Pb²⁺). Add a few drops of NaOH to each: all three form white precipitates.
2. **Now add excess NaOH to all three:** Tubes Q and R turn **crystal clear**, whereas in Tube P the white precipitate **stays undissolved** at the bottom.
3. **Rule:** Whenever you see a white precipitate with NaOH, never stop after a few drops—always add excess NaOH! If the white precipitate dissolves, it is Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺; if it does not dissolve, it is **Ca²⁺**.

Quick check: if a white precipitate remains unchanged after adding excess NaOH, can the solution contain Zn²⁺? No—Zn(OH)₂ would have dissolved into colorless sodium zincate. It must be **Ca²⁺**.

## Quick Recap

- NaOH is a **strong alkali** that supplies abundant OH⁻ ions: Mⁿ⁺ + nOH⁻ → M(OH)ₙ↓.
- Procedure: add a few drops first, then **excess**; always performed with the teacher wearing goggles and gloves. NaOH is corrosive, lead compounds are toxic, and copper salts are harmful if swallowed.
- Precipitate colors with NaOH: Cu²⁺ pale blue, Fe²⁺ dirty green (slowly turns brown at the top in air), Fe³⁺ reddish-brown, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺ white (Al(OH)₃ is gelatinous), Ca²⁺ slight white.
- **In excess NaOH:** Zn(OH)₂, Pb(OH)₂, and Al(OH)₃ **dissolve** to form colorless solutions of sodium zincate (Na₂ZnO₂), sodium plumbite (Na₂PbO₂), and sodium aluminate (NaAlO₂). Meanwhile, Ca(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₂, Fe(OH)₃, and Cu(OH)₂ **do not dissolve**.
- **Amphoteric hydroxides** act as bases toward acids and as acids toward strong alkalis.
- Distinguish Fe²⁺ from Fe³⁺ by precipitate color (dirty green vs. reddish-brown); distinguish Ca²⁺ from Zn²⁺/Pb²⁺/Al³⁺ by adding excess NaOH.
- NaOH alone cannot distinguish among Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺.
- Common pitfall: not every white precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH—Ca(OH)₂ stays insoluble!

10. A student wants to repeat the colour magic at home using a kitchen drain cleaner because it contains NaOH. Give three reasons why this is unsafe.
11. 3.9 g of Al(OH)₃ is dissolved in NaOH (exact amounts). Find the mass of NaOH needed, the mass of NaAlO₂ and of water formed, and check conservation of mass. (Al 27, Na 23, O 16, H 1.)

### Answers

1. Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6NaOH → 2Al(OH)₃↓ + 3Na₂SO₄. Spectator ions: **Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻**. Check: Al 2 = 2, S 3 = 3, O: 12 + 6 = 18 = 6 + 12, H 6 = 6, Na 6 = 6 ✅.
2. It could be **Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺**, because all three form white hydroxide precipitates that dissolve in excess NaOH to give colorless solutions. Since all three behave identically with NaOH, a second reagent (such as NH₄OH in Topic 3) is needed to tell them apart.
3. P = **Fe³⁺** (reddish-brown), Q = **Cu²⁺** (pale blue), R = **Fe²⁺** (dirty green that turns brown at the top on standing in air), S = **Ca²⁺** (white, insoluble in excess NaOH). Check: every color matches our Part B table ✅.
4. Molar mass of Zn(OH)₂ = 65 + 34 = 99 g/mol, so moles = 14.85 ÷ 99 = **0.15 mol**. From Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O: mass of NaOH = 0.30 × 40 = **12.0 g**. Molar mass of Na₂ZnO₂ = 46 + 65 + 32 = 143 g/mol, so mass = 0.15 × 143 = **21.45 g**. Mass of water = 0.30 × 18 = **5.4 g**. Check: 14.85 + 12.0 = 26.85 g; 21.45 + 5.4 = 26.85 g ✅.
5. Pb(OH)₂ + 2HNO₃ → Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2H₂O and Pb(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂PbO₂ + 2H₂O. Both equations are balanced (Pb 1, O 8 = 8, H 4 = 4, N 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2 ✅). This shows that **lead(II) hydroxide is amphoteric**—it reacts both with an acid and with a strong alkali. (Lead compounds are toxic.)
6. Molecular: Ca(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Ca(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃. Net ionic: Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂↓. Spectator ions: **Na⁺ and NO₃⁻**. Check: Ca 1, N 2, O 8, H 2, Na 2 on both sides ✅.
7. (a) **False** ❌: Cu(OH)₂ does not dissolve in excess NaOH; it stays as a pale blue precipitate. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌: Ca(OH)₂ is a white precipitate that does not dissolve in excess NaOH.
8. No, he is wrong. Because Zn(OH)₂ **dissolves** in excess NaOH, a white precipitate that fails to dissolve in excess NaOH cannot be zinc—it indicates **Ca²⁺**.
9. Zn(OH)₂ is **amphoteric**: it acts as a base toward acids and as an acid toward strong alkalis like NaOH, reacting to form soluble sodium zincate (Na₂ZnO₂). Cu(OH)₂ is strictly basic, so it does not react with an alkali and stays undissolved.

Molar mass of NaAlO₂ = 23 + 27 + 32 = 82 g/mol, so mass = 0.05 × 82 = **4.1 g**. Mass of water = 0.10 × 18 = **1.8 g**. Check: 3.9 + 2.0 = 5.9 g; 4.1 + 1.8 = 5.9 g ✅.
