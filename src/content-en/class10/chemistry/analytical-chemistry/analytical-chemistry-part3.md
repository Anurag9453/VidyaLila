# Topic 3: Ammonium Hydroxide and Amphoteric Behavior

In Topic 2 we tested our seven metal cations with a single reagent, NaOH, and saw that the white precipitates of Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺ all dissolve in excess NaOH. Today we bring in our second alkali: **ammonium hydroxide, NH₄OH** (aqueous ammonia). We will test the exact same seven cations with the exact same two-stage procedure: first a few drops, then excess. The results with NH₄OH have a twist of their own—and when we combine our NaOH and NH₄OH observations, we will have a complete **"Which Ion Is It?" decision table**.

---

## The Bilingual Castle Guide

Uncle Henrik took his nephew Jonas on a weekend visit to an old hilltop castle in Switzerland. Right at the main gate, two tour groups arrived at the same time: a French-speaking family and a German-speaking school group. The castle guide stood between them with a warm smile. When the family asked a question in French, the guide answered in fluent French; when the students asked a question in German, the same guide replied in fluent German. One person, two different languages—and both groups felt right at home.

Jonas leaned over and whispered, "Uncle Henrik, how does he switch like that without missing a beat?" Uncle Henrik smiled. "Because he is **bilingual**—he speaks two languages. Whichever group steps up to him, he responds in their language. In chemistry, certain precipitates work the same way: **when they meet an acid, they react like a base; when they meet a strong alkali, they react like an acid**. They speak both chemical languages."

"Do all precipitates do that?" asked Jonas. Uncle Henrik shook his head. "No. Most precipitates speak only one language, and even when we test with a second reagent like **NH₄OH**, only specific precipitates respond to it. Today we'll see which ions respond to NH₄OH and why."

```
   French speakers  --->  [ CASTLE GUIDE ]  <---  German speakers
                            one person,
                            two languages

   Acid             --->  [ Zn(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, Pb(OH)₂ ]  <---  Strong alkali
                            one precipitate,
                            dual behavior (amphoteric)
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Just as a bilingual guide responds to two different groups in their own language, an amphoteric hydroxide reacts as a base with acids and as an acid with strong alkalis. (This is an analogy.)

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ammonium hydroxide** | An aqueous solution of ammonia gas; a weak alkali containing a low concentration of OH⁻ ions | NH₄OH |
| **Excess** | Adding more than enough reagent—continuing to add drops after the precipitate has already formed | Excess NH₄OH |
| **Amphoteric hydroxide** | A hydroxide that reacts and dissolves in both acids and strong alkalis | Zn(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, Pb(OH)₂ |

| **Gelatinous** | Jelly-like, slightly translucent and clumpy | The white precipitate of Al(OH)₃ |

## Part A: NH₄OH and Precipitates: How a Weak Alkali Gets the Job Done

**Strict Safety First.** Every test in this topic is performed **in a school lab with the teacher** (wearing goggles and gloves, with the teacher dispensing drops) or observed as a **teacher demonstration**. NH₄OH gives off **pungent, irritating ammonia vapors**: bottles are kept in a fume hood or well-ventilated lab, and no one ever sniffs the mouth of a bottle or tube (if needed, a teacher demonstrates the gentle hand-"wafting" motion—covered here for study only). Copper salts are harmful if swallowed, and all lead compounds are toxic. Students never perform these tests at home or pour heavy-metal waste down the drain.

In Topic 1 (Figure 1.3), we saw that NH₄OH is a **weak alkali** (a weak electrolyte from Chapter 4), so its solution contains **far fewer OH⁻ ions** than an NaOH solution of the same concentration. Even so, NH₄OH still precipitates six of our seven cations!

Why? Because the hydroxides of Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺ are **so insoluble** in water that even a small supply of OH⁻ ions is enough to make them clump together and settle out. As each OH⁻ ion is used up, more NH₄OH dissociates to replace it. The one exception is **Ca(OH)₂**: because calcium hydroxide is slightly soluble in water (remember limewater from Chapter 1), precipitating it requires a high concentration of OH⁻ ions that weak NH₄OH simply cannot provide! As a result, adding NH₄OH to a **Ca²⁺** solution produces **no precipitate (or at most a barely visible haze)**.

Look at the equations below: the anion originally paired with the metal cation (such as Cl⁻ or NO₃⁻) stays in solution and pairs up with NH₄⁺ as a spectator.

### Example 1. CuCl₂ + NH₄OH: Stage 1 (A Few Drops)

(Copper salts are harmful if swallowed—teacher demonstration only.) The teacher adds a few drops of NH₄OH to a blue-green solution of CuCl₂. A **pale blue precipitate** of Cu(OH)₂ settles down.

Molecular equation: CuCl₂ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄Cl
Ionic equation: Cu²⁺ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄⁺

Atom count (ionic): Cu 1 = 1, N 2 = 2, H: 10 = 2 + 8 = 10, O 2 = 2 ✅. Charge: +2 = 0 + 2 × (+1) = +2 ✅. Cl⁻ is the spectator ion (Topic 1), remaining unchanged on both sides.

**Check:** In the molecular equation, Cu 1 = 1, Cl 2 = 2, N 2 = 2, H: 10 = 2 + 8 = 10, O 2 = 2 ✅.

### Example 2. Fe(NO₃)₃ + NH₄OH

The teacher adds NH₄OH to a yellow-brown solution of Fe(NO₃)₃. A **reddish-brown precipitate of Fe(OH)₃** forms, and **it does not dissolve in excess NH₄OH**.

Molecular equation: Fe(NO₃)₃ + 3NH₄OH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄NO₃
Ionic equation: Fe³⁺ + 3NH₄OH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄⁺

**Check:** Fe 1 = 1, N: 3 + 3 = 6 = 6, O: 9 + 3 = 12 = 3 + 9 = 12, H: 15 = 3 + 12 = 15 ✅. Ionic charge: +3 = 3 × (+1) ✅.

Similarly, with any soluble Fe²⁺ salt, NH₄OH gives a **dirty green precipitate of Fe(OH)₂** that does not dissolve in excess NH₄OH. So just as with NaOH in Topic 2, **Fe²⁺ and Fe³⁺ are told apart immediately by the color of their precipitates**.

### Example 3. CaCl₂ + NH₄OH: No Precipitate!

When NH₄OH is added to a CaCl₂ solution, **no precipitate forms** (or at most a faint cloudiness), and adding excess NH₄OH causes no change. (Recall from Topic 2 that with strong NaOH, Ca²⁺ gave a slight white precipitate.) Even if we write CaCl₂ + 2NH₄OH ⇌ Ca(OH)₂ + 2NH₄Cl, the concentration of OH⁻ in weak NH₄OH is too low to precipitate slightly soluble Ca(OH)₂.

**Check:** Ca 1 = 1, Cl 2 = 2, N 2 = 2, H: 10 = 2 + 8 = 10, O 2 = 2 ✅. This makes NH₄OH a great tool for separating Ca²⁺ from the other cations!

### Example 4. How Much Precipitate? (A Quick Mole Calculation)

Suppose 13.45 g of CuCl₂ in solution reacts with the exact amount of NH₄OH needed to precipitate all the copper as Cu(OH)₂. What mass of Cu(OH)₂ forms? (Cu = 63.5, Cl = 35.5, O = 16, H = 1, N = 14.)

Molar mass of CuCl₂ = 63.5 + 71 = 134.5 g/mol. Moles of CuCl₂ = 13.45 ÷ 134.5 = **0.1 mol**. In CuCl₂ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄Cl, the mole ratio of CuCl₂ to Cu(OH)₂ is 1 : 1, so 0.1 mol of Cu(OH)₂ forms. Molar mass of Cu(OH)₂ = 63.5 + 34 = 97.5 g/mol. Mass = 0.1 × 97.5 = **9.75 g**.

**Check:** Mass of 2NH₄OH = 0.2 mol × 35 = 7.0 g; mass of 2NH₄Cl = 0.2 × 53.5 = 10.7 g. Total reactants = 13.45 + 7.0 = 20.45 g; total products = 9.75 + 10.7 = 20.45 g ✅.

## Part B: Two Signature Results in Excess NH₄OH: Deep Inky Blue and Colorless

Now comes the stage where NH₄OH behaves completely differently from NaOH: adding **excess NH₄OH**.

### Cu²⁺: Pale Blue Precipitate, Then a Deep Inky-Blue Solution!

Add a few drops of NH₄OH to a Cu²⁺ solution and you get the familiar pale blue Cu(OH)₂ precipitate. Now keep adding NH₄OH in excess: the pale blue precipitate **dissolves completely**, turning the liquid into a dramatic **deep (inky) blue solution**! That vivid inky-blue color belongs to a new species in water: the tetraamminecopper(II) ion, **[Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺**, a **complex ion** (whose bonding structure is covered in higher grades). Remember from Topic 2 that Cu(OH)₂ did *not* dissolve in excess NaOH—so dissolving into a deep inky-blue solution with excess NH₄OH is the unmistakable signature of **Cu²⁺**!

### Zn²⁺: White Precipitate, Then a Clear Colorless Solution!

When a few drops of NH₄OH are added to a ZnCl₂ or Zn(NO₃)₂ solution, a **white precipitate of Zn(OH)₂** forms; when **excess NH₄OH** is added, that white precipitate dissolves completely to give a **clear, colorless solution** containing the complex ion **[Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺** (studied in higher grades).

```
   :              :    :              :    :              :
   :    CuCl₂     :    :  few drops   :    : excess NH₄OH :
   :   solution   :    :  of NH₄OH    :    : clear, deep  :
   : (blue-green) :    :              :    :  inky-blue   :
   :              :    :              :    :   solution   :
   :              :    :  ##########  :    :  (no ppt)    :
   '--------------'    '--------------'    '--------------'
   Cu²⁺ solution       pale blue ppt       ppt dissolved!
```

### Example 5. Step-by-Step With Zn(NO₃)₂

In a clean test tube, the teacher takes a small volume of Zn(NO₃)₂ solution and adds **a few drops** of NH₄OH with a dropper: a white precipitate appears. Then the teacher adds **excess NH₄OH drop by drop**: the white precipitate dissolves completely.

Stage 1: Zn(NO₃)₂ + 2NH₄OH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄NO₃. Ionic: Zn²⁺ + 2NH₄OH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄⁺.
Stage 2: Zn(OH)₂ dissolves in excess NH₄OH to form the colorless complex ion [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺ (covered in higher grades).

**Check:** Zn 1 = 1, N: 2 + 2 = 4 = 4, O: 6 + 2 = 8 = 2 + 6 = 8, H: 10 = 2 + 8 = 10 ✅; ionic charge: +2 = 2 × (+1) ✅.

Memory tip: pair **Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺** together in your mind—**they are the only two cations on our list whose precipitates dissolve in excess NH₄OH** (Cu²⁺ giving a deep inky-blue solution, Zn²⁺ giving a colorless solution).

## Part C: Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺: The Stubborn Twins in NH₄OH

In Topic 2 we saw that Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ both form white precipitates with NaOH that dissolve in excess NaOH. What happens when we test Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ with NH₄OH? Both form **white precipitates** (Al(OH)₃ is gelatinous white, Pb(OH)₂ is chalky white), and **neither precipitate dissolves in excess NH₄OH**! That immediately separates Zn²⁺ (which *does* dissolve in excess NH₄OH) from Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺, leaving Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ as "twins" in both NaOH and NH₄OH tests.

**Lead(II) acetate—toxic (teacher demonstration only):** Here we use **lead(II) acetate, (CH₃COO)₂Pb**, as another water-soluble lead salt alongside Pb(NO₃)₂. Remember that all lead salts are **toxic**: students never handle them, and all waste is collected by the teacher. For aluminum we use **AlCl₃**.

(CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2NH₄OH → Pb(OH)₂↓ + 2CH₃COONH₄
AlCl₃ + 3NH₄OH → Al(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄Cl

**Check:** For Pb: Pb 1 = 1, C 4 = 4, H: 6 + 10 = 16 = 2 + 14 = 16, O: 4 + 2 = 6 = 2 + 4 = 6, N 2 = 2 ✅. For Al: Al 1 = 1, Cl 3 = 3, N 3 = 3, H: 15 = 3 + 12 = 15, O 3 = 3 ✅.

```
   Tube 1              Tube 2              Tube 3
   :              :    :              :    :              :
   : Pb²⁺ +       :    : Al³⁺ +       :    : Zn²⁺ +       :
   : excess NH₄OH :    : excess NH₄OH :    : excess NH₄OH :
   :              :    :              :    :              :
   :              :    :              :    :              :
   :  ##########  :    :  ##########  :    :  (no ppt)    :
   '--------------'    '--------------'    '--------------'
```

> **Figure 3.4** – After adding excess NH₄OH: the white precipitates in the Pb²⁺ tube (Tube 1) and the Al³⁺ tube (Tube 2) remain undissolved at the bottom (`#`), whereas the white precipitate in the Zn²⁺ tube (Tube 3) has dissolved completely. Thus excess NH₄OH cleanly separates Zn²⁺ from Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺.

### Example 6. How Do We Tell Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ Apart?

Since Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ behave so similarly in both NaOH and NH₄OH, how does a detective distinguish between them? We use a **separate confirming test for lead(II) ions** (for study / teacher demonstration only):

- Add a few drops of **dilute HCl** to a fresh portion of the solution: if it is a lead(II) solution (such as lead acetate), a **white precipitate of PbCl₂** forms: (CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2HCl → PbCl₂↓ + 2CH₃COOH. With AlCl₃ (or any Al³⁺ solution), no precipitate forms because aluminum chloride is soluble in water.
- Or add a few drops of **potassium iodide (KI)** solution: a lead(II) solution gives a **bright yellow precipitate of PbI₂** (recall the famous "golden rain" teacher demo from Grade 9!): (CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2KI → PbI₂↓ + 2CH₃COOK. An Al³⁺ solution gives no yellow precipitate with KI.

(Because all lead precipitates are toxic, these tests are performed only by the teacher.)
**Check:** (CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2HCl: Pb 1 = 1, C 4 = 4, H: 6 + 2 = 8 = 8, O 4 = 4, Cl 2 = 2 ✅. (CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2KI: Pb 1 = 1, K 2 = 2, I 2 = 2, C 4 = 4, H 6 = 6, O 4 = 4 ✅.

## Part D: Amphoteric Behavior Revisited: Speaking Two Languages

In Chapter 2 (Part E) we saw that **amphoteric oxides** (Al₂O₃ and ZnO) react with both acids and strong alkalis. The exact same dual behavior applies to our three **amphoteric hydroxides: Zn(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, and Pb(OH)₂**.

- **Acting as a base with an acid (forming a salt + water):**
  Zn(OH)₂ + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + 2H₂O
  Al(OH)₃ + 3HCl → AlCl₃ + 3H₂O
  Pb(OH)₂ + 2CH₃COOH → (CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2H₂O
- **Acting as an acid with a strong alkali (forming a soluble salt + water):**
  Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O
  Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O
  Pb(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂PbO₂ + 2H₂O

**Check:** Zn(OH)₂ + 2HCl: Zn 1, O 2, H 4 = 4, Cl 2 ✅. Al(OH)₃ + 3HCl: Al 1, O 3, H 6 = 6, Cl 3 ✅. Pb(OH)₂ + 2CH₃COOH: C 4, H 10 = 6 + 4, O 6 ✅. Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH: Na 2, Zn 1, O 4, H 4 ✅. Al(OH)₃ + NaOH: O 4 = 2 + 2, H 4 = 4 ✅. Pb(OH)₂ + 2NaOH: Na 2, Pb 1, O 4, H 4 ✅.

```
           Acid (HCl)                       Strong alkali (NaOH)
   ZnCl₂ + 2H₂O  <----  Zn(OH)₂  ---->  Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O
   (acts like a base)                   (acts like an acid)
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Amphoteric Zn(OH)₂: reacts with an acid to form ZnCl₂ and water, and reacts with a strong alkali (NaOH) to form Na₂ZnO₂ and water. One compound, two chemical languages.

### Example 7. Does Being "Amphoteric" Mean Dissolving in Excess NH₄OH?

**No!** Look closely at the evidence:
- **Al(OH)₃** and **Pb(OH)₂** are amphoteric hydroxides (they dissolve in excess strong NaOH), yet **they do NOT dissolve in excess NH₄OH**, because NH₄OH is too weak an alkali to make them react as acids.
- Why, then, do **Cu(OH)₂** and **Zn(OH)₂** dissolve in excess NH₄OH? For a **completely different reason**: their metal ions bind NH₃ molecules to form water-soluble **ammine complex ions** ([Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ and [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺). Notice that Cu(OH)₂ is *not* amphoteric at all (it does not dissolve in excess NaOH), yet it dissolves readily in excess NH₄OH!

**Check:** In our table, Al³⁺ in excess NH₄OH is "insoluble" ✅, Zn²⁺ "dissolves (colorless)" ✅, and Cu²⁺ "dissolves (deep inky blue)" ✅.

## Part E: The Complete "Which Ion Is It?" Decision Table

Now let's put our NaOH results (Topic 2) and NH₄OH results (Topic 3) side by side in one master table!

| Ion | NaOH: A Few Drops | NaOH: Excess | NH₄OH: A Few Drops | NH₄OH: Excess |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ca²⁺ | Slight white ppt | Insoluble (does not dissolve) | No ppt (or very faint haze) | No change |
| Fe²⁺ | Dirty green ppt | Insoluble | Dirty green ppt | Insoluble |
| Fe³⁺ | Reddish-brown ppt | Insoluble | Reddish-brown ppt | Insoluble |
| Cu²⁺ | Pale blue ppt | Insoluble | Pale blue ppt | Dissolves → deep (inky) blue solution |
| Zn²⁺ | White ppt | Dissolves → colorless solution | White ppt | Dissolves → colorless solution |
| Pb²⁺ | White ppt | Dissolves → colorless solution | White ppt | Insoluble |
| Al³⁺ | White gelatinous ppt | Dissolves → colorless solution | White gelatinous ppt | Insoluble |

```
   Ion   :  A Few Drops of NH₄OH      :  Excess NH₄OH
   -------------------------------------------------------------------
   Ca²⁺  :  no ppt (or faint haze)    :  no change
   Fe²⁺  :  dirty green ppt           :  ppt remains undissolved
   Fe³⁺  :  reddish-brown ppt         :  ppt remains undissolved
   Cu²⁺  :  pale blue ppt             :  ppt dissolves -> inky-blue solution
   Zn²⁺  :  white ppt                 :  ppt dissolves -> colorless solution
   Pb²⁺  :  white ppt                 :  ppt remains undissolved
   Al³⁺  :  white gelatinous ppt      :  ppt remains undissolved
```

> **Figure 3.6** – Quick reference card for all seven cations with NH₄OH. Only two precipitates (Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺) dissolve in excess NH₄OH!

And here is the **master decision flowchart** that separates all seven cations step by step:

```
   Unknown solution + a few drops of NaOH: What color is the precipitate?
      :
      +--> Reddish-brown       : Fe³⁺
      +--> Dirty green         : Fe²⁺
      +--> Pale blue           : Cu²⁺ (confirm: excess NH₄OH gives a deep inky-blue solution)
      +--> White
             :
             +--> Insoluble in excess NaOH : Ca²⁺ (confirm: no ppt with NH₄OH)
             +--> Dissolves in excess NaOH : Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺
                    :
                    +--> Dissolves in excess NH₄OH     : Zn²⁺
                    +--> Insoluble in excess NH₄OH     : Pb²⁺ or Al³⁺
                           :
                           +--> White ppt with dil. HCl (or yellow ppt with KI) : Pb²⁺
                           +--> No ppt with dil. HCl or KI                      : Al³⁺
```

> **Figure 3.7** – Master decision flowchart for the seven cations. Start with precipitate color in NaOH, then test white precipitates with excess NaOH, excess NH₄OH, and finally the confirming test for lead(II). Every branch ends at a single unique ion!

### Example 8. Identifying an Unknown Solution from the Table

A clear, colorless solution **P** (known to be CaCl₂, ZnCl₂, or AlCl₃) gives the following test results:
- With a few drops of NaOH: white gelatinous precipitate, which dissolves in excess NaOH.
- With a few drops of NH₄OH: white gelatinous precipitate, which does not dissolve in excess NH₄OH.
Which salt is in solution P?

- Because the precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH, it cannot be Ca²⁺ (Ca(OH)₂ does not dissolve in excess NaOH).
- Because the precipitate does not dissolve in excess NH₄OH, it cannot be Zn²⁺ (Zn(OH)₂ dissolves in excess NH₄OH).
- That leaves **Al³⁺**, which also matches the gelatinous white appearance of Al(OH)₃: the salt is **AlCl₃**.

**Check:** Compare with the Al³⁺ row of our decision table: NaOH drops → white gelatinous ✅; excess NaOH → dissolves ✅; NH₄OH drops → white gelatinous ✅; excess NH₄OH → insoluble ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Thinking that excess NH₄OH dissolves all the same precipitates that excess NaOH dissolved."**
Students often fall into this trap because three white precipitates dissolved in excess NaOH, making it tempting to assume "adding excess alkali dissolves amphoteric precipitates in general." Let's compare three tubes with excess NH₄OH:

1. **Tube 1 (Cu²⁺):** A few drops of NH₄OH give a pale blue precipitate; in excess NH₄OH it dissolves to form a deep inky-blue solution. (Notice that Cu(OH)₂ did *not* dissolve in excess NaOH!)
2. **Tube 2 (Fe³⁺):** Gives a reddish-brown precipitate that **remains undissolved** in excess NH₄OH.
3. **Tube 3 (Al³⁺):** Gives a white gelatinous precipitate that **remains undissolved** in excess NH₄OH—even though Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric! Weak NH₄OH simply does not have a high enough OH⁻ concentration to dissolve Al(OH)₃ or Pb(OH)₂.

**Rule:** In **excess NH₄OH**, **only two** precipitates from our list dissolve: **Cu²⁺ (giving a deep inky-blue solution)** and **Zn²⁺ (giving a colorless solution)**. Meanwhile, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺, Fe²⁺, and Fe³⁺ precipitates stay undissolved, and Ca²⁺ forms no precipitate with NH₄OH in the first place.

## Quick Recap

- **NH₄OH** is a weak alkali (low OH⁻ concentration, Chapter 4), yet it still precipitates the hydroxides of Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺. With Ca²⁺ it gives no precipitate (or at most a faint haze).
- General ionic equation for precipitation with NH₄OH: M²⁺ + 2NH₄OH → M(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄⁺ (spectator anions like Cl⁻ and NO₃⁻ stay unchanged).
- **In excess NH₄OH, only two precipitates dissolve:** **Cu²⁺** (forming a deep inky-blue solution of [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺) and **Zn²⁺** (forming a colorless solution of [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺).
- The precipitates of **Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺** do **not** dissolve in excess NH₄OH; Ca²⁺ shows no change.
- **Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺** behave alike in both NaOH and NH₄OH; tell them apart by testing a fresh portion with dilute HCl (white PbCl₂↓) or KI (bright yellow PbI₂↓)—teacher demo only, as lead is toxic.
- **Amphoteric hydroxides** (Zn(OH)₂, Al(OH)₃, Pb(OH)₂) react with both acids and strong alkalis (NaOH). Dissolving in excess NH₄OH is due to complex-ion formation, not amphoteric behavior.
- **Decision flowchart:** check precipitate color with NaOH → test white precipitates with excess NaOH → test with excess NH₄OH → use HCl/KI to distinguish Pb²⁺ from Al³⁺.

(All questions are meant to be solved on paper; students never perform these chemical tests themselves.)

Use these values where needed: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Al 27, Cl 35.5, Zn 65.

1. A teacher adds a few drops of NH₄OH to AlCl₃ solution and then excess. Write what is seen at each stage, and the molecular and ionic equations for the first stage.
2. A student says, "Zinc nitrate gives a white precipitate with NH₄OH, so it must behave like lead acetate." Do both give the same result in excess NH₄OH? Use the table to answer.
3. 9.45 g of Zn(NO₃)₂ reacts exactly with NH₄OH to form Zn(OH)₂ and NH₄NO₃. Find the mass of Zn(OH)₂ formed and of NH₄NO₃, and check using total mass (N 14, O 16, H 1, Zn 65).
4. Four colourless solutions are labelled W, X, Y, Z (ZnCl₂, CaCl₂, AlCl₃ and lead acetate, in some order). Few drops of NH₄OH: W gives no precipitate, the other three give white precipitates. Excess NH₄OH: X's precipitate dissolves; Y's and Z's remain. Dilute HCl: Y gives a white precipitate, Z gives none. Identify W, X, Y and Z.
5. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) NH₄OH contains many OH⁻ ions, more than NaOH of the same strength. (b) Excess NH₄OH dissolves the precipitate formed by Fe³⁺. (c) Zn(OH)₂ reacts with both an acid and a strong alkali. (d) Ca²⁺ gives a heavy white precipitate with NH₄OH.
6. Why does a weak alkali like NH₄OH still precipitate Fe³⁺ but gives almost nothing with Ca²⁺? Answer in two or three sentences, using the idea of how little each hydroxide dissolves.
7. Solution R gives a pale blue precipitate with a few drops of NH₄OH, which dissolves in excess to give an inky blue solution. Name the metal ion, give the formula of the complex ion formed, and write one possible chloride salt of R.
8. Write the equations that show Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric (one with HCl, one with NaOH). Verify the atom count of each.
9. Which one of NaOH or NH₄OH can be used in excess to tell Zn²⁺ from Al³⁺? Explain the result seen with each reagent for both ions.
10. A student writes "CuCl₂ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂ + NH₄Cl". Find the error by counting atoms, correct it, and write the ionic equation.
11. Draw a test-tube figure with the labels "Fe³⁺ solution", "few drops NH₄OH" and "excess NH₄OH" and show where the precipitate lies. What is the colour of the precipitate?
12. In the reaction of Fe(NO₃)₃ with NH₄OH, name the spectator ion and explain why it does not appear in the ionic equation.
13. List all the ions from the seven that give a precipitate with NH₄OH that does NOT dissolve in excess, and group them by colour.
14. Lead acetate solution is tested with dilute HCl in a teacher demo. State what is seen, write the equation, and give two safety rules for handling lead compounds.

### Answers

1. With a few drops of NH₄OH: a **white gelatinous precipitate of Al(OH)₃** forms. With excess NH₄OH: **the precipitate remains undissolved**. Molecular: AlCl₃ + 3NH₄OH → Al(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄Cl. Ionic: Al³⁺ + 3NH₄OH → Al(OH)₃↓ + 3NH₄⁺. Check: Al 1, Cl 3, N 3, H: 15 = 3 + 12, O 3 ✅; ionic charge: +3 = 3 × (+1) ✅.
2. **No.** From our decision table, the white precipitate from Zn²⁺ **dissolves** in excess NH₄OH to give a colorless solution, whereas the white precipitate from Pb²⁺ **does not dissolve** in excess NH₄OH. They look alike after a few drops, but behave oppositely in excess! Check: Zn²⁺ "dissolves", Pb²⁺ "insoluble" ✅.
3. Molar mass of Zn(NO₃)₂ = 65 + 2 × 62 = 189 g/mol; moles = 9.45 ÷ 189 = **0.05 mol**. From the 1 : 1 : 2 ratio in Zn(NO₃)₂ + 2NH₄OH → Zn(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄NO₃: mass of Zn(OH)₂ = 0.05 mol × 99 = **4.95 g**; mass of NH₄NO₃ = 0.10 mol × 80 = **8.0 g**; mass of NH₄OH reacted = 0.10 × 35 = 3.5 g. Check: 9.45 + 3.5 = 12.95 g and 4.95 + 8.0 = 12.95 g ✅.
4. **W = CaCl₂** (gives no precipitate with NH₄OH). **X = ZnCl₂** (its white precipitate dissolves in excess NH₄OH). Between Y and Z, the one that gives a white precipitate (PbCl₂) with dilute HCl is lead acetate: **Y = lead(II) acetate**, and **Z = AlCl₃**. Check: Y's precipitate stays in excess NH₄OH ✅, and Z gives no precipitate with HCl ✅.
5. (a) **False** ❌: NH₄OH is a weak alkali and contains far fewer OH⁻ ions than NaOH of the same concentration. (b) **False** ❌: Fe(OH)₃ does not dissolve in excess NH₄OH. (c) **True** ✅: Zn(OH)₂ is amphoteric. (d) **False** ❌: Ca²⁺ gives no precipitate (or at most a faint haze) with NH₄OH.
6. Fe(OH)₃ is so extremely insoluble in water that even the small concentration of OH⁻ ions in weak NH₄OH is enough to precipitate Fe³⁺ completely. Ca(OH)₂, however, is slightly soluble in water and requires a much higher concentration of OH⁻ ions to precipitate than weak NH₄OH can supply. Thus Ca²⁺ gives no precipitate with NH₄OH.
7. Dissolving in excess NH₄OH to give a deep inky-blue solution identifies the metal ion as **Cu²⁺**, forming the complex ion **[Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺** (studied in higher grades). A soluble chloride salt is **CuCl₂**. Check: matches the Cu²⁺ row of the table ✅.
8. With acid: Al(OH)₃ + 3HCl → AlCl₃ + 3H₂O (Al 1, O 3, H 6, Cl 3 on both sides ✅). With strong alkali: Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O (Al 1, Na 1, O 4, H 4 on both sides ✅). Reacting with both proves Al(OH)₃ is amphoteric.
9. **NH₄OH (in excess).** With excess NH₄OH, Zn²⁺'s white precipitate dissolves to form a colorless solution, whereas Al³⁺'s white gelatinous precipitate remains undissolved. (In excess NaOH, both precipitates dissolve to give colorless solutions, so NaOH cannot tell them apart.) Check: Zn dissolves in both; Al dissolves in excess NaOH but not in excess NH₄OH ✅.
10. In the student's unbalanced equation, Cl is 2 on the left vs. 1 on the right; N is 2 vs. 1; H is 10 vs. 6. Balanced molecular equation: **CuCl₂ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄Cl** (Cu 1, Cl 2, N 2, H 10, O 2 ✅). Ionic equation: **Cu²⁺ + 2NH₄OH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NH₄⁺**.
11. Sketch three tubes: Tube 1 holds the yellow-brown Fe³⁺ solution; Tube 2 ("few drops NH₄OH") has a settled precipitate (`###`) at the bottom; Tube 3 ("excess NH₄OH") still has the exact same precipitate (`###`) at the bottom. The color of the precipitate is **reddish-brown**. Check: matches the Fe³⁺ row of the table ✅.
12. The spectator ion is **NO₃⁻**. Because NO₃⁻ remains dissolved and unchanged in the solution before and after the reaction, it is omitted from the ionic equation. Check: 3 NO₃⁻ on both sides ✅.
13. Cations whose precipitates with NH₄OH do NOT dissolve in excess: **Fe²⁺ (dirty green)**, **Fe³⁺ (reddish-brown)**, and **Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ (white; Al(OH)₃ is gelatinous white)**. (Ca²⁺ forms no precipitate with NH₄OH, while Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺ precipitates dissolve in excess.)
14. A **white precipitate of PbCl₂** is seen: (CH₃COO)₂Pb + 2HCl → PbCl₂↓ + 2CH₃COOH (Pb 1, C 4, H 8, O 4, Cl 2 on both sides ✅). Safety rules: (i) all lead compounds are toxic—never touch or taste them, and only the teacher performs the demonstration with goggles and gloves; (ii) never pour lead waste down the sink—collect it in the heavy-metal waste container.
