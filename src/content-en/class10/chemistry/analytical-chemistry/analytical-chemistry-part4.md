# Topic 4: Identifying Anions: Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, CO₃²⁻, and NH₄⁺

In the previous topics we learned how to identify the **cation** (positive ion) of a salt using NaOH and NH₄OH. Every salt, however, has two halves (Chapter 1): a cation and an **anion** (negative ion). So half of our detective work is still ahead! Today we learn the classic tests for three major anions—**chloride (Cl⁻), sulfate (SO₄²⁻), and carbonate (CO₃²⁻)**—plus one special positive ion that is identified by releasing a gas rather than forming a hydroxide precipitate: **ammonium (NH₄⁺)**.

---

## The Apartment Gatehouse: Every Visitor Has a Dedicated Check

Uncle Martin was standing with his niece Sophia by the security gatehouse of their apartment complex. Every minute or two someone arrived at the gate, and the security officer, Mr. Brooks, stopped each person for a quick verification. Sophia noticed right away that **Mr. Brooks didn't ask everyone the exact same question**.

When a food-delivery courier rode up, Mr. Brooks checked the four-digit passkey on the courier's phone against the apartment's system. Next, when a family guest walked up, Mr. Brooks buzzed the resident's apartment on the intercom to confirm, "Are you expecting a visitor?" Then a resident drove up, and Mr. Brooks simply glanced at the parking decal on the windshield. Sophia looked puzzled. "Uncle Martin, why doesn't Mr. Brooks use the same check for everyone—like asking everyone for a passkey?"

Uncle Martin smiled. "Think about it: if he asked a family guest for a courier passkey, the guest wouldn't have one! And looking for a windshield decal wouldn't identify a delivery rider. **Each type of visitor has a specific, dedicated check** that gives **definitive proof** for that visitor. And notice something else: sometimes **two different visitors look similar at first glance**, so the guard performs a quick pre-check first to make sure there's no mix-up."

"Do the ions inside a salt work the same way?" asked Sophia. Uncle Martin nodded. "Exactly the same way. Chloride has its own dedicated check, sulfate has its own, carbonate has its own, and ammonium has its own. And that 'quick pre-check' to avoid a false alarm is the sharpest trick in today's lesson."

```
   Visitor            Dedicated Check              Anion / Ion         Dedicated Test
   ----------------   --------------------------   -----------------   ----------------------------
   Delivery courier --> Match 4-digit passkey      Chloride Cl⁻    -->  dil. HNO₃, then AgNO₃
   Family guest     --> Call on intercom           Sulfate SO₄²⁻   -->  dil. HCl, then BaCl₂
   Resident         --> Windshield decal           Carbonate CO₃²⁻ -->  dilute acid, limewater
   Parcel package   --> Barcode scanner            Ammonium NH₄⁺   -->  warm gently with NaOH
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The gatehouse analogy: just as each type of visitor is verified by a specific check, each ion is identified by its own dedicated chemical test. (This is an analogy; we unpack the real chemical tests below.)

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion; the part of a salt that comes from an acid | Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, CO₃²⁻ |
| **Curdy precipitate** | A clumpy, cottage-cheese-like white solid that forms in solution | AgCl |
| **Effervescence** | Rapid bubbling of a gas escaping from a liquid | CO₂ bubbling out when acid hits Na₂CO₃ |
| **Brisk effervescence** | Immediate, vigorous fizzing of gas bubbles | Carbonate + dilute acid |
| **Limewater** | A clear aqueous solution of Ca(OH)₂; turns milky when CO₂ gas is bubbled through it | Test for CO₂ (Chapter 1) |
| **Acidify** | To add a few drops of dilute acid to a sample solution before adding the main test reagent | Adding dil. HNO₃ before AgNO₃ |
| **Ammonia gas** | NH₃, a pungent gas that irritates the eyes, nose, and throat | Released from NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ on warming |
| **Moist red litmus** | Damp red litmus paper, which turns blue in the presence of an alkaline gas like NH₃ | Test for NH₃ gas |
| **Fume hood (fume cupboard)** | An enclosed lab cabinet with an exhaust fan that safely draws away irritating or toxic gases | Used for teacher demonstrations |

## Safety First (Read Carefully Before Proceeding)

Every test in this topic is performed **in a school lab with the teacher** (wearing goggles and gloves, using very dilute solutions dispensed by the teacher) or observed as a **teacher demonstration**. Students never perform these tests alone or at home. Keep two special hazards in mind: **silver nitrate (AgNO₃) and barium chloride (BaCl₂) are toxic**, and AgNO₃ leaves dark black stains on skin, so students never handle them directly. **Ammonia gas (NH₃)** irritates the eyes, nose, and throat, so its test is performed in a fume hood (or studied on paper). Never taste any chemical or sniff directly at the mouth of a test tube. Leftover solutions containing silver or barium ions are never poured down the sink—the teacher collects them in a designated waste bottle.

## Part A: Chloride Ion (Cl⁻): Curdy White Precipitate with AgNO₃

In Chapter 1 we saw that mixing silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution immediately produces a white precipitate: AgNO₃ + NaCl → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃ ([acids-bases-and-salts-part5.md](../acids-bases-and-salts/acids-bases-and-salts-part5.md)). There we used it as a way to prepare an insoluble salt. Now we flip it into a detective test: **if an unknown solution contains chloride ions (Cl⁻), adding silver nitrate solution will produce that exact same curdy white precipitate**, making it our **proof for chloride**!

Only two ions do the actual work here, while Na⁺ (or whatever cation is present) and NO₃⁻ act as spectator ions:

Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓ (white, curdy precipitate)

Silver chloride (AgCl) has one more confirming signature: **it dissolves readily when excess dilute NH₄OH is added** (forming a colorless water-soluble complex ion, [Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺, studied in higher grades). That confirms the white precipitate really is silver chloride.

### Why Do We Acidify with Dilute HNO₃ First?

In the official test for chloride, we **first add a few drops of dilute nitric acid (HNO₃)** before adding AgNO₃(aq) (or we check that the white precipitate is insoluble in dilute HNO₃). Why? Because **carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻)** also react with Ag⁺ to form a **whitish (pale yellowish-white) precipitate** of silver carbonate, Ag₂CO₃! If a solution contained carbonate instead of chloride, AgNO₃ alone could fool us into thinking chloride was present. Adding dilute HNO₃ destroys any carbonate ions (releasing CO₂ gas) and dissolves Ag₂CO₃, whereas true **AgCl is completely insoluble in dilute HNO₃**:

Na₂CO₃ + 2AgNO₃ → Ag₂CO₃↓ + 2NaNO₃
Ag₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → 2AgNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑

And why must we use **HNO₃** rather than dilute HCl to acidify the sample? Because **HCl already contains Cl⁻ ions!** Adding HCl would dump chloride ions into the tube and produce a white AgCl precipitate with AgNO₃ even if the original sample had zero chloride in it! In our gatehouse analogy, that would be like the security guard handing a delivery passkey to a stranger and then saying, "Look, he has a passkey!"

```
   In the test tube:              After adding AgNO₃:

    :              :              :              :
    : clear liquid :              : clear liquid :
    :  (sample +   :              :    above     :
    :  dil. HNO₃)  :              :              :
    :              :              :  ########    :   <-- AgCl: white, curdy
    :              :              :  ########    :       precipitate settled at bottom
    :______________:              :______________:
      before (clear)                 after
```

> **Figure 4.2** – The test for chloride: acidifying the sample solution with dilute HNO₃ and adding a few drops of AgNO₃ produces a curdy white precipitate of AgCl at the bottom of the tube.

### Example 1. Testing for Cl⁻ in NaCl Solution

**Question:** A colorless solution contains NaCl. The teacher adds a few drops of dilute HNO₃ followed by AgNO₃ solution. What is observed? Write the molecular and net ionic equations.

**Step 1 (What was added):** Dilute HNO₃ (pre-check), then AgNO₃ solution.
**Step 2 (What was seen):** A curdy white precipitate (insoluble in dilute HNO₃, soluble in dilute NH₄OH).
**Step 3 (What it means):** Chloride ions (Cl⁻) are present.
**Step 4 (Equations):**
Molecular: NaCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl↓ + NaNO₃
Net ionic: Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓ (spectator ions: Na⁺ and NO₃⁻)

**Check:** Atoms: Na 1 = 1, Cl 1 = 1, Ag 1 = 1, N 1 = 1, O 3 = 3 ✅. Ionic charge: left (+1) + (−1) = 0, right neutral AgCl = 0 ✅.

### Example 2. MgCl₂ Has Two Chloride Ions per Formula Unit

**Question:** A solution of MgCl₂ is acidified with dilute HNO₃ and treated with AgNO₃ solution. Write the balanced molecular equation and explain what happens to the Mg²⁺ ion.

MgCl₂ + 2AgNO₃ → 2AgCl↓ + Mg(NO₃)₂

Because one formula unit of MgCl₂ releases **2 Cl⁻ ions**, it requires **2 Ag⁺ ions** and forms **2 formula units of AgCl**. Meanwhile, the Mg²⁺ ion remains dissolved in the water alongside the NO₃⁻ ions—it is a spectator ion and does not interfere with the chloride test at all.

**Check:** Mg 1 = 1, Cl 2 = 2, Ag 2 = 2, N 2 = 2, O 6 = 6 ✅. For each chloride ion, the net ionic reaction is still Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓ ✅.

## Part B: Sulfate Ion (SO₄²⁻): White Precipitate with BaCl₂

The dedicated test for sulfate ions uses **barium chloride (BaCl₂) solution**. Because **barium sulfate (BaSO₄)** is insoluble in water (recall our solubility rules in Chapter 1, Topic 5), mixing Ba²⁺ ions with SO₄²⁻ ions immediately produces a heavy white precipitate:

Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓ (white precipitate)

**Key signature of BaSO₄:** It is **insoluble in dilute mineral acids** (it does not dissolve in dilute HCl or dilute HNO₃). That property is crucial for our pre-check!

### Why Do We Acidify with Dilute HCl First?

Once again, carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻) can set a trap: **barium carbonate (BaCO₃)** is *also* a white precipitate! If we added BaCl₂ to a solution containing carbonate, white BaCO₃ would precipitate out and mimic sulfate. To prevent that false alarm, we **first add dilute HCl** (or add dilute HCl to the white precipitate). Any carbonate present breaks down with brisk effervescence (releasing CO₂ gas), and BaCO₃ dissolves completely in dilute HCl—whereas **BaSO₄ stays completely undissolved**:

Na₂CO₃ + BaCl₂ → BaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl
BaCO₃ + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑

Here we acidify with dilute **HCl** (or dilute HNO₃), but **never with sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄)**—because H₂SO₄ already contains SO₄²⁻ ions and would immediately form a white BaSO₄ precipitate with BaCl₂ even if the sample contained no sulfate!

```
   Sample + BaCl₂:                Then add dilute HCl:

    :              :              :              :
    :  liquid      :              :  liquid      :
    :              :              :              :
    :  ########    :   ------>    :  ########    <-- BaSO₄: does NOT dissolve
    :______________:              :______________:        (sulfate is present)

    :              :              :              :
    :  liquid      :              : clear liquid :
    :  ########    :   ------>    : (bubbles of  :
    :______________:              :  CO₂ rise)   :
                                  :______________:   <-- BaCO₃: dissolves with fizzing
                                                         (it was carbonate, not sulfate!)
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Top pair: a BaSO₄ precipitate remains undissolved after adding dilute HCl, confirming sulfate (SO₄²⁻). Bottom pair: a BaCO₃ precipitate dissolves with fizzing when dilute HCl is added, showing the white solid came from carbonate (CO₃²⁻) rather than sulfate.

### Example 3. Testing for SO₄²⁻ in Na₂SO₄ Solution

**Question:** To a solution of Na₂SO₄, the teacher adds a few drops of dilute HCl followed by BaCl₂ solution. What is observed? Write the equations and name the spectator ions.

**Step 1:** Add dilute HCl (pre-check to rule out carbonate), then BaCl₂ solution.
**Step 2:** A white precipitate forms that does not dissolve even when more dilute HCl is added.
**Step 3:** Sulfate ions (SO₄²⁻) are present.
**Step 4:** Molecular: Na₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NaCl. Net ionic: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓. Spectator ions: Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

**Check:** Na 2 = 2, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4, Ba 1 = 1, Cl 2 = 2 ✅. Ionic charge: (+2) + (−2) = 0 ✅. (Barium salts are toxic—teacher demonstration only.)

### Example 4. MgSO₄ and (NH₄)₂SO₄: Same Anion, Different Cations

**Question:** Both MgSO₄ and (NH₄)₂SO₄ solutions are acidified with dilute HCl and treated with BaCl₂ solution. Will both give the exact same white precipitate? Write both molecular equations.

Yes! Both form the exact same white precipitate of **BaSO₄**, because **this test responds to the SO₄²⁻ anion regardless of which cation is paired with it**.
MgSO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + MgCl₂
(NH₄)₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NH₄Cl

**Check:** First equation: Mg 1, S 1, O 4, Ba 1, Cl 2 on both sides ✅. Second equation: N 2, H 8, S 1, O 4, Ba 1, Cl 2 on both sides ✅. Both share the identical net ionic equation: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓.

## Part C: Carbonate Ion (CO₃²⁻): Add Dilute Acid, Watch for Bubbles and Milky Limewater

The test for carbonate is our most direct anion test because instead of forming a precipitate in the sample tube, **it releases a gas**! Recall from Chapter 1 that whenever an acid meets a metal carbonate, carbon dioxide gas (CO₂) fizzes out rapidly. That reaction serves as our dedicated test: **add dilute acid (such as dilute HCl or HNO₃), observe brisk effervescence (vigorous bubbling), and pass the escaping gas through clear limewater—the limewater turns milky!**

Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑
CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑
Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O (limewater turns milky due to fine white CaCO₃↓)

In net ionic form, every soluble carbonate follows the same equation: **CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑**

```
   Teacher demonstration: carbonate + dilute acid

                 delivery tube (carries CO₂ gas across)
        .--------------------------.
        :                          :
     [stopper]                     :
     :    :                   :    :    :
     :~~~~:                   :~~~~:~~~~:   <-- clear limewater, Ca(OH)₂(aq)
     :o o :                   :    o    :       (turns milky as CO₂ bubbles through)
     :_o__:                   :_________:
   test tube                  second tube
   (carbonate +               (limewater)
    dilute acid)
```

> **Figure 4.4** – Teacher demonstration of the carbonate test: adding dilute acid to a carbonate produces brisk bubbles of CO₂ gas. A stopper fitted with a delivery tube channels the gas into a tube of clear limewater, turning it milky. No heating is required for this test.

**Safety and good lab technique:** During the demonstration, the teacher makes sure to **lift the delivery tube out of the limewater as soon as the bubbling slows down**, so that cold limewater cannot get sucked backward ("suck-back") into the reaction tube. Because carbonates fizz vigorously with dilute acid at room temperature, no burner is needed.

### Example 5. Complete Carbonate Test on Na₂CO₃ Solution

**Question:** Dilute HCl is added to a solution of Na₂CO₃. What is observed, how is the gas identified, and what are the balanced equations?

**Step 1:** Add dilute HCl.
**Step 2:** Immediate brisk effervescence; passing the colorless, odorless gas through clear limewater turns the limewater milky.
**Step 3:** The gas is CO₂, proving that carbonate ions (CO₃²⁻) are present.
**Step 4:** Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂↑ and Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃↓ + H₂O.

**Check:** First equation: Na 2 = 2, C 1 = 1, O: 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ✅ (1 in H₂O, 2 in CO₂), H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2 ✅. Limewater equation: Ca 1 = 1, O: 2 + 2 = 4 = 3 + 1 ✅, H 2 = 2, C 1 = 1 ✅.

### Example 6. Testing an Insoluble Carbonate: Solid CaCO₃ (Limestone / Chalk)

**Question:** Solid calcium carbonate (CaCO₃) does not dissolve in water. How can we test it for carbonate?

Teacher demonstration: place a small pinch of the solid directly in a test tube and add dilute HCl. It fizzes briskly as CO₂ gas is released, turning limewater milky—while the solid dissolves to leave Ca²⁺ ions in the resulting CaCl₂ solution: CaCO₃ + 2HCl → CaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑.

**Check:** Ca 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O: 3 = 1 + 2 = 3, H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2 ✅.

## Part D: Ammonium Ion (NH₄⁺): Warm Gently with NaOH Solution

Now let's look at one special cation: the **ammonium ion, NH₄⁺**. Unlike our seven metal cations in Topics 2 and 3, NH₄⁺ does **not** form an insoluble hydroxide precipitate when NaOH is added—instead, **it releases a gas**! Recall from Chapter 7 (Chemical Bonding II) that NH₄⁺ is a polyatomic ion (one N bonded to four H atoms with a +1 charge). When an ammonium salt is **warmed gently with NaOH solution**, the OH⁻ ion pulls an H⁺ off NH₄⁺ to form water and release **ammonia gas (NH₃)**:

NH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + NH₃↑ + H₂O
Net ionic: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O

Ammonia gas (NH₃) has two classic signatures:
1. It has a **sharp, pungent odor**.
2. Because it is an alkaline gas, it turns **moist red litmus paper blue** when held near the mouth of the test tube.

(Students never sniff the gas directly; if a teacher demonstrates checking the odor, they use a gentle hand-"waft" inside a fume hood. We cover this test here for study.)

```
   Teacher demonstration (in a fume hood): ammonium salt + NaOH, warmed gently

        moist red litmus held near mouth:   red --> turns BLUE
               ^
               :   NH₃ gas rises out of the open tube
               :
      :                :
      :    solution    :   <-- ammonium salt + NaOH
      : (warmed gently):
      :________________:
              ^
         gentle warming (by teacher)
```

> **Figure 4.5** – Teacher demonstration for ammonium ions: warming an ammonium salt with NaOH solution releases NH₃ gas, which rises and turns a strip of moist red litmus paper held near the mouth of the open test tube blue.

### Example 7. Testing for NH₄⁺ in NH₄Cl

**Question:** A solution of NH₄Cl is warmed gently with NaOH solution. What is observed? Write the balanced molecular and net ionic equations.

**Step 1:** Add NaOH solution and warm gently (performed by the teacher in a fume hood).
**Step 2:** A pungent gas evolves that turns moist red litmus paper held at the mouth of the tube blue.
**Step 3:** Ammonium ions (NH₄⁺) are present.
**Step 4:** Molecular: NH₄Cl + NaOH → NaCl + NH₃↑ + H₂O. Net ionic: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O. Spectator ions: Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

**Check:** N 1 = 1, H: 4 + 1 = 5 = 3 + 2 ✅, Cl 1 = 1, Na 1 = 1, O 1 = 1 ✅. Ionic charge: (+1) + (−1) = 0 = 0 ✅.

### Example 8. (NH₄)₂SO₄: One Salt, Two Separate Ion Tests

**Question:** How would a chemist identify both ions in ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄?

This salt contains the **cation NH₄⁺** and the **anion SO₄²⁻**, and **each requires its own separate test on a fresh portion of the sample**:
- **For NH₄⁺:** Warm gently with NaOH solution to release NH₃ gas (turns moist red litmus blue):
  (NH₄)₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2NH₃↑ + 2H₂O
- **For SO₄²⁻:** Acidify a second portion with dilute HCl and add BaCl₂ solution to get a white precipitate of BaSO₄ (insoluble in acid):
  (NH₄)₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2NH₄Cl

**Check:** First equation: N 2 = 2, H: 8 + 2 = 10 = 6 + 4 ✅, S 1 = 1, O: 4 + 2 = 6 = 4 + 2 ✅, Na 2 = 2 ✅. Second equation: N 2, H 8, S 1, O 4, Ba 1, Cl 2 on both sides ✅. One test identifies one ion—so identifying the whole salt takes **two separate tests**.

## Part E: Calculating the Mass of a Precipitate

Can we predict the exact mass of precipitate formed in an anion test? Yes—using the exact same 4-step mole method from Chapter 5! (Atomic masses: Mg = 24, S = 32, O = 16, Cl = 35.5, Ba = 137.)

### Example 9. How Much BaSO₄ Forms from MgSO₄?

**Question:** In a teacher demonstration, a solution containing 1.20 g of MgSO₄ is treated with enough BaCl₂ solution to precipitate all the sulfate ions. What mass of BaSO₄ precipitate forms?

**Step 1 (Balanced equation):** MgSO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + MgCl₂. Mole ratio is 1 : 1.
**Step 2 (Molar masses):** MgSO₄ = 24 + 32 + 4 × 16 = 24 + 32 + 64 = **120 g/mol**. BaSO₄ = 137 + 32 + 64 = **233 g/mol**.
**Step 3 (Moles of MgSO₄):** 1.20 ÷ 120 = **0.01 mol**.
**Step 4 (From the 1 : 1 mole ratio):** Moles of BaSO₄ formed = 0.01 mol, so mass of BaSO₄ = 0.01 × 233 = **2.33 g**.

**Check (conservation of mass):** Mass of BaCl₂ needed = 0.01 × (137 + 71 = 208) = 2.08 g. Total reactants = 1.20 + 2.08 = 3.28 g; total products = 2.33 g BaSO₄ + (0.01 × 95 = 0.95 g MgCl₂) = 3.28 g ✅.

## Part F: All Four Tests at a Glance

```
   Unknown salt solution (colorless):

   Chloride (Cl⁻)?    dil. HNO₃, then AgNO₃     --> curdy white ppt (dissolves in dil. NH₄OH)
   Sulfate (SO₄²⁻)?   dil. HCl, then BaCl₂      --> white ppt (does NOT dissolve in dil. acid)
   Carbonate (CO₃²⁻)? dilute acid               --> brisk bubbles of CO₂ (turns limewater milky)
   Ammonium (NH₄⁺)?   NaOH + gentle warming     --> pungent NH₃ gas (turns moist red litmus blue)

   None of the three anions above?              --> could be another anion, such as nitrate (NO₃⁻)
```

> **Figure 4.6** – Summary of the four tests: the reagent added, what is observed, and what it proves. (The specific test for nitrate, NO₃⁻, is covered in higher grades.)

| Ion | Reagent (and Acid Pre-check) | Observation | Net Ionic Equation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chloride, Cl⁻ | Acidify with dil. HNO₃, then add AgNO₃ | Curdy white precipitate; dissolves in dilute NH₄OH | Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓ |
| Sulfate, SO₄²⁻ | Acidify with dil. HCl, then add BaCl₂ | White precipitate; insoluble in dilute acid | Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓ |
| Carbonate, CO₃²⁻ | Add dilute acid (HCl or HNO₃) | Brisk effervescence of CO₂; turns limewater milky | CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑ |
| Ammonium, NH₄⁺ | Add NaOH and warm gently | Pungent NH₃ gas; turns moist red litmus blue | NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O |

One helpful tip for elimination puzzles: if a soluble salt tests negative for chloride, sulfate, and carbonate (no precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ or BaCl₂, and no bubbles with dilute acid), and you are told the shelf contains only chlorides, sulfates, carbonates, and nitrates, then by elimination the anion must be **nitrate (NO₃⁻)**!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Adding BaCl₂ alone gave a white precipitate, so the solution definitely contains sulfate (SO₄²⁻)."**
This looks convincing because BaSO₄ is a famous white precipitate. However, **barium carbonate (BaCO₃) is also a white precipitate!** Let's see how a detective avoids this trap in three steps (teacher demonstration only, as barium salts are toxic):

1. **The teacher takes a solution of Na₂CO₃ (which contains zero sulfate) and adds BaCl₂ directly:** Na₂CO₃ + BaCl₂ → BaCO₃↓ + 2NaCl. A heavy white precipitate appears! Stopping here would lead to the false conclusion "sulfate is present."
2. **Now the teacher adds dilute HCl to that white precipitate:** BaCO₃ + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑. The white precipitate fizzes and **dissolves completely**, proving it was BaCO₃ (carbonate), not sulfate!
3. **Next, the teacher repeats the experiment on Na₂SO₄ solution:** BaCl₂ gives a white precipitate, and when dilute HCl is added, **the precipitate does not dissolve at all**. That proves it is genuine **BaSO₄**.

| Sample Solution | Upon Adding BaCl₂ Alone | Upon Adding Dilute HCl | True Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na₂CO₃ | White precipitate (BaCO₃) | Dissolves with fizzing (CO₂ bubbles) | Carbonate (CO₃²⁻) |
| Na₂SO₄ | White precipitate (BaSO₄) | Does not dissolve | Sulfate (SO₄²⁻) |

**Rule:** A white precipitate with BaCl₂ is **not** proof of sulfate unless you also acidify with **dilute HCl** (either before adding BaCl₂ to remove any carbonate, or afterward to confirm the white precipitate does not dissolve in dilute acid). And never use H₂SO₄ to acidify—it brings its own SO₄²⁻ ions!

## Quick Recap

- Every salt has a cation and an anion: Topics 2 and 3 covered cations, and Topic 4 covers anions plus ammonium.
- **Chloride (Cl⁻):** Acidify with dilute HNO₃, then add AgNO₃ → curdy white AgCl↓ (insoluble in HNO₃, soluble in dilute NH₄OH). Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓. Never acidify with HCl, because HCl contains Cl⁻.
- **Sulfate (SO₄²⁻):** Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂ → white BaSO₄↓ (insoluble in dilute HCl). Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓. Never acidify with H₂SO₄, because H₂SO₄ contains SO₄²⁻.
- **Carbonate (CO₃²⁻):** Add dilute acid → brisk effervescence of CO₂ gas, which turns clear limewater milky. CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑.
- **Ammonium (NH₄⁺):** Warm gently with NaOH solution → pungent NH₃ gas that turns moist red litmus paper blue. NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O.
- Anion precipitates depend only on the anion: both MgSO₄ and (NH₄)₂SO₄ give the exact same BaSO₄ precipitate with BaCl₂.
- Silver and barium compounds are toxic, and ammonia gas is irritating: all tests are teacher demonstrations or supervised school-lab procedures.
- Precipitate masses follow Chapter 5 mole ratios: 1.20 g of MgSO₄ (0.01 mol) yields 2.33 g of BaSO₄ (0.01 mol).
- **Avoid the carbonate trap:** Always use the dilute acid pre-check before (or with) AgNO₃ and BaCl₂ so carbonate ions cannot mimic chloride or sulfate.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are meant to be solved on paper. Atomic masses: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, S 32, Cl 35.5, K 39, Ag 108, Ba 137.)

1. A teacher tests a colourless solution of potassium chloride, KCl, by adding dilute nitric acid and then silver nitrate solution. State what is seen, write the balanced equation and the ionic equation, and name the spectator ions.
2. Potassium sulphate, K₂SO₄, solution is acidified with dilute HCl and barium chloride solution is added. State what is seen, write the balanced equation and the ionic equation. What would be seen if dilute HCl is added to the precipitate?
3. Sodium carbonate solution is treated with dilute nitric acid. State what is seen, how the gas is tested, and write the balanced equation and the ionic equation. Which spectator ions are present?
4. Ammonium nitrate solution, NH₄NO₃, is warmed with NaOH solution in a teacher demo. Write the balanced equation and the ionic equation, and state what happens to a moist red litmus paper held near the mouth of the test tube. Why is the paper held near the mouth and not dipped in the liquid?
5. A teacher adds excess barium chloride to a solution containing 5.22 g of potassium sulphate, so that all the sulphate forms a precipitate. Find the moles of K₂SO₄ and the mass of BaSO₄ formed.
6. A solution contains 5.35 g of ammonium chloride, NH₄Cl. Excess acidified silver nitrate is added in a teacher demo. Find the mass of silver chloride formed and the mass of AgNO₃ used up. Which two ions of NH₄Cl are present, and which test would show the other one?
7. Three colourless solutions are labelled P, Q and R. They are MgCl₂, K₂SO₄ and Na₂CO₃ in some order. Dilute HCl produces brisk bubbles only in P. After the dilute HCl treatment, BaCl₂ produces a white precipitate only in Q. Identify P, Q and R, and name the test that confirms R.
8. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Dilute HCl is a good acid to add before the silver nitrate test for chloride. (b) BaSO₄ dissolves in dilute HCl. (c) The white precipitate AgCl dissolves in dilute NH₄OH. (d) NH₃ turns moist red litmus blue. (e) MgSO₄ gives no precipitate with BaCl₂.
9. Why is dilute H₂SO₄ not used to acidify a solution before the barium chloride test for sulphate? What would the test falsely show?
10. A student adds BaCl₂ to a colourless solution and gets a white precipitate. The precipitate dissolves completely in dilute HCl with bubbles. Which ion was present, which ion was NOT present, and what is the gas?
11. Write the ionic equation for each of the four tests of this topic, and for each say which common ions in the unreacted solution are spectators. Use the salts NaCl, MgSO₄, Na₂CO₃ and NH₄Cl.
12. Describe the safe way a teacher carries out the ammonia test and the carbonate test: which gas is dangerous, where is the test done, how is the gas smelt or tested, what precautions are taken with the delivery tube, and why can a student never do these at home?

### Answers

1. Dilute HNO₃ is added first (to rule out carbonate), followed by AgNO₃: a **curdy white precipitate of AgCl** forms, which dissolves in dilute NH₄OH. Molecular equation: KCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl↓ + KNO₃. Net ionic: Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓. Spectator ions: **K⁺ and NO₃⁻**. Check: K 1, Cl 1, Ag 1, N 1, O 3 on both sides ✅.
2. A **white precipitate of BaSO₄** forms. Molecular: K₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2KCl. Net ionic: Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓. If dilute HCl is added to the precipitate, **the precipitate remains undissolved** (BaSO₄ is insoluble in dilute acids). Check: K 2, S 1, O 4, Ba 1, Cl 2 on both sides ✅; ionic charge (+2) + (−2) = 0 ✅.
3. **Immediate brisk effervescence (bubbles)**; when the escaping gas is passed through clear limewater, the **limewater turns milky**, confirming the gas is CO₂. Molecular: Na₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → 2NaNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂↑. Net ionic: CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑. Spectator ions: **Na⁺ and NO₃⁻**. Check: Na 2, C 1, O: 3 + 6 = 9 = 6 + 1 + 2, H 2, N 2 ✅.
4. Molecular: NH₄NO₃ + NaOH → NaNO₃ + NH₃↑ + H₂O. Net ionic: NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O. The moist red litmus paper held near the mouth of the tube **turns blue**. It must be held near the mouth (in the rising gas) rather than dipped into the liquid because the liquid already contains NaOH (an alkali), which would turn red litmus blue on contact even if no ammonia gas were produced! Check: N: 2 = 1 + 1, H: 4 + 1 = 5 = 3 + 2, O: 3 + 1 = 4 = 3 + 1, Na 1 = 1 ✅.
5. Molar mass of K₂SO₄ = 2 × 39 + 32 + 64 = **174 g/mol**. Moles of K₂SO₄ = 5.22 ÷ 174 = **0.03 mol**. From K₂SO₄ + BaCl₂ → BaSO₄↓ + 2KCl (1 : 1 ratio), moles of BaSO₄ = 0.03 mol. Molar mass of BaSO₄ = 233 g/mol, so mass = 0.03 × 233 = **6.99 g**. Check: BaCl₂ needed = 0.03 × 208 = 6.24 g; total reactants = 5.22 + 6.24 = 11.46 g; total products = 6.99 + (0.06 × 74.5 = 4.47 g KCl) = 11.46 g ✅.
6. Molar mass of NH₄Cl = 14 + 4 + 35.5 = **53.5 g/mol**. Moles = 5.35 ÷ 53.5 = **0.1 mol**. From NH₄Cl + AgNO₃ → AgCl↓ + NH₄NO₃ (1 : 1 : 1 ratio): molar mass of AgCl = 108 + 35.5 = 143.5 g/mol, so mass = 0.1 × 143.5 = **14.35 g**. Molar mass of AgNO₃ = 108 + 14 + 48 = 170 g/mol, so mass = 0.1 × 170 = **17.0 g**. The two ions in NH₄Cl are **NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻**. To test for the other ion (NH₄⁺), warm a fresh portion gently with NaOH solution to release NH₃ gas, which turns moist red litmus blue. Check: 14.35 + (0.1 × 80 = 8.0 g NH₄NO₃) = 22.35 g = 5.35 + 17.0 ✅.
7. Brisk bubbles with dilute HCl occur only in **P**, so **P = Na₂CO₃** (carbonate). After dilute HCl treatment, BaCl₂ gives a white precipitate only in **Q**, so **Q = K₂SO₄** (sulfate). That leaves **R = MgCl₂** (chloride). To confirm R: acidify with dilute HNO₃ and add AgNO₃ solution to get a curdy white precipitate of AgCl (soluble in dilute NH₄OH). Check: each solution matches one unique anion ✅.
8. (a) **False** ❌: HCl contains Cl⁻ ions of its own and would give a false white AgCl precipitate with AgNO₃; **dilute HNO₃** must be used instead. (b) **False** ❌: BaSO₄ is insoluble in dilute HCl. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌: MgSO₄ contains SO₄²⁻ ions and gives a white precipitate of BaSO₄ with BaCl₂.
9. Dilute H₂SO₄ contains SO₄²⁻ ions of its own. Adding H₂SO₄ before BaCl₂ would introduce sulfate ions into the sample and immediately precipitate white BaSO₄, **falsely indicating that sulfate was present** in the original sample even if there was none. Therefore, dilute **HCl** is used instead.
10. Because the white precipitate dissolved completely in dilute HCl with fizzing, it was **BaCO₃**, proving that **carbonate (CO₃²⁻)** was present and **sulfate (SO₄²⁻) was NOT present** (since BaSO₄ does not dissolve in dilute HCl). The gas released is **CO₂** (which turns limewater milky). Equation: BaCO₃ + 2HCl → BaCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂↑ ✅ (Ba 1, C 1, O 3, H 2, Cl 2 on both sides).
11. Chloride (NaCl + AgNO₃): **Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓**; spectators: Na⁺ and NO₃⁻. Sulfate (MgSO₄ + BaCl₂): **Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓**; spectators: Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻. Carbonate (Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl): **CO₃²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H₂O + CO₂↑**; spectators: Na⁺ and Cl⁻. Ammonium (NH₄Cl + NaOH): **NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O**; spectators: Cl⁻ and Na⁺. Check: charges balance on both sides in all four equations ✅.
12. **Irritating/hazardous gas:** ammonia (NH₃), which irritates the eyes, nose, and throat. The ammonia test is performed by the teacher in a **fume hood**, holding moist red litmus paper near the mouth of the open test tube and never sniffing the tube directly (using at most a gentle hand-waft). In the carbonate test, the teacher keeps the reaction unheated and **lifts the delivery tube out of the limewater before bubbling stops** so cold limewater cannot suck back into the reaction tube. Students must never try these at home because home kitchens lack a fume hood, lab PPE, teacher supervision, and safe chemical waste disposal, and reagents like NaOH, AgNO₃, and BaCl₂ are corrosive or toxic.
