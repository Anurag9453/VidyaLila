# Topic 5: Identifying an Unknown Salt: Putting All the Tests Together

In Topics 2 and 3 we learned how to test for **cations** (positive ions) using NaOH and NH₄OH—watching precipitate colors and whether they dissolve in excess reagent. In Topic 4 we learned how to test for **anions** (negative ions) and ammonium. Up to now, we have practiced each tool one at a time. Today we open the full detective toolkit to solve the ultimate lab mystery: given an **unknown salt**, how do we identify its **cation**, its **anion**, and its complete **chemical formula**?

---

## When the Rain Washed the Labels Away

After a heavy weekend thunderstorm, Mr. Vance, the senior chemistry teacher in charge of the school laboratories, brought his niece Maya along while he checked on the chemistry prep room. Rainwater had seeped through a crack in the window frame and soaked the bottom shelf of a storage cabinet, washing the paper labels right off four glass reagent bottles! Not a single letter on the चार labels was readable.

Maya put on her safety goggles and stood behind the yellow safety line on the floor (in the prep room, students only observe unless the teacher hands them a prepared item under supervision). "Uncle Vance, how are we going to figure out what's in each bottle? Taste a tiny drop?" Mr. Vance laughed and shook his head. "Never in a million years! Tasting, sniffing, or touching unknown chemicals isn't detective work—it's a trip to the hospital. We'll identify every bottle cleanly using **systematic chemical tests**: first we **look**, then we **dissolve** (if it's a solid), then we test for the **cation**, and finally we test for the **anion**."

"How many tests will each bottle take?" asked Maya. "Just enough to answer our questions," said Mr. Vance. "One test identifies the cation; a second test on a fresh portion identifies the anion. Put the two halves together, and we can print a brand-new label for every bottle!"

```
   Four unlabeled bottles on the shelf:

    [ ? ]      [ ? ]      [ ? ]      [ ? ]
    Bottle 1   Bottle 2   Bottle 3   Bottle 4
    yellow-    pale       blue       colorless
    brown      green      solution   solution
    solution   solution              (clear like water)
```

> **Figure 5.1** – The four unlabeled bottles from the storeroom shelf. Step 1 requires no chemicals at all—just **looking at the color of the solution**. That color gives us a helpful first clue about the cation, though never the complete answer!

We will crack the identity of all four bottles—and three bonus mysteries—step by step in Part C.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Unknown salt** | A salt whose chemical identity is not given and must be deduced through systematic tests | A salt from an unlabeled bottle |
| **Observation table** | A structured log recording each test performed alongside what was visibly observed | Test: drops of NaOH. Observation: reddish-brown ppt |
| **Cation test** | Tests used to identify the positive ion (using NaOH and NH₄OH in drops and in excess) | Cu²⁺: pale blue ppt, deep blue in excess NH₄OH |
| **Anion test** | Tests used to identify the negative ion (using dilute acid, AgNO₃, or BaCl₂) | Cl⁻: curdy white ppt with acidified AgNO₃ |
| **Charge balance** | Writing a salt's formula so that total positive charge and total negative charge sum to zero | Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ give Fe₂(SO₄)₃ |
| **Elimination** | Ruling out possibilities one by one through negative tests until only one candidate remains | Not Cl⁻, not SO₄²⁻, not CO₃²⁻ → must be NO₃⁻ |

## Part A: The Five-Step Detective Plan

When identifying an unknown salt, we always follow the exact same five-step sequence. Skipping steps or jumbling their order leads straight into traps (as we will see in Common Pitfalls).

1. **Look (Inspect the color).** Note the color of the solid or its aqueous solution. This is a helpful **initial clue**, not final proof.
2. **Dissolve in distilled water.** If the sample is a solid salt, dissolve a small pinch in clean distilled water. Our wet-chemical tests require **free mobile ions**, which are released only when the salt dissolves in water.
3. **Identify the cation.** Divide the solution into small portions in clean test tubes. To one portion, add a few drops of NaOH and then excess NaOH. To a second portion, add a few drops of NH₄OH and then excess NH₄OH. Match the results against our shared cation table (Topics 2 and 3).
4. **Identify the anion.** Using fresh portions of the solution, run the Topic 4 anion tests: dilute acid for carbonate, dilute HNO₃ + AgNO₃ for chloride, and dilute HCl + BaCl₂ for sulfate.
5. **Write the formula and equations.** Combine the cation and anion using charge balance (criss-cross), and write the balanced equations for the confirming reactions.

```
   Step 1           Step 2         Step 3                Step 4            Step 5
   LOOK      --->   DISSOLVE --->  CATION: NaOH,   --->  ANION: acid  ---> FORMULA
   (color)          (in pure       then NH₄OH            first, then       (charge
                    water)         (drops, excess)       AgNO₃ / BaCl₂     balance)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – The five-step detective workflow for identifying an unknown salt: inspect color → dissolve in water → test cation → test anion → write the formula.

**Safety reminder:** All tests are performed **in a school lab under teacher supervision** (wearing goggles and gloves, using dilute solutions dispensed by the teacher). Compounds of barium, silver, and lead are toxic; even dilute NaOH is corrosive to eyes and skin; and no one ever peers straight down into a test tube or sniffs a gas directly. All heavy-metal waste is collected by the teacher in designated waste bottles—never poured down the drain.

### What Solution Color Tells Us (and What It Doesn't)

Three of our cations give their aqueous solutions a characteristic color, while the rest leave the solution **colorless**:

| Solution Color | Possible Cation |
|---|---|
| Blue (or blue-green) | Cu²⁺ |
| Pale green | Fe²⁺ |
| Yellow-brown | Fe³⁺ |
| Colorless | Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺ (as well as NH₄⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, etc.) |

Notice two crucial points: first, solution color gives a clue **only about the cation**, telling us nothing about the anion (for example, CuSO₄, CuCl₂, and Cu(NO₃)₂ solutions are all blue); second, four of our seven cations (plus NH₄⁺) are completely **colorless** in water. So color is always Step 1—never the final verdict!

## Part B: Writing the Salt Formula by Charge Balance

Recall the criss-cross method from Grade 9 ([language-of-chemistry-part5.md](../../../class9/chemistry/language-of-chemistry/language-of-chemistry-part5.md)): once your tests identify the cation and the anion, balance their charges so the overall salt formula is electrically neutral.

### Example 1. Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻

The charge magnitudes are 3 (on Fe³⁺) and 2 (on SO₄²⁻). Criss-crossing places a subscript `2` on Fe and a subscript `3` outside parentheses around SO₄: **Fe₂(SO₄)₃** (parentheses are essential because the entire polyatomic SO₄²⁻ group is taken 3 times).

**Check:** Total positive charge = 2 × (+3) = +6; total negative charge = 3 × (−2) = −6; sum = 0 ✅. Atom count: 2 Fe, 3 S, 12 O.

### Example 2. Fe²⁺ and Cl⁻

Charge magnitudes are 2 and 1. Criss-crossing gives 1 Fe²⁺ and 2 Cl⁻: **FeCl₂**.

**Check:** (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.

### Example 3. Cu²⁺ and NO₃⁻

Charge magnitudes are 2 and 1. Pairing 1 Cu²⁺ with 2 NO₃⁻ groups gives **Cu(NO₃)₂** (with parentheses around NO₃).

**Check:** (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅. Formula mass: 63.5 + 2 × (14 + 48) = 63.5 + 124 = 187.5.

## Part C: Seven Unknown Salt Mysteries Solved Step by Step

Now let's crack Mr. Vance's four unlabeled storeroom bottles—plus three extra cases! For each mystery, we examine the observation table and deduce the cation, the anion, and the formula.

### Puzzle 1: Bottle 1 (Yellow-Brown Solution)

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Yellow-brown |
| A few drops of NaOH | Reddish-brown precipitate |
| Excess NaOH | Precipitate does not dissolve |
| Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂ | White precipitate, insoluble in dilute acid |

**Deduction:** A yellow-brown solution points toward Fe³⁺. Adding NaOH gives a reddish-brown precipitate of Fe(OH)₃ that stays insoluble in excess NaOH—confirming **Fe³⁺** from our shared cation table. For the anion, a white precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ that does not dissolve in acid is BaSO₄, confirming **SO₄²⁻**. (Barium salts are toxic—teacher demonstration only.)

Net ionic equations:
- Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓
- Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓

Combining Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ gives the formula **Fe₂(SO₄)₃** (Example 1), iron(III) sulfate! Balanced molecular equation with NaOH: Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 6NaOH → 2Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3Na₂SO₄.

**Check:** Fe 2 = 2, S 3 = 3, O: 12 + 6 = 18 = 6 + 12, Na 6 = 6, H 6 = 6 ✅.

### Puzzle 2: Bottle 2 (Pale Green Solution)

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Pale green |
| A few drops of NaOH | Dirty green precipitate |
| Excess NaOH | Does not dissolve |
| A few drops and excess of NH₄OH | Dirty green precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| Acidify with dilute HNO₃, then add AgNO₃ | Curdy white precipitate, soluble in dilute NH₄OH |

**Deduction:** Both the pale green solution and the dirty green precipitate insoluble in excess NaOH and NH₄OH identify **Fe²⁺** (remember from Topic 2 that on standing in air, the top of this dirty green Fe(OH)₂ precipitate slowly turns brown). For the anion, a curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ that dissolves in dilute NH₄OH is AgCl, confirming **Cl⁻**. (Silver nitrate is toxic and stains skin—dispensed only by the teacher.)

- Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂↓
- Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓

Combining Fe²⁺ and Cl⁻ gives the formula **FeCl₂**, iron(II) chloride! Molecular equation: FeCl₂ + 2NaOH → Fe(OH)₂↓ + 2NaCl.

**Check:** Fe 1 = 1, Cl 2 = 2, Na 2 = 2, O 2 = 2, H 2 = 2 ✅.

### Puzzle 3: Bottle 3 (Blue Solution), Using Elimination

For this bottle, Mr. Vance notes one extra fact from the storeroom inventory: this shelf holds only **chlorides, sulfates, and nitrates**. (The direct wet test for nitrate is taught in higher grades.)

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Blue |
| A few drops of NaOH | Pale blue precipitate |
| Excess NaOH | Does not dissolve |
| A few drops of NH₄OH | Pale blue precipitate |
| Excess NH₄OH | Precipitate dissolves to give a deep (inky) blue solution |
| Acidify with dilute HNO₃, then add AgNO₃ | No precipitate |
| Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂ | No precipitate |

```
   Test tube 1          Test tube 2           Test tube 3
   (solution alone)     (few drops NH₄OH)     (excess NH₄OH)

   :            :       :            :        :            :
   :    blue    :       :    blue    :        :  deep inky :
   :  solution  :       :   liquid   :        :    blue    :
   :            :       :            :        :  solution  :
   :            :       :  ########  :        :            :
   '------------'       '------------'        '------------'
                        pale blue ppt         no ppt at bottom!
                        (at bottom)
```

> **Figure 5.3** – The NH₄OH test tubes for Bottle 3: starting with the blue solution, adding a few drops of NH₄OH forms a pale blue precipitate at the bottom of Tube 2, and adding excess NH₄OH dissolves the precipitate completely into a deep inky-blue solution in Tube 3.

**Deduction:** A blue solution giving a pale blue precipitate that dissolves in excess NH₄OH to form a deep inky-blue solution is the unmistakable signature of **Cu²⁺** (forming the complex ion [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺, studied in higher grades). For the anion: acidified AgNO₃ gave no precipitate (so **not Cl⁻**), and acidified BaCl₂ gave no precipitate (so **not SO₄²⁻**). Since the shelf holds only chlorides, sulfates, and nitrates, **by elimination the anion must be nitrate, NO₃⁻**!

Formula: **Cu(NO₃)₂** (Example 3), copper(II) nitrate! Molecular equation: Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃.

**Check:** Cu 1 = 1, N 2 = 2, O: 6 + 2 = 8 = 2 + 6, Na 2 = 2, H 2 = 2 ✅.

### Puzzle 4: Bottle 4 (Colorless Solution), Telling Two Twins Apart

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Colorless |
| A few drops of NaOH | White gelatinous precipitate |
| Excess NaOH | Dissolves to give a colorless solution |
| A few drops and excess of NH₄OH | White gelatinous precipitate, insoluble in excess |
| Add a few drops of KI solution | No yellow precipitate forms |
| Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂ | White precipitate, insoluble in dilute acid |

**Deduction:** Three cations give a white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH: Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺. Because the precipitate did *not* dissolve in excess NH₄OH, we rule out Zn²⁺ (which dissolves in excess NH₄OH). That leaves our two twins from Topic 3: Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺. Adding KI solution produced no yellow precipitate of PbI₂, ruling out Pb²⁺ and confirming **Al³⁺**! (All lead compounds are toxic—the KI check is a teacher demonstration.)

For the anion: a white precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ that is insoluble in acid confirms **SO₄²⁻**. So Bottle 4 contains **Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻** ions (aluminum sulfate, Al₂(SO₄)₃)!

- Al³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Al(OH)₃↓
- Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O

**Check:** Al 1 = 1, O: 3 + 1 = 4 = 2 + 2, H: 3 + 1 = 4 = 4, Na 1 = 1 ✅.

### Puzzle 5: Another Colorless Solution—Zn²⁺ or Al³⁺?

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Colorless |
| A few drops of NaOH | White precipitate |
| Excess NaOH | Dissolves to give a colorless solution |
| A few drops of NH₄OH | White precipitate |
| Excess NH₄OH | Dissolves to give a colorless solution |
| Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂ | No precipitate |
| Acidify with dilute HNO₃, then add AgNO₃ | Curdy white precipitate, soluble in dilute NH₄OH |

**Deduction:** Dissolving in excess NaOH narrows the cation to Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺. Dissolving in **excess NH₄OH** singles out **Zn²⁺** immediately (since neither Pb(OH)₂ nor Al(OH)₃ dissolves in excess NH₄OH). For the anion: curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ that dissolves in NH₄OH confirms **Cl⁻**. Thus the ions are **Zn²⁺ and Cl⁻** (zinc chloride, ZnCl₂).

- Zn²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Zn(OH)₂↓
- Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O

**Check:** Zn 1 = 1, O: 2 + 2 = 4 = 2 + 2, H: 2 + 2 = 4 = 4, Na 2 = 2 ✅.

### Puzzle 6: A White Precipitate That Refuses to Dissolve in Excess NaOH

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Colorless |
| A few drops of NaOH | Slight white precipitate |
| Excess NaOH | Does not dissolve |
| A few drops and excess of NH₄OH | No precipitate (or very faint haze) |
| Acidify with dilute HNO₃, then add AgNO₃ | Curdy white precipitate, soluble in dilute NH₄OH |

**Deduction:** A slight white precipitate with NaOH that **does not dissolve in excess NaOH**, paired with no precipitate in NH₄OH, is the unmistakable fingerprint of **Ca²⁺** (since Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺ all dissolve in excess NaOH). The anion test confirms **Cl⁻**. Thus the ions are **Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻** (calcium chloride, CaCl₂).

**An honest note on the limits of our plan:** What if an unknown solid salt does not dissolve in water at all? Then Step 2 stops right there, because our NaOH and NH₄OH tests require dissolved ions. For an insoluble carbonate like CaCO₃, we can still test the solid directly with dilute acid to identify CO₃²⁻ (Topic 4, Example 6), while systematic testing of other insoluble salts is covered in higher grades. Likewise, if a solution gives no precipitate with either NaOH or NH₄OH at room temperature, it simply doesn't contain any of our seven metal cations—which brings us right to Puzzle 7!

### Puzzle 7: No Precipitate with Cold NaOH, But a Gas on Warming!

| Test | Observation |
|---|---|
| Solution color | Colorless |
| A few drops of NaOH (cold) | No precipitate forms |
| Warm gently with NaOH solution | A pungent gas evolves that turns moist red litmus paper blue |
| Acidify with dilute HCl, then add BaCl₂ | White precipitate, insoluble in dilute acid |

**Deduction:** Giving no precipitate with cold NaOH rules out our seven metal cations, but releasing pungent ammonia gas (turning moist red litmus blue) upon gentle warming with NaOH proves the cation is **NH₄⁺** (Topic 4)! The BaCl₂ test confirms the anion is **SO₄²⁻**. Thus the ions are **NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻** (ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄).

NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O (on gentle warming).

**Check:** N 1 = 1, H: 4 + 1 = 5 = 3 + 2, O 1 = 1; charge (+1) + (−1) = 0 = 0 ✅. (Because ammonia gas is irritating, this test is performed by the teacher in a fume hood or studied on paper.)

## Part D: Telling Look-Alike Pairs Apart with a Single Test

In exams and lab puzzles, you are often handed **two look-alike ions** and asked for **one single reagent test** that tells them apart. Here is your quick-reference cheat sheet:

| Pair of Ions | Distinguishing Reagent Test | What You See for Each Ion |
|---|---|---|
| Fe²⁺ vs. Fe³⁺ | Solution color, or add NaOH / NH₄OH | Fe²⁺ gives dirty green ppt; Fe³⁺ gives reddish-brown ppt |
| Zn²⁺ vs. Al³⁺ | Add **excess NH₄OH** | Zn²⁺ white ppt **dissolves**; Al³⁺ white ppt **does not dissolve** |
| Pb²⁺ vs. Al³⁺ | Add a few drops of **KI** (or dil. HCl) | Pb²⁺ gives bright yellow PbI₂↓ (or white PbCl₂↓); Al³⁺ gives no ppt |
| Ca²⁺ vs. Zn²⁺ | Add **excess NaOH** (or NH₄OH) | In excess NaOH, Zn²⁺ white ppt **dissolves** while Ca²⁺ white ppt **does not** |

```
   Colorless solution: add a few drops of NaOH, then excess NaOH
                      :
          +-----------+------------+
          :                        :
      ppt dissolves           ppt does NOT dissolve
          :                        :
    Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺           Ca²⁺
          :
    Add excess NH₄OH
          :
    +-----+------+
    :            :
  dissolves   does NOT dissolve
    :            :
   Zn²⁺      Pb²⁺ or Al³⁺
                 :
               KI test (yellow ppt = Pb²⁺; no ppt = Al³⁺)
```

> **Figure 5.4** – Rapid decision tree for a colorless solution that forms a white precipitate with NaOH: test with excess NaOH, then excess NH₄OH, and finally KI. One question at each fork!

## Part E: One Quantitative Check on Precipitate Mass

Let's connect Puzzle 1 (Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + NaOH) to a quick mole calculation from Chapter 5.

### Example 4. How Many Grams of Fe(OH)₃ Precipitate Will Form?

**Question:** In a demonstration, the teacher dissolves 40 g of Fe₂(SO₄)₃ in water and adds the exact amount of NaOH required to precipitate all the Fe³⁺ ions. What mass of Fe(OH)₃ precipitate forms, and what mass of NaOH is consumed?

**Balanced equation:** Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 6NaOH → 2Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3Na₂SO₄ (checked in Puzzle 1).

**Molar masses (using standard atomic masses: Fe = 56, S = 32, O = 16, Na = 23, H = 1):**
- Fe₂(SO₄)₃ = 2 × 56 + 3 × (32 + 64) = 112 + 288 = **400 g/mol**.
- Fe(OH)₃ = 56 + 3 × 17 = **107 g/mol**.
- NaOH = **40 g/mol**.

**Moles of Fe₂(SO₄)₃:** 40 ÷ 400 = **0.1 mol**.

**From the mole ratio (1 : 6 : 2):** 0.1 mol of Fe₂(SO₄)₃ reacts with **0.6 mol of NaOH** to produce **0.2 mol of Fe(OH)₃** (and 0.3 mol of Na₂SO₄).

**Masses:**
- Mass of Fe(OH)₃ = 0.2 × 107 = **21.4 g**.
- Mass of NaOH = 0.6 × 40 = **24.0 g**.

**Check (conservation of mass):** Total reactants = 40 + 24.0 = 64.0 g; total products = 21.4 g Fe(OH)₃ + (0.3 × 142 = 42.6 g Na₂SO₄) = 64.0 g ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Trying to identify the entire salt from the color of the solution alone."**
Demo: Suppose Bottle A holds a blue solution. Saying "It's blue, so it must be copper(II) sulfate!" is a classic rookie mistake: the blue color tells us only that **Cu²⁺** is likely present, while the anion could equally well be sulfate (CuSO₄), chloride (CuCl₂), or nitrate (Cu(NO₃)₂)—all three form blue solutions! Even worse, four of our seven cations (Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺) plus NH₄⁺ form **colorless** solutions that look identical to plain water. Always follow the full chain: **color clue + cation test + anion test**.

**Mistake 2: "Sprinkling liquid test reagents directly onto a dry solid salt instead of dissolving the salt in water first."**
Demo: Suppose a student tries to test for sulfate by dropping BaCl₂ onto dry crystals of a salt without dissolving the salt in water first. In a dry crystal, the ions are locked tightly inside a rigid 3D lattice rather than moving freely through solution, so the two reagents cannot mix at the ionic level to form a clean precipitate. Once you dissolve the salt in distilled water, its cations and anions break free and move throughout the liquid, allowing Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓ to happen instantaneously! (The one common exception where we add dilute acid directly to a solid is testing an insoluble carbonate like CaCO₃ for CO₂ bubbles.)

```
   Dry solid salt                     Salt dissolved in water
   (ions locked in crystal lattice)   (free mobile ions ready to react)

   +  -  +  -  +  -                    +     -      +
   -  +  -  +  -  +                 -     +       -
   +  -  +  -  +  -                      -      +
```

> **Figure 5.5** – In a dry solid crystal, ions are locked in a rigid lattice; dissolving the salt in water frees the ions so they can collide and react immediately with test reagents.

## Quick Recap

- **5-step sequence for an unknown salt:** **Look (color) → Dissolve in water → Test cation → Test anion → Write formula**.
- Solution color is only an initial clue for the cation: blue = Cu²⁺, pale green = Fe²⁺, yellow-brown = Fe³⁺; Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺, and NH₄⁺ solutions are colorless.
- **Cation tests:** Add NaOH (few drops, then excess) and NH₄OH (few drops, then excess) to separate portions; match against the shared table. If cold NaOH gives no precipitate, warm gently to test for NH₄⁺.
- **Anion tests:** Use fresh portions—dilute acid for CO₃²⁻; dilute HNO₃ + AgNO₃ for Cl⁻; dilute HCl + BaCl₂ for SO₄²⁻.
- **Elimination:** When a list of possible anions is given and tests for Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, and CO₃²⁻ are all negative, the remaining candidate (such as NO₃⁻) is identified by elimination.
- **Formulas by charge balance:** Fe₂(SO₄)₃, FeCl₂, Cu(NO₃)₂, Al₂(SO₄)₃, ZnCl₂, CaCl₂, (NH₄)₂SO₄.
- **Distinguishing look-alike pairs:** Zn²⁺ vs. Al³⁺ → excess NH₄OH; Ca²⁺ vs. Zn²⁺ → excess NaOH; Pb²⁺ vs. Al³⁺ → KI (or dilute HCl); Fe²⁺ vs. Fe³⁺ → precipitate color.
- Never rely on color alone, and always dissolve soluble salts in distilled water before running precipitation tests.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are meant to be solved on paper. Students never perform these chemical tests alone or at home.)

1. A pale green solution gives a dirty green precipitate with NaOH. On standing in air the top of the precipitate turns brown. With dilute HCl and then BaCl₂ it gives a white precipitate that does not dissolve in acid. Name the cation and the anion.
2. A colourless solution gives a white precipitate with NaOH that dissolves in excess. With NH₄OH the white precipitate does not dissolve in excess. On adding a few drops of KI a yellow precipitate forms. Name the cation. Why are lead compounds not handled by students?
3. Write the formula of the salt made of Al³⁺ and NO₃⁻ by charge balance, and find its formula mass (Al 27, N 14, O 16).
4. A teacher dissolves 25.4 g of FeCl₂ in water and adds exactly the NaOH needed to precipitate all Fe²⁺. Write the balanced equation and find the mass of Fe(OH)₂ formed and the mass of NaOH used (Fe 56, Cl 35.5, Na 23, O 16, H 1).
5. A blue solution gives a pale blue precipitate with NH₄OH drops, which dissolves in excess giving a deep blue solution. With dilute HCl and then BaCl₂ a white precipitate forms that is insoluble in acid. Name the ions. Why can the colour alone not give the anion?
6. A colourless solution gives a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH that dissolves in excess NaOH. With NH₄OH it gives a white precipitate that also dissolves in excess NH₄OH. With dilute HNO₃ and then AgNO₃ nothing forms, but with dilute HCl and then BaCl₂ a white precipitate forms that does not dissolve in acid. Name the ions and write the formula of the salt.
7. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) A yellow-brown solution contains Fe²⁺. (b) Excess NH₄OH dissolves the precipitate of both Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺. (c) To test for sulphate, BaCl₂ is sprinkled on the dry solid. (d) A salt whose solution gives no precipitate with NaOH can still contain NH₄⁺.
8. Put these steps of identifying an unknown salt in the correct order: test the anion, dissolve the salt in water, test the cation with NaOH and NH₄OH, write the formula, observe the colour.
9. A student wants to separate Zn²⁺ and Al³⁺ solutions, both colourless and both giving a white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH. Which single reagent should he add in excess, and what will he see in each tube?
10. Calcium and zinc solutions are both colourless and both give a white precipitate with NaOH drops. Which reagent in excess separates them, and what is seen?
11. Write the ionic equation for the reaction of Fe³⁺ with OH⁻ and show that charge and atoms balance.
12. A teacher dissolves 20 g of Fe₂(SO₄)₃ in water and adds exactly enough BaCl₂ solution to precipitate all the sulphate. Write the balanced equation and find the mass of BaSO₄ formed and of BaCl₂ used. Check by conservation of mass (Fe 56, S 32, O 16, Ba 137, Cl 35.5).
13. A salt solution gives no precipitate with AgNO₃ after HNO₃, no precipitate with BaCl₂ after HCl, and no gas with dilute acid. The shelf holds only chlorides, sulphates, nitrates and carbonates. Which anion remains? Why is carbonate excluded?

### Answers

1. Pale green solution and dirty green precipitate with NaOH that turns brown at the top on standing in air: **Fe²⁺** (iron(II)). White precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ that is insoluble in acid: **SO₄²⁻** (sulfate). Ions: **Fe²⁺ and SO₄²⁻**.
2. Dissolving in excess NaOH narrows the cation to Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺. Not dissolving in excess NH₄OH rules out Zn²⁺, leaving Pb²⁺ or Al³⁺. Forming a bright yellow precipitate (PbI₂) with KI confirms **Pb²⁺** (Pb²⁺ + 2I⁻ → PbI₂↓). Lead compounds are not handled by students because all lead compounds are toxic and accumulate in the body; they are used only in teacher demonstrations.
3. Charge magnitudes are 3 (on Al³⁺) and 1 (on NO₃⁻). Criss-crossing gives 1 Al³⁺ and 3 NO₃⁻ groups: **Al(NO₃)₃**. Check: (+3) + 3 × (−1) = 0 ✅. Formula mass = 27 + 3 × (14 + 48) = 27 + 186 = **213**.
4. Balanced equation: FeCl₂ + 2NaOH → Fe(OH)₂↓ + 2NaCl (Fe 1, Cl 2, Na 2, O 2, H 2 on both sides ✅). Molar mass of FeCl₂ = 56 + 71 = 127 g/mol, so moles = 25.4 ÷ 127 = **0.2 mol**. Molar mass of Fe(OH)₂ = 56 + 34 = 90 g/mol; NaOH = 40 g/mol. From the 1 : 2 : 1 ratio: mass of Fe(OH)₂ = 0.2 mol × 90 = **18.0 g**; mass of NaOH = 0.4 mol × 40 = **16.0 g**. Check: 25.4 + 16.0 = 41.4 g and 18.0 + (0.4 × 58.5 = 23.4 g NaCl) = 41.4 g ✅.
5. Pale blue precipitate with NH₄OH that dissolves in excess to give a deep inky-blue solution: **Cu²⁺**. White precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ insoluble in acid: **SO₄²⁻**. Solution color alone cannot identify the anion because all soluble Cu²⁺ salts (whether sulfate, chloride, or nitrate) give blue solutions in water.
6. A white precipitate that dissolves in **both** excess NaOH and excess NH₄OH uniquely identifies **Zn²⁺** (since Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ do not dissolve in excess NH₄OH). No precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ rules out Cl⁻; a white acid-insoluble precipitate with acidified BaCl₂ confirms **SO₄²⁻**. Combining Zn²⁺ (+2) and SO₄²⁻ (−2) gives the formula **ZnSO₄**. Check: (+2) + (−2) = 0 ✅.
7. (a) **False** ❌: a yellow-brown solution indicates Fe³⁺; Fe²⁺ solutions are pale green. (b) **True** ✅: both Cu(OH)₂ (deep blue solution) and Zn(OH)₂ (colorless solution) dissolve in excess NH₄OH. (c) **False** ❌: the salt must first be dissolved in water, acidified with dilute HCl, and then tested with BaCl₂ solution. (d) **True** ✅: NH₄⁺ gives no precipitate with cold NaOH, but releases NH₃ gas when warmed with NaOH.
8. Correct sequence: (1) observe the color, (2) dissolve the salt in water, (3) test the cation with NaOH and NH₄OH, (4) test the anion, (5) write the formula.
9. Add **excess NH₄OH** to fresh portions of each solution. In the **Zn²⁺** tube, the white precipitate dissolves completely in excess NH₄OH to form a clear, colorless solution. In the **Al³⁺** tube, the white gelatinous precipitate remains undissolved in excess NH₄OH.
10. Add **excess NaOH** (or test with NH₄OH). In **excess NaOH**, the white precipitate from **Zn²⁺** dissolves completely to give a colorless solution, whereas the slight white precipitate from **Ca²⁺** does not dissolve.
11. **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓**. Atoms: Fe 1 = 1, O 3 = 3, H 3 = 3 ✅. Charge: (+3) + 3 × (−1) = 0 = 0 ✅.
12. Balanced equation: Fe₂(SO₄)₃ + 3BaCl₂ → 3BaSO₄↓ + 2FeCl₃ (Fe 2, S 3, O 12, Ba 3, Cl 6 on both sides ✅). Molar mass of Fe₂(SO₄)₃ = 400 g/mol, so moles = 20 ÷ 400 = **0.05 mol**. From the 1 : 3 : 3 : 2 mole ratio: moles of BaSO₄ = 0.15 mol × 233 = **34.95 g**; moles of BaCl₂ = 0.15 mol × 208 = **31.2 g**; mass of FeCl₃ = 0.10 mol × 162.5 = 16.25 g. Check: 20 + 31.2 = 51.2 g and 34.95 + 16.25 = 51.2 g ✅. (Barium salts are toxic—teacher demonstration only.)
13. By elimination, the remaining anion is **nitrate, NO₃⁻**. Carbonate is ruled out because adding dilute acid produced no gas (a carbonate would have fizzed briskly with CO₂ bubbles), and the negative AgNO₃ and BaCl₂ tests rule out chloride and sulfate.
