## Mystery Box Game Day

Friday afternoon was **"Mystery Box Game Day"** for the school Science Club. Mr. Grant, the club advisor, stood in the corner of the hall with his nephew Ethan. Ethan's team had been handed a shuffled stack of twenty **Clue Cards**—each card printed with a single lab observation such as *"a white precipitate settled at the bottom,"* *"dissolved completely when excess drops were added,"* or *"released a pungent gas that turned moist red litmus blue."* Across the table sat six small Mystery Boxes, each labeled with the name of a salt. The challenge: sort the clue cards into the right boxes to prove which salt was which.

At first, Ethan's teammates grabbed handfuls of cards at random and got completely tangled up—two cards seemed to say the same thing, one card didn't seem to fit anywhere, and another looked as if it could belong to three different boxes! Ethan frowned. "Uncle Grant, this is a total maze. Where do we even start?"

Mr. Grant smiled and tapped the table. "Sort your cards into three neat piles first: **'Color Observed,'** **'Cation Test,'** and **'Anion Test.'** Once you read the clues in that exact order, every card will drop straight into its rightful box. A good detective never reads clues in a jumble—first inspect the scene, then lock down the positive ion, then lock down the negative ion, and finally write the full name."

Ethan's team sorted the deck into those three piles—and solved all six Mystery Boxes in record time. "Order first, answer second!" said Ethan. "Exactly," replied Mr. Grant. "And that is our 4-step master method for today."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Master method** | The 4-step sequence used to solve any unknown-solution or unknown-salt problem, always in the same order | Inspect color → test cation → test anion → write formula & equations |
| **Observation table** | A structured record pairing each reagent added with what was visibly observed | Added NaOH drops → white precipitate formed |
| **Reagent** | The known chemical added to carry out a test | NaOH, NH₄OH, AgNO₃, BaCl₂ |
| **Net ionic equation** | The equation showing only the ions that actually react to form the precipitate, gas, or water—omitting spectator ions | Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓ |
| **Spectator ion** | An ion present in the solution that remains unchanged before and after the reaction | Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ in Cu(NO₃)₂ + NaOH |
| **State symbol** | The symbol in parentheses showing physical state: `(s)`, `(l)`, `(g)`, or `(aq)` | Cu(OH)₂(s), NaNO₃(aq) |
| **Dissolving of a precipitate** | When an insoluble precipitate reacts with excess reagent to form a clear, soluble species | Zn(OH)₂ dissolving in excess NaOH |

## Part A: The 4-Step Master Method

Whenever you are given an unknown salt or solution problem, follow these four steps in order:

1. **Inspect the color.** Note the color of the solid or its aqueous solution: **blue** points toward Cu²⁺, **pale green** toward Fe²⁺, **yellow-brown** toward Fe³⁺, and **colorless** toward Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺, or NH₄⁺ (as well as Na⁺ and K⁺). Remember that color is only an opening clue, never final proof (Topic 5).
2. **Test the cation.** In one small portion of the solution, add **a few drops of NaOH**, then **excess NaOH**. In a second clean test tube, add **a few drops of NH₄OH**, then **excess NH₄OH**. Record the precipitate color and whether it dissolves in excess (Topics 2 and 3). If cold NaOH gives no precipitate, warm gently to test for NH₄⁺.
3. **Test the anion.** In fresh portions of the solution, run the anion tests from Topic 4: dilute HNO₃ + AgNO₃ for Cl⁻; dilute HCl + BaCl₂ for SO₄²⁻; dilute acid + limewater for CO₃²⁻.
4. **Write the name, formula, and balanced equations.** Use charge balance (criss-cross) to write the salt's formula, then write the net ionic and molecular equations for each positive test.

```
   Step 1           Step 2              Step 3             Step 4
   LOOK      --->   CATION test  --->   ANION test  --->   NAME & FORMULA
   (color)          (NaOH, NH₄OH:       (AgNO₃, BaCl₂,     (charge balance,
                    drops & excess)     dilute acid)       balanced equations)

   At every step, ask the three questions: What was added? What was seen? What does it mean?
```

> **Figure 6.1** – The 4-step master method. At every stage, apply our three detective questions from Topic 1: What was added? What was seen? What does it mean?

One essential lab rule: **always run cation and anion tests on separate small portions** of the sample solution in clean test tubes—dumping multiple test reagents into the same tube mixes up precipitates and ruins the clues!

### How to Sketch a Precipitate Test Tube

In diagrams, a precipitate sits **at the bottom** of the test tube with clear liquid above it, while a precipitate that dissolves in excess reagent leaves a completely clear tube:

```
    After a few drops of NaOH                  After excess NaOH

         :  clear liquid :                         :  clear liquid :
         :  (colorless)  :                         :  (colorless)  :
         :               :                         :               :
         :   white ppt   :                         :    no ppt     :
         :_______________:                         :_______________:
              (a)                                       (b)
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Testing a Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺ solution with NaOH: (a) a few drops produce a white precipitate at the bottom; (b) adding excess NaOH dissolves the precipitate completely, leaving a clear, colorless solution.

Recall from Topic 2 that Zn(OH)₂, Pb(OH)₂, and Al(OH)₃ dissolve in excess NaOH because they are **amphoteric hydroxides** (Chapter 2, Part E).

## Part B: Master Cation Reference Table

Here is the complete 7-cation reference table from Topics 2 and 3 (using fresh, dilute solutions—first adding 2–3 drops, then excess):

| Ion | With a Few Drops of NaOH | With Excess NaOH | With a Few Drops of NH₄OH | With Excess NH₄OH |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ca²⁺ | Slight white ppt, Ca(OH)₂ | Insoluble (does not dissolve) | No ppt (or very faint haze) | No change |
| Fe²⁺ | Dirty green ppt, Fe(OH)₂ | Insoluble | Dirty green ppt | Insoluble |
| Fe³⁺ | Reddish-brown ppt, Fe(OH)₃ | Insoluble | Reddish-brown ppt | Insoluble |
| Cu²⁺ | Pale blue ppt, Cu(OH)₂ | Insoluble | Pale blue ppt | Dissolves → deep (inky) blue solution |
| Zn²⁺ | White ppt, Zn(OH)₂ | Dissolves → colorless solution (sodium zincate, Na₂ZnO₂) | White ppt | Dissolves → colorless solution |
| Pb²⁺ | White ppt, Pb(OH)₂ | Dissolves → colorless solution (sodium plumbite, Na₂PbO₂) | White ppt | Insoluble |
| Al³⁺ | White gelatinous ppt, Al(OH)₃ | Dissolves → colorless solution (sodium aluminate, NaAlO₂) | White gelatinous ppt | Insoluble |

(The deep inky-blue copper species [Cu(NH₃)₄]²⁺ and colorless zinc species [Zn(NH₃)₄]²⁺ in excess NH₄OH are **complex ions**, covered in detail in higher grades.)

This gives us a super-compact **"Who Dissolves in Excess?"** map:

```
   Dissolve in EXCESS NaOH     :  Zn²⁺   Pb²⁺   Al³⁺
   Do NOT dissolve in excess NaOH :  Ca²⁺   Fe²⁺   Fe³⁺   Cu²⁺

   Dissolve in EXCESS NH₄OH    :  Cu²⁺ (deep inky blue)   Zn²⁺ (colorless)
   Do NOT dissolve in excess NH₄OH:  Ca²⁺   Fe²⁺   Fe³⁺   Pb²⁺   Al³⁺
```

> **Figure 6.3** – The "Who Dissolves in Excess?" map. Notice that **Zn²⁺** is the only cation whose precipitate dissolves in *both* excess NaOH and excess NH₄OH, while **Cu²⁺** is the only one whose precipitate stays insoluble in excess NaOH yet dissolves in excess NH₄OH!

## Part C: Mixed Detective Puzzles (Worked Examples)

**Safety reminder for all puzzles:** Compounds of lead, barium, and silver are toxic (and AgNO₃ stains skin black); copper salts are harmful if swallowed; even dilute NaOH is corrosive to eyes and skin; and ammonia gas is pungent and irritating. **Students never perform any of these tests alone or at home.** All heavy-metal waste is collected by the teacher in designated waste containers—never poured down the drain.

### Puzzle 1. A White Precipitate That Dissolves in One Alkali and Not the Other

A colorless solution **P** gives a **white precipitate** with a few drops of NaOH, and the precipitate **dissolves** in excess NaOH. In a second tube, a few drops of NH₄OH give a **white precipitate** that **does not dissolve** in excess NH₄OH.

**Step 1:** Colorless solution rules out Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, and Fe³⁺.
**Step 2:** A white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH narrows P to Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺. Because the precipitate does **not** dissolve in excess NH₄OH, Zn²⁺ is ruled out (Zn(OH)₂ dissolves in excess NH₄OH). That leaves **Pb²⁺ or Al³⁺**.
**Step 3:** Since NaOH and NH₄OH alone cannot separate Pb²⁺ from Al³⁺ (Topic 3), we check one extra clue: adding a few drops of **dilute HCl** to a fresh portion of P produces a **white precipitate** (teacher demonstration). That identifies **Pb²⁺** (forming sparingly soluble lead(II) chloride, PbCl₂↓, from Chapter 1).
**Step 4:** Cation is **Pb²⁺**. Equations: Pb²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Pb(OH)₂↓ and Pb(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂PbO₂ + 2H₂O.

**Check:** In Pb²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Pb(OH)₂↓: Pb 1 = 1, O 2 = 2, H 2 = 2, charge (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅. In the second equation: left has Pb 1, Na 2, O: 2 + 2 = 4, H: 2 + 2 = 4; right has Na₂PbO₂ (Na 2, Pb 1, O 2) + 2H₂O (O 2, H 4) → O 4, H 4 ✅.

### Puzzle 2. Pale Blue to Deep Inky Blue (Using a Flowchart)

Solution **Q** is **blue**. Trace its reactions in the flowchart below:

```
   Solution Q (blue)
        :
        v
   Few drops of NaOH  --->  pale blue precipitate
        :
        v
   Excess NaOH        --->  precipitate stays unchanged (insoluble)
   
   Second portion:
   Few drops of NH₄OH --->  pale blue precipitate
        :
        v
   Excess NH₄OH       --->  precipitate dissolves -> DEEP (inky) blue solution
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Flowchart of results for Solution Q with NaOH and NH₄OH. Dissolving in excess NH₄OH to form a deep inky-blue solution is the unmistakable signature of Cu²⁺.

**Step 1:** Blue solution points toward Cu²⁺.
**Step 2:** Pale blue precipitate with both alkalis, insoluble in excess NaOH, and dissolving in excess NH₄OH to give a **deep inky-blue solution** confirms **Cu²⁺** beyond a shadow of a doubt.
**Step 3:** The bottle sits on a shelf of chlorides, sulfates, and nitrates, and tests with acidified AgNO₃ and acidified BaCl₂ both give **no precipitate** (ruling out Cl⁻ and SO₄²⁻). By elimination, the anion is **NO₃⁻**.
**Step 4:** Pairing Cu²⁺ (+2) with two NO₃⁻ (−1) ions gives **Cu(NO₃)₂**. Equation: Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃.

**Check:** Cu 1 = 1, N 2 = 2, O: left 6 (in nitrate) + 2 (in NaOH) = 8, right 2 (in Cu(OH)₂) + 6 (in 2NaNO₃) = 8 ✅. Na 2 = 2 ✅, H 2 = 2 ✅.

### Puzzle 3. A Pale Green Solution Whose Precipitate Turns Brown in Air

Solution **R** is **pale green**. Adding a few drops of NaOH gives a **dirty green precipitate** that **does not dissolve** in excess NaOH. With NH₄OH it also gives a **dirty green precipitate** that **does not dissolve** in excess NH₄OH. When left standing exposed to air, the top edge of the green precipitate slowly turns **reddish-brown**.

**Step 1:** Pale green solution points toward Fe²⁺.
**Step 2:** Dirty green precipitate insoluble in excess of both alkalis confirms **Fe²⁺** (whereas Fe³⁺ would have given a reddish-brown precipitate right from the first drop).
**Steps 3 & 4:** The browning at the top occurs because oxygen in the air slowly oxidizes iron(II) hydroxide toward iron(III) hydroxide (Topic 2). Net ionic equation: **Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂↓**.

**Check:** Fe 1 = 1, O 2 = 2, H 2 = 2, charge (+2) + 2(−1) = 0 ✅.

### Puzzle 4. A Slight White Precipitate That Never Dissolves

Colorless solution **S** gives a **slight white precipitate** with a few drops of NaOH, which **does not dissolve** in excess NaOH. Adding NH₄OH to a fresh portion gives **no precipitate** (or at most a faint haze).

**Step 2:** Among our four cations that form white precipitates (Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺), **only Ca²⁺** forms a white precipitate that does not dissolve in excess NaOH—and giving no precipitate with weak NH₄OH clinches **Ca²⁺**!
**Step 4:** Net ionic equation: **Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂↓**.

**Check:** Ca 1 = 1, O 2 = 2, H 2 = 2, charge +2 − 2 = 0 ✅.

### Puzzle 5. Anion Detective: Curdy White Precipitate

Colorless solution **T** is acidified with a few drops of dilute HNO₃, and then AgNO₃ solution is added: a **curdy white precipitate** forms immediately. When dilute NH₄OH is added, the precipitate **dissolves completely**. (AgNO₃ is toxic and stains skin—teacher demonstration only.)

**Step 3:** A curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ that dissolves in dilute NH₄OH is the dedicated proof for **chloride, Cl⁻**.
**Step 4:** Net ionic equation: **Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓**.

**Check:** Ag 1 = 1, Cl 1 = 1, charge (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅.

### Puzzle 6. A Solid Salt That Releases Two Different Gases in Two Tests!

White solid **U** is tested in two separate portions by the teacher:
- **(i)** Adding dilute acid to the first portion produces **brisk effervescence**, and passing the gas through clear limewater turns the limewater **milky**.
- **(ii)** Warming the second portion gently with NaOH solution in a fume hood releases a **pungent gas** that turns **moist red litmus paper blue**.

**Step 3:** Test (i) proves the gas is CO₂, identifying the anion as **carbonate, CO₃²⁻**. Test (ii) proves the gas is NH₃, identifying the cation as **ammonium, NH₄⁺**.
**Step 4:** Combining NH₄⁺ (+1) and CO₃²⁻ (−2) by charge balance gives **(NH₄)₂CO₃**, ammonium carbonate! Reaction with warm NaOH: **(NH₄)₂CO₃ + 2NaOH → Na₂CO₃ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃↑**.

**Check:** N 2 = 2 ✅; H: left 8 + 2 = 10, right 4 (in 2H₂O) + 6 (in 2NH₃) = 10 ✅; C 1 = 1 ✅; O: 3 + 2 = 5 = 3 + 2 ✅; Na 2 = 2 ✅.

### Puzzle 7. Putting Cation and Anion Flowcharts Together

To a portion of yellow-brown solution **V**, the teacher **first adds dilute HCl**: no bubbles form. Next, the teacher adds **BaCl₂ solution**: a **white precipitate** forms that **does not dissolve** in dilute acid. To a second portion of V, the teacher adds NaOH: a **reddish-brown precipitate** forms that **does not dissolve** in excess NaOH. (Barium salts are toxic—teacher demonstration only.)

```
   Portion 1 of V :  dilute HCl  --->  no bubbles (not carbonate)
                         :
                         v
                     add BaCl₂   --->  white ppt, insoluble in acid
                         :
                         v
                     anion = SO₄²⁻

   Portion 2 of V :  add NaOH    --->  reddish-brown ppt, insoluble in excess
                         :
                         v
                     cation = Fe³⁺
```

> **Figure 6.5** – Flowchart for Solution V: the anion and cation are identified cleanly in two separate portions.

Net ionic equations: **Ba²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → BaSO₄↓** and **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓**. Thus Solution V contains **Fe³⁺ and SO₄²⁻** ions (iron(III) sulfate, Fe₂(SO₄)₃).

**Check:** Ba 1 = 1, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4, charge (+2) + (−2) = 0 ✅. Fe 1 = 1, O 3 = 3, H 3 = 3, charge (+3) + 3(−1) = 0 ✅.

### Puzzle 8. Two Colorless Bottles: Which Is Zn²⁺ and Which Is Al³⁺?

Two colorless solutions **X** and **Y** (known to contain no lead) both give a **white precipitate** with a few drops of NaOH, and **both precipitates dissolve** in excess NaOH. With a few drops of NH₄OH, both again give a **white precipitate**. When **excess NH₄OH** is added, the precipitate in **X dissolves completely**, whereas the precipitate in **Y remains undissolved**.

**Step 1:** Both are colorless.
**Step 2:** Dissolving in excess NaOH narrows both to Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺; since lead is absent, they must be Zn²⁺ and Al³⁺.
**Step 3:** In excess NH₄OH, Zn(OH)₂ dissolves while Al(OH)₃ does not. Therefore, **X = Zn²⁺** and **Y = Al³⁺**!
**Step 4:** For X: Zn²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Zn(OH)₂↓ and Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O. For Y: Al³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Al(OH)₃↓ and Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O.

**Check:** Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O: Zn 1, Na 2, O 4 = 2 + 2, H 4 = 4 ✅. Al(OH)₃ + NaOH → NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O: Al 1, Na 1, O 4 = 2 + 2, H 4 = 4 ✅.

## Part D: How Much Precipitate Forms? (Applying the Chapter 5 Mole Method)

### Example 1. Calculating Precipitate and Dissolved Salt Masses from Cu(NO₃)₂ + NaOH

**Question:** Suppose **18.75 g** of Cu(NO₃)₂ in solution reacts with the exact required amount of NaOH. What mass of Cu(OH)₂ precipitate settles at the bottom, and what mass of NaNO₃ remains dissolved in the solution above it? (Cu = 63.5, N = 14, O = 16, H = 1, Na = 23.)

**Balanced equation:** Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃

**Step 1 (Molar masses):**
- Cu(NO₃)₂ = 63.5 + 2 × (14 + 3 × 16) = 63.5 + 2 × 62 = **187.5 g/mol**.
- Cu(OH)₂ = 63.5 + 2 × 17 = **97.5 g/mol**.
- NaNO₃ = 23 + 14 + 48 = **85 g/mol**.

**Step 2 (Moles of Cu(NO₃)₂):** 18.75 ÷ 187.5 = **0.1 mol**.
**Step 3 (Mole ratios):** 1 mol of Cu(NO₃)₂ produces 1 mol of Cu(OH)₂ and 2 mol of NaNO₃. So 0.1 mol produces **0.1 mol of Cu(OH)₂** and **0.2 mol of NaNO₃**.
**Step 4 (Masses):**
- Mass of Cu(OH)₂ precipitate = 0.1 × 97.5 = **9.75 g**.
- Mass of dissolved NaNO₃ = 0.2 × 85 = **17.0 g**.

```
       :  clear solution (17.0 g NaNO₃)  :
       :                                 :
       :   Cu(OH)₂ precipitate: 9.75 g   :
       :_________________________________:
```

> **Figure 6.6** – Inside the test tube after complete reaction: 9.75 g of pale blue Cu(OH)₂ precipitate sits at the bottom, while 17.0 g of NaNO₃ stays dissolved in the clear liquid above.

**Check (conservation of mass):** Mass of NaOH reacted = 0.2 mol × 40 = 8.0 g. Total reactants = 18.75 + 8.0 = 26.75 g; total products = 9.75 + 17.0 = 26.75 g ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Squeezing in a huge squirt of NaOH all at once, and missing the precipitate stage completely!"**
Demo: Suppose a student tests a Zn²⁺ or Al³⁺ solution by dumping a full dropper of NaOH into the test tube all at once. Because NaOH is immediately in **excess**, any white Zn(OH)₂ or Al(OH)₃ precipitate that forms dissolves almost instantaneously, leaving a **clear, colorless solution**! The student then writes down *"No precipitate formed—no metal ion present,"* completely missing Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺!

```
   Wrong technique                        Correct technique
   (dumping excess NaOH all at once)      (a few drops first, then excess)

   solution ---> clear colorless liquid   few drops ---> white precipitate seen!
   (precipitate stage missed completely!) excess    ---> precipitate dissolves
   
   Result: Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, Al³⁺ missed!       Result: amphoteric ion spotted cleanly!
```

> **Figure 6.7** – Adding a large squirt of NaOH all at once skips right past the initial precipitate stage for Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺. Always add **just 2–3 drops first**, observe the precipitate, and only then add excess!

**Mistake 2: "Writing 'dissolves in excess' in your notes without recording WHICH alkali (NaOH or NH₄OH) was used!"**
Demo: Suppose an Al³⁺ solution forms a white precipitate that dissolves in excess **NaOH**. If a student carelessly jots down *"white ppt, dissolves in excess"* without naming the reagent, and later reads that note as *"dissolves in excess NH₄OH,"* they will misidentify the ion as **Zn²⁺**! Remember: Al³⁺ dissolves in excess **NaOH**, but does **not** dissolve in excess **NH₄OH**.

Always write the reagent name right next to "excess":

| Observation Situation | Vague / Confusing Note | Clear, Accurate Note |
|---|---|---|
| White ppt that dissolved in excess | "dissolved in excess" | "dissolved in excess **NaOH**" (or "in excess **NH₄OH**") |
| Al³⁺ + excess NH₄OH | "dissolves" ❌ | "**insoluble** in excess NH₄OH" ✅ |
| Al³⁺ + excess NaOH | "insoluble" ❌ | "**dissolves** in excess NaOH" ✅ |

**Mistake 3: "Leaving spectator ions inside a net ionic equation."**
Demo: For Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃, writing Cu²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻ + 2Na⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2Na⁺ + 2NO₃⁻ gives the *complete ionic equation*, not the **net ionic equation**. Whenever a question asks for the net ionic (or simply ionic) equation of a precipitation test, cancel the spectator ions (2Na⁺ and 2NO₃⁻) that appear identically on both sides to leave the clean reaction: **Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓**.

**Check:** 2Na⁺ and 2NO₃⁻ cancel on both sides ✅, leaving Cu 1 = 1, O 2 = 2, H 2 = 2, charge +2 + 2(−1) = 0 ✅.

**Mistake 4: "Labeling a precipitate as `(aq)` instead of `(s)` (or `↓`)."**
Demo: Writing Cu²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Cu(OH)₂(aq) contradicts your own test-tube drawing! The state symbol `(aq)` means *dissolved in water*, whereas a precipitate is an **insoluble solid** that settles out of the water. Always mark a precipitate with **`(s)`** or a downward arrow **`↓`** (or both: `Cu(OH)₂(s)↓`), dissolved ions and soluble salts with `(aq)`, gases with `(g)` or `↑`, and liquid water with `(l)`.

## Quick Recap

- **4-step master method:** (1) Inspect color → (2) Test cation with NaOH and NH₄OH (a few drops first, then excess) → (3) Test anion in fresh portions (acid pre-check + AgNO₃ or BaCl₂; dilute acid for carbonate) → (4) Write the salt formula by charge balance and balanced equations.
- **Cation summary:** Ca²⁺ slight white (insoluble in excess NaOH; no ppt with NH₄OH); Fe²⁺ dirty green (insoluble in excess of both); Fe³⁺ reddish-brown (insoluble in excess of both); Cu²⁺ pale blue (insoluble in excess NaOH; dissolves in excess NH₄OH to give a deep inky-blue solution); Zn²⁺ white (dissolves in excess of **both** NaOH and NH₄OH to give colorless solutions); Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ white / gelatinous white (dissolve in excess NaOH, insoluble in excess NH₄OH; distinguish Pb²⁺ with KI → yellow PbI₂↓ or dil. HCl → white PbCl₂↓).
- **Anion & ammonium summary:** Cl⁻ → curdy white AgCl↓ with dil. HNO₃ + AgNO₃ (dissolves in NH₄OH); SO₄²⁻ → white BaSO₄↓ with dil. HCl + BaCl₂ (insoluble in acid); CO₃²⁻ → brisk CO₂↑ bubbles with dilute acid (turns limewater milky); NH₄⁺ → pungent NH₃↑ gas on warming with NaOH (turns moist red litmus blue).
- **Equations:** Cancel spectator ions to write net ionic equations; mark precipitates with `(s)` or `↓` and dissolved species with `(aq)`.
- **Stoichiometry:** 18.75 g of Cu(NO₃)₂ (0.1 mol) reacts with 8.0 g of NaOH (0.2 mol) to yield 9.75 g of Cu(OH)₂↓ and 17.0 g of NaNO₃.
- **Four pitfalls to avoid:** Never dump excess reagent in all at once; always specify *which* reagent is in excess; cancel spectator ions in net ionic equations; never label a solid precipitate `(aq)`.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are meant to be solved on paper. Atomic masses: H 1, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Al 27, Fe 56.)

1. Solution A is yellow-brown. With a few drops of NaOH it gives a reddish-brown precipitate that does not dissolve in excess NaOH. With a few drops of NH₄OH it gives a reddish-brown precipitate that does not dissolve in excess NH₄OH. Name the cation and write the ionic equation for the precipitate.
2. Solution B is colourless. With a few drops of NaOH it gives a white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH. With a few drops of NH₄OH it gives a white precipitate that also dissolves in excess NH₄OH. Which of the seven ions is present? Write the equation for the dissolving of the precipitate in NaOH.
3. A teacher uses 4.26 g of Al(NO₃)₃ in solution and adds exactly the right amount of NaOH. Write the balanced equation, and find the mass of Al(OH)₃ precipitate and the mass of NaNO₃ formed. Check that mass is conserved.
4. How many grams of Fe(NO₃)₂ are needed to give 4.5 g of Fe(OH)₂ precipitate with NaOH? Write the equation first, then find the mass of NaOH that reacts.
5. Solution C gives a slight white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH, which stays unchanged in excess NaOH. NH₄OH gives no precipitate. Separately, the silver nitrate test (after dilute HNO₃) and the barium chloride test (after dilute HCl) give no precipitate, and dilute acid gives no gas. The bottle is known to hold a chloride, a sulphate, a carbonate or a nitrate. Name the cation and the anion, and write the formula of the salt.
6. Solid D, with warm NaOH, gives a pungent gas that turns moist red litmus blue. A solution of D, with dilute HNO₃ and then silver nitrate, gives a white curdy precipitate that dissolves in NH₄OH. Name the two ions in D, and write the ionic equation for the gas-forming test.
7. Two colourless solutions E and F both give a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH. In excess NaOH, E's precipitate dissolves but F's does not. With NH₄OH, E gives a white precipitate that stays in excess NH₄OH, while F gives no precipitate. It is known that neither solution contains aluminium. Name each ion and explain your logic.
8. A student adds a large dropper-full of NaOH to a colourless solution and sees only a clear solution. Which of the seven ions could be present? Why can copper not be one of them? What should the student do differently?
9. One bottle contains a pale green solution and another a yellow-brown solution. Both give precipitates with NaOH that do not dissolve in excess. Name the ions, write the two ionic equations, and say which solution may slowly change colour on standing in air.
10. Draw a flowchart, using only NaOH and NH₄OH, to separate Zn²⁺, Al³⁺ and Ca²⁺ in three colourless solutions.
11. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) A white precipitate with NaOH proves the ion is Zn²⁺. (b) In the net ionic equation for Fe²⁺ with NaOH, Na⁺ is a spectator ion. (c) Cu(OH)₂ is written with (aq) because it forms in a solution. (d) Excess NH₄OH dissolves the precipitates of both Cu²⁺ and Zn²⁺.
12. Complete this table from memory for Pb²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺ and Ca²⁺: colour of precipitate with NaOH drops, effect of excess NaOH, effect of excess NH₄OH.
13. A student writes the formula of the salt of Fe²⁺ with the nitrate ion as FeNO₃. Use charges to write the correct formula and say what is wrong with the student's formula.

### Answers

1. Yellow-brown solution giving a reddish-brown precipitate insoluble in excess of both NaOH and NH₄OH identifies **Fe³⁺**. Net ionic equation: **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓**. Check: Fe 1, O 3, H 3, charge (+3) + 3(−1) = 0 ✅.
2. Colorless solution giving a white precipitate that dissolves in **both** excess NaOH and excess NH₄OH uniquely identifies **Zn²⁺** (since Pb²⁺ and Al³⁺ precipitates do not dissolve in excess NH₄OH). Equation for dissolving in excess NaOH: **Zn(OH)₂ + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + 2H₂O**. Check: Zn 1, Na 2, O: 4 = 2 + 2, H 4 = 4 ✅.
3. Balanced equation: **Al(NO₃)₃ + 3NaOH → Al(OH)₃↓ + 3NaNO₃**. Molar masses: Al(NO₃)₃ = 27 + 3 × 62 = 213 g/mol; Al(OH)₃ = 27 + 3 × 17 = 78 g/mol; NaNO₃ = 85 g/mol. Moles of Al(NO₃)₃ = 4.26 ÷ 213 = **0.02 mol**. Mass of Al(OH)₃ = 0.02 × 78 = **1.56 g**. Mass of NaNO₃ = 0.06 × 85 = **5.10 g**. Mass of NaOH reacted = 0.06 × 40 = 2.40 g. Check: 4.26 + 2.40 = 6.66 g and 1.56 + 5.10 = 6.66 g ✅. Atom check: Al 1 = 1, N 3 = 3, O: 9 + 3 = 12 = 3 + 9, H 3 = 3, Na 3 = 3 ✅.
4. Balanced equation: **Fe(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Fe(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃**. Molar mass of Fe(OH)₂ = 56 + 34 = 90 g/mol, so moles = 4.5 ÷ 90 = **0.05 mol**. From the 1 : 1 mole ratio, 0.05 mol of Fe(NO₃)₂ is required. Molar mass of Fe(NO₃)₂ = 56 + 124 = 180 g/mol, so mass = 0.05 × 180 = **9.0 g**. Mass of NaOH = 2 × 0.05 = 0.10 mol × 40 = **4.0 g**. Check: mass of NaNO₃ = 0.10 × 85 = 8.5 g; left = 9.0 + 4.0 = 13.0 g, right = 4.5 + 8.5 = 13.0 g ✅.
5. A slight white precipitate with NaOH that stays insoluble in excess NaOH, paired with no precipitate in NH₄OH, identifies the cation as **Ca²⁺**. Negative tests with acidified AgNO₃ (not Cl⁻), acidified BaCl₂ (not SO₄²⁻), and dilute acid (not CO₃²⁻) leave **nitrate, NO₃⁻**, by elimination. Pairing Ca²⁺ (+2) with two NO₃⁻ (−1) ions gives the formula **Ca(NO₃)₂**. Check: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.
6. Releasing a pungent gas with warm NaOH that turns moist red litmus blue identifies **NH₄⁺**. Giving a curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ that dissolves in NH₄OH identifies **Cl⁻**. Net ionic equation for the gas test: **NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ → NH₃↑ + H₂O**. Check: N 1 = 1, H: 4 + 1 = 5 = 3 + 2, O 1 = 1, charge (+1) + (−1) = 0 = 0 ✅.
7. **In E:** The white precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH (narrowing it to Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺) but stays undissolved in excess NH₄OH (ruling out Zn²⁺). Since aluminum is absent, **E = Pb²⁺** (lead compounds are toxic—teacher demo only). **In F:** The white precipitate does not dissolve in excess NaOH and gives no precipitate with NH₄OH, proving **F = Ca²⁺**. Check: E matches the Pb²⁺ row and F matches the Ca²⁺ row of our table ✅.
8. Dumping a large squirt of NaOH all at once can immediately dissolve an amphoteric white precipitate, so the solution could contain **Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, or Al³⁺** (or Ca²⁺ only if no precipitate formed yet, though Ca²⁺ gives a slight white ppt in excess NaOH). Copper (**Cu²⁺**) cannot be present because a Cu²⁺ solution is blue (not colorless) and its pale blue Cu(OH)₂ precipitate **does not dissolve** in excess NaOH. The student should start with a fresh portion and add **just 2–3 drops of NaOH first**, observe whether a white precipitate forms, and only then add excess NaOH.
9. Pale green solution: **Fe²⁺**, net ionic equation **Fe²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₂↓** (dirty green precipitate). Yellow-brown solution: **Fe³⁺**, net ionic equation **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓** (reddish-brown precipitate). The pale green **Fe²⁺** solution (and its dirty green precipitate) slowly turns brown on standing in air as oxygen oxidizes Fe²⁺ to Fe³⁺. Check: both equations balance atoms and charge ✅.
10. Add a few drops of NaOH to all three: all three form white precipitates (slight white for Ca²⁺). Now add **excess NaOH**: the precipitates in the Zn²⁺ and Al³⁺ tubes dissolve, whereas the precipitate in the **Ca²⁺** tube stays undissolved—identifying **Ca²⁺**. Next, take fresh portions of the remaining two solutions and add **excess NH₄OH**: the precipitate that dissolves is **Zn²⁺**, and the precipitate that stays undissolved is **Al³⁺**. Flowchart:

```
   Few drops of NaOH  --->  white precipitate in all three (slight in Ca²⁺)
          :
          v
   Excess NaOH        --->  dissolves     : Zn²⁺ or Al³⁺
          :                 stays intact  : Ca²⁺
          v
   Excess NH₄OH       --->  dissolves     : Zn²⁺
                            stays intact  : Al³⁺
```

   Check: all three ions are cleanly separated ✅.
11. (a) **False** ❌: Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺ all form white precipitates with NaOH; you must test with excess NaOH and excess NH₄OH to identify Zn²⁺. (b) **True** ✅: Na⁺ remains unchanged in solution on both sides. (c) **False** ❌: Cu(OH)₂ is an insoluble solid precipitate, so its state symbol is **`(s)`**, not `(aq)`. (d) **True** ✅: both Cu(OH)₂ (forming a deep inky-blue solution) and Zn(OH)₂ (forming a colorless solution) dissolve in excess NH₄OH.
12. **Pb²⁺:** white precipitate; dissolves in excess NaOH (colorless); insoluble in excess NH₄OH. **Cu²⁺:** pale blue precipitate; insoluble in excess NaOH; dissolves in excess NH₄OH (deep inky-blue solution). **Fe²⁺:** dirty green precipitate; insoluble in excess NaOH; insoluble in excess NH₄OH. **Ca²⁺:** slight white precipitate; insoluble in excess NaOH; no precipitate (no change) with NH₄OH.
13. Because Fe²⁺ has a **+2** charge and NO₃⁻ has a **−1** charge, two NO₃⁻ ions are needed to balance one Fe²⁺ ion, giving the correct formula **Fe(NO₃)₂**. The student's formula `FeNO₃` has a net charge of (+2) + (−1) = +1, so its charges are not balanced! Parentheses around `NO₃` are required to show two complete nitrate groups. Check: Fe 1, N 2, O 6; charge +2 + 2(−1) = 0 ✅.
