# Topic 1: Detective Chemistry: Tests, Precipitates, and Tracking Ions

In the previous chapter we saw how ionic and covalent bonds can coexist inside a single compound, and how polyatomic clusters like OH⁻ and NH₄⁺ drift through water as **intact ions**. Now let's flip the question around: suppose you are handed a clear solution in a beaker and its appearance alone gives nothing away—how can you find out **which ions are hiding inside**? Throughout this chapter, we will learn to work like a **chemical detective**, reading one clue at a time (a color, a precipitate, a gas). Today we lay the foundation: what a chemical test actually is, and how to keep track of every ion in the beaker.

---

## Three Identical Blue Backpacks

Uncle Arthur was riding the coach back with his nephew Oliver after a one-day school field trip. As everyone stepped off at the station platform, three completely identical **navy-blue backpacks** sat lined up on a bench: one belonged to Oliver, one to his friend Liam, and one to Uncle Arthur. None of the three had a name tag on the outside, and even their zippers and side pockets were the exact same model.

"Uncle Arthur, which one is mine? All three look exactly the same!" Oliver exclaimed. Uncle Arthur smiled. "We don't even need to unzip them. First, **look for a clue**. I remember you clipped a little keychain onto your zipper pull this morning. Liam's bag will have its own clue, and mine probably has my notebook sticking out."

Oliver looked closely: clipped to the front zipper of the first bag was a **miniature soccer-ball keychain**. "That one's mine!" Peeking out of the mesh side pocket of the second bag was the corner of a **stainless-steel lunchbox**—Liam's. And sticking out of the top pocket of the third bag was the corner of **Uncle Arthur's leather notebook**. All three identical bags were identified cleanly from **one small clue each**, without dumping anyone's belongings onto the platform.

"That was just like detective work!" said Oliver. "Exactly," replied Uncle Arthur. "And that is the true meaning of a **chemical test**: you perform one small check (inspect the outside of the bag), **observe what shows up** (the keychain), and then reason out **what that clue means** (this bag is Oliver's). A chemist in the lab does the exact same thing—except the 'bags' are unknown solutions, and the clues are colors, precipitates, and gases."

```
   Bag A            Bag B            Bag C
   +-------+        +-------+        +-------+
   [ blue  ]        [ blue  ]        [ blue  ]   <- all three look identical at first glance
   +-------+        +-------+        +-------+
   soccer-ball      corner of        corner of
   keychain         lunchbox         notebook
   (clue 1)         (clue 2)         (clue 3)
      v                v                v
   Oliver           Liam             Uncle Arthur <- identified by reading the clue
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Three identical backpacks: indistinguishable at a casual glance, yet each carries a distinct clue (keychain, lunchbox, notebook). Reading the clue tells us whose bag is whose. Every chemical test follows this exact three-part structure: perform a check, note the observation, and draw the conclusion.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Analysis** | Finding out what substances are present in a sample, or how much of each is present | Identifying which ion is in a solution |
| **Qualitative analysis** | Answering **"What is inside?"**—identifying which ions or substances are present (not how much) | The solution contains Cu²⁺ ions |
| **Quantitative analysis** | Answering **"How much is inside?"**—finding the amount or mass (using Chapter 5 mole calculations) | 1.6 g of NaOH is required |
| **Reagent** | A known chemical substance added to a sample to carry out a test | Aqueous NaOH solution |
| **Test** | Adding a reagent, observing the visible change (color, precipitate, or gas), and deducing what it proves | Add NaOH → pale blue precipitate appears |
| **Observation** | What you directly see (or detect) during a test, stated without guessing the conclusion yet | "A pale blue solid settled at the bottom" |
| **Precipitate (ppt)** | An insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed and settles to the bottom of the tube (↓) | Cu(OH)₂↓ |
| **Cation, Anion** | The positively charged ion and negatively charged ion that make up a salt | In CuSO₄: Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ |
| **Spectator ion** | An ion that remains unchanged in solution both before and after the reaction | Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ in CuSO₄ + NaOH |
| **Ionic equation** | An equation written with dissolved ions shown separately; cancelling the spectator ions leaves the **net ionic equation** | Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓ |
| **Decant, filter** | Letting a precipitate settle and gently pouring off the clear liquid above it (decanting), or separating the solid through filter paper (filtering) | Gently pouring off the top liquid from a test tube |

## Part A: What Is Analysis? "What Is Present" vs. "How Much Is Present"

In chemistry, we can ask two fundamentally different questions about an unknown sample:

- **"What is in it?"** This is **qualitative** analysis. The answer is a name or identity: *"This solution contains copper(II) ions."*
- **"How much of it is in there?"** This is **quantitative** analysis. The answer is a measured number with units: *"0.01 mol"* or *"1.6 grams."* You learned how to calculate these amounts in Chapter 5 (Mole Concept and Stoichiometry).

Throughout this chapter, our main focus is the **first question**—identifying which ions are present in an unknown salt or solution. In a few places we will also do a short calculation to find **"how much precipitate forms,"** using the familiar mole method from Chapter 5.

### Example 1. Qualitative or Quantitative?

**Question:** Classify each task below: (a) "Does this salt solution contain a copper salt or not?" (b) "How many grams of NaOH are dissolved in 100 mL of this solution?" (c) "Which salt is this white powder?"

- (a) **Qualitative**: asks only "is it present or not?"
- (b) **Quantitative**: asks "how much?"
- (c) **Qualitative**: asks for the identity of the salt, not the amount.

**Check:** Whenever the answer is a chemical identity or yes/no, it is qualitative; whenever the answer is a numerical amount, it is quantitative ✅.

### Three Questions for Every Chemical Test

Uncle Arthur gave Oliver a golden rule: **to understand any chemical test, always ask three questions in order.**

| Question | In the Backpack Story | In Chemistry |
|---|---|---|
| 1. **What was added?** | Looked closely at the zipper | The reagent added, such as aqueous NaOH |
| 2. **What was seen?** | A soccer-ball keychain | A color change, a precipitate, or a gas |
| 3. **What does it mean?** | This is Oliver's backpack | That specific ion is present in the solution |

```
   WHAT WAS ADDED?  ------>  WHAT WAS SEEN?  ------>  WHAT DOES IT MEAN?
   (reagent)                 (observation)            (conclusion)
   NaOH solution             pale blue ppt            Cu²⁺ is present
```

> **Figure 1.2** – The three-step framework of every chemical test. Step 1 is what we do, Step 2 is what our eyes see, and Step 3 is what our mind deduces from that evidence—never from guesswork.

## Part B: Why We Test for Individual Ions

In Chapter 1 we learned that every **salt** is built from a positive ion (a **cation**, coming from a base) paired with a negative ion (an **anion**, coming from an acid). When a soluble salt dissolves in water, its cations and anions separate and move independently (just like the mobile ions we saw in Chapter 4 on Electrolysis). Therefore, identifying a salt in solution really means **identifying its cation and its anion**.

### Example 2. Writing the Ions Formed by a Salt in Water

**Question:** Which ions do CuSO₄, FeCl₃, and Cu(NO₃)₂ release when dissolved in water? Check the charge balance for each.

- CuSO₄ → Cu²⁺ + SO₄²⁻. Charge: (+2) + (−2) = 0 ✅.
- FeCl₃ → Fe³⁺ + 3Cl⁻. Charge: (+3) + 3 × (−1) = 0 ✅.
- Cu(NO₃)₂ → Cu²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻. Charge: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.

**Check:** In every formula the total ionic charge adds up to zero (0), and the atom count matches: in Cu(NO₃)₂ there are 2 N and 6 O atoms, matching two NO₃⁻ ions ✅.

**Key insight:** The exact same **cation** can show up in different salts—for instance, Cu²⁺ is paired with SO₄²⁻ in copper(II) sulfate and with NO₃⁻ in copper(II) nitrate. A chemical detective asks two separate questions: **"Which cation is present?"** and **"Which anion is present?"** Across this chapter we will learn how to test for both. In this first topic, we will meet just two cations—**Cu²⁺ and Fe³⁺**—while treating Na⁺, SO₄²⁻, Cl⁻, and NO₃⁻ as **spectator ions** (plus a one-line preview of Ca²⁺).

## Part C: Two Reagents Delivering the Same Ion: OH⁻

To identify metal cations in solution, our two most useful reagents are **sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** and **ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH)**. Both are **alkalis**, meaning both supply hydroxide ions (**OH⁻**) in water. That is the very same polyatomic OH⁻ ion we studied in Chemical Bonding II, Topic 4: an oxygen-hydrogen cluster carrying a −1 charge.

Here is the key difference between the two reagents:

- **NaOH** is a **strong alkali**. As we saw in Chapter 4, it is a **strong electrolyte**: in water it dissociates completely into Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions, supplying a **high concentration of OH⁻ ions**.
- **NH₄OH** is a **weak alkali** (a weak electrolyte from Chapter 4): only a small fraction of it splits into ions at any moment, written with a reversible arrow (⇌): NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻. Thus its solution contains a **much lower concentration of OH⁻ ions**.

```
   NaOH solution (strong alkali)   NH₄OH solution (weak alkali)
   8 out of 8 NaOH units split     1 out of 8 NH₄OH units split

   Na⁺ OH⁻   Na⁺ OH⁻               NH₄OH  NH₄OH  NH₄OH  NH₄OH
   Na⁺ OH⁻   Na⁺ OH⁻               NH₄OH  NH₄OH  NH₄OH  NH₄⁺ OH⁻
   Na⁺ OH⁻   Na⁺ OH⁻
   Na⁺ OH⁻   Na⁺ OH⁻               (only 1 free OH⁻ shown)
   (8 free OH⁻ shown)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – A schematic comparison: in a strong alkali all formula units dissociate to release OH⁻, whereas in a weak alkali only a small fraction dissociates at a time. Count: 8 Na⁺ and 8 OH⁻ on the left; 7 intact NH₄OH units and 1 NH₄⁺/OH⁻ pair on the right. (In a real beaker there are trillions upon trillions of particles; 8 units are drawn simply to illustrate the contrast.)

Even so, precipitating most metal hydroxides does not require a huge flood of OH⁻ ions—**even a modest supply of OH⁻ ions meeting Cu²⁺ or Fe³⁺ in solution is enough** to form an insoluble solid. That is why **both reagents** successfully precipitate Cu²⁺ and Fe³⁺! What happens when you add *excess* drops of each reagent is a fascinating story we will unpack in **Topic 2 (NaOH)** and **Topic 3 (NH₄OH)**. Today, let's focus on why an insoluble **metal hydroxide** precipitate forms the moment OH⁻ ions meet these metal cations.

## Part D: Accounting for the Precipitate: Net Ionic Equations

Back in Acids, Bases, and Salts (Topic 5), we learned how precipitation reactions work: mix solutions of two **soluble** salts, and if an exchanged pair of ions forms an **insoluble** compound, that solid settles out at the bottom (we saw BaCl₂ + Na₂SO₄ and AgNO₃ + NaCl there). Now let's apply that exact idea to **metal hydroxides**. Most metal hydroxides are **insoluble** in water (in Chapter 1 we met Cu(OH)₂ and Fe(OH)₃ on our list of insoluble weak bases).

### Example 3. CuSO₄ + NaOH: Writing Out All the Ions

**Teacher demonstration (in the school lab with the teacher):** A clean test tube holds 1–2 mL of dilute blue CuSO₄ solution. Using a dropper, the teacher adds a few drops of dilute NaOH solution. Immediately, a **pale blue precipitate** forms and settles toward the bottom.

**Step 1: Balanced molecular equation.**
CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄

Atom count: Cu: 1 = 1; S: 1 = 1; O: 4 + 2 = 6 and 2 + 4 = 6 ✅; Na: 2 = 2; H: 2 = 2 ✅.

**Step 2: Split every soluble substance into its free ions** (keep the insoluble precipitate Cu(OH)₂ intact as one formula):

Cu²⁺ + SO₄²⁻ + 2Na⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2Na⁺ + SO₄²⁻

**Step 3: Cancel the ions that appear identically on both sides.** Both 2Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ sit unchanged on the left and right. Those are our **spectator ions**.

**Step 4: Net ionic equation.**

**Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓**

**Check:** Atoms: Cu 1 = 1, O 2 = 2, H 2 = 2 ✅. Charge: left side (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0; right side neutral Cu(OH)₂ = 0 ✅.

This net ionic equation tells us the heart of the reaction: forming the pale blue precipitate requires **only Cu²⁺ ions meeting OH⁻ ions**.

### Example 4. FeCl₃ + NaOH

**Teacher demonstration (in the school lab with the teacher):** A few drops of NaOH are added to a dilute yellow-brown solution of FeCl₃. This time, a **reddish-brown precipitate** forms immediately.

Molecular equation: FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl

Atom count: Fe 1 = 1; Cl 3 = 3; Na 3 = 3; O 3 = 3; H 3 = 3 ✅.

Splitting soluble compounds into ions: Fe³⁺ + 3Cl⁻ + 3Na⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3Na⁺ + 3Cl⁻

Cancel spectator ions (3Na⁺ and 3Cl⁻). **Net ionic equation:** **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓**

**Check:** Atoms: Fe 1, O 3, H 3 on both sides ✅. Charge: left (+3) + 3 × (−1) = 0, right 0 ✅. Notice that each Fe³⁺ ion requires **3** OH⁻ ions (because its charge is +3), whereas each Cu²⁺ ion required **2** OH⁻ ions. Charge balance automatically dictates how many OH⁻ ions combine with the metal cation!

### Example 5. Change the Spectator Ion, Get the Exact Same Precipitate

**Question:** Suppose we replace CuSO₄ solution with Cu(NO₃)₂ solution and add a few drops of NaOH. What will we see, and what will the net ionic equation be?

**Teacher demonstration:** Now Cu²⁺ is accompanied by NO₃⁻ ions instead of SO₄²⁻ ions.

Molecular equation: Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + 2NaNO₃. Atom count: Cu 1, N 2, O: 6 + 2 = 8 and 2 + 6 = 8 ✅, Na 2, H 2 ✅.

Now the spectator ions are **Na⁺ and NO₃⁻**. Once we cancel them, the net ionic equation is identical: **Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓**. Therefore, we see the exact same **pale blue precipitate**!

**Check:** Same cation + different spectator anion = identical net ionic equation and identical precipitate ✅. That proves **the precipitate comes from the metal cation and OH⁻**, not from the spectator ions.

### Ca²⁺ in One Line

Calcium ions also combine with OH⁻ in the same ratio as Cu²⁺: **Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂↓**. However, this precipitate is much fainter: a **slight white precipitate** (because Ca(OH)₂ is slightly soluble in water—that is how limewater is made in Chapter 1). We will explore the full story of Ca²⁺ in Topic 2.

**Check:** Atoms: Ca 1, O 2, H 2 on both sides; charge: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.

```
   Ion accounting at a glance (CuSO₄ + NaOH)

   In solution before mixing:   Cu²⁺   SO₄²⁻   Na⁺   OH⁻
                                 :                    :
                                 +----- combine ------+
                                           :
                                           v
                                     Cu(OH)₂ (ppt)
   Remain unchanged in water:          SO₄²⁻   Na⁺      <- spectator ions
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Cu²⁺ and OH⁻ combine to form the insoluble Cu(OH)₂ precipitate, while SO₄²⁻ and Na⁺ remain unchanged in the water as spectator ions. (The diagram pairs Cu²⁺ with OH⁻ schematically; the balanced equation uses 2 OH⁻ per Cu²⁺ as shown in Example 3.)

## Part E: How a Precipitate Settles: Inside the Test Tube

Because a precipitate is an insoluble solid formed inside a liquid, it gradually **settles to the bottom of the test tube**, leaving a clear liquid layer above it. If we want to separate that clear liquid (which holds the spectator ions such as Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻) from the solid precipitate at the bottom, we can either **decant** it (gently pour off the top liquid without disturbing the settled solid) or **filter** it through a funnel lined with filter paper—just like straining tea leaves with a kitchen strainer.

```
   BEFORE                      AFTER (adding drops of NaOH)

   :              :            :              :
   :    CuSO₄     :            : clear liquid :   <- Na⁺, SO₄²⁻ (spectator ions)
   :   solution   :            :              :
   :    (blue)    :            :              :
   :              :            :  ##########  :   <- Cu(OH)₂ ppt
   '--------------'            '--------------'      (pale blue, settled at bottom)
```

> **Figure 1.5** – The test tube before and after adding NaOH drops. In the right-hand tube, the pale blue Cu(OH)₂ precipitate has settled at the bottom, leaving a clear solution of spectator ions above it (once enough NaOH has been added to react with all the Cu²⁺).

### Laboratory Procedure (In the School Lab With the Teacher)

1. Take a **clean test tube** containing 1–2 mL of the dilute salt solution. (Clean glassware ensures leftover traces of other ions don't give a false clue.)
2. Using a **dropper**, add **a few drops** of NaOH solution (dispensed by the teacher).
3. **Record your observation** right away: color, precipitate, or no visible change.
4. Next, add **excess** NaOH to see what happens to the precipitate. Precipitates of Cu(OH)₂ and Fe(OH)₃ **do not dissolve** in excess NaOH; certain other metal hydroxides *do* dissolve, which is the central mystery of Topic 2!
5. Ask our three detective questions: What was added? What was seen? What does it mean?

**Strict Safety Rules:** Every test in this chapter is carried out **in a school lab under teacher supervision** (wearing safety goggles and gloves, using dilute solutions dispensed by the teacher). Even dilute NaOH solution is **corrosive**: if a drop touches skin or eyes, rinse immediately with plenty of running water and inform the teacher. Copper salts are **harmful if swallowed**, so keep all solutions away from your mouth. **Never taste** any chemical, and never put your nose directly over the mouth of a test tube to smell a gas (when needed, a teacher demonstrates the safe "wafting" motion with a hand—covered here for study only). **Never pour heavy-metal solutions down the sink**: the teacher collects all heavy-metal waste in designated waste bottles. In upcoming topics we will also discuss compounds of lead, barium, and silver—those are **toxic**, and students never handle them directly.

### Example 6. How Much Precipitate Forms? A Mass Calculation

**Question (teacher demonstration, exact reacting quantities):** Suppose 1.595 g of CuSO₄ is mixed with the exact amount of NaOH required for complete reaction. What mass of Cu(OH)₂ precipitate forms, and what mass of NaOH is used? (Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1, Na = 23.)

- Molar mass of CuSO₄ = 63.5 + 32 + 4 × 16 = 63.5 + 32 + 64 = **159.5 g/mol**.
- Moles of CuSO₄ = 1.595 / 159.5 = **0.01 mol**.
- From CuSO₄ + 2NaOH → Cu(OH)₂↓ + Na₂SO₄: 1 mol of CuSO₄ reacts with 2 mol of NaOH to form 1 mol of Cu(OH)₂.
- Mass of NaOH needed = 2 × 0.01 = **0.02 mol** × 40 g/mol = **0.8 g**.
- Molar mass of Cu(OH)₂ = 63.5 + 2 × (16 + 1) = 63.5 + 34 = **97.5 g/mol**. Mass of Cu(OH)₂ formed = 0.01 × 97.5 = **0.975 g**.

**Check (conservation of mass):** Mass of Na₂SO₄ formed = 0.01 × 142 = 1.42 g. Total mass before reaction = 1.595 + 0.8 = **2.395 g**; total mass after reaction = 0.975 + 1.42 = **2.395 g** ✅.

## Part F: A Detective's Notebook: Reading the Clues

Let's practice turning an observation into a solid conclusion. In this introductory topic we focus on **Cu²⁺ and Fe³⁺** (the remaining five cations join our lineup in Topics 2 and 3).

| Test | Reagent Added | Observation | Conclusion |
|---|---|---|---|
| a | A few drops of NaOH | Pale blue precipitate | Cu²⁺ is present |
| b | A few drops of NaOH | Reddish-brown precipitate | Fe³⁺ is present |
| c | A few drops of NaOH | No precipitate forms | Neither Cu²⁺ nor Fe³⁺ is present |

### Example 7. From Observation to Conclusion

**Question:** During a teacher demonstration, a few drops of NaOH are added to an unknown solution and a **reddish-brown precipitate** settles to the bottom. Between Cu²⁺ and Fe³⁺, which ion is present? Write the net ionic equation.

- What was added: NaOH. What was seen: reddish-brown precipitate. From our table, reddish-brown is the signature of Fe(OH)₃.
- Conclusion: **Fe³⁺** is present in the solution.
- Net ionic equation: **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓**.

**Check:** Cu(OH)₂ is pale blue (Example 3), so a reddish-brown precipitate cannot be Cu(OH)₂ ✅. Atoms and charges balance on both sides ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "The precipitate forms from the metal ion alone without needing OH⁻, or the spectator ions also become part of the precipitate."**
This misconception is tempting because a CuSO₄ solution is already blue and the precipitate is also blue, making it look as if "the copper just clumped together on its own." Let's disprove that with three quick demonstrations:

1. **Cu²⁺ alone, without OH⁻:** Take a test tube of blue CuSO₄ solution and add only **plain distilled water** (teacher demonstration). Even though Cu²⁺ ions are floating everywhere in the tube, **zero precipitate forms**—the blue color simply becomes paler. Now add drops of NaOH to a second tube of CuSO₄: a pale blue precipitate forms immediately! The only difference was the addition of **OH⁻ ions**, proving that **OH⁻ is essential to form the precipitate**.
2. **What the spectator ions are doing:** Decant the clear liquid above the settled precipitate in Example 3 (that clear liquid contains the Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions after all the Cu²⁺ has precipitated out). Adding more NaOH to that clear liquid produces **no new precipitate**, because Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ simply stay dissolved in water (Na₂SO₄ and NaOH are both soluble). That is why Na⁺ and SO₄²⁻ are **left out of the net ionic equation** altogether.
3. **Swapping the spectator ion changes nothing:** When we replaced CuSO₄ with Cu(NO₃)₂ in Example 5, the spectator ion changed from SO₄²⁻ to NO₃⁻, yet the precipitate had the exact same pale blue color and the exact same formula, Cu(OH)₂. If the spectator anion were part of the precipitate, changing it would have changed the precipitate!

**Rule:** The hydroxide precipitate is built strictly from **metal cation + OH⁻**. Spectator ions remain dissolved in the surrounding water.

## Quick Recap

- **Analysis**: **Qualitative** = "what is present?"; **Quantitative** = "how much is present?" (using Chapter 5 mole calculations). This chapter focuses primarily on qualitative analysis.
- Every **test** = add a reagent → record the observation (color, precipitate, or gas) → state the conclusion. Always ask the three questions: **What was added? What was seen? What does it mean?**
- A salt consists of a cation + an anion, which separate when dissolved in water. Chemical tests identify these individual ions.
- **NaOH is a strong alkali** (high OH⁻ concentration) and **NH₄OH is a weak alkali** (low OH⁻ concentration), yet both supply enough OH⁻ to precipitate insoluble metal hydroxides.
- **Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓** (pale blue); **Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓** (reddish-brown); **Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂↓** (slight white).
- **Spectator ions** (such as Na⁺, SO₄²⁻, Cl⁻, NO₃⁻) remain unchanged on both sides of the reaction and are omitted from the net ionic equation.
- A precipitate settles to the bottom of the test tube and can be separated by **decanting or filtering**.
- Safety: all tests are done **in a school lab with the teacher**, using dilute solutions, goggles, and gloves—never taste or directly inhale any chemical, and never pour heavy-metal waste down the drain.

### Answers

1. (a) **Qualitative**: asks "is it present or not?" (b) **Quantitative**: "0.03 mol" is a numerical amount. (c) **Qualitative**: identifies which bottle holds the copper salt. Check: identity or yes/no = qualitative; measured number = quantitative ✅.
2. Fe(NO₃)₃ → Fe³⁺ + 3NO₃⁻. Charge balance: (+3) + 3 × (−1) = **0** ✅. Molecular equation: Fe(NO₃)₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaNO₃ (reddish-brown precipitate). Spectator ions: **Na⁺ and NO₃⁻**. Atom check: Fe 1 = 1, N 3 = 3, O: 9 + 3 = 12 and 3 + 9 = 12, Na 3 = 3, H 3 = 3 ✅.
3. **NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻.** Atoms: N 1, H 5, O 1 on both sides; charge: 0 = (+1) + (−1) ✅. Even though only a small fraction of NH₄OH splits into OH⁻ at a time, Cu(OH)₂ is so insoluble that even a small supply of OH⁻ ions combines with Cu²⁺ (Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓), and as those OH⁻ ions are used up, more NH₄OH dissociates to replace them (covered in detail in Topic 3).
4. Na⁺ does **not** appear in the net ionic equation at all because Na⁺ remains dissolved and unchanged on both sides of the reaction (it is a spectator ion). The precipitate Cu(OH)₂ is made strictly of Cu²⁺ and OH⁻ ions; NaOH merely served as the **source of OH⁻ ions**. Check: Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓ balances both atoms and charge with zero Na atoms ✅.
5. Molar mass of Cu(NO₃)₂ = 63.5 + 2 × (14 + 48) = 63.5 + 124 = **187.5 g/mol**. Moles = 3.75 / 187.5 = **0.02 mol**. Mass of NaOH = 2 × 0.02 = 0.04 mol × 40 = **1.6 g**. Mass of Cu(OH)₂ = 0.02 × 97.5 = **1.95 g**. Mass of NaNO₃ = 0.04 × 85 = 3.4 g. Check: total mass before = 3.75 + 1.6 = **5.35 g**; total mass after = 1.95 + 3.4 = **5.35 g** ✅.
6. Molar mass of Fe(OH)₃ = 56 + 3 × 17 = 56 + 51 = **107 g/mol**. Moles = 1.07 / 107 = **0.01 mol**. From FeCl₃ + 3NaOH → Fe(OH)₃↓ + 3NaCl: mass of FeCl₃ = 0.01 mol × (56 + 106.5 = 162.5) = **1.625 g**. Mass of NaOH = 3 × 0.01 = 0.03 mol × 40 = **1.2 g**. Mass of NaCl = 0.03 × 58.5 = 1.755 g. Check: 1.625 + 1.2 = **2.825 g**; 1.07 + 1.755 = **2.825 g** ✅.
7. Each Cu²⁺ ion requires 2 OH⁻ ions: 6 × 2 = **12 OH⁻ ions**, forming **6 units of Cu(OH)₂**. Each Fe³⁺ ion requires 3 OH⁻ ions: 7 × 3 = **21 OH⁻ ions**, forming **7 units of Fe(OH)₃**. Check: 6 Cu²⁺ = +12 balanced by 12 OH⁻ = −12 ✅; 7 Fe³⁺ = +21 balanced by 21 OH⁻ = −21 ✅.
8. (a) **False** ❌: even though NH₄OH is a weak alkali, it still supplies enough OH⁻ to precipitate Cu²⁺ and Fe³⁺ (Part C). (b) **False** ❌: spectator ions are cancelled and omitted from the net ionic equation. (c) **True** ✅: Fe³⁺ has a +3 charge. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌: plain water does not supply the OH⁻ concentration needed to precipitate Cu(OH)₂.
9. A pale blue precipitate is the signature of **Cu(OH)₂**, not Fe(OH)₃ (which is reddish-brown). Correct conclusion: **Cu²⁺** is present. Net ionic equation: **Cu²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Cu(OH)₂↓**. Check: Cu 1, O 2, H 2; charge 0 = 0 ✅.
10. **Ca²⁺ + 2OH⁻ → Ca(OH)₂↓**: forms a **slight white** precipitate. Check: Ca 1, O 2, H 2 on both sides; charge (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.
11. **What was added:** A few drops of NaOH solution. **What was seen:** A reddish-brown precipitate settled at the bottom. **What does it tell:** Fe³⁺ ions are present (forming Fe(OH)₃). Check: Fe³⁺ + 3OH⁻ → Fe(OH)₃↓ ✅.
12. The test tube must be **clean** so that leftover traces of other ions (such as Cu²⁺) do not produce a false precipitate. The **teacher** dispenses the NaOH solution because even dilute NaOH is **corrosive** to eyes and skin. Leftover solutions containing heavy-metal ions must **never be poured down the sink**—the teacher collects them in a chemical waste bottle.
