# Topic 2: Homologous Series and Functional Groups

In Topic 1, we saw that carbon is a four-handed element capable of linking into straight chains, branched chains, and rings, giving rise to **millions of organic compounds**. That immediately raises a practical question: **how can anyone study millions of compounds without getting lost?** Do chemists have to memorise millions of separate reactions? Not at all! Chemists group carbon compounds into **families**, where all members of a family share the same chemical behaviour and each member is simply **one CH₂ unit longer** than the one before it. Today we will learn how these families (**homologous series**) work and how a small tag of atoms (**functional group**) defines each family's personality.

---

## Adding One More Car to the Metro Train

On Sunday morning, Mr. Kim took his nephew Lucas to the city metro station. A large poster on the platform announced: "Line Expansion: Trains Upgrading from 3 Cars to 6 Cars." As a 3-car train glided into the station, Lucas pulled out his chemistry notebook, where he had jotted down the formulas of several hydrocarbon gases the day before.

"Uncle, look at that poster," Lucas said. "Between a 3-car train and a 6-car train, what stays the same and what changes?" Mr. Kim smiled. "Look closely. Every middle car has the exact same design—same doors, same seats, same windows—and both ends of the train have the same driver's cab. What stays the same is **the type of train and how it runs on the tracks**. What changes gradually as you add cars is the **length, total mass, passenger capacity, and braking distance**. And notice one more thing: train length always grows by **one whole car at a time**—you never attach half a car."

Lucas glanced down at his notebook: CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈, C₄H₁₀. "Wait, Uncle—these gases follow the exact same pattern! CH₄ has 1 carbon, C₂H₆ has 2, C₃H₈ has 3... each one adds 1 carbon and 2 hydrogens, and all of them burn as fuels!" Mr. Kim nodded. "You just discovered what chemists call a **homologous series**: it's like a **metro train**, where each successive member adds one **–CH₂– car** to the chain. Let's see how these molecular trains are built and what gives each family its identity."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Homologous series** | A family of organic compounds having the same functional group and general formula, in which successive members differ by a –CH₂– unit | CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈, C₄H₁₀, C₅H₁₂ |
| **Homologue (member)** | Any individual compound belonging to a homologous series | C₃H₈ is a homologue in the alkane series |
| **General formula** | An algebraic formula (in terms of carbon count *n*) that generates the molecular formula of every member of a series | alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ |
| **Functional group** | An atom or group of atoms in a molecule that determines its characteristic chemical reactions | –OH (hydroxyl), –COOH (carboxyl) |
| **Hydrocarbon** | A compound made of carbon and hydrogen only | CH₄, C₂H₄, C₂H₂ |
| **Alkane, alkene, alkyne** | Three families of hydrocarbons: all C–C single bonds, one C=C double bond, and one C≡C triple bond | C₂H₆, C₂H₄, C₂H₂ |
| **Alcohol, carboxylic acid** | Organic families containing the –OH group or the –COOH group | C₂H₅OH, CH₃COOH |
| **Saturated** | Having only single bonds between carbon atoms (fully "saturated" with hydrogen) | ethane, C₂H₆ |
| **Unsaturated** | Containing at least one carbon-carbon double or triple bond | ethene (C₂H₄), ethyne (C₂H₂) |
| **Structural formula** | A diagram showing every atom and every covalent bond in a molecule | full dash diagram of ethane |
| **Condensed formula** | A compact text version of a structural formula grouped carbon by carbon | CH₃CH₂OH |

## Part A: What a "Series" Means: A Metro Line of Molecules

Let's start with the simplest family of carbon compounds: hydrocarbons containing only C–C single bonds, called **alkanes**. Let's line up the first five members by carbon count (*n* = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5):

| Name | Molecular Formula | Step from Previous Member | Molar Mass (H = 1, C = 12) |
|---|---|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ | first member | 16 |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | +CH₂ | 30 |
| propane | C₃H₈ | +CH₂ | 44 |
| butane | C₄H₁₀ | +CH₂ | 58 |
| pentane | C₅H₁₂ | +CH₂ | 72 |

Look at how you step from methane to ethane: you insert one **C** and two **H** atoms—one **–CH₂– car**. The exact same +CH₂ step takes you from ethane to propane, and from propane to butane! Since the mass of one CH₂ unit is **12 + (2 × 1) = 14**, every member of a homologous series is **14 g/mol heavier** than the member right before it: 16, 30, 44, 58, 72 (+14 at every step).

Read Figure 2.1 like a metro train: an H atom at the front cab, an H atom at the rear cab, and *n* identical **[CH₂] cars** coupled in between:

```
  CH₄   :   H-[CH₂]-H
  C₂H₆  :   H-[CH₂]-[CH₂]-H
  C₃H₈  :   H-[CH₂]-[CH₂]-[CH₂]-H
  C₄H₁₀ :   H-[CH₂]-[CH₂]-[CH₂]-[CH₂]-H
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Viewing the alkane series as a train of CH₂ cars: every formula consists of two end H atoms plus *n* middle [CH₂] units. For *n* = 4, four CH₂ cars supply 4 C and 8 H, plus the 2 end H atoms = C₄H₁₀.

Now let's draw the actual covalent bonds (Figure 2.2), checking that every carbon has **4 bonds** and every hydrogen has **1 bond** (Topic 1):

```
      H                 H   H                 H   H   H
      I                 I   I                 I   I   I
  H - C - H         H - C - C - H         H - C - C - C - H
      I                 I   I                 I   I   I
      H                 H   H                 H   H   H

     CH₄                 C₂H₆                     C₃H₈
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Structural formulas of the first three alkanes. Every C atom forms 4 bonds and every H atom forms 1 bond. Stepping from one homologue to the next inserts one –CH₂– unit into the chain.

**Five defining features of any homologous series:**
1. All members contain the **same functional group** (or the same bond type).
2. All members fit the **same general formula**.
3. Any two successive members differ by **one –CH₂– unit** (and **14 u** in molecular mass).
4. All members show **similar chemical properties** (because they share the same functional group).
5. Their **physical properties** (such as boiling point, melting point, and density) show a **gradual, regular change** as molecular mass increases.

### Example 1. Checking the mass difference between successive alkanes

**Problem:** Calculate the molar masses of C₃H₈, C₄H₁₀, and C₅H₁₂, and verify that successive members differ by the mass of a CH₂ unit.

**Step 1:** C₃H₈ = (3 × 12) + (8 × 1) = 36 + 8 = **44** g/mol.
**Step 2:** C₄H₁₀ = (4 × 12) + 10 = 48 + 10 = **58** g/mol.
**Step 3:** C₅H₁₂ = (5 × 12) + 12 = 60 + 12 = **72** g/mol.
**Step 4:** Differences: 58 − 44 = **14**, and 72 − 58 = **14**.

**Check:** Mass of CH₂ = 12 + 2 = 14 ✅. Both steps match the mass of one CH₂ unit ✅.

**Try it on paper:** Cut 4 small paper slips labelled "CH₂" and 2 end slips labelled "H". Lay them out in a row to build H–CH₂–H, H–CH₂–CH₂–H, and so on. For *n* "CH₂" slips, the total H count is always **2n + 2** (2 H on each of the *n* slips, plus the 2 end H slips).

## Part B: General Formula: One Formula for Every Member

Lucas asked, "Uncle, if I want the formula of the 10-carbon alkane, do I have to draw 10 carbons and count all the hydrogens one by one?" "Not at all—we can derive a general formula straight from carbon's valency of 4."

- **Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂):** In a chain of *n* carbon atoms joined by single bonds, there are *n* middle CH₂ units (giving 2*n* hydrogens) plus 2 hydrogens capping the two ends of the chain: **2n + 2** hydrogens in total. Thus, the general formula of alkanes is **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**.
- **Alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ):** Introducing **one C=C double bond** between two carbons uses up 1 extra valency on each of those two carbons, removing **2 hydrogen atoms** compared to the alkane: (2*n* + 2) − 2 = **2n**. Thus, alkenes have the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ** (for *n* ≥ 2, since a C=C bond requires at least 2 carbons).
- **Alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂):** Introducing **one C≡C triple bond** uses up 2 extra valencies on each of two carbons, removing **4 hydrogen atoms** compared to the alkane: (2*n* + 2) − 4 = **2n − 2**. Thus, alkynes have the general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₋₂** (for *n* ≥ 2).
- **Alcohols (CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH):** Replace one H atom of an alkane (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) with an **–OH** group, leaving 2*n* + 1 hydrogens on the carbon chain plus the OH group: **CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH**.
- **Carboxylic acids (CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH):** Replace one H atom with a **–COOH** group. Note that the –COOH group itself contains 1 carbon atom, so the total number of carbons in CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH is *n* + 1 (starting with *n* = 0 gives methanoic acid, HCOOH).

| Homologous Series | General Formula | First Three Members |
|---|---|---|
| Alkane | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈ |
| Alkene | CₙH₂ₙ | C₂H₄, C₃H₆, C₄H₈ |
| Alkyne | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | C₂H₂, C₃H₄, C₄H₆ |
| Alcohol | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | CH₃OH, C₂H₅OH, C₃H₇OH |
| Carboxylic acid | CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH | HCOOH, CH₃COOH, C₂H₅COOH |

### Example 2. Finding carbon count from hydrogen count

**Problem:** An alkane molecule contains 12 hydrogen atoms. Find its molecular formula and molar mass.

**Step 1:** Set 2*n* + 2 = 12 → 2*n* = 10 → **n = 5**.
**Step 2:** Formula is **C₅H₁₂** (pentane).
**Step 3:** Molar mass = (5 × 12) + 12 = **72** g/mol.

**Check:** Valency check: 5 carbons have 5 × 4 = 20 valencies; 4 C–C bonds use 8 valencies, leaving 12 valencies for 12 H atoms ✅.

### Example 3. Finding a member from its molar mass

**Problem:** An alkene has a molar mass of 56 g/mol. Find its molecular formula.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of CₙH₂ₙ = 12*n* + 2*n* = **14n**.
**Step 2:** 14*n* = 56 → **n = 4**.
**Step 3:** Formula is **C₄H₈** (butene).

**Check:** (4 × 12) + (8 × 1) = 48 + 8 = 56 ✅.

### Example 4. Molar masses in the alcohol series

**Problem:** Calculate the molar masses of CH₃OH, C₂H₅OH, and C₃H₇OH, and verify the step difference. (O = 16.)

**Step 1:** CH₃OH = 12 + 3 + 16 + 1 = **32** g/mol.
**Step 2:** C₂H₅OH = 24 + 5 + 16 + 1 = **46** g/mol.
**Step 3:** C₃H₇OH = 36 + 7 + 16 + 1 = **60** g/mol.
**Step 4:** Differences: 46 − 32 = **14**, and 60 − 46 = **14**.

**Check:** Using the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH, molar mass = 14*n* + 18, which gives 32, 46, and 60 for *n* = 1, 2, 3 ✅.

### Example 5. Molar masses in the carboxylic acid series (and an interesting coincidence!)

**Problem:** Calculate the molar masses of HCOOH, CH₃COOH, and C₂H₅COOH.

**Step 1:** HCOOH = 12 + 1 + 32 + 1 = **46** g/mol.
**Step 2:** CH₃COOH = 24 + 4 + 32 = **60** g/mol.
**Step 3:** C₂H₅COOH = 36 + 6 + 32 = **74** g/mol.

**Check:** Differences: 60 − 46 = 14, and 74 − 60 = 14 ✅.

**An interesting coincidence:** Notice that ethanol (C₂H₅OH) and methanoic acid (HCOOH) both have a molar mass of **46**, and propanol (C₃H₇OH) and ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) both have a molar mass of **60**! Yet their molecular formulas (C₂H₆O vs CH₂O₂) and functional groups (–OH vs –COOH) are completely different, so they belong to different homologous series. Having the same molar mass does not put two compounds in the same series!

## Part C: Boiling Points: The Longer the Train, the Harder It Is to Lift

Just as a longer metro train has more mass and takes more effort to get moving, **longer carbon chains have higher boiling points**. Let's look at the boiling points of the first six straight-chain alkanes:

| Alkane | Formula | Boiling Point (approx., °C) | Physical State at 25 °C |
|---|---|---|---|
| methane | CH₄ | −162 | gas |
| ethane | C₂H₆ | −89 | gas |
| propane | C₃H₈ | −42 | gas |
| butane | C₄H₁₀ | −1 | gas |
| pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 | liquid |
| hexane | C₆H₁₄ | 69 | liquid |

```
  methane    -162 °C   #
  ethane      -89 °C   ########
  propane     -42 °C   #############
  butane       -1 °C   #################
  pentane      36 °C   #####################
  hexane       69 °C   ########################
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Bar chart of alkane boiling points (shifted by +170 °C and divided by 10 so every bar is positive: methane = 1, ethane = 8, propane = 13, butane = 17, pentane = 21, hexane = 24 `#` marks). Notice the smooth, **gradual** progression as chain length increases.

Why does the boiling point rise smoothly with chain length? In Class 9, we learned that covalent molecules have weak intermolecular attractions ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)). As the molecule grows longer and heavier with each added –CH₂– unit, the **attractive forces between neighbouring molecules grow stronger**, requiring more thermal energy (a higher temperature) to pull the molecules apart into a gas.

**The same trend holds in other series:** in the alcohol series, methanol boils at **65 °C** and ethanol at **78 °C** (+13 °C). Comparing different series with the same 2-carbon chain—ethane (−89 °C), ethanol (78 °C), and ethanoic acid (118 °C)—shows that the **functional group** also strongly affects intermolecular attraction and boiling point.

### Example 6. Predicting physical state from boiling point

**Problem:** In a room at 25 °C, what are the physical states of butane (b.p. −1 °C) and pentane (b.p. 36 °C)? Why are propane and butane stored as **liquids** inside an LPG cooking gas cylinder?

**Step 1:** Since butane's boiling point (−1 °C) is **below** 25 °C, it has already boiled into a **gas** at room temperature.
**Step 2:** Since pentane's boiling point (36 °C) is **above** 25 °C, it remains a **liquid** at room temperature.
**Step 3:** Although propane (−42 °C) and butane (−1 °C) are gases at ordinary atmospheric pressure, compressing them under **high pressure** inside a steel cylinder liquefies them so a large amount of fuel can be stored in a compact tank. Opening the valve releases the pressure, allowing the liquid to vaporise back into gas for the stove burner.

**Check:** 25 > −1 (gas) ✅; 25 < 36 (liquid) ✅.

## Part D: Functional Groups: The "Tag" That Decides Chemical Personality

Lucas asked, "Uncle, why do all members of the alcohol series react so similarly?" "Because every one of them carries the exact same reactive 'tag' of atoms—called a **functional group**." Replace one H atom on an alkane chain with a functional group, and you create a **brand-new chemical family**:

```
      H   H                   H   H
      I   I                   I   I
  H - C - C - H    ---->  H - C - C - O - H
      I   I                   I   I
      H   H                   H   H

     ethane                 ethanol
```

> **Figure 2.4** – Replacing one H atom of ethane (C₂H₆) with a hydroxyl group (–OH) turns it into ethanol (C₂H₅OH). Every C still has 4 bonds, every H has 1 bond, and the O atom has 2 bonds (one to C, one to H).

Similarly, replacing one H of methane (CH₄) with –OH gives methanol (CH₃OH), and replacing one H of propane (C₃H₈) with –OH gives propanol (C₃H₇OH). All three belong to the **alcohol** family because all three carry the **–OH** functional group.

| Functional Group | Family Name | Representative Example |
|---|---|---|
| **–OH** | Alcohol | ethanol, C₂H₅OH |
| **–CHO** | Aldehyde | ethanal, CH₃CHO |
| **>C=O** | Ketone | propanone, CH₃COCH₃ |
| **–COOH** | Carboxylic acid | ethanoic acid, CH₃COOH |
| **–Cl, –Br** | Haloalkane (halogen compound) | chloromethane (CH₃Cl), bromoethane (C₂H₅Br) |

*(We will learn the systematic naming rules for all of these in Topic 3; for now, focus on spotting each functional group.)* Note that the **C=C double bond** in alkenes and the **C≡C triple bond** in alkynes also act as the characteristic reactive sites of their respective families.

```
      H       H
       \     /
        C = C                     H - C ≡ C - H
       /     \
      H       H

     ethene (C₂H₄)               ethyne (C₂H₂)
```

> **Figure 2.5** – Ethene (C=C double bond) and ethyne (C≡C triple bond). In ethene, each C has 2 C–H bonds + 1 double bond (2) = 4 bonds. In ethyne, each C has 1 C–H bond + 1 triple bond (3) = 4 bonds.

```
      H   O
      I   II
  H - C - C - O - H
      I
      H

    ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH)
```

> **Figure 2.6** – Ethanoic acid, showing the **–COOH** (carboxyl) group on the right carbon: 1 C–C bond + 1 C=O double bond (2) + 1 C–O single bond = 4 bonds on the carboxyl carbon.

### Example 7. Identifying functional groups and families

**Problem:** Identify the functional group and family for each compound: (a) CH₃OH, (b) CH₃COOH, (c) C₂H₅Br, (d) CH₃CHO, (e) CH₃COCH₃.

**Step 1:** (a) Functional group **–OH**, family: **alcohol**.
**Step 2:** (b) Functional group **–COOH**, family: **carboxylic acid**.
**Step 3:** (c) Functional group **–Br**, family: **haloalkane (halogen compound)**.
**Step 4:** (d) Functional group **–CHO** (at the end of the chain), family: **aldehyde**.
**Step 5:** (e) Functional group **>C=O** (attached to two carbons in the middle), family: **ketone**.

**Check:** Each formula displays its characteristic group directly ✅.

## Part E: Saturated and Unsaturated Compounds

**Saturated** means "full": all carbon-carbon bonds in the chain are **single bonds**, so the carbon skeleton holds the maximum possible number of hydrogen atoms. All alkanes are saturated hydrocarbons. **Unsaturated** means "capable of taking in more atoms": the chain contains at least one **C=C double bond** or **C≡C triple bond**, which can open up in chemical reactions to add more atoms (Topic 5).

You can spot unsaturation in an open-chain hydrocarbon straight from its hydrogen count: for *n* carbons, the maximum (saturated) hydrogen count is **2n + 2**. Fewer hydrogens mean multiple bonds (or a ring).

### Example 8. Classifying hydrocarbons as saturated or unsaturated

**Problem:** Among the straight-chain hydrocarbons C₄H₁₀, C₄H₈, and C₄H₆, which is saturated and which are unsaturated?

**Step 1:** For *n* = 4, 2*n* + 2 = 2(4) + 2 = **10**. Thus **C₄H₁₀** is **saturated** (butane).
**Step 2:** **C₄H₈** has 8 = 10 − 2 hydrogens (fits CₙH₂ₙ, one double bond): **unsaturated** (butene).
**Step 3:** **C₄H₆** has 6 = 10 − 4 hydrogens (fits CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, one triple bond): **unsaturated** (butyne).

**Check:** Missing 2 H corresponds to 1 double bond ✅; missing 4 H corresponds to 1 triple bond ✅.

## Part F: Three Ways to Write a Formula and How to Find the Next Member

Chemists write the formula of an organic compound in three ways depending on how much detail they need. Using ethanol as our example:

- **Molecular formula:** C₂H₆O (shows only the total number of atoms of each element).
- **Condensed formula:** CH₃CH₂OH or C₂H₅OH (groups the atoms around each carbon in a single text line and highlights the functional group).
- **Structural formula:** the full dash diagram (Figure 2.4) showing every single bond.

*(Sometimes a single molecular formula can represent more than one structural formula—we will explore those isomers in Topic 3!)*

**Two quick ways to write the next member of any homologous series:**
1. **Add CH₂** (increase C by 1 and H by 2 in the formula).
2. **Substitute *n* + 1** into the general formula of the series.

### Example 9. Finding the next homologue

**Problem:** What is the next alkene after propene (C₃H₆)? What is the next alkyne after propyne (C₃H₄)?

**Step 1:** Propene (C₃H₆) + CH₂ = **C₄H₈** (butene).
**Step 2:** Propyne (C₃H₄) + CH₂ = **C₄H₆** (butyne).

**Check:** For *n* = 4, alkene CₙH₂ₙ gives H = 8 ✅, and alkyne CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ gives H = 6 ✅. Mass difference: C₄H₈ (56) − C₃H₆ (42) = 14 ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Since all members of a homologous series belong to the same family, they must have the same physical properties too."**

Let's test this claim against the data in our alkane table:
- **Butane (C₄H₁₀)** is a **gas** at 25 °C (b.p. −1 °C), whereas the very next member, **pentane (C₅H₁₂)**, is a **liquid** (b.p. 36 °C), and **hexane (C₆H₁₄)** boils higher still at 69 °C ( mientras paraffin wax with 20+ carbons is a **solid**!). Clearly, physical properties **change gradually** down a series as the chain gets longer and heavier.
- What **stays similar** across a homologous series is the **chemical behaviour** (determined by the functional group). For example, methane and propane both undergo complete combustion in oxygen to yield CO₂ and H₂O:
  - CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 2 + 2 ✅)
  - C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O (C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 6 + 4 ✅)
  And in the alcohol series, ethanol burns similarly: C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O (C 2 = 2, H 6 = 6, O 7 = 4 + 3 ✅).

**Rule to remember:** Across a homologous series, **chemical properties remain similar** (same functional group), while **physical properties change gradually** (increasing chain length and molecular mass).

## Quick Recap

- **Homologous series:** a family of organic compounds sharing the same functional group and general formula, where successive members differ by **–CH₂– (14 u in mass)**.
- **General formulas:** alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, alkenes CₙH₂ₙ, alkynes CₙH₂ₙ₋₂, alcohols CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH, carboxylic acids CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH.
- **Boiling points** increase gradually with chain length as intermolecular attractions grow stronger (alkanes: −162, −89, −42, −1, 36, 69 °C; alcohols: 65 °C for methanol, 78 °C for ethanol).
- **Functional groups:** –OH (alcohol), –CHO (aldehyde), >C=O (ketone), –COOH (carboxylic acid), –Cl / –Br (haloalkane).
- **Saturated** hydrocarbons have only single C–C bonds; **unsaturated** hydrocarbons have at least one C=C double or C≡C triple bond.
- **Pitfall to avoid:** across a series, chemical properties stay similar, but physical properties change gradually.
- **Safety:** methanol is toxic (never taste or sniff); short-chain alkanes and ethanol are highly flammable.

9. Name the functional group (with its symbol) in each, or say "none": (a) C₆H₁₃OH, (b) C₄H₉COOH, (c) C₉H₂₀.
10. Hexane boils at about 69 °C. Without looking up any data, say whether octane (C₈H₁₈) is likely to boil above or below 69 °C, whether it is a gas or a liquid at 25 °C, and how decane (C₁₀H₂₂) compares with octane.
11. Two alkanes of the same series have molar masses 86 and 114. Find their formulas, and the formula and mass of the member between them.
12. Find the percentage of carbon (by mass) in C₆H₁₄ and in C₁₀H₂₂ (give to one decimal place). Which is higher? What value does the percentage approach for a very long chain? What does this tell you about physical properties across a series?
13. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) C₃H₇COOH and C₅H₁₁COOH belong to the same series and differ in molar mass by 28. (b) C₆H₁₄ and C₆H₁₃OH are members of the same series because both have 6 carbons. (c) Every member of the alcohol series has the same boiling point. (d) C₉H₂₀ is a saturated hydrocarbon.
14. Draw the structural formula of the straight-chain alcohol C₄H₉OH with the –OH on an end carbon. Count the valency of every C, H, and O.
15. A cleaning-product bottle in a storeroom has a faded label that says only "methanol". A student says it "looks like water and smells like hand sanitiser, so it must be safe". Explain why the student is wrong and what he should do.

### Answers

1. C₆H₁₄ (*n* = 6, H = 2 × 6 + 2 = 14): molar mass = 6 × 12 + 14 = 72 + 14 = **86**. C₁₀H₂₂ (*n* = 10, H = 22): molar mass = 120 + 22 = **142**. They differ by 10 − 6 = **4 CH₂ units**, and their molar masses differ by 142 − 86 = **56** = 4 × 14. Check: 4 × 14 = 56 ✅.
2. 2*n* + 2 = 18 → *n* = **8**. Formula: **C₈H₁₈**. Molar mass = 8 × 12 + 18 = 96 + 18 = **114**. Check: 14*n* + 2 = 112 + 2 = 114 ✅.
3. Molar mass of an alkane = 12*n* + (2*n* + 2) = 14*n* + 2. Setting 14*n* + 2 = 128 gives 14*n* = 126 → **n = 9**. Formula: **C₉H₂₀**. Check: 9 × 12 + 20 = 108 + 20 = 128 ✅.
4. The member between C₇H₁₆ and C₉H₂₀ is **C₈H₁₈**. Molar masses: C₇H₁₆ = 84 + 16 = **100**, C₈H₁₈ = 96 + 18 = **114**, C₉H₂₀ = 108 + 20 = **128**. Differences: 114 − 100 = 14 ✅, and 128 − 114 = 14 ✅.
5. For *n* = 5: **C₅H₁₁OH**, molar mass = 60 + 11 + 16 + 1 = **88**. Next member (*n* = 6): **C₆H₁₃OH**, molar mass = 72 + 13 + 17 = **102**. Difference: 102 − 88 = 14 ✅.
6. Molar mass of CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH = 12*n* + (2*n* + 1) + 16 + 1 = 14*n* + 18. Setting 14*n* + 18 = 130 gives 14*n* = 112 → **n = 8**. Formula: **C₈H₁₇OH**. Check: 96 + 17 + 17 = 130 ✅.
7. Molar mass of CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH = 14*n* + 46. Setting 14*n* + 46 = 116 gives 14*n* = 70 → **n = 5**. Formula: **C₅H₁₁COOH**. Total carbon atoms in the molecule = *n* + 1 = **6** (molecular formula C₆H₁₂O₂). Check: 72 + 12 + 32 = 116 ✅.
8. For alkanes, H = 2*n* + 2: C₆ → 14 ✅, C₇ → 16 ✅, C₈ → should be **18** (given 16), C₉ → 20 ✅, C₁₀ → 22 ✅. Thus **C₈H₁₆** does not belong to the alkane series; it fits **CₙH₂ₙ** (*n* = 8), the alkene general formula.
9. (a) C₆H₁₃OH: **–OH**, alcohol. (b) C₄H₉COOH: **–COOH**, carboxylic acid. (c) C₉H₂₀: **none**—it is a saturated hydrocarbon (alkane, H = 2 × 9 + 2 = 20 ✅).
10. Because boiling point increases with chain length down a homologous series, octane (C₈) has two more CH₂ units than hexane (C₆) and will boil **above 69 °C**. Since its boiling point is well above 25 °C, octane is a **liquid** at 25 °C. Decane (C₁₀) has two more CH₂ units than octane, so its boiling point is **higher still** than octane's.
11. Using 14*n* + 2: 14*n* + 2 = 86 → *n* = **6** (**C₆H₁₄**); 14*n* + 2 = 114 → *n* = **8** (**C₈H₁₈**). The member between them has *n* = 7: **C₇H₁₆**, with molar mass = 84 + 16 = **100**. Step differences: 100 − 86 = 14 ✅, and 114 − 100 = 14 ✅.
12. In C₆H₁₄, % C = 72 ÷ 86 × 100 = **83.7 percent**. In C₁₀H₂₂, % C = 120 ÷ 142 × 100 = **84.5 percent** (higher in C₁₀H₂₂). Since % C = 12*n* ÷ (14*n* + 2) × 100, for a very long chain the "+ 2" becomes negligible and % C approaches 12 ÷ 14 × 100 = **85.7 percent**. This gradual shift in mass composition mirrors the gradual change in physical properties across a series.
13. (a) **True** ✅: *n* = 3 and *n* = 5 differ by two CH₂ units, or 2 × 14 = 28 (masses 88 and 116 ✅). (b) **False** ❌: despite both having 6 carbons, they have different functional groups and general formulas (alkane vs alcohol). (c) **False** ❌: boiling points increase gradually with chain length (e.g., methanol 65 °C, ethanol 78 °C). (d) **True** ✅: 2(9) + 2 = 20 H, all single bonds.
14. Structural formula:

```
      H   H   H   H
      I   I   I   I
  H - C - C - C - C - O - H
      I   I   I   I
      H   H   H   H
```

> **Figure 2.7** – Straight-chain C₄H₉OH (butan-1-ol). Every C has 4 bonds (left end C: 3 H + 1 C = 4; two middle C atoms: 2 H + 2 C = 4; right C: 2 H + 1 C + 1 O = 4). The O atom has 2 bonds (1 to C, 1 to H), and every H has 1 bond. Total H = 8 (on C) + 1 (on O) = **9** ✅.

15. The student is **completely wrong**. **Methanol is a deadly poison**—swallowing or even inhaling a small amount can cause permanent blindness or death. Because it is a colourless liquid that looks like water or ethanol, you can **never judge safety by appearance or smell**. The student must **never open, sniff, or taste** the bottle, keep flames away (methanol is also flammable), and immediately report the bottle to a teacher or adult for safe handling.
