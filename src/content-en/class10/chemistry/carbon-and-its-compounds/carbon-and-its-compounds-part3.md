# Topic 3: Naming and Isomers: First Steps in IUPAC

In Topic 2, we grouped carbon compounds into homologous series and learned to spot their functional groups. Names like ethanal, propanone, chloromethane, and bromoethane appeared in our tables—but how are those names **constructed**? And back in Topic 1, we noticed something intriguing: the formula C₄H₁₀ could be drawn in **two different shapes** (a straight chain and a branched chain). Today we solve both puzzles: how to give every organic compound an unambiguous **international name** (so chemists in Tokyo, London, Dubai, and New York know the exact structure from the name alone), and how a single molecular formula can represent **multiple distinct compounds (isomers)**.

---

## TEA, EAT, and ATE

On Sunday afternoon, Mr. Davis and his niece Hannah were playing a word game over tea. Hannah wrote three words on a notepad: **TEA, EAT, ATE**. "Look, Uncle! All three words use the exact same three letters—T, E, and A—yet they mean three completely different things! That's an anagram." Mr. Davis clapped. "Spot on. And notice why: the **letters stay the same, but the order in which they are connected changes**, giving each word its own meaning."

A few minutes later, Hannah opened her chemistry notebook to two structural diagrams she had drawn in class, both labelled **C₄H₁₀**. "Uncle, look at this—my teacher said these are two different gases with different boiling points, yet both have the exact same formula: 4 carbons and 10 hydrogens!" Mr. Davis smiled. "That is chemistry's version of an anagram, Hannah! **Same atoms (letters), connected in a different order (structure)**, creating two different compounds—just like TEA and EAT."

Then he pointed out the window at their residential street, where a delivery driver was checking house numbers along a block of 6 houses. "Suppose a shop is at the 5th house counting from the west end of the block," Mr. Davis said. "If you count from the east end instead, that exact same shop is house number 2! Which number is simpler to use?" "The smaller number—2!" Hannah said. "That 'lowest possible number' rule is the exact rule chemists use when numbering carbon chains. Let's see how it works."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **IUPAC name** | The systematic international name assigned to a chemical compound so that every structure has one unique name worldwide | ethanol for CH₃CH₂OH |
| **Root (word root)** | The core part of an IUPAC name that tells how many carbon atoms are in the longest continuous chain | `meth-`, `eth-`, `prop-`, `but-` |
| **Suffix (ending)** | The ending of an IUPAC name that identifies the principal functional group or bond type | `-ane`, `-ene`, `-yne`, `-ol` |
| **Prefix** | The part placed in front of the root to name attached branches or halogen atoms and their positions | `2-methyl-`, `bromo-` |
| **Longest continuous chain** | The longest unbroken path of carbon-carbon bonds in a molecule (even if drawn with a bend) | 4 carbons in CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃ |
| **Branch (alkyl group)** | A side group attached to the main carbon chain | `–CH₃` (methyl) |
| **Methyl group** | The one-carbon branch `–CH₃` (formed by removing one H atom from methane) | branch in 2-methylpropane |
| **Isomers** | Compounds that have the **exact same molecular formula** but **different structural arrangements** of atoms | butane and 2-methylpropane |
| **Chain isomers** | Isomers that differ in the arrangement of their carbon skeleton (straight vs branched chain) | the two isomers of C₄H₁₀ |
| **Position isomers** | Isomers with the same carbon chain and functional group, but with the double bond or group at a **different position** | but-1-ene and but-2-ene |
| **Functional isomers** | Isomers that have the same molecular formula but **different functional groups** (belonging to different families) | ethanol and methoxymethane |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Compounds Mentioned in This Topic**
> - This topic is entirely about **names and structural diagrams on paper**. Students never prepare, sniff, taste, or burn any of these substances.
> - Short-chain alkanes, ethanol, propanol, propanone (acetone), and ethers (like methoxymethane) are **highly flammable**: keep all flames and sparks away from them. Methanal (formaldehyde, HCHO) is a **toxic gas** (and solution) whose vapour severely irritates the eyes and nose.

## Part A: The Three Parts of an IUPAC Name: Where, How Many, and Which Family

Every IUPAC name is built like a clear, logical label that answers three questions: **what branches are attached (and where), how many carbons are in the main chain, and what family does the compound belong to?** Look at Figure 3.1:

```
      2   -   methyl   +   but   +   ane
      I          I          I         I
   where?     which      how many   which
  (position)  branch?    carbons?   family?
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Anatomy of the name **2-methylbutane**: the locant number (`2-` tells which carbon carries the branch), the prefix (`methyl` names the –CH₃ branch), the root (`but-` means a 4-carbon main chain), and the suffix (`-ane` means all single C–C bonds). Written together as a single word: `2-methylbutane`.

### 1. The Word Root: Counting the Main Carbon Chain

| Carbon Count | Word Root | Alkane Name | Formula (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **meth-** | methane | CH₄ |
| 2 | **eth-** | ethane | C₂H₆ |
| 3 | **prop-** | propane | C₃H₈ |
| 4 | **but-** | butane | C₄H₁₀ |
| 5 | **pent-** | pentane | C₅H₁₂ |
| 6 | **hex-** | hexane | C₆H₁₄ |
| 7 | **hept-** | heptane | C₇H₁₆ |
| 8 | **oct-** | octane | C₈H₁₈ |
| 9 | **non-** | nonane | C₉H₂₀ |
| 10 | **dec-** | decane | C₁₀H₂₂ |

Once you memorise the first four (`meth-`, `eth-`, `prop-`, `but-`), the roots from 5 to 10 match familiar everyday geometry and sports words: **pent**agon (5 sides), **hex**agon (6), **hept**athlon (7 events), **oct**agon (8), **non**agon (9), and **dec**ade (10 years).

### 2. Suffixes and Prefixes: Naming the Family

| Family | Suffix / Prefix | Naming Pattern | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane (C–C single bonds) | `-ane` | root + `ane` | ethane (C₂H₆) |
| Alkene (C=C double bond) | `-ene` | root + `ene` | ethene (C₂H₄) |
| Alkyne (C≡C triple bond) | `-yne` | root + `yne` | ethyne (C₂H₂) |
| Alcohol (–OH) | `-ol` | alkane − `e` + `ol` | ethanol (C₂H₅OH) |
| Aldehyde (–CHO) | `-al` | alkane − `e` + `al` | methanal (HCHO), ethanal (CH₃CHO) |
| Ketone (>C=O) | `-one` | alkane − `e` + `one` | propanone (CH₃COCH₃) |
| Carboxylic acid (–COOH) | `-oic acid` | alkane − `e` + `oic acid` | methanoic acid (HCOOH), ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH) |
| Haloalkane (–Cl, –Br) | `chloro-`, `bromo-` (prefix) | prefix + alkane | chloromethane (CH₃Cl), bromoethane (C₂H₅Br) |

Keep two golden rules in mind:
1. **Drop the final "e" of the alkane name** whenever the suffix starts with a vowel (`-ol`, `-al`, `-one`, `-oic acid`): ethane + `-ol` → **ethanol** (not "ethaneol"); ethane + `-al` → **ethanal**; propane + `-one` → **propanone**; ethane + `-oic acid` → **ethanoic acid**.
2. **Always count the carbon atom inside –CHO or –COOH as part of the main carbon chain!** For example, CH₃COOH has **2 carbon atoms** in total (one in CH₃– and one in –COOH), so its root is **eth-**: **ethanoic acid** (never "methanoic").

### Example 1. Writing formulas from IUPAC names

**Problem:** Write the condensed formula for each name: (a) nonane, (b) propanoic acid, (c) bromoethane, (d) ethanal.

**Step 1:** (a) `non-` = 9 carbons, `-ane` = alkane: H = 2(9) + 2 = 20 → **C₉H₂₀**.
**Step 2:** (b) `prop-` = 3 carbons in total (including the –COOH carbon): **C₂H₅COOH** (or CH₃CH₂COOH, C₃H₆O₂).
**Step 3:** (c) `eth-` = 2 carbons, with one H replaced by Br: **C₂H₅Br** (or CH₃CH₂Br).
**Step 4:** (d) `eth-` = 2 carbons in total (including the –CHO carbon): **CH₃CHO** (C₂H₄O).

**Check:** (b) C₂H₅ (2 C, 5 H) + COOH (1 C, 1 H, 2 O) = 3 C, 6 H, 2 O ✅. (d) CH₃ (1 C, 3 H) + CHO (1 C, 1 H, 1 O) = 2 C, 4 H, 1 O ✅.

## Part B: Naming Branched Alkanes

In Topic 1, we saw that carbon chains can carry side branches. Naming a branched alkane takes just **three steps**, using our street-numbering rule (**always number from the end that gives the branch the smaller number**):

1. **Find the longest continuous carbon chain.** Trace along the carbon-carbon bonds from one end to the other (turning corners if needed); the path with the most carbons gives the **word root**.
2. **Number the main chain from 1 to *n*.** Try numbering from both ends and pick the direction that gives the attached branch the **lowest possible number (locant)**.
3. **Assemble the name:** write the position number, a hyphen, the branch name, and the parent alkane name as one word (e.g., **2-methylbutane**). A 1-carbon branch (–CH₃) is called **methyl** (methane with `-ane` changed to `-yl`). If two identical methyl branches are present, use the prefix **di-** and give both numbers separated by a comma (e.g., **2,2-dimethylpropane**).

### Example 2. Naming 2-methylpropane

**Problem:** Give the IUPAC name of the branched C₄H₁₀ structure shown in Figure 3.2.

```
              H
              I
          H - C - H
              I
      H       I       H
      I       I       I
  H - C ----- C ----- C - H
      I       I       I
      H       H       H

      1       2       3
```

> **Figure 3.2** – The branched isomer of C₄H₁₀. The central carbon is bonded to three carbon atoms and one H atom (4 bonds total); each of the three outer carbons is bonded to 3 H atoms and 1 C atom (4 bonds total), giving 4 C and 10 H. The longest continuous chain has 3 carbons, numbered 1, 2, 3.

**Step 1 (longest chain):** Tracing any continuous path from one end to another passes through at most **3 carbons** (never 4 in a row). So the parent chain is **propane**.
**Step 2 (branch and position):** The top –CH₃ group is a **methyl** branch attached to the middle carbon (**C2**, whether counted from left or right).
**Step 3 (name):** **2-methylpropane**.

**Check:** Total carbons = 3 (main chain) + 1 (methyl branch) = 4; total hydrogens = 10 → C₄H₁₀ ✅. And why can a compound **never** be named "1-methylpropane"? Because attaching a methyl group to C1 (an end carbon) simply lengthens the continuous chain from 3 carbons to 4 carbons—making it ordinary unbranched **butane**!

### Example 3. 2-methylbutane: Which end do we number from?

**Problem:** Name the C₅H₁₂ structure in Figure 3.3, showing why we number from the left end rather than the right end.

```
              H
              I
          H - C - H
              I
      H       I       H       H
      I       I       I       I
  H - C ----- C ----- C ----- C - H
      I       I       I       I
      H       H       H       H

      1       2       3       4    <-- numbering from the left (C2: smaller!)
      4       3       2       1    <-- numbering from the right (C3: larger)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – A branched C₅H₁₂ isomer. Numbering from the left places the methyl branch at **C2**; numbering from the right places it at **C3**. We choose the smaller number: 2.

**Step 1 (longest chain):** The horizontal chain has **4 carbons** → **butane**.
**Step 2 (branch):** One **methyl** (–CH₃) group.
**Step 3 (numbering):** Counting from the left gives **2**; counting from the right gives **3**. Following the lowest-number rule, we choose **2**.
**Step 4 (name):** **2-methylbutane** (never "3-methylbutane").

**Check:** 4 + 1 = 5 C, and 3 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 12 H → C₅H₁₂ ✅.

### Example 4. Drawing a structure from its name: 2,2-dimethylpropane

**Problem:** Draw the full structural formula of **2,2-dimethylpropane** and verify its molecular formula.

**Step 1:** `propane` means a 3-carbon main chain: C1–C2–C3.
**Step 2:** `2,2-dimethyl` means **two methyl (–CH₃) groups, both attached to C2**.
**Step 3:** Fill in hydrogens so every carbon has 4 bonds: the central carbon (C2) is already bonded to 4 carbons (C1, C3, and the two methyl carbons), so **C2 carries zero H atoms**. Each of the four outer carbons carries 3 H atoms.
**Step 4:** Count atoms: 5 C atoms and 4 × 3 = 12 H atoms → **C₅H₁₂**.

```
              H
              I
          H - C - H
              I
      H       I       H
      I       I       I
  H - C ----- C ----- C - H
      I       I       I
      H       I       H
              I
          H - C - H
              I
              H
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Structural formula of 2,2-dimethylpropane (C₅H₁₂). The central carbon is bonded to 4 carbon neighbours and 0 hydrogens; each of the four outer carbons is bonded to 1 carbon and 3 hydrogens.

**Check:** Formula is C₅H₁₂ ✅, and every carbon has 4 bonds ✅.

### Example 5. Numbering haloalkanes the same way

**Problem:** Give the IUPAC names of `CH₃–CHBr–CH₃` and `CH₃–CH₂–CH₂Br`.

**Step 1:** Both have a 3-carbon chain (`propane`) with a bromine substituent (`bromo-` prefix).
**Step 2:** In `CH₃–CHBr–CH₃`, the Br atom sits on the middle carbon (**C2** from either end): **2-bromopropane**.
**Step 3:** In `CH₃–CH₂–CH₂Br`, the Br atom sits on an end carbon (**C1** when numbered from the right end, vs C3 from the left): **1-bromopropane**.

**Check:** Both have the molecular formula C₃H₇Br, yet different IUPAC names because the bromine atom is attached at different positions ✅.

## Part C: Isomers: Same Letters, Different Words

Just like the anagrams **TEA, EAT, and ATE**, compounds that share the **exact same molecular formula** but have **different structural arrangements of atoms** are called **isomers** (and the phenomenon is called **isomerism**). We can create isomers in three main ways: by changing the **carbon skeleton** (chain isomers), by shifting the **position** of a double bond or functional group (position isomers), or by changing the **functional group itself** (functional isomers). Let's start with **chain isomers**.

### The Two Chain Isomers of Butane (C₄H₁₀)

Compare straight-chain **butane** (Figure 3.5) with branched **2-methylpropane** (Figure 3.2):

```
      H   H   H   H
      I   I   I   I
  H - C - C - C - C - H
      I   I   I   I
      H   H   H   H
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Straight-chain butane (C₄H₁₀): 4 carbons in a single continuous row, with 10 hydrogens.

**How do their connections differ?** In butane, no carbon atom is bonded to more than two other carbons; in 2-methylpropane, the central carbon is bonded to **three** other carbons. Same molecular formula (C₄H₁₀), same molar mass (58 g/mol), different carbon skeletons—making them **chain isomers** with different boiling points (−1 °C vs −12 °C). Methane (CH₄), ethane (C₂H₆), and propane (C₃H₈) have **no chain isomers** because 1, 2, or 3 carbons can only be connected in a single continuous chain.

### Example 6. Finding all chain isomers of pentane (C₅H₁₂) systematically

To find every chain isomer without missing any or counting duplicates, **start with the longest possible chain and shorten it by one carbon at a time**:

**Problem:** How many chain isomers does C₅H₁₂ have? Draw and name each one.

- **Step 1 (5-carbon main chain):** Place all 5 carbons in a continuous row → **pentane** (Figure 3.6).
- **Step 2 (4-carbon main chain + 1 methyl branch):** Shorten the main chain to 4 carbons (butane) and attach the 5th carbon as a methyl (–CH₃) branch. It cannot go on C1 or C4 (the ends), because that would just make a 5-carbon chain again. Placing it on C2 gives **2-methylbutane** (Figure 3.3). (Placing it on C3 is identical to C2 counted from the other end.)
- **Step 3 (3-carbon main chain + 2 methyl branches):** Shorten the main chain to 3 carbons (propane) and attach the remaining 2 carbons as two methyl branches on the middle carbon (C2) → **2,2-dimethylpropane** (Figure 3.4).
- **Step 4 (2-carbon chain?):** Impossible, because any branch on a 2-carbon chain is on an end carbon and lengthens the chain.

```
      H   H   H   H   H
      I   I   I   I   I
  H - C - C - C - C - C - H
      I   I   I   I   I
      H   H   H   H   H
```

> **Figure 3.6** – Straight-chain pentane (C₅H₁₂): 5 carbons in a continuous chain and 12 hydrogens.

| Longest Chain | Branches | IUPAC Name | Condensed Formula |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 carbons | none | **pentane** | `CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃` |
| 4 carbons | one methyl at C2 | **2-methylbutane** | `CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃` |
| 3 carbons | two methyls at C2 | **2,2-dimethylpropane** | `CH₃–C(CH₃)₂–CH₃` |

**Check:** All three have the molecular formula C₅H₁₂ and molar mass **72 g/mol** ✅. Thus, C₅H₁₂ has **exactly 3 chain isomers**. (As carbon count grows, the number of isomers skyrockets: C₄H₁₀ has 2, C₅H₁₂ has 3, C₆H₁₄ has 5, and C₁₀H₂₂ has 75!)

## Part D: Where the Double Bond or –OH Sits: Position Isomers

When a molecule contains a **C=C double bond**, a **C≡C triple bond**, or a functional group like **–OH**, we number the main carbon chain **from whichever end gives the double bond or –OH group the lowest possible number**, and place that number right before the suffix (e.g., **but-1-ene**, **propan-2-ol**).

### Example 7. Two position isomers of straight-chain butene (C₄H₈)

**Problem:** In a straight 4-carbon chain, a C=C double bond can sit in two different positions. Name both isomers shown in Figure 3.7.

```
      H   H   H   H
      I   I   I   I
      C = C - C - C - H
      I       I   I
      H       H   H

      H   H   H   H
      I   I   I   I
  H - C - C = C - C - H
      I           I
      H           H
```

> **Figure 3.7** – Top: `CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃` (double bond between C1 and C2). Bottom: `CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃` (double bond between C2 and C3). Both have 4 carbons and 8 hydrogens (C₄H₈), with 4 bonds on every carbon.

**Step 1:** Both have a 4-carbon chain (`but-`) and one double bond (`-ene`).
**Step 2 (top structure):** Numbering from the left end puts the double bond between C1 and C2 (we use the smaller number of the two carbons in the double bond: **1**) → **but-1-ene**.
**Step 3 (bottom structure):** The double bond sits between C2 and C3 (number **2** from either end) → **but-2-ene**.

**Check:** Both have the formula C₄H₈ ✅. Because the carbon chain is the same (4 carbons in a row) and only the **position** of the double bond differs, they are **position isomers**. Note: if someone writes `CH₃–CH₂–CH=CH₂`, numbering from the right end still puts the double bond at C1—so it is still **but-1-ene** (just drawn flipped left-to-right)!

### Example 8. Two position isomers of propanol (C₃H₇OH)

**Problem:** In a 3-carbon alcohol (C₃H₇OH), the –OH group can attach to two different carbons. Name both isomers in Figure 3.8.

```
      H   H   H
      I   I   I
  H - C - C - C - O - H
      I   I   I
      H   H   H


          O - H
          I
      H   I   H
      I   I   I
  H - C - C - C - H
      I   I   I
      H   H   H
```

> **Figure 3.8** – Top: `CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–OH` (–OH on an end carbon, C1). Bottom: `CH₃–CH(OH)–CH₃` (–OH on the middle carbon, C2). Both have 3 C, 8 H, and 1 O (C₃H₈O).

**Step 1:** Three-carbon chain (`prop-`) + alcohol (`-ol`).
**Step 2 (top):** –OH is on C1 (numbering from the right end) → **propan-1-ol**.
**Step 3 (bottom):** –OH is on the middle carbon, C2 → **propan-2-ol**.

**Check:** Both have the formula C₃H₈O and molar mass **60 g/mol** ✅. Why don't methanol (CH₃OH) or ethanol (C₂H₅OH) need a position number? Because on a 1-carbon or 2-carbon chain, every carbon is an end carbon (C1), so there is only one possible position!

## Part E: Functional Isomers: A Different Family Altogether

In our third type of isomerism, the atoms rearrange to form a **completely different functional group**. The classic example is the molecular formula **C₂H₆O**:

```
      H   H                 H       H
      I   I                 I       I
  H - C - C - O - H     H - C - O - C - H
      I   I                 I       I
      H   H                 H       H

    ethanol               methoxymethane
```

> **Figure 3.9** – Two functional isomers of C₂H₆O. Left: **ethanol** (`CH₃CH₂OH`), an **alcohol** with an `–OH` group. Right: **methoxymethane** (`CH₃–O–CH₃`, dimethyl ether), an **ether** in which the oxygen atom sits bridged between two carbons (`C–O–C`). Both have 2 C, 6 H, and 1 O, with 4 bonds on every C, 2 on O, and 1 on every H.

Because their functional groups belong to different families (alcohol vs ether), their properties are dramatically different (we will see this again in Topic 6). Here are all **three types of structural isomers** side by side:

| Type of Isomerism | What Changes Between the Isomers | Classic Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Chain isomerism** | The carbon skeleton (straight vs branched chain) | butane and 2-methylpropane (C₄H₁₀) |
| **Position isomerism** | The position of the double/triple bond or functional group on the same carbon chain | but-1-ene and but-2-ene (C₄H₈); propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol (C₃H₈O) |
| **Functional isomerism** | The functional group itself (different chemical families) | ethanol (`CH₃CH₂OH`) and methoxymethane (`CH₃OCH₃`) (C₂H₆O) |

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "A carbon chain must be drawn in a straight horizontal line—if a chain is drawn bent into an L-shape or U-shape, it becomes a new branched isomer."**

Hannah drew two diagrams of C₅H₁₂ in her notebook (Figure 3.10): one in a straight horizontal line, and one with the right end bent downward at a 90° corner. "Uncle, these look different on the page! Does that mean C₅H₁₂ has a fourth isomer?" Mr. Davis said, "Let's test them: put your finger on one end carbon and trace along the continuous chain to the other end."

```
  CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH₂-CH₃            CH₃-CH₂-CH₂
                                         I
                                         CH₂
                                         I
                                         CH₃
```

> **Figure 3.10** – Two drawings of the exact same molecule, **pentane** (C₅H₁₂). Left: drawn in a straight horizontal line. Right: the exact same continuous 5-carbon chain drawn with a bend at C3.

Trace your finger from C1 → C2 → C3 → C4 → C5 in both drawings: both are a **single continuous, unbranched chain of 5 carbons**! Single C–C bonds can rotate freely in 3D space (just like bending a beaded necklace into an L-shape doesn't change the necklace). What defines a molecule is **which atom is bonded to which**, not whether it is drawn straight or bent on paper. Both drawings in Figure 3.10 are ordinary **pentane**.

The flip side of this trap appears in Figure 3.11:

```
  CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH₂             CH₃-CH-CH₃
              I                   I
              CH₃                 CH₂
                                  I
                                  CH₃
```

> **Figure 3.11** – Left: a –CH₃ drawn hanging down at the end of a chain is **not** a branch—tracing continuously gives 5 carbons in a row (**pentane**). Right: although the horizontal row has only 3 carbons, tracing from the left CH₃ → middle CH → down to CH₂ → bottom CH₃ gives a **4-carbon longest chain** with a methyl branch at C2 (**2-methylbutane**).

**Rule to remember:** Never judge a chain by how straight it looks on the page. **Trace every continuous path with your finger to find the true longest carbon chain**, and only count groups sticking off the middle of that longest path as branches!

## Quick Recap

- **IUPAC name structure:** `position-branch + word root + suffix` (e.g., `2-methylbutane`). Word roots from 1 to 10 carbons: `meth-`, `eth-`, `prop-`, `but-`, `pent-`, `hex-`, `hept-`, `oct-`, `non-`, `dec-`.
- **Suffixes:** `-ane` (alkane), `-ene` (alkene, C=C), `-yne` (alkyne, C≡C), `-ol` (alcohol, –OH), `-al` (aldehyde, –CHO), `-one` (ketone, >C=O), `-oic acid` (carboxylic acid, –COOH). **Prefixes:** `chloro-`, `bromo-`, `methyl-`. Drop the final `e` of the alkane name before a vowel suffix (`ethanol`, `ethanal`, `propanone`, `ethanoic acid`).
- **Naming branched alkanes:** (1) find the longest continuous carbon chain (even if bent), (2) number from the end that gives the branch the **lowest possible number**, (3) combine as `number-branch + alkane` (use `di-` for two identical branches, e.g., `2,2-dimethylpropane`). A methyl branch can never be at C1.
- **Isomers:** compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures. **Chain isomers:** C₄H₁₀ has 2 (`butane`, `2-methylpropane`); C₅H₁₂ has 3 (`pentane`, `2-methylbutane`, `2,2-dimethylpropane`). **Position isomers:** `but-1-ene` and `but-2-ene`; `propan-1-ol` and `propan-2-ol`. **Functional isomers:** `ethanol` and `methoxymethane` (C₂H₆O).
- **Pitfall to avoid:** bending a chain on paper does not make a new isomer—only changing which carbon is bonded to which creates a new isomer.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are for written work on paper. Atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

1. Hexane has a straight chain of 6 carbon atoms. Write its molecular formula and its condensed formula, and say how many CH₃ groups and how many CH₂ groups it has.
2. Name the compound `CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃` by the numbering rule. Show the position of the methyl branch when numbered from the left end and from the right end.
3. Draw the full structural formula of 3-methylpentane (C₆H₁₄) and show that every carbon has 4 bonds and every hydrogen has 1.
4. (a) A student calls the compound of Question 2 "4-methylpentane". What is wrong? (b) Another student writes "1-methylpentane". Explain why this name can never be correct, and say which compound it really is.
5. Find all the chain isomers of C₆H₁₄ and name each one. (Hint: start from the longest chain and shorten it one carbon at a time.)
6. For each pair, say "same compound", "isomers", or "neither", with a reason: (a) hexane and a 6-carbon chain drawn with a bend in the middle; (b) 2-methylpentane and 3-methylpentane; (c) 2-methylpentane and 2-methylbutane; (d) hexane and 2-methylpentane.
7. A chemist writes three structures of C₄H₈ with a double bond: (a) `CH₃–CH₂–CH=CH₂`, (b) `CH₂=CH–CH₂–CH₃`, (c) `CH₃–CH=CH–CH₃`. Name each, and say how many different compounds there are.
8. A sample has 28 g of C₄H₈. Find the number of moles and the masses of carbon and hydrogen in it. Would 28 g of the other position isomer give the same answers? Why?
9. Methoxyethane has the structure `CH₃–O–CH₂–CH₃`. Show that its formula is C₃H₈O and check the valency of each C and O. Is methoxyethane a functional or a position isomer of propan-1-ol and of propan-2-ol? Which pair among these three compounds is of the position kind?
10. Find the number of moles in 15 g of C₃H₈O, and the masses of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen in it. Are the answers the same for propan-1-ol, propan-2-ol, and methoxyethane?
11. For a straight chain of six carbon atoms, write the name and molecular formula of (a) the alkene with its double bond between C2 and C3, (b) the alcohol with –OH on the end carbon, (c) the alkyne with its triple bond at the first carbon.
12. A compound has the formula C₆H₁₄, only methyl branches, and its longest chain has exactly 4 carbon atoms. (A branch on an end carbon would lengthen the chain, so it is not allowed.) How many methyl branches does it have, and how many such compounds exist? Name them.
13. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Isomers always have the same molar mass. (b) A 6-carbon chain drawn with a bend is a new isomer of hexane. (c) Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol are functional isomers. (d) Methoxyethane and propan-1-ol are position isomers. (e) The ending `-ol` shows that a compound is an alcohol.
14. On a street, the houses are numbered 1 to 8 from the west end. A bakery stands at house number 6 from the west end. What number does the bakery get from the east end, and which number will the postman use if he follows the "lowest number" rule? How does this help in numbering a methyl branch on the fifth carbon of a six-carbon chain counted from one end?

### Answers

1. For *n* = 6: H = 2(6) + 2 = 14 → **C₆H₁₄**. Condensed formula: **`CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃`**. It has **2 CH₃ groups** (the two ends) and **4 CH₂ groups** (in the middle). Check: H = (2 × 3) + (4 × 2) = 6 + 8 = 14 ✅.
2. The longest continuous chain has 5 carbons (**pentane**). Numbering from the left end puts the methyl branch at **C2**; numbering from the right end puts it at **C4**. Choosing the smaller number (2) gives **2-methylpentane**. Check: C = 5 + 1 = 6, H = 3 + 1 + 3 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 14 → C₆H₁₄ ✅.
3. Main chain of 5 carbons with a methyl branch on the middle carbon (C3):

```
                      H
                      I
                  H - C - H
                      I
      H       H       I       H       H
      I       I       I       I       I
  H - C ----- C ----- C ----- C ----- C - H
      I       I       I       I       I
      H       H       H       H       H
```

> **Figure 3.12** – Structural formula of 3-methylpentane (C₆H₁₄). C3 has 4 bonds (two chain carbons, one methyl carbon, one H); all other carbons have 4 bonds and every H has 1 bond. Total H = 3 (methyl) + 3 + 2 + 1 + 2 + 3 = **14** ✅.

4. (a) Numbering from the left gives C2, whereas numbering from the right gives C4. By the lowest-number rule, "4-methylpentane" is wrong; the correct name is **2-methylpentane**. (b) A methyl group attached to C1 (an end carbon) simply extends the continuous chain from 5 carbons to 6 carbons, so the molecule is actually unbranched **hexane**.
5. Systematic list by longest chain: (i) 6-carbon chain: **hexane** (`CH₃–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃`). (ii) 5-carbon chain + 1 methyl branch: **2-methylpentane** (`CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₂–CH₃`) and **3-methylpentane** (`CH₃–CH₂–CH(CH₃)–CH₂–CH₃`). (iii) 4-carbon chain + 2 methyl branches: **2,2-dimethylbutane** (`CH₃–C(CH₃)₂–CH₂–CH₃`) and **2,3-dimethylbutane** (`CH₃–CH(CH₃)–CH(CH₃)–CH₃`). Total = **5 isomers**. Check: all five have 6 C and 14 H ✅.
6. (a) **Same compound**: bending a chain on paper does not change connectivity (both are hexane). (b) **Isomers**: both are C₆H₁₄, with the methyl branch at C2 vs C3. (c) **Neither**: 2-methylpentane is C₆H₁₄ while 2-methylbutane is C₅H₁₂ (different molecular formulas). (d) **Isomers**: both are C₆H₁₄ (straight chain vs branched chain).
7. (a) Numbering from the right end puts the double bond at C1: **but-1-ene**. (b) Numbering from the left end puts the double bond at C1: **but-1-ene**. (c) Double bond between C2 and C3: **but-2-ene**. Since (a) and (b) are the exact same molecule drawn in opposite directions, there are **2 different compounds**.
8. Molar mass of C₄H₈ = (4 × 12) + 8 = **56** g/mol. Moles = 28 ÷ 56 = **0.5 mol**. Mass of carbon = 0.5 × 48 = **24 g**; mass of hydrogen = 0.5 × 8 = **4 g**. Check: 24 + 4 = 28 g ✅. Yes, 28 g of the other position isomer gives the **exact same answers**, because both isomers share the identical molecular formula C₄H₈ and molar mass 56 g/mol.
9. Atom count: 3 C, 3 + 2 + 3 = 8 H, 1 O → **C₃H₈O** ✅. Valencies: left CH₃ has 3 H + 1 O = 4; CH₂ has 2 H + 1 O + 1 C = 4; right CH₃ has 3 H + 1 C = 4; O has 2 bonds (one to CH₃, one to CH₂) ✅. Methoxyethane (`–O–` ether link) is a **functional isomer** of both propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol (`–OH` alcohol group). **Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol** are **position isomers** of each other.
10. Molar mass of C₃H₈O = 36 + 8 + 16 = **60** g/mol. Moles = 15 ÷ 60 = **0.25 mol**. Carbon = 0.25 × 36 = **9 g**, hydrogen = 0.25 × 8 = **2 g**, oxygen = 0.25 × 16 = **4 g**. Check: 9 + 2 + 4 = 15 g ✅. **Yes**, the answers are identical for all three isomers because all three have the same molecular formula C₃H₈O.
11. (a) **hex-2-ene**, C₆H₁₂ (fits CₙH₂ₙ, *n* = 6). (b) **hexan-1-ol**, C₆H₁₃OH (fits CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH). (c) **hex-1-yne**, C₆H₁₀ (fits CₙH₂ₙ₋₂). Check: 2(6) = 12 ✅; 2(6) + 1 = 13 ✅; 2(6) − 2 = 10 ✅.
12. With 4 carbons in the main chain, the remaining 6 − 4 = **2 carbons** form **2 methyl branches** (placed on C2 or C3). Placing both on C2 gives **2,2-dimethylbutane**; placing one on C2 and one on C3 gives **2,3-dimethylbutane** (placing both on C3 is identical to 2,2-dimethylbutane numbered from the other end). Thus there are **2 such compounds**.
13. (a) **True** ✅: isomers share the same molecular formula and therefore the same molar mass. (b) **False** ❌: bending a chain does not change connectivity; it is still hexane. (c) **False** ❌: they are **position isomers** (differing only in the position of –OH). (d) **False** ❌: they are **functional isomers** (ether vs alcohol). (e) **True** ✅.
14. Counting from the east end, the bakery is at house number 8 + 1 − 6 = **3**. Following the lowest-number rule, the postman uses **3**. Similarly, a methyl branch on the 5th carbon from one end of a 6-carbon chain is at carbon **2** when counted from the other end—so we number from the nearer end and call it `2-methylhexane`.
