# Topic 4: Alkanes: From Methane to Octane

In Topic 2, we visualised a homologous series as a molecular "metro train," and in Topic 3, we mastered IUPAC rules and isomerism. Now we step inside the very **first and most fundamental family** of organic compounds: **alkanes**, where every single bond between carbon atoms is a **single covalent bond**. These are the primary hydrocarbon gases burning in domestic cookstoves, powering compressed natural gas (CNG) buses, and forming the bulk of automotive gasoline (petrol). Today we investigate how alkanes are structured, how they combust cleanly (and why they sometimes burn dangerously), and the exact chemical reactions they undergo.

## The Biogas Cookstove

On a bright Saturday morning, Uncle Marcus took his nephew Leo to visit a community-run organic farm on the city outskirts. Near the rear garden, Leo noticed an underground concrete dome with a large green metal hatch and a thick yellow delivery pipe running directly into the community kitchen. 

"Uncle Marcus, what is that dome?" Leo asked, peering at the structure. 

"That is an anaerobic **biogas digester**," Uncle Marcus replied. "Every morning, organic food scraps, dairy manure, and water are fed into the sealed underground chamber. Because no atmospheric oxygen can enter, anaerobic micro-organisms break down the complex organic matter. That decomposition generates a flammable gas mixture that collects under the dome."

At noon, the cook ignited the kitchen burner. Leo watched closely: the flame burned with a steady, translucent **blue cone**, leaving no smoky haze and keeping the bottom of the stainless steel soup pot completely spotless. Leo remembered his own stove at home: "Uncle, last week our stove at home developed a lazy yellow flame, and the cooking pots turned completely black with soot underneath. Why is this biogas flame so crisp and blue?"

Uncle Marcus smiled. "You have posed two great questions at once: what gas is feeding this burner, and what determines whether a flame burns blue or smoky yellow? Both answers lie within the chemistry of the simplest hydrocarbon family: the **alkanes**."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alkane** | A saturated hydrocarbon containing only single covalent bonds between carbon atoms; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | CH₄, C₂H₆, C₈H₁₈ |
| **Saturated hydrocarbon** | A carbon compound in which all available carbon valencies are occupied by single covalent bonds, leaving no spare capacity for addition | methane, propane, butane |
| **Biogas** | A combustible gas mixture generated by the anaerobic microbial breakdown of organic waste, consisting predominantly of methane | farm digester gas (~60% CH₄) |
| **LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas)** | A combustible mixture of propane and butane compressed into liquid form inside portable metal cylinders | domestic cooking gas |
| **Complete combustion** | Rapid oxidation of a fuel in abundant oxygen, converting all carbon to CO₂ and hydrogen to H₂O | blue, non-luminous gas flame |
| **Incomplete combustion** | Burning in a limited supply of oxygen, producing toxic carbon monoxide (CO) and incandescent carbon particles (soot) | yellow, smoky flame |
| **Soot** | Fine, unburned powdery carbon particles produced during incomplete combustion | black residue on cookware |
| **Substitution reaction** | A reaction in which one atom or group of atoms in a saturated molecule is replaced by another atom without altering the carbon chain skeleton | chlorination of methane to CH₃Cl |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Flammable Hydrocarbon Gases**
> - Methane, propane, butane, and biogas are **highly flammable gases**. Mixed with atmospheric air in specific proportions, a single electrical spark or open pilot flame can trigger a catastrophic **gas explosion**. Students must never handle fuel cylinders, digesters, or compressed gas lines.
> - All chemical equations and reaction mechanisms in this topic are presented **strictly for theoretical study on paper**.
> - **Carbon monoxide (CO)** is a colourless, odourless, and lethal toxic gas. Incomplete combustion inside closed, unventilated rooms causes fatal asphyxiation.
> - **Chlorine gas** is a potent respiratory poison; its photochemical reaction with methane must only be studied theoretically on paper.
> - **Immediate Protocol if You Detect a Gas Leak:** Open all doors and windows immediately to ventilate the space. **Do not operate any electrical switches, fans, or appliances.** Do not strike matches or lighters. Close the master gas valve/regulator if safe to do so, evacuate everyone outside, and call the gas utility emergency number.

## Part A: Alkanes: All Single Bonds

An **alkane** is an open-chain hydrocarbon in which every carbon atom forms four single covalent bonds, sharing one pair of electrons with an adjacent carbon atom or a hydrogen atom. Because all four bonding positions of every carbon atom are fully occupied, alkanes are termed **saturated hydrocarbons**. They possess the universal general formula **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂**.

Figure 4.1 shows the full structural formulas of the first three members. Count the bonds around each carbon atom: exactly **four**; count the bonds around each hydrogen atom: exactly **one**.

```
        H              H   H              H   H   H
        |              |   |              |   |   |
    H - C - H      H - C - C - H      H - C - C - C - H
        |              |   |              |   |   |
        H              H   H              H   H   H
     methane          ethane              propane
       CH₄             C₂H₆                 C₃H₈
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The first three alkanes. Methane (CH₄): 1 carbon bonded to 4 hydrogen atoms via 4 single covalent bonds. Ethane (C₂H₆): 2 carbons linked by 1 C–C single bond, each carbon carrying 3 hydrogen atoms. Propane (C₃H₈): 3 carbons in a chain; the central carbon bonds to 2 carbons and 2 hydrogens, while the two terminal carbons bond to 1 carbon and 3 hydrogens.

As the carbon chain lengthens, the molecule can branch. Butane (C₄H₁₀) can arrange its four carbon atoms into either a continuous four-carbon chain or a branched three-carbon chain with a methyl branch, forming two structural isomers (introduced in Topic 3):

```
      H   H   H   H                    H
      |   |   |   |                    |
  H - C - C - C - C - H            H - C - H
      |   |   |   |                    |
      H   H   H   H            H       |       H
                               |       |       |
     butane                H - C ----- C ----- C - H
  (straight chain)             |       |       |
                               H       H       H
                           2-methylpropane
                           (branched chain)
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Straight-chain butane and branched 2-methylpropane: both share the identical molecular formula C₄H₁₀ (4 carbons, 10 hydrogens). In straight-chain butane, the two internal carbons form 2 C–C bonds and 2 C–H bonds; in 2-methylpropane, the central carbon forms 3 C–C bonds and 1 C–H bond.

Because every bond in an alkane is a single covalent bond, we can deduce an exact mathematical relationship for the total number of covalent bonds present in any open-chain alkane.

### Example 1. Counting Bonds in C₈H₁₈

**Problem:** How many C–C single bonds, C–H single bonds, and total covalent bonds exist in an unbranched molecule of octane, C₈H₁₈?

**Step 1:** In an unbranched chain of 8 carbon atoms, the number of C–C links connecting them is $n - 1 = 8 - 1 =$ **7 C–C bonds**.  
**Step 2:** Each of the 18 hydrogen atoms forms exactly one single bond with carbon: **18 C–H bonds**.  
**Step 3:** Total single covalent bonds = $7 + 18 =$ **25 bonds**.  

**Verification:** Carbon provides 4 valence electrons ($8 \times 4 = 32$) and hydrogen provides 1 valence electron ($18 \times 1 = 18$), giving 50 shared valence electrons. Since each single covalent bond consists of 2 shared electrons, the number of bonds is $50 \div 2 = 25$ ✅.  
*(General formula: Total covalent bonds in any alkane CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ equals $(n - 1) + (2n + 2) = 3n + 1$. For $n = 8$: $3(8) + 1 = 25$ ✅.)*

### Example 2. Molecular Mass Across the Series

**Problem:** Calculate the molecular mass from CH₄ to C₈H₁₈ and demonstrate that each successive member increases by exactly 14 u. (Atomic masses: H = 1 u, C = 12 u.)

The molecular mass of CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ is $12n + (2n + 2) \times 1 = \mathbf{14n + 2}$ u.

| Alkane | Carbon count ($n$) | Molecular Formula | Molecular Mass (u) | Stepwise Increase |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Methane | 1 | CH₄ | 16 | — |
| Ethane | 2 | C₂H₆ | 30 | +14 |
| Propane | 3 | C₃H₈ | 44 | +14 |
| Butane | 4 | C₄H₁₀ | 58 | +14 |
| Pentane | 5 | C₅H₁₂ | 72 | +14 |
| Hexane | 6 | C₆H₁₄ | 86 | +14 |
| Heptane | 7 | C₇H₁₆ | 100 | +14 |
| Octane | 8 | C₈H₁₈ | 114 | +14 |

**Verification:** For octane, $8 \times 12 + 18 \times 1 = 96 + 18 = 114$ u ✅; via formula, $14(8) + 2 = 114$ u ✅. Every successive step adds one methylene unit (–CH₂–), whose mass is $12 + 2(1) = 14$ u.

## Part B: Where Do Methane and LPG Come From?

The clean fuel powering Uncle Marcus's farm kitchen is **methane (CH₄)**. Commercially and naturally, alkanes are sourced from three principal reservoirs:

1. **Biogas:** Produced inside airtight digesters where anaerobic bacteria ferment agricultural waste, food scraps, or animal manure. The resulting raw biogas consists of approximately **55% to 65% methane**, with the remainder being primarily carbon dioxide (CO₂) and traces of moisture and hydrogen sulphide. Because methane is significantly lighter than air, it naturally migrates to the collection dome.
2. **Natural Gas:** Trapped deep within subterranean geological rock formations, often above petroleum crude oil deposits. Raw natural gas is 85% to 95% methane. When compressed under high pressure (200–250 bar), it is distributed as **CNG (Compressed Natural Gas)**, a clean-burning vehicular fuel.
3. **Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG):** Extracted during the fractional distillation of crude petroleum and natural gas processing. LPG consists predominantly of **propane (C₃H₈) and butane (C₄H₁₀)**. Under moderate pressure (5–10 bar) at ambient temperature, these gases liquefy, allowing large volumes of energy to be stored inside portable steel cylinders.

```
                                               Gas delivery pipe
                                            .----------------> Cookstove
                                            |                 (blue flame)
                                         .--+--.
     Ground level   ____________________/  Gas  \_________________________
                                       : (CH₄ +  :
     Organic waste                     :   CO₂)  :       Spent slurry
     + water                           :         :       (organic bio-fertiliser)
     ---> [ Inlet ]-----+              :         :  +------[ Outlet ]---->
                        |     +--------+---------+--+--+
                        |     :  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  :
                        +-----:     Anaerobic slurry    :
                              :     (fermenting organic :
                              :      matter + bacteria) :
                              +-------------------------+
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Cross-section of a fixed-dome biogas plant. Organic feed enters via the inlet pipe into the airtight underground digestion pit. In the absence of atmospheric oxygen, anaerobic bacteria decompose the organic matter, liberating methane and carbon dioxide. The light gas rises and accumulates under the masonry dome, forcing the digested slurry out through the overflow outlet as rich fertiliser.

### A Laboratory Preparation of Methane (For Reading Only)

In a school laboratory setting, methane can theoretically be prepared by heating dry anhydrous **sodium ethanoate (CH₃COONa)** with **soda lime** (an intimate mixture of sodium hydroxide, NaOH, and calcium oxide, CaO). Calcium oxide keeps the sodium hydroxide dry and prevents it from melting and attacking the glass test tube.

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{NaOH} \xrightarrow{\text{CaO, heat}} \text{CH}_4 \uparrow + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$$

**Atom Balance Check:**  
Reactants: C = 2, H = $3 + 1 = 4$, Na = $1 + 1 = 2$, O = $2 + 1 = 3$.  
Products: C = $1 + 1 = 2$, H = 4, Na = 2, O = 3 ✅.  
*(This reaction is called a **decarboxylation** reaction because a carboxyl group is removed as a carbonate salt.)*

## Part C: Physical Properties: Longer Chains, Higher Boiling Points

In Topic 2, we observed that boiling points rise systematically across a homologous series. Why does this happen, and what dictates the physical state of an alkane at room temperature?

Alkanes are **non-polar molecules**. The electronegativity difference between carbon (2.5) and hydrogen (2.1) is a modest 0.4, making C–H bonds virtually non-polar. Moreover, the symmetrical tetrahedral geometry of alkanes cancels out any minor local dipoles. According to the principle of "like dissolves like":
- Alkanes are **insoluble in water** (which is strongly polar and hydrogen-bonded).
- Alkanes dissolve readily in non-polar organic solvents (such as benzene or ether).
- Liquid alkanes are less dense than water ($\approx 0.65\text{ to }0.75\text{ g/cm}^3$) and float as an immiscible layer on top of water.

Between non-polar alkane molecules, the only intermolecular forces are weak **London dispersion forces**. As the carbon chain grows longer, the molecular surface area increases, creating stronger temporary dipoles. Consequently, more thermal kinetic energy is required to separate the molecules into the vapour phase, driving boiling points steadily upward:

| Alkane | Molecular Formula | Boiling Point (°C) | Physical State at 20 °C (1 atm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | −162 | Gas |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | −89 | Gas |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | −42 | Gas |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | −1 | Gas |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | +36 | Volatile Liquid |
| Hexane | C₆H₁₄ | +69 | Liquid |

The first four alkanes (C₁ to C₄) are gases at ordinary temperatures; C₅ to C₁₇ are liquids (such as octane in gasoline); while alkanes with 18 or more carbon atoms are waxy solids (such as paraffin wax).

```
   Boiling point relative to methane (−162 °C baseline): each '#' ≈ 10 °C
   
   CH₄     #                   (0 °C above baseline: −162 °C)
   C₂H₆    #######             (+73 °C rise)
   C₃H₈    ############        (+120 °C rise)
   C₄H₁₀   ################    (+161 °C rise)
   C₅H₁₂   #################### (+198 °C rise)
   C₆H₁₄   ####################### (+231 °C rise)
```

> **Figure 4.4** – Progression of alkane boiling points relative to methane (−162 °C baseline). As the carbon chain lengthens, the boiling point rises steadily, but the incremental gain per additional –CH₂– group diminishes as molecular mass becomes large.

### Example 3. How Much Does Boiling Point Rise with Each Step?

**Problem:** Using the boiling point data in the table, calculate the temperature increase at each successive step from methane to hexane.

- CH₄ $\rightarrow$ C₂H₆: $-89 - (-162) =$ **+73 °C**
- C₂H₆ $\rightarrow$ C₃H₈: $-42 - (-89) =$ **+47 °C**
- C₃H₈ $\rightarrow$ C₄H₁₀: $-1 - (-42) =$ **+41 °C**
- C₄H₁₀ $\rightarrow$ C₅H₁₂: $+36 - (-1) =$ **+37 °C**
- C₅H₁₂ $\rightarrow$ C₆H₁₄: $+69 - (+36) =$ **+33 °C**

**Total Check:** $73 + 47 + 41 + 37 + 33 = 231$ °C. Overall difference: $69 - (-162) = 231$ °C ✅. Notice that while every step adds an identical –CH₂– unit, the initial percentage change in molecular surface area is enormous (methane to ethane doubles the carbon count), whereas subsequent additions produce progressively smaller fractional changes.

### Example 4. Why Is LPG Stored as a Liquid in Cylinders?

**Problem:** On a freezing sub-zero winter morning, the ambient temperature outdoors is −5 °C. At standard atmospheric pressure (1 atm), would butane (b.p. −1 °C) and propane (b.p. −42 °C) exist as gases or liquids? Explain why LPG cylinders function reliably.

- For **butane**: the ambient temperature (−5 °C) is **below** its normal boiling point (−1 °C). At atmospheric pressure, pure butane condenses into a **liquid**.
- For **propane**: the ambient temperature (−5 °C) is **above** its normal boiling point (−42 °C). Propane remains a **gas** at 1 atm.

Inside an LPG cylinder, the fuel is kept under several atmospheres of pressure, forcing both butane and propane into the liquid state. When the cylinder valve opens, the reduction in pressure allows the liquid to boil into gas instantly, supplying the cookstove. In cold climates, LPG mixtures are enriched with more propane so the fuel continues to vaporise freely even in sub-zero weather.

## Part D: Combustion: Complete and Incomplete

The single most economically vital reaction of alkanes is **combustion**—exothermic oxidation in air that releases vast quantities of heat energy:

In an **abundant supply of oxygen**, alkanes undergo **complete combustion**, oxidising completely to carbon dioxide and water vapour:

$$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}$$
$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}$$
$$2\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18} + 25\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 16\text{CO}_2 + 18\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}$$

**Atom Check (Octane):**  
Reactants: C = $2 \times 8 = 16$; H = $2 \times 18 = 36$; O = $25 \times 2 = 50$.  
Products: C = 16; H = $18 \times 2 = 36$; O = $16 \times 2 + 18 = 50$ ✅.

If the oxygen supply is **restricted or insufficient**, alkanes undergo **incomplete combustion**, producing toxic carbon monoxide (CO) gas, unburned carbon soot, and water:

$$2\text{CH}_4 + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{toxic carbon monoxide})$$

**Atom Check:** Reactants: C = 2, H = 8, O = 6; Products: C = 2, H = 8, O = $2 + 4 = 6$ ✅.

Under severely starved oxygen conditions, solid carbon soot is deposited:

$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{C} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{black carbon soot})$$

### Why Is the Flame Blue or Yellow?

In a properly functioning gas burner, high-velocity gas streams out of a tiny orifice nozzle into a mixing tube (venturi). The fast stream draws in surrounding air through adjustable **air-intake ports**. 

- **Air ports OPEN:** Abundant atmospheric oxygen thoroughly pre-mixes with the hydrocarbon gas before it reaches the burner crown. Combustion is complete: the flame is hot, compact, non-luminous, and distinctly **blue**.
- **Air ports BLOCKED:** Dust, grease, or spillage clogs the air ports, drastically reducing oxygen intake. Combustion becomes incomplete. Unburned carbon particles are heated to incandescence, glowing with a bright **yellow luminosity**. As these glowing carbon particles strike cooler cookware, they condense as a layer of black **soot**, and dangerous levels of **carbon monoxide** escape into the room.

```
    Air-intake ports OPEN                 Air-intake ports BLOCKED
    (complete combustion, blue flame)     (incomplete combustion, yellow/sooty)

          Blue, clean flame                     Yellow, luminous flame
               /\                                       /\    /\
              /  \                                     /  \  /  \
           +--------+                              +--------+
           | Mixing |                              | Mixing |
     Air   |  tube  |  Air                  Blocked|  tube  | Blocked
    ------>|        |<------                - - - X|        |X - - -
           +---::---+                              +---::---+
               ::                                      ::
               :: High-speed gas jet                   :: Gas jet
```

> **Figure 4.5** – Operational schematic of a domestic gas burner. Left: Clear air-intake ports allow atmospheric air to blend with the gas, producing a clean, non-luminous blue flame. Right: Clogged air ports starve the flame of oxygen, causing incomplete combustion, yellow luminosity, soot deposition, and CO generation.

### Example 5. Complete Combustion of Butane: How Much CO₂?

**Problem:** 29 g of butane (C₄H₁₀) undergoes complete combustion: $2\text{C}_4\text{H}_{10} + 13\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 8\text{CO}_2 + 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Determine: (a) the mass of oxygen consumed, (b) the mass and STP volume of CO₂ evolved, and (c) the mass of water formed. (Atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16; molar volume at STP = 22.4 L/mol.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of C₄H₁₀ = $4(12) + 10(1) = 58$ g/mol.  
Moles of butane = $29\text{ g} \div 58\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.50 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 2 : 13 : 8 : 10):**  
For 1.0 mol butane, we require $6.5\text{ mol O}_2$, producing $4.0\text{ mol CO}_2$ and $5.0\text{ mol H}_2\text{O}$.  
For 0.50 mol butane:  
- $\text{Moles of O}_2 = 0.50 \times 6.5 = \mathbf{3.25\text{ mol}}$. Mass of $\text{O}_2 = 3.25 \times 32 =$ **104 g**.  
- $\text{Moles of CO}_2 = 0.50 \times 4.0 = \mathbf{2.0\text{ mol}}$. Mass of $\text{CO}_2 = 2.0 \times 44 =$ **88 g**.  
  $\text{Volume of CO}_2\text{ at STP} = 2.0\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} =$ **44.8 L**.  
- $\text{Moles of H}_2\text{O} = 0.50 \times 5.0 = \mathbf{2.5\text{ mol}}$. Mass of $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 2.5 \times 18 =$ **45 g**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:**  
Total reactant mass = $29\text{ g (fuel)} + 104\text{ g (O}_2) = 133\text{ g}$.  
Total product mass = $88\text{ g (CO}_2) + 45\text{ g (H}_2\text{O}) = 133\text{ g}$ ✅.

### Example 6. Stoichiometry of Octane (A Major Component of Gasoline)

**Problem:** 57 g of liquid octane burns completely in an engine cylinder: $2\text{C}_8\text{H}_{18} + 25\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 16\text{CO}_2 + 18\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Calculate the mass of oxygen required, the STP volume of CO₂ released, and the mass of water vapour produced.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of octane = $8(12) + 18(1) = 114$ g/mol.  
Moles of octane = $57\text{ g} \div 114\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.50 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 2 : 25 : 16 : 18):**  
Per mole of octane: $12.5\text{ mol O}_2$, $8\text{ mol CO}_2$, $9\text{ mol H}_2\text{O}$.  
For 0.50 mol octane:  
- $\text{O}_2\text{ required} = 0.50 \times 12.5 = 6.25\text{ mol} = 6.25 \times 32 =$ **200 g**.  
- $\text{CO}_2\text{ formed} = 0.50 \times 8 = 4.0\text{ mol}$. $\text{STP volume} = 4.0 \times 22.4 =$ **89.6 L** (mass = $4 \times 44 = 176$ g).  
- $\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{ formed} = 0.50 \times 9 = 4.5\text{ mol} = 4.5 \times 18 =$ **81 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Total reactants = $57 + 200 = 257$ g; Total products = $176 + 81 = 257$ g ✅.

### Example 7. Methane: Limited Air Yields Different Products

**Problem:** Compare the combustion of 3.2 g of methane under:  
Case (a) Abundant air: $\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$  
Case (b) Deficient air: $2\text{CH}_4 + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$  
Calculate the mass of oxygen consumed and the carbon-containing product formed in each case. (CH₄ = 16, O₂ = 32, CO₂ = 44, CO = 28.)

**Step 1:** Moles of methane = $3.2\text{ g} \div 16\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.20 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Case a - Complete):**  
- $\text{O}_2\text{ needed} = 0.20 \times 2 = 0.40\text{ mol} = 0.40 \times 32 =$ **12.8 g**.  
- $\text{CO}_2\text{ formed} = 0.20\text{ mol} = 0.20 \times 44 =$ **8.8 g**.  
**Step 3 (Case b - Incomplete):**  
- $\text{O}_2\text{ needed} = 0.20 \times 1.5 = 0.30\text{ mol} = 0.30 \times 32 =$ **9.6 g**.  
- $\text{CO formed} = 0.20\text{ mol} = 0.20 \times 28 =$ **5.6 g**.  

**Critical Insight:** Notice that both reactions consume the exact same 0.20 mol of carbon. However, Case (b) requires less oxygen (9.6 g vs 12.8 g) and generates **lethal carbon monoxide (5.6 g)** instead of benign carbon dioxide. Incomplete combustion alters the chemical identity of the products entirely!

## Part E: Alkanes and Chlorine: Substitution Reactions

Because all four valence bonds of every carbon atom in an alkane are fully engaged in single bonds, alkanes **cannot undergo addition reactions**. They cannot take in any additional atoms without first expelling an existing atom. 

In the presence of **diffuse sunlight (or ultraviolet light)**, alkanes react with halogens such as chlorine. A chlorine atom displaces and replaces a hydrogen atom one by one. This is termed a **substitution reaction**:

$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu\text{ (diffuse sunlight)}} \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$$

```
        H                             H
        |                             |
    H - C - H   +  Cl - Cl   -->  H - C - Cl   +  H - Cl
        |        (sunlight)           |
        H                             H
     methane                    chloromethane
```

> **Figure 4.6** – Photochemical substitution reaction between methane and chlorine. Ultraviolet light cleaves the Cl–Cl bond into reactive chlorine free radicals. One chlorine atom replaces a hydrogen atom on methane to yield chloromethane (CH₃Cl), while the displaced hydrogen bonds with the second chlorine atom to form hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas.

If chlorine is supplied in excess, substitution continues progressively until all four hydrogen atoms have been replaced:

1. $\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$ (chloromethane)
2. $\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 + \text{HCl}$ (dichloromethane)
3. $\text{CH}_2\text{Cl}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CHCl}_3 + \text{HCl}$ (trichloromethane or chloroform)
4. $\text{CHCl}_3 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CCl}_4 + \text{HCl}$ (tetrachloromethane or carbon tetrachloride)

*(In direct, intense sunlight, the reaction proceeds explosively: $\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{C} + 4\text{HCl}$.)*

### Example 8. Forming CH₃Cl from 1.6 g of Methane

**Problem:** 1.6 g of methane reacts completely with chlorine in diffuse sunlight to form chloromethane: $\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$. Calculate the mass of Cl₂ consumed and the masses of CH₃Cl and HCl formed. (C = 12, H = 1, Cl = 35.5.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of CH₄ = 16 g/mol. Moles = $1.6 \div 16 = \mathbf{0.10\text{ mol}}$.  
Stoichiometric ratio is 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.  
**Step 2:**  
- $\text{Mass of Cl}_2 = 0.10\text{ mol} \times 71\text{ g/mol} =$ **7.10 g**.  
- $\text{Mass of CH}_3\text{Cl} = 0.10\text{ mol} \times (12 + 3 + 35.5)\text{ g/mol} = 0.10 \times 50.5 =$ **5.05 g**.  
- $\text{Mass of HCl} = 0.10\text{ mol} \times 36.5\text{ g/mol} =$ **3.65 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $1.60 + 7.10 = 8.70$ g; Products = $5.05 + 3.65 = 8.70$ g ✅.

### Why Are Alkanes Relatively Unreactive?

Historically, alkanes were called **paraffins** (from Latin *parum affinis*, meaning "little affinity"). Alkanes exhibit remarkable chemical inertness toward most laboratory reagents at room temperature:

| Reagent | Reaction with Alkanes at 25 °C | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Dilute aqueous acids (HCl, H₂SO₄) | **No reaction** | Non-polar C–C and C–H bonds lack polar attack sites for H⁺ ions |
| Dilute or concentrated alkalis (NaOH) | **No reaction** | No electrophilic centers for OH⁻ nucleophiles |
| Strong oxidising agents (cold KMnO₄) | **No reaction** | Saturated bonds resist mild oxidation |
| Oxygen + spark | **Rapid combustion** | High thermal activation overcomes strong bond energies |
| Chlorine + sunlight | **Photochemical substitution** | UV photons provide enough energy to split Cl–Cl bonds |

## Part F: Gaseous Fuels: Uses and Safety Guidelines

Because alkanes burn with high calorific value and leave no solid ash residue, they serve as the world's premier domestic and industrial heating fuels:

| Alkane | Primary Applications |
|---|---|
| **Methane (CH₄)** | Piped Natural Gas (PNG), CNG for motor vehicles, biogas for eco-farms |
| **Propane (C₃H₈) & Butane (C₄H₁₀)** | LPG for domestic cookstoves, portable camping burners, cigarette lighters |
| **Octane (C₈H₁₈) and isomers** | Major constituent of automotive gasoline (petrol) |
| **Long-chain alkanes (C₂₀+)** | Paraffin wax for candles, waterproof coatings, petroleum jelly lubricants |

### Gas Leak Safety Rules

Because pure propane, butane, and methane are completely odourless, commercial fuel suppliers deliberately inject a minute trace of an intensely foul-smelling organic sulphur compound—**ethyl mercaptan (ethanethiol, C₂H₅SH)**—so that even a 0.5% gas leak is detected instantly by human smell.

If you ever detect the pungent odour of leaking cooking gas:

1. **Do not touch any electrical switch, fan, telephone, or appliance.** The microscopic electrical arc inside an ordinary light switch can ignite an explosive gas-air mixture instantly.
2. **Extinguish all naked flames immediately.** Do not strike matches or lighters.
3. **Open all doors and windows fully** to establish maximum cross-ventilation.
4. **Turn off the cylinder regulator valve** or main supply shut-off cock if it is safe to approach.
5. **Evacuate the premises** and call the gas distributor or fire emergency services from a mobile phone safely located outside the building.

**Relative Density and Accumulation Behaviour:**  
- **LPG (propane M = 44, butane M = 58)** is significantly **heavier than atmospheric air** (average molecular mass $\approx 29$). Leaking LPG flows downward, pooling along the **floor**, under cabinetry, and in basement depressions.
- **Natural gas and biogas (methane M = 16)** are significantly **lighter than air**. Leaking methane rises rapidly toward the **ceiling** and escapes through upper ventilators.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Alkanes are completely unreactive, so gas cylinders pose zero chemical hazard."**  
   *Correction:* Alkanes are relatively inert toward mild laboratory acids, alkalis, and aqueous reagents at room temperature, which is why they are described as having "low reactivity." However, they react vigorously with atmospheric oxygen when ignited, releasing tremendous thermal energy that can cause devastating explosions if contained improperly. They also undergo rapid photochemical halogenation in sunlight. Never confuse "resistant to dilute acids" with "non-combustible."

2. **Mistake 2: "Complete and incomplete combustion produce the exact same substances, just at different temperatures."**  
   *Correction:* Incomplete combustion is not just a weaker version of complete combustion—it produces fundamentally different chemical substances! Complete combustion yields benign **carbon dioxide (CO₂)** and water vapour. Incomplete combustion starves the reaction of oxygen, creating **carbon monoxide (CO)**, an insidious, odourless, deadly blood poison, along with carcinogenic **carbon soot**.

## Quick Recap

- **Alkanes:** Saturated hydrocarbons containing only single covalent bonds; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂. Total covalent bonds in any open-chain alkane equal $3n + 1$.
- **Natural Sources:** Biogas (anaerobic digestion of organic biomass, 55–65% CH₄), natural gas/CNG (subterranean deposits, >90% CH₄), and LPG (petroleum refining, propane and butane).
- **Physical Trends:** Non-polar, insoluble in water, float on water. Boiling points increase with chain length due to growing dispersion forces, but the incremental rise per –CH₂– diminishes down the series.
- **Complete Combustion:** Abundant oxygen converts alkanes to CO₂ and H₂O with a clean, hot blue flame.
- **Incomplete Combustion:** Oxygen deficiency produces toxic CO, black soot, and a luminous yellow flame.
- **Substitution Reaction:** Saturated alkanes cannot undergo addition. In diffuse sunlight, halogens replace hydrogen atoms step-by-step: $\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$.
- **Safety Protocol:** LPG is heavier than air (sinks to the floor); methane is lighter than air (rises). If a leak is smelled, never operate electrical switches; ventilate immediately and isolate the gas supply.

### Answers

1. Butane (C₄H₁₀): $n = 4$. C–C bonds = $4 - 1 =$ **3**; C–H bonds = **10**; total covalent bonds = $3 + 10 =$ **13**. Formula check: $3(4) + 1 = 13$ ✅. Ethane (C₂H₆): $n = 2$. C–C bonds = $2 - 1 =$ **1**; C–H bonds = **6**; total bonds = $1 + 6 =$ **7**. Formula check: $3(2) + 1 = 7$ ✅.

The **2** terminal carbon atoms each carry 3 hydrogens and 1 C–C bond (4 bonds); the **6** internal methylene carbons each carry 2 hydrogens and 2 C–C bonds (4 bonds). Total hydrogens = $2(3) + 6(2) = 18$ ✅. Total bonds = $7\text{ C–C} + 18\text{ C–H} = 25$ ($3 \times 8 + 1 = 25$ ✅).
3. Molar mass of C₂H₆ = 30 g/mol. Moles = $15 \div 30 = 0.50$ mol. From $2\text{C}_2\text{H}_6 + 7\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O}$:  
   - $\text{O}_2 = 0.50 \times 3.5 = 1.75\text{ mol} = 1.75 \times 32 =$ **56 g**.  
   - $\text{CO}_2 = 0.50 \times 2 = 1.0\text{ mol} = 1.0 \times 44 =$ **44 g**.  
   - $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 0.50 \times 3 = 1.5\text{ mol} = 1.5 \times 18 =$ **27 g**.  
   Mass conservation: Reactants = $15 + 56 = 71$ g; Products = $44 + 27 = 71$ g ✅.
4. Molar mass of C₃H₈ = 44 g/mol. Moles = $22 \div 44 = 0.50$ mol.

   Mass check: Reactants = $22 + 80 = 102$ g; Products = $1.5(44) + 2.0(18) = 66 + 36 = 102$ g ✅.
5. Molar mass of C₈H₁₈ = 114 g/mol. Moles of octane = $285 \div 114 =$ **2.5 mol**.

   Both consume the identical 0.80 mol of carbon, but incomplete combustion produces **22.4 g of lethal carbon monoxide** instead of benign carbon dioxide.

    Difference in mass = $114 - 58 = 56$ u.  
    Since each –CH₂– unit has mass $12 + 2 = 14$ u: $56 \div 14 = \mathbf{4\text{ methylene units}}$ (corresponding to $8 - 4 = 4$ carbons) ✅.
11. (a) **False.** Alkanes are **saturated** hydrocarbons; they combust because the formation of strong C=O and O–H bonds is thermodynamically highly exothermic.  
    (b) **True.** Yellow luminosity indicates incandescent unburned carbon soot from oxygen deprivation.  
    (c) **False.** It is a **substitution** reaction, where a chlorine atom replaces a hydrogen atom.  
    (d) **False.** Methane (M = 16) is lighter than air (M ≈ 29) and rises toward the **ceiling**; LPG pools on the floor.

12. Saturated means that every carbon valence bond is a **single bond**, leaving no capacity for addition reactions. It does **not** mean inert. Two disproving reactions:  
    (i) Combustion: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6 + 3.5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}$.  
    (ii) Halogenation: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_6 + \text{Cl}_2 \xrightarrow{h\nu} \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$.
13. Emergency protocol: (1) Do not flip any electrical switches or ignite flames; (2) Open all windows and doors for ventilation; (3) Close the cylinder valve/regulator if safe; (4) Evacuate and notify emergency services from outside. Flipping a switch generates a microscopic internal electrical spark that can ignite the gas-air mixture. LPG (M = 44) is heavier than air (M ≈ 29) and pools on the **floor**; biogas (methane, M = 16) is lighter than air and rises to the **ceiling**.
