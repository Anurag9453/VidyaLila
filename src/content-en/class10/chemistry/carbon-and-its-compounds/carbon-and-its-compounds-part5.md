# Topic 5: Alkenes and Alkynes: Double and Triple Bonds

In Topic 4, we saw that alkanes are "filled to capacity" (saturated) hydrocarbons: every single carbon valence bond is occupied by a single covalent bond, rendering them relatively unreactive toward most reagents at room temperature. Now we examine the opposite scenario: what happens when two adjacent carbon atoms **share two or three pairs of valence electrons**? In Class 9, you studied multiple covalent bonds in oxygen ($O=O$) and nitrogen ($N \equiv N$). Carbon can also form multiple bonds with another carbon atom, giving rise to two new families: **alkenes** (carbon-carbon double bonds) and **alkynes** (carbon-carbon triple bonds). Today we explore how these "extra shared electron pairs" transform these hydrocarbons into high-energy fuels, chemically versatile reagents, and the building blocks of modern polymers.

## The Welder's Torch and the Plastic Shopping Bag

On a Saturday afternoon, Mr. Campbell took his niece Maya to visit an automotive metal restoration workshop. Inside, a certified welder wearing a heavy dark-tinted visor connected two high-pressure gas cylinders to a dual-feed torch. When the nozzle sparked, a piercing, intensely luminous **blue-white flame** erupted with a sharp hiss. Held against two thick plates of structural steel, the flame heated the joint to cherry red within seconds, melting the metal edges until they fused seamlessly together. Maya and Mr. Campbell observed from a designated visitor area across the room, well away from the intense ultraviolet radiation.

"Uncle, why is that flame so blistering hot?" Maya asked in awe. "Our kitchen stove at home also burns gas, but its soft blue flame could never melt solid steel!"

Mr. Campbell explained, "That torch burns **ethyne** (commonly called acetylene) mixed with pure compressed oxygen. In an ethyne molecule, two carbon atoms are locked together by a **triple bond**. When that high-energy bond breaks and oxidises completely in pure oxygen, it generates temperatures exceeding 3,000 °C! Notice the two distinct cylinders: one holds fuel gas, and the other supplies pure oxygen under high pressure. Only trained professionals operate such equipment."

On their walk home, Maya spotted someone by a garden waste bin preparing to set fire to a pile of leaves along with a discarded **plastic grocery bag**. Mr. Campbell stepped forward immediately: "Excuse me, please do not burn plastic! Burning plastic generates hazardous, toxic fumes." Turning to Maya, he added with a smile, "Did you know that everyday plastic bag is made directly from a gas closely related to the welder's ethyne? It is made from **ethene**, an organic gas containing a **double bond**. Let us explore how double and triple bonds unlock both structural materials and extreme heat."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Unsaturated hydrocarbon** | A hydrocarbon containing at least one carbon-carbon double or triple covalent bond, having fewer hydrogens than the corresponding alkane | ethene (C₂H₄), ethyne (C₂H₂) |
| **Alkene** | An unsaturated open-chain hydrocarbon containing one carbon-carbon double bond (C=C); general formula CₙH₂ₙ | ethene C₂H₄, propene C₃H₆ |
| **Alkyne** | An unsaturated open-chain hydrocarbon containing one carbon-carbon triple bond (C≡C); general formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | ethyne C₂H₂, propyne C₃H₄ |
| **Addition reaction** | A reaction in which reagent atoms add directly across a multiple bond, opening a double or triple bond to yield a single saturated product | C₂H₄ + Br₂ → C₂H₄Br₂ |
| **Substitution reaction** | A reaction in which an existing atom in a saturated molecule is displaced by another atom, generating two products | CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl |
| **Catalyst** | A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without undergoing permanent chemical change itself | Finely divided nickel (Ni) in hydrogenation |
| **Monomer** | A small, reactive repeating molecular unit from which a polymer macromolecule is constructed | ethene (CH₂=CH₂) |
| **Polymer (Polymerisation)** | A massive macromolecule formed by linking thousands of monomer units together (and the process of forming it) | polyethylene / polythene |
| **Bromine water** | An orange-brown aqueous solution of bromine (Br₂); used as a diagnostic reagent for unsaturation | Br₂ in water (decolourised by alkenes/alkynes) |
| **Calcium carbide** | CaC₂: a greyish-black solid that reacts vigorously with water to generate ethyne gas | CaC₂ + 2H₂O → C₂H₂ + Ca(OH)₂ |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Reactive Hydrocarbon Gases and Hazardous Reagents**
> - **Ethene and ethyne** are exceptionally volatile and flammable gases. Mixed with air across wide concentration ranges, they ignite with explosive force. Never bring open flames or unrated electrical devices near gas generators or cylinders.
> - **Concentrated sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄)** is extremely corrosive and hygroscopic; contact causes severe chemical burns.
> - **Calcium carbide** reacts exothermically with water or atmospheric humidity, releasing flammable ethyne gas. Improvised "carbide guns" or DIY gas generators are illegal and can cause catastrophic blast injuries and permanent blindness.
> - **Bromine and bromine water** are highly toxic, fuming, and corrosive liquids; bromine vapour severely burns the eyes and respiratory tract.
> - All reactions in this topic are presented **strictly for theoretical study on paper or as teacher-led fume hood demonstrations**. Students must never conduct these preparations independently.
> - **Never burn plastic materials:** incinerating polymers releases carcinogenic dioxins, furans, and toxic particulate smoke.

## Part A: What an Extra Shared Pair Means: Unsaturated Hydrocarbons

In Class 9, you learned that when two atoms require multiple electrons to complete their valence shells, they share multiple electron pairs: oxygen forms a double bond ($O=O$), while nitrogen forms a triple bond ($N \equiv N$). 

Carbon possesses four valence electrons. If two carbon atoms share **two electron pairs** ($C=C$), each carbon atom has only two remaining valence electrons available to bond with hydrogen. If they share **three electron pairs** ($C \equiv C$), each carbon atom has only one remaining valence electron for hydrogen:

```
   Ethane C₂H₆ (single)    Ethene C₂H₄ (double)     Ethyne C₂H₂ (triple)

       H   H                 H         H
       |   |                  \       /
   H - C - C - H               C  =  C               H - C ≡ C - H
       |   |                  /       \
       H   H                 H         H
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Comparative structures of ethane, ethene, and ethyne. Count the covalent bonds around each carbon atom: in ethane, 3 C–H + 1 C–C = 4; in ethene, 2 C–H + 2 (double bond) = 4; in ethyne, 1 C–H + 3 (triple bond) = 4. Every carbon maintains its full tetravalency, but the hydrogen count drops progressively from 6 to 4 to 2.

Do not misunderstand: both ethene and ethyne satisfy carbon's tetravalency completely. Why then are they called **unsaturated**? Because their carbon skeletons possess the capacity to **add more hydrogen atoms** without expelling any existing atoms. When one of the shared electron pairs in the multiple bond breaks open, each carbon atom gains a new vacant bonding position. This reserve capacity makes unsaturated hydrocarbons far more chemically reactive than alkanes.

```
   Ethene                           Ethyne

      H     H
      :     :
      C :: C                        H : C ::: C : H
      :     :
      H     H
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Lewis electron-dot representations. Each colon (:) represents one shared electron pair (a single covalent bond). Ethene shares two pairs between the carbon atoms, giving each carbon a complete octet of 8 electrons. Ethyne shares three electron pairs between the carbon atoms, again completing each carbon's octet.

### Example 1. Counting Valency and Bonds in Ethene

**Problem:** Using the structural formula of ethene (C₂H₄), verify that each atom satisfies its standard valency. How many total covalent bonds exist in the molecule?

**Solution:**  
- Each carbon atom forms 2 single bonds with hydrogen and shares 2 bonds in the C=C double bond: $2(1) + 2 = \mathbf{4\text{ bonds}}$ (valency 4 satisfied ✅).  
- Each hydrogen atom forms exactly 1 single covalent bond: valency 1 satisfied ✅.  
- Total covalent bond count = $4\text{ (C–H bonds)} + 2\text{ (C=C double bond)} = \mathbf{6\text{ shared pairs}}$ (6 covalent bonds).  

**Verification via Valence Sum:** Total valence electrons = $2(4\text{ from C}) + 4(1\text{ from H}) = 12$ electrons. Since each bond uses 2 electrons, total bonds = $12 \div 2 = \mathbf{6\text{ bonds}}$ ✅.

### Example 2. Counting Valency and Bonds in Ethyne

**Problem:** How many total covalent bonds exist in ethyne, C₂H₂? Verify that each atom satisfies its valency.

**Solution:**  
- Each carbon forms 1 single C–H bond and shares 3 bonds in the C≡C triple bond: $1 + 3 = \mathbf{4\text{ bonds}}$ (valency 4 satisfied ✅).  
- Each hydrogen forms 1 single covalent bond: valency 1 satisfied ✅.  
- Total covalent bond count = $2\text{ (C–H bonds)} + 3\text{ (C≡C triple bond)} = \mathbf{5\text{ shared pairs}}$ (5 covalent bonds).  

**Verification:** Total valence electrons = $2(4) + 2(1) = 10$. Total bonds = $10 \div 2 = \mathbf{5\text{ bonds}}$ ✅.  
*(Notice the progression in two-carbon hydrocarbons: ethane has 7 covalent bonds, ethene has 6, and ethyne has 5. As C–C bond multiplicity increases, hydrogen count decreases.)*

### The Alkene and Alkyne Families

Just like alkanes, alkenes and alkynes form continuous homologous series where each successive member differs by one –CH₂– unit (14 u). Because a double or triple bond requires at least two adjacent carbon atoms to exist, **there is no one-carbon alkene or alkyne** (compounds like "methene" or "methyne" cannot exist).

| Homologous Family | General Formula | First Member ($n=2$) | Second Member ($n=3$) | Third Member ($n=4$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Alkene (C=C)** | **CₙH₂ₙ** | Ethene (C₂H₄) | Propene (C₃H₆) | Butene (C₄H₈: but-1-ene & but-2-ene) |
| **Alkyne (C≡C)** | **CₙH₂ₙ₋₂** | Ethyne (C₂H₂) | Propyne (C₃H₄) | Butyne (C₄H₆: but-1-yne & but-2-yne) |
| **Alkane (C–C)** | **CₙH₂ₙ₊₂** | Ethane (C₂H₆) | Propane (C₃H₈) | Butane (C₄H₁₀) |

## Part B: Preparation Methods (For Reading Only, Teacher Demonstration)

### Ethene: Dehydration of Ethanol

In laboratory chemistry, ethene is prepared by the **dehydration of ethanol** (C₂H₅OH). Concentrated sulfuric acid acts as a powerful dehydrating agent, stripping the elements of water (one –OH group from one carbon and one –H atom from the adjacent carbon) when heated to approximately **170 °C**:

$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ 170\ ^\circ\text{C}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_4 \uparrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

```
              Thermometer
                   |
              +----|----+   Stopper
              |    |    |===================+   Delivery tube
              +----|----+                   |
                |  |  |                     |
             ,--'  |  '--.                  |             Gas jar (inverted,
            /      |      \                 |             filled with water)
           |   Ethanol +   |                |              +---------+
           | conc. H₂SO₄   |                |              |  Ethene |
           |  (flask) *    |                |              |  (gas)  |
            \             /                 |              |         |
             `-----------'       ~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~|~~~~
                   ^             ~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~|~~~~
              High heat          ~~~~~~~~~~~'------------------> ~~~~~~~~~
              (teacher)          ~~~~~~~~ Water in pneumatic trough ~~~~~~
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Laboratory preparation of ethene by catalytic dehydration of ethanol (teacher demonstration in a fume hood). The reaction mixture of ethanol and excess concentrated sulfuric acid is maintained at 160–170 °C. The evolved ethene gas passes through a delivery tube and is collected over water in an inverted gas jar by downward displacement of water (ethene is virtually insoluble in water). *Precaution:* To prevent "suck-back" of cold water into the hot boiling flask, the delivery tube is withdrawn from the trough before the Bunsen flame is extinguished.

During this process, hot concentrated sulfuric acid also partially chars some ethanol to elemental carbon, producing sulfur dioxide (SO₂) and carbon dioxide (CO₂) as acidic impurities. In an industrial or research apparatus, the gas stream is passed through a wash bottle containing dilute sodium hydroxide (NaOH) to neutralise and scrub out these acidic vapours.

### Example 3. Stoichiometry of Ethene from Ethanol

**Problem:** In a teacher demonstration, 4.6 g of ethanol is completely dehydrated: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. Calculate: (a) the mass of ethene formed, (b) the STP volume of ethene evolved, and (c) the mass of water eliminated. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of ethanol = $2(12) + 6(1) + 16 = 46$ g/mol.  
Moles of ethanol = $4.6\text{ g} \div 46\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.10 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 1 : 1):**  
- Moles of ethene = 0.10 mol. Mass of ethene = $0.10\text{ mol} \times 28\text{ g/mol} =$ **2.8 g**.  
- STP volume of ethene = $0.10\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} =$ **2.24 L**.  
- Moles of water = 0.10 mol. Mass of water = $0.10\text{ mol} \times 18\text{ g/mol} =$ **1.8 g**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:** Reactant = 4.6 g; Products = $2.8\text{ g (ethene)} + 1.8\text{ g (water)} = 4.6\text{ g}$ ✅.

### Ethyne: Action of Water on Calcium Carbide

Ethyne (acetylene) is prepared at room temperature by the vigorous reaction between **calcium carbide (CaC₂)** and water:

$$\text{CaC}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 \uparrow + \text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{heat}$$

```
        Dropping funnel (water added dropwise)
               |
          +----|----+  Stopper
          |    v    |==================+   Delivery tube
          +----|----+                  |
             |   |                     |            Gas jar (inverted,
         ,---'   '---.                 |            filled with water)
        /   CaC₂ grey \                |             +---------+
       |     lumps     |               |             | Ethyne  |
        \             /                |             |  (gas)  |
         `-----------'     ~~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~|~~~~
                           ~~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~~~~~|~~~~~~~~~|~~~~
           No external     ~~~~~~~~~~~~'------------------> ~~~~~~~~
           heat required   ~~~~~~~~ Water in pneumatic trough ~~~~~~
```

> **Figure 5.4** – Laboratory generation of ethyne from calcium carbide. Water is introduced dropwise from a separatory funnel onto lumps of calcium carbide placed on a thin layer of sand. The exothermic reaction evolves ethyne gas immediately at room temperature, which is collected over water.

Commercial technical-grade calcium carbide contains traces of calcium phosphide (Ca₃P₂) and calcium sulfide (CaS), releasing minute quantities of phosphine (PH₃) and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) that impart a characteristic **garlic-like odor** to raw acetylene gas. Pure ethyne is a colourless, ethereal-smelling gas.

### Example 4. Stoichiometry of Calcium Carbide Hydrolysis

**Problem:** 6.4 g of pure calcium carbide reacts completely with excess water: $\text{CaC}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + \text{Ca(OH)}_2$. Calculate the mass and STP volume of ethyne gas evolved, the mass of calcium hydroxide produced, and the mass of water consumed. (Ca = 40, C = 12, H = 1, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of CaC₂ = $40 + 2(12) = 64$ g/mol.  
Moles of CaC₂ = $6.4\text{ g} \div 64\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.10 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Mole ratio CaC₂ : H₂O : C₂H₂ : Ca(OH)₂ = 1 : 2 : 1 : 1):**  
- Moles of ethyne = 0.10 mol. Mass of ethyne = $0.10 \times 26 =$ **2.6 g**.  
  STP volume of ethyne = $0.10 \times 22.4 =$ **2.24 L**.  
- Moles of Ca(OH)₂ = 0.10 mol. Mass of Ca(OH)₂ = $0.10 \times [40 + 2(17)] = 0.10 \times 74 =$ **7.4 g**.  
- Moles of H₂O consumed = $0.10 \times 2 = 0.20\text{ mol}$. Mass of H₂O = $0.20 \times 18 =$ **3.6 g**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:** Reactants = $6.4\text{ g} + 3.6\text{ g} = 10.0\text{ g}$; Products = $2.6\text{ g} + 7.4\text{ g} = 10.0\text{ g}$ ✅.

## Part C: Physical Properties and Combustion: Why Are Flames Sooty?

Both ethene and ethyne are colourless gases that are virtually insoluble in water (due to their non-polar character), which is why both can be collected over water in laboratory demonstrations.

When ignited in air or oxygen, they combust exothermically:

$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}$$
$$2\text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 4\text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{heat}$$

### Example 5. Combustion of Ethene

**Problem:** 8.4 g of ethene is burned completely in oxygen. Determine the volume of oxygen required at STP, the mass of CO₂ evolved, and the mass of water vapour produced.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of C₂H₄ = 28 g/mol. Moles = $8.4 \div 28 =$ **0.30 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 3 : 2 : 2):**  
- $\text{O}_2\text{ consumed} = 0.30 \times 3 = 0.90\text{ mol}$. $\text{STP volume} = 0.90 \times 22.4 =$ **20.16 L** (mass = $0.90 \times 32 = 28.8$ g).  
- $\text{CO}_2\text{ produced} = 0.30 \times 2 = 0.60\text{ mol}$. $\text{Mass} = 0.60 \times 44 =$ **26.4 g**.  
- $\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{ produced} = 0.30 \times 2 = 0.60\text{ mol}$. $\text{Mass} = 0.60 \times 18 =$ **10.8 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $8.4 + 28.8 = 37.2$ g; Products = $26.4 + 10.8 = 37.2$ g ✅.

### Why Is the Flame Sooty?

When burned in **ordinary open air**, why does ethane burn with a relatively clear blue flame, ethene burn with a yellow luminous flame, and ethyne burn with a heavily smoky, violently sooty flame? 

The answer lies in the **percentage of carbon by mass**. When a hydrocarbon has a very high carbon-to-hydrogen ratio, the ambient oxygen diffusing from surrounding air is insufficient to oxidise all carbon atoms instantly to CO₂. The excess unoxidised carbon atoms cluster into microscopic solid particles. These solid particles glow white-hot (incandescence), imparting yellow luminosity, and escape as black clouds of **soot**.

### Example 6. Carbon Mass Percentage Comparison

**Problem:** Calculate the percentage of carbon by mass in ethane (C₂H₆), ethene (C₂H₄), and ethyne (C₂H₂), and correlate this with flame sootiness.

- **Ethane (C₂H₆):** Molar mass = 30 g/mol. Carbon mass % = $\frac{24}{30} \times 100 =$ **80.0%**.
- **Ethene (C₂H₄):** Molar mass = 28 g/mol. Carbon mass % = $\frac{24}{28} \times 100 =$ **85.7%**.
- **Ethyne (C₂H₂):** Molar mass = 26 g/mol. Carbon mass % = $\frac{24}{26} \times 100 =$ **92.3%**.

**Conclusion:** Ethyne contains over **92% elemental carbon by mass**! In unpressurised room air, it burns with extreme sootiness. However, when supplied with **pure compressed oxygen** in an oxy-acetylene torch, the stoichiometric ratio of oxygen to carbon is fully satisfied. All carbon oxidises completely, soot is entirely eliminated, and the flame contracts into a razor-sharp, non-luminous blue-white cone reaching temperatures over **3,000 °C**—hot enough to slice and weld structural steel.

## Part D: Addition Reactions: Opening the Extra Shared Pair

Because multiple bonds contain extra shared pairs of electrons that can break open without disrupting the underlying carbon-carbon single bond framework, alkenes and alkynes undergo **addition reactions**. In an addition reaction, two reactant molecules combine directly to form **a single product molecule**.

### 1. Addition of Hydrogen (Catalytic Hydrogenation)

In the presence of a finely divided transition metal catalyst such as **nickel (Ni)**, palladium, or platinum at elevated temperatures (~200 °C), hydrogen gas adds across the double bond of ethene, converting it into saturated ethane:

$$\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni catalyst, } 200\ ^\circ\text{C}} \text{CH}_3\text{–CH}_3$$

```
     H       H                                 H   H
      \     /                                  |   |
       C = C    +   H - H    ---Ni, heat--->   H - C - C - H
      /     \                                  |   |
     H       H                                 H   H
     ethene           hydrogen                   ethane
```

> **Figure 5.5** – Catalytic addition of hydrogen to ethene. One of the electron pairs in the C=C double bond opens up, allowing each carbon atom to form a new single covalent bond with a hydrogen atom. Two reactants yield one single, fully saturated product.

For ethyne, hydrogenation proceeds in two consecutive stages:
1. First addition: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_4$ (ethyne to ethene)
2. Second addition: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_6$ (ethene to ethane)  
Overall: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + 2\text{H}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Ni}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_6$.

*(In industry, this exact catalytic hydrogenation process converts polyunsaturated liquid vegetable oils into semi-solid fats, as studied in Class 9.)*

### Example 7. Complete Hydrogenation of Ethyne to Ethane

**Problem:** 13 g of ethyne is completely hydrogenated to ethane: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + 2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_6$. Calculate the STP volume of hydrogen gas consumed and the mass of ethane produced.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of C₂H₂ = 26 g/mol. Moles = $13 \div 26 =$ **0.50 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 2 : 1):**  
- Moles of H₂ required = $0.50 \times 2 = 1.0\text{ mol}$.  
  STP volume of H₂ = $1.0\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} =$ **22.4 L** (mass = 2.0 g).  
- Moles of ethane formed = 0.50 mol.  
  Mass of ethane = $0.50\text{ mol} \times 30\text{ g/mol} =$ **15.0 g**.  

**Mass Check:** $13.0\text{ g (ethyne)} + 2.0\text{ g (H}_2) = 15.0\text{ g (ethane)}$ ✅.

### 2. Addition of Bromine and the Bromine Water Test

When ethene gas is bubbled through **bromine water** (or a solution of bromine in tetrachloromethane), the reddish-brown halogen molecules add across the double bond:

$$\text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 + \text{Br}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_2\text{Br–CH}_2\text{Br} \quad (\text{1,2-dibromoethane})$$

Because elemental bromine (Br₂) is consumed and converted into colourless 1,2-dibromoethane, the distinctive **orange-brown colour of bromine water is discharged (decolourised)** rapidly at room temperature without requiring sunlight.

With ethyne, two molar equivalents of bromine add sequentially:

$$\text{CH}\equiv\text{CH} + 2\text{Br}_2 \rightarrow \text{CHBr}_2\text{–CHBr}_2 \quad (\text{1,1,2,2-tetrabromoethane})$$

In sharp contrast, saturated alkanes (such as ethane) **do not react with bromine water in the dark** at room temperature. Therefore, decolourisation of bromine water serves as the universal laboratory **chemical test for unsaturation**!

```
     Test Tube A                             Test Tube B
     (Alkane, e.g., ethane)                  (Alkene, e.g., ethene)

       Gas inlet                               Gas inlet
          |                                       |
       +--|--+                                 +--|--+
       |  |  |                                 |  |  |
       | o|o |  <- bubbling gas                | o|o |  <- bubbling gas
       |~~~~~|                                 |~~~~~|
       |~~~~~|  Bromine water stays            |     |  Bromine water
       +-----+  ORANGE-BROWN                   +-----+  DECOLOURISED (Colourless)
```

> **Figure 5.6** – The bromine water test for unsaturation (teacher demonstration in a fume hood). Left: Saturated alkanes do not react with aqueous bromine at room temperature; the orange-brown colour persists. Right: Unsaturated alkenes and alkynes rapidly add bromine across their multiple bonds, discharging the colour to leave a clear, colourless solution.

### Example 8. Stoichiometry of Bromine Addition

**Problem:** 7.0 g of ethene gas reacts completely with an aqueous solution of bromine: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{Br}_2 \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{Br}_2$. Calculate the mass of bromine consumed and the mass of 1,2-dibromoethane formed. (H = 1, C = 12, Br = 80.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of C₂H₄ = 28 g/mol. Moles = $7.0 \div 28 =$ **0.25 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 1 : 1):**  
- Molar mass of Br₂ = $2 \times 80 = 160$ g/mol.  
  Mass of Br₂ consumed = $0.25\text{ mol} \times 160\text{ g/mol} =$ **40.0 g**.  
- Molar mass of C₂H₄Br₂ = $2(12) + 4(1) + 2(80) = 188$ g/mol.  
  Mass of C₂H₄Br₂ formed = $0.25\text{ mol} \times 188\text{ g/mol} =$ **47.0 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $7.0 + 40.0 = 47.0$ g; Product = $47.0$ g ✅. Only one product is formed, which is the hallmark of an addition reaction.

## Part E: Polymerisation: The Story of Polyethylene

Under conditions of high pressure (1,000–2,000 atm) and elevated temperature (200 °C) in the presence of a trace initiator or specialized catalyst (such as a Ziegler-Natta catalyst), the double bond of an ethene molecule can break open to link covalently with adjacent ethene molecules.

This repeated concatenation of thousands of monomer molecules into a giant macromolecular chain is called **addition polymerisation**:

$$n\ \text{CH}_2=\text{CH}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{catalyst, heat, pressure}} \text{(–CH}_2\text{–CH}_2\text{–)}_n \quad (\text{polyethylene or polythene})$$

```
   Individual ethene monomers                 Giant polymer macromolecule (polyethylene)

   CH₂=CH₂   CH₂=CH₂   CH₂=CH₂   ...   --->   –CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–CH₂–  ...

   (Double bonds open simultaneously, forming durable C–C single bonds throughout)
```

> **Figure 5.7** – Addition polymerisation of ethene to polyethylene. The $\pi$-electron pair of each C=C double bond opens to form covalent $\sigma$-bonds linking neighboring monomer units together into an extended chain of saturated methylene units.

### Example 9. Calculating the Polymer Chain Mass

**Problem:** A specific polymer chain of polyethylene contains 500 polymerized ethene repeating units. Determine: (a) the molecular formula of the chain, (b) its molecular mass, and (c) the factor by which its mass exceeds that of a single ethene monomer. (H = 1, C = 12.)

**Solution:**  
- Each repeating unit is –CH₂–CH₂– (C₂H₄, mass = 28 u).  
- For 500 units: Formula = $\text{C}_{2 \times 500}\text{H}_{4 \times 500} = \mathbf{C_{1000}H_{2000}}$.  
- Total molecular mass = $500 \times 28\text{ u} = \mathbf{14,000\text{ u}}$ (or $1000 \times 12 + 2000 \times 1 = 14,000$ u ✅).  
- Mass factor = exactly **500 times** the mass of one ethene monomer.

**Environmental Imperative:** Polyethylene is chemically inert, flexible, waterproof, and an excellent electrical insulator. However, because its molecular backbone consists entirely of robust, non-polar C–C and C–H single bonds, natural micro-organisms cannot degrade it. It is **non-biodegradable**. 
- Never discard plastic bags into storm drains, waterways, or open soil.
- **Never incinerate waste plastics:** uncontrolled burning generates carcinogenic aromatic hydrocarbons, dioxins, and toxic acidic fumes. Always recycle or reuse plastic packaging.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Addition and substitution reactions are basically the same thing because both introduce new atoms into a molecule."**  
*Correction:* This is a major misconception! While both reactions introduce external atoms, their mechanisms and stoichiometric outcomes are fundamentally different:

- **Substitution (Characteristic of Saturated Alkanes):** A saturated carbon has no vacant capacity. To accept an incoming atom, an existing hydrogen atom must be expelled. Consequently, substitution always produces **two distinct product substances** (the substituted organic compound and a small byproduct such as HCl):
  $$\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$$
- **Addition (Characteristic of Unsaturated Alkenes and Alkynes):** The multiple bond contains an extra electron pair that breaks open, providing two brand-new bonding sites. No atoms are expelled. Consequently, addition combines two reactants into **one single product substance**:
  $$\text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{Br}_2 \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{Br}_2$$

| Reaction Type | Hydrocarbon Class | Number of Products Formed | Byproduct Eliminated? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Substitution** | Alkanes (Saturated) | 2 products | Yes (e.g., HCl) |
| **Addition** | Alkenes & Alkynes (Unsaturated) | 1 single product | No |

## Quick Recap

- **Unsaturated hydrocarbons** contain carbon-carbon double bonds (alkenes, CₙH₂ₙ) or triple bonds (alkynes, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂). The simplest members are ethene (C₂H₄) and ethyne (C₂H₂).
- Carbon maintains its tetravalency (4 bonds) across all families; multiple bonds simply reduce the number of attached hydrogen atoms.
- **Laboratory Preparations:**
  - Ethene: Catalytic dehydration of ethanol by excess concentrated sulfuric acid at 170 °C ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$).
  - Ethyne: Hydrolysis of calcium carbide at room temperature ($\text{CaC}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{C}_2\text{H}_2 + \text{Ca(OH)}_2$).
- **Combustion:** Higher carbon percentages produce smoky, sooty flames in open air. In pure oxygen, ethyne burns cleanly at >3,000 °C in oxy-acetylene torches.
- **Addition Reactions:** Extra shared pairs open to bind external atoms. Alkenes add one equivalent of H₂ (with Ni catalyst) or Br₂; alkynes add two equivalents.
- **Bromine Water Test:** Unsaturated compounds decolourise reddish-brown bromine water to colourless 1,2-dibromo derivatives; saturated alkanes do not react in the dark.
- **Polymerisation:** High pressure and catalysts link thousands of ethene monomers into durable, non-biodegradable polyethylene.
- **Environmental Safety:** Never burn plastics; toxic smoke contains hazardous pollutants.

### Answers

1. Propene: condensed formula $\text{CH}_3\text{CH=CH}_2$. Structural formula: $\text{H}_3\text{C–CH=CH}_2$. Carbon-1: forms 3 C–H bonds + 1 C–C bond = 4 bonds. Carbon-2 (central): forms 1 C–H bond + 1 C–C bond + 2 bonds from C=C = 4 bonds. Carbon-3: forms 2 C–H bonds + 2 bonds from C=C = 4 bonds ✅. Valence electron sum = $3(4) + 6(1) = 18$ electrons $\rightarrow 18 \div 2 =$ **9 covalent bonds**.
2. Bromine adds across the carbon atoms participating in the double bond:  
   - From but-1-ene: $\text{CH}_2\text{Br–CHBr–CH}_2\text{–CH}_3$ (1,2-dibromobutane).  
   - From but-2-ene: $\text{CH}_3\text{–CHBr–CHBr–CH}_3$ (2,3-dibromobutane).  
   Both share the molecular formula C₄H₈Br₂, but the bromine atoms are attached to different carbon positions along the chain; therefore, they are **positional structural isomers** (distinct chemical compounds).
3. Propyne: $\text{CH}_3\text{–C}\equiv\text{CH}$. Valence sum = $3(4\text{ from C}) + 4(1\text{ from H}) = 16$ electrons $\rightarrow 16 \div 2 =$ **8 covalent bonds**. Bond breakdown: 4 C–H bonds + 1 C–C single bond + 3 bonds in C≡C triple bond = $4 + 1 + 3 = 8$ bonds ✅.
4. - C₃H₆: matches CₙH₂ₙ ($n=3$) $\rightarrow$ **Alkene, unsaturated**.  
   - C₂H₂: matches CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ ($n=2$) $\rightarrow$ **Alkyne, unsaturated**.  
   - C₄H₈: matches CₙH₂ₙ ($n=4$) $\rightarrow$ **Alkene, unsaturated**.  
   - C₃H₄: matches CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ ($n=3$) $\rightarrow$ **Alkyne, unsaturated**.  
   - C₂H₄: matches CₙH₂ₙ ($n=2$) $\rightarrow$ **Alkene, unsaturated**.
5. Balancing check: Reactants: C = 6, H = 12, O = 18; Products: C = 6, H = 12, O = $12 + 6 = 18$ ✅. According to Gay-Lussac's law, gas volumes react in the ratio of their stoichiometric coefficients (2 : 9 : 6).  
   - $\text{Volume of O}_2 = 8.0\text{ L} \times \frac{9}{2} =$ **36.0 L**.  
   - $\text{Volume of CO}_2 = 8.0\text{ L} \times \frac{6}{2} =$ **24.0 L**.
6. Molar mass of butene (C₄H₈) = 56 g/mol. Moles = $14.0 \div 56 =$ **0.25 mol**.  
   - $\text{CO}_2\text{ formed} = 0.25 \times 4 = 1.0\text{ mol} = 1.0 \times 44 =$ **44.0 g**.  
   - $\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{ formed} = 0.25 \times 4 = 1.0\text{ mol} = 1.0 \times 18 =$ **18.0 g**.  
   - $\text{O}_2\text{ consumed} = 0.25 \times 6 = 1.5\text{ mol} = 1.5 \times 32 =$ **48.0 g**.

7. Molar mass of C₄H₈ = 56 g/mol. Moles = $11.2 \div 56 =$ **0.20 mol**.  
   - $\text{Volume of H}_2\text{ at STP} = 0.20\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} =$ **4.48 L**.  
   - Mass of butane (C₄H₁₀, M = 58 g/mol) = $0.20 \times 58 =$ **11.6 g**.

8. Molar mass of CaC₂ = 64 g/mol. Moles = $16.0 \div 64 =$ **0.25 mol**.
