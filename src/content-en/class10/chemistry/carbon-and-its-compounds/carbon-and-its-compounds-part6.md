# Topic 6: Ethanol: From Hand Sanitiser to Fermentation

Up to this point, we have focused primarily on **hydrocarbons**—molecules built purely from carbon and hydrogen atoms (alkanes, alkenes, alkynes). In Topic 2, we introduced the concept of a functional group: a specific atom or cluster of atoms that dictates the chemical personality of an entire organic molecule. Today, we conduct an in-depth study of our very first functionalised organic compound: **ethanol (C₂H₅OH)**. In ethanol, a non-polar ethyl hydrocarbon group is bonded to a polar **hydroxyl group (–OH)**. That single oxygen atom completely transforms its physical and chemical properties, making ethanol miscible with water, an effective antiseptic in hand sanitiser, and a versatile industrial chemical. Because this family also includes a **lethal, toxic homologue (methanol)**, safety and chemical vigilance are paramount.

---

## "70 Percent" at the School Entrance

On a brisk Monday morning, Mr. Miller accompanied his nephew Liam to the school entrance. Beside the main doorway stood an automated stainless-steel hand sanitiser dispenser. Every student paused to place their hands beneath the sensor, receiving a small dollop of gel and rubbing their palms together. Liam rubbed the liquid over his hands.

"Uncle, my hands feel instantly cold," Liam observed. "And within just ten seconds, the gel completely **evaporated**—my skin is totally dry, neither wet nor sticky!"

Mr. Miller pointed to the manufacturer's regulatory label on the side of the dispenser: **"Active Ingredient: Ethyl Alcohol 70% v/v. Flammable. Keep away from heat and open flame. For external use only. Keep out of reach of children."**

Liam read the label carefully. "Uncle, what does 70 percent v/v mean? What makes up the other 30 percent? And wouldn't 100 percent pure alcohol kill germs even faster?"

Mr. Miller smiled. "Those are three excellent chemical questions. First, your hands felt cold because ethanol has a lower boiling point than water and **vaporises rapidly**, absorbing heat energy directly from your skin. As for the 70 percent concentration, water is actually a necessary partner: pure 100 percent alcohol dries out so fast that it evaporates before it can penetrate bacterial cell membranes, whereas a 70 percent water-alcohol blend allows the ethanol to diffuse inside and coagulate bacterial proteins."

"Today," Mr. Miller continued, "we will explore how ethanol is synthesised from simple sugars by fermentation, how it reacts chemically, and why an almost identical-looking liquid called **methanol** is an insidious, fatal poison."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Alcohol (homologous series)** | An organic family in which a hydroxyl group (–OH) is covalently bonded to a saturated carbon atom; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁OH | methanol CH₃OH, ethanol C₂H₅OH, propanol C₃H₇OH |
| **Hydroxyl group (–OH)** | The functional group of alcohols, consisting of an oxygen atom covalently linked to a hydrogen atom | –OH group in C₂H₅OH |
| **Fermentation** | The biochemical breakdown of complex carbohydrates or sugars into ethanol and carbon dioxide by yeast enzymes in the absence of oxygen | C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂ |
| **Yeast** | A unicellular microscopic fungus containing biocatalytic enzymes (zymase and invertase) that drive fermentation | *Saccharomyces cerevisiae* |
| **Enzyme** | A biological protein catalyst that accelerates specific biochemical transformations at mild temperatures | zymase converting glucose to ethanol |
| **Oxidation** | A chemical process involving the gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen | C₂H₅OH + 2[O] → CH₃COOH + H₂O |
| **Dehydration** | The chemical elimination of a water molecule from an organic compound | C₂H₅OH → C₂H₄ + H₂O |
| **Denatured spirit** | Industrial ethanol deliberately contaminated with toxic or foul-tasting additives (such as methanol or pyridine) to render it unfit for consumption | methylated spirit |
| **Methanol (CH₃OH)** | The simplest alcohol ($n=1$); a highly toxic, colourless volatile liquid causing blindness and death if ingested | industrial solvent |
| **Fractional distillation** | Separation of a mixture of miscible liquids based on differences in their boiling points | distilling ethanol (78 °C) from water (100 °C) |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Volatile Flammable Solvents and Toxic Alcohols**
> - **Ethanol is highly flammable:** its invisible vapours travel along surfaces and ignite readily from remote sparks or pilot flames. Hand sanitiser and surgical spirits must **never be applied near open flames, cookstoves, or candles**. Always ensure your hands are completely dry before approaching a flame.
> - **Methanol (CH₃OH) is violently toxic.** Swallowing, inhaling, or absorbing even small volumes (as little as 10–30 mL) through the skin causes irreversible optic nerve destruction (permanent blindness), metabolic acidosis, and death. Never taste, sniff, or handle unlabelled bottles.
> - Hand sanitisers and denatured spirits are **strictly for external topical disinfection or fuel use; never ingest them**. If splashed into eyes, flush immediately with copious clean water and seek medical attention.
> - All demonstrations involving sodium metal, concentrated sulfuric acid, acidified potassium dichromate, and fractional distillation are **strictly teacher-led fume hood procedures**. Sodium reacts exothermically, concentrated H₂SO₄ causes severe acid burns, and potassium dichromate is a toxic hexavalent chromium oxidiser.

## Part A: The Ethanol Molecule: An Alkyl Tail and a Polar –OH Head

Ethanol possesses the molecular formula **C₂H₆O** and the condensed structural formula **C₂H₅OH**. As established in Topic 2, it is the second member of the alcohol homologous series ($n=2$). In Topic 3, we noted that C₂H₆O forms two structural isomers: ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$) and methoxymethane ($\text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3$). Today we focus on ethanol.

Inspect its structural formula in Figure 6.1: every carbon atom forms 4 single covalent bonds, the oxygen atom forms 2 covalent bonds, and each hydrogen atom forms 1 covalent bond.

```
         H   H
         |   |
    H -- C - C -- O -- H
         |   |
         H   H
```

> **Figure 6.1** – The complete structural formula of ethanol (C₂H₅OH). Carbon-1 bonds to 3 hydrogens and 1 carbon (4 bonds). Carbon-2 bonds to 2 hydrogens, 1 carbon, and 1 oxygen (4 bonds). The divalent oxygen atom links the carbon skeleton to a terminal hydrogen atom via single covalent bonds.

The O–H covalent bond is distinctly polar: oxygen has a high electronegativity of 3.5, while hydrogen has an electronegativity of 2.1, resulting in a large electronegativity difference of **1.4**. Oxygen pulls shared electrons toward itself, acquiring a partial negative charge ($\delta^-$), while hydrogen carries a partial positive charge ($\delta^+$). In contrast, the C–C and C–H bonds in the ethyl group ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{–}$) are virtually non-polar.

Therefore, an ethanol molecule has a dual chemical nature:

```
       C₂H₅ --------- O -- H
         |               |
     Non-polar       Polar –OH
     alkyl tail     hydrophilic head
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Dual polarity of the ethanol molecule. The polar –OH head forms strong hydrogen bonds with water molecules ("like dissolves like"), explaining why ethanol is **infinitely miscible with water in all proportions**. Meanwhile, the non-polar ethyl tail allows ethanol to dissolve organic non-polar solutes like oils, resins, and fragrances.

### The First Three Members of the Alcohol Homologous Series

```
      H                  H   H                  H   H   H
      |                  |   |                  |   |   |
 H -- C -- O -- H   H -- C - C -- O -- H   H -- C - C - C -- O -- H
      |                  |   |                  |   |   |
      H                  H   H                  H   H   H
   methanol             ethanol                propan-1-ol
    CH₃OH               C₂H₅OH                  C₃H₇OH
```

> **Figure 6.3** – From left to right: methanol (CH₃OH, $n=1$), ethanol (C₂H₅OH, $n=2$), and propan-1-ol (C₃H₇OH, $n=3$). Every successive member adds exactly one –CH₂– unit (mass = 14 u).

- **Methanol (CH₃OH):** Molar mass = $12 + 4(1) + 16 = \mathbf{32\text{ g/mol}}$. Boiling point $\approx 65\ ^\circ\text{C}$.  
- **Ethanol (C₂H₅OH):** Molar mass = $2(12) + 6(1) + 16 = \mathbf{46\text{ g/mol}}$. Boiling point $\approx 78\ ^\circ\text{C}$.  
- **Propanol (C₃H₇OH):** Molar mass = $3(12) + 8(1) + 16 = \mathbf{60\text{ g/mol}}$. Boiling point $\approx 97\ ^\circ\text{C}$.  

Notice that despite being relatively small molecules, alcohols have significantly higher boiling points than their parent alkanes (ethane boils at −89 °C, whereas ethanol boils at +78 °C) because the polar –OH groups establish intermolecular **hydrogen bonding** between neighboring alcohol molecules.

### Physical Properties of Ethanol

| Physical Property | Observed Characteristic |
|---|---|
| **Appearance & Odour** | Clear, colourless, volatile liquid with a characteristic pleasant spirituous scent |
| **Normal Boiling Point** | **78 °C** (substantially lower than water at 100 °C; evaporates rapidly at skin temperature) |
| **Melting Point** | **−114 °C** (remains a liquid in extreme sub-zero conditions; used in alcohol thermometers) |
| **Solubility in Water** | **Completely miscible in all proportions** due to intermolecular hydrogen bonding |
| **Electrical Conductivity** | **Non-conductor** (covalent compound; does not dissociate into free ions in aqueous solution) |
| **Litmus Test** | **Neutral**; shows no colour change with either red or blue litmus paper |
| **Combustion** | Highly flammable; burns with a clean, smokeless, pale blue flame |

### Example 1. Understanding "70% v/v" in Hand Sanitiser

**Problem:** A standard commercial hand sanitiser dispenser bottle contains 250 mL of formulation labelled **"70% v/v Ethyl Alcohol."** Assuming volumes are additive, calculate the volume of pure ethanol and the volume of water/additives contained in the bottle.

**Step 1:** "70% v/v" denotes volume-by-volume percentage: every 100 mL of solution contains 70 mL of pure ethanol.  
**Step 2:** In 250 mL of sanitiser:  
$$\text{Volume of pure ethanol} = 250\text{ mL} \times \frac{70}{100} = \mathbf{175\text{ mL}}$$  
**Step 3:** Volume of water and inactive excipients (emollients, gel thickeners):  
$$\text{Volume of aqueous base} = 250\text{ mL} - 175\text{ mL} = \mathbf{75\text{ mL}}$$  

**Check:** $\frac{175}{250} = 0.70 = 70\%$ ✅. *(In precise physical chemistry, mixing ethanol and water causes a slight molecular volume contraction due to close hydrogen bonding packing, so this calculation represents an idealised operational volume.)*

## Part B: How Is Ethanol Produced?

### Method 1: Fermentation of Sugars Using Yeast

The traditional and renewable biological method of producing ethanol is the **anaerobic fermentation of natural sugars** (such as glucose, fruit juice, or sugar-cane molasses) by **yeast**:

$$\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \xrightarrow{\text{yeast (zymase enzyme), } 30\text{–}35\ ^\circ\text{C}} 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2 \uparrow + \text{heat}$$

**Atom Balance Check:**  
Reactants: C = 6, H = 12, O = 6.  
Products: C = $2(2) + 2(1) = 6$; H = $2(6) = 12$; O = $2(1) + 2(2) = 6$ ✅.

**Key Biochemical Conditions for Successful Fermentation:**
1. **Optimum Temperature (30–35 °C):** Yeast organisms operate through living protein enzymes (such as invertase and zymase). At lower temperatures (<15 °C), enzyme kinetics are sluggish; at elevated temperatures (**>40–50 °C**), the delicate three-dimensional protein structures **denature**, permanently deactivating the enzymes and halting fermentation completely.
2. **Anaerobic Environment:** Fermentation must occur in the absence of atmospheric oxygen. If air is admitted, aerobic bacteria (*Acetobacter*) oxidise the ethanol into sour ethanoic acid (vinegar).
3. **Byproduct Carbon Dioxide:** The vigorous evolution of CO₂ gas creates foaming bubbles, which can be verified by turning clear lime water milky: $\text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{CO}_2 \rightarrow \text{CaCO}_3 \downarrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.

```
          Delivery tube (open exhaust)
        .----------------------.
        |                      |
     [Stopper]                 |
      |  |                  |  |       |   <-- Open beaker
      |  |                  |~~|~~~~~~~|   <-- Clear limewater (Ca(OH)₂)
      |  |                  |~~o~~~~~~~|       Turns milky as CO₂ bubbles through
   ___|  |___               |~~~~~~~~~~|
  /          \              +----------+
 |  Glucose + |
 |  yeast     |   Warm environment: 30–35 °C incubator
 |  solution  |
  \__________/
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Experimental setup for yeast fermentation (teacher demonstration). A warm aqueous solution of glucose and active baker's yeast ferments inside a conical flask. The liberated carbon dioxide bubbles through clear limewater, turning it milky, while the ethanol accumulates inside the aqueous fermentation broth.

### Example 2. Stoichiometry of Glucose Fermentation

**Problem:** In a laboratory demonstration, 90 g of pure glucose undergoes complete anaerobic fermentation: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2$. Determine: (a) the theoretical mass of ethanol produced, (b) the mass of carbon dioxide released, and (c) the volume of CO₂ gas evolved at STP. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16; molar volume at STP = 22.4 L/mol.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$) = $6(12) + 12(1) + 6(16) = 180$ g/mol.  
Moles of glucose = $90\text{ g} \div 180\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.50 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 2 : 2):**  
- Moles of ethanol = $0.50 \times 2 = \mathbf{1.0\text{ mol}}$.  
  Mass of ethanol = $1.0\text{ mol} \times 46\text{ g/mol} =$ **46.0 g**.  
- Moles of $\text{CO}_2 = 0.50 \times 2 = \mathbf{1.0\text{ mol}}$.  
  Mass of $\text{CO}_2 = 1.0\text{ mol} \times 44\text{ g/mol} =$ **44.0 g**.  
  Volume of $\text{CO}_2\text{ at STP} = 1.0\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} =$ **22.4 L**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:** Reactant = 90.0 g; Products = $46.0\text{ g (ethanol)} + 44.0\text{ g (CO}_2) = 90.0\text{ g}$ ✅.  
*(Given the density of ethanol is approximately 0.79 g/mL, 46 g corresponds to a liquid volume of $46 \div 0.79 \approx 58.2$ mL.)*

### Purifying Ethanol by Fractional Distillation

Natural yeast cells cannot survive in high concentrations of alcohol; once the ethanol content reaches approximately **12% to 15% by volume**, the alcohol poisons the yeast cells, halting biological fermentation.

To concentrate the ethanol, the fermented wash is subjected to **fractional distillation**. Ethanol boils at **78 °C**, whereas water boils at **100 °C**. When the mixture is gently heated, ethanol vaporises preferentially:

```
        Thermometer
             |
          +--+--+
          |Cork |----- Vapour ----->\
   _______|     |_______             \_____________________
  /        +---+        \              Liebig Condenser (cold water jacket)
 |  Ethanol + water      |              Water OUT ^ (upper nozzle)
 |  fermented wash       |                        |
 |  (+ boiling chips)    |    Sloping downward:   |
  \_____________________/               Water IN  | (lower nozzle)
    ~ Electric mantle ~                           |
    (NO open flames near                          v
     flammable ethanol)                      [ Receiver flask: open ]
                                             (Ethanol-rich distillate)
```

> **Figure 6.5** – Fractional distillation apparatus for purifying ethanol (teacher demonstration). The flask is heated safely using an electric heating mantle or water bath to prevent open flame contact. The thermometer bulb is positioned precisely adjacent to the side-arm outlet to measure vapour temperature (78 °C). Cold cooling water flows into the bottom nozzle of the condenser jacket and exits through the top nozzle to maintain a counter-current thermal gradient. The receiver collects a concentrated distillate of up to 95.6% ethanol.

Ordinary fractional distillation cannot exceed a concentration of 95.6% ethanol because ethanol and water form a constant-boiling azeotropic mixture at that ratio. To produce **absolute alcohol (99.5–100% pure ethanol)**, specialized dehydrating chemicals (like quicklime, CaO) or industrial pressure-swing distillation are employed.

### Method 2: Industrial Hydration of Ethene (Overview)

In large-scale chemical manufacturing, ethanol is produced petrochemically by the **catalytic hydration of ethene** using superheated steam under high pressure (60–70 atm) at 300 °C in the presence of a phosphoric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$) catalyst:

$$\text{CH}_2\text{=CH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O (steam)} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4,\ 300\ ^\circ\text{C},\ 65\text{ atm}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

This is an **electrophilic addition reaction** (as introduced in Topic 5) across the C=C double bond.

| Feature Comparison | Yeast Fermentation | Industrial Ethene Hydration |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary Raw Material** | Renewable biomass (sugar cane, corn, molasses) | Non-renewable petroleum cracking products (ethene) |
| **Operating Conditions** | Mild atmospheric pressure, warm ambient (30–35 °C) | High temperature (300 °C), severe pressure (65 atm) |
| **Product Purity** | Dilute aqueous wash (~10–14%), requires distillation | Continuous, direct high-purity ethanol stream |
| **Byproduct Stream** | Carbon dioxide gas (CO₂) | Negligible side-products |

> **Figure 6.6** – The chemical reaction map of ethanol. Upward: Catalytic dehydration yields unsaturated ethene. Left: Reaction with sodium liberates hydrogen gas. Right: Complete combustion produces carbon dioxide and water. Downward: Controlled oxidation yields ethanoic acid (acetic acid).

### 1. Combustion of Ethanol

Ethanol ignites easily, burning with a steady, clean, pale blue flame with high calorific output:

$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O} + 1367\text{ kJ/mol}$$

Because it burns without smoke or soot, ethanol is used in spirit lamps, fondue burners, and as an eco-friendly **biofuel blend (e.g., E10 or E20: 10% to 20% ethanol blended into automotive gasoline)** to reduce carbon monoxide exhaust emissions.

**Example 3. Complete Combustion of 9.2 g of Ethanol**

**Problem:** 9.2 g of ethanol combusts completely in oxygen: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Calculate the mass of oxygen consumed, the mass and STP volume of CO₂ evolved, and the mass of water formed.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of ethanol = 46 g/mol. Moles = $9.2 \div 46 =$ **0.20 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 3 : 2 : 3):**  
- $\text{Moles of O}_2 = 0.20 \times 3 = 0.60\text{ mol}$. Mass of $\text{O}_2 = 0.60 \times 32 =$ **19.2 g**.  
- $\text{Moles of CO}_2 = 0.20 \times 2 = 0.40\text{ mol}$. Mass of $\text{CO}_2 = 0.40 \times 44 =$ **17.6 g**.  
  $\text{Volume of CO}_2\text{ at STP} = 0.40 \times 22.4 =$ **8.96 L**.  
- $\text{Moles of H}_2\text{O} = 0.20 \times 3 = 0.60\text{ mol}$. Mass of $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 0.60 \times 18 =$ **10.8 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $9.2 + 19.2 = 28.4$ g; Products = $17.6 + 10.8 = 28.4$ g ✅.

### 2. Reaction with Sodium Metal (Teacher Demonstration Only)

When a freshly cut, pea-sized pellet of clean **sodium metal** is dropped into absolute, water-free ethanol, it sinks to the bottom and effervesces steadily, liberating **hydrogen gas** and forming a clear solution of **sodium ethoxide**:

$$2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{Na} \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$$

**Critical Observations:**
- Only the single hydrogen atom attached to the oxygen atom is displaced; the five hydrogens on the carbon skeleton remain intact.
- The reaction is significantly calmer and gentler than the violent, explosive reaction between sodium and liquid water, but the liberated hydrogen gas still burns with a characteristic **"pop" sound** when tested with a burning splint.
- Evaporating the resulting solution leaves a white crystalline solid: sodium ethoxide ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{O}^-\text{Na}^+$).

**Example 4. Reaction of 2.3 g of Sodium with Ethanol**

**Problem:** 2.3 g of sodium metal reacts completely with dry ethanol. Determine the mass of ethanol consumed, the STP volume of hydrogen evolved, and the mass of sodium ethoxide produced. (Na = 23, C = 12, H = 1, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Moles of sodium = $2.3\text{ g} \div 23\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.10 mol**.  
Mole ratio $2\text{ Na} : 2\text{ C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} : 2\text{ C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa} : 1\text{ H}_2$.  
**Step 2:**  
- Moles of ethanol required = 0.10 mol. Mass = $0.10 \times 46 =$ **4.6 g**.  
- Moles of sodium ethoxide ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa}$, M = $24 + 5 + 16 + 23 = 68$ g/mol) = 0.10 mol.  
  Mass of sodium ethoxide = $0.10 \times 68 =$ **6.8 g**.  
- Moles of $\text{H}_2 = 0.10 \div 2 = 0.050\text{ mol}$.  
  Volume of $\text{H}_2\text{ at STP} = 0.050 \times 22.4 =$ **1.12 L** (mass = $0.050 \times 2 = 0.10$ g).  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $2.3 + 4.6 = 6.9$ g; Products = $6.8 + 0.10 = 6.9$ g ✅.

### 3. Oxidation: Converting Ethanol to Ethanoic Acid

When warmed with powerful chemical oxidising agents, such as **acidified potassium dichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7 / \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)** or **alkaline potassium permanganate ($\text{KMnO}_4 / \text{NaOH}$)**, ethanol oxidises to **ethanoic acid (acetic acid)**:

$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2[\text{O}] \xrightarrow{\text{acidified } \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7,\ \text{heat}} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Observable Diagnostic Colour Changes:**
- **Acidified Potassium Dichromate:** The orange solution of dichromate ions ($\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7^{2-}$) is reduced to deep **green chromium ions ($\text{Cr}^{3+}$)** as it oxidises ethanol. *(This distinct orange-to-green colour transition formed the chemical basis of early police breathalyser tests for alcohol screening).*
- **Alkaline Potassium Permanganate:** The intense **purple colour of $\text{MnO}_4^-$ ions is decolourised**.

**Example 5. Controlled Oxidation of 4.6 g of Ethanol**

**Problem:** 4.6 g of ethanol is completely oxidised to ethanoic acid: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2[\text{O}] \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. Calculate the mass of ethanoic acid formed, the mass of water generated, and the mass of nascent oxygen $[\text{O}]$ consumed. (Ethanoic acid CH₃COOH = 60 g/mol.)

**Step 1:** Moles of ethanol = $4.6 \div 46 =$ **0.10 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Mole ratio 1 : 2 : 1 : 1):**  
- Moles of $[\text{O}]$ consumed = $0.10 \times 2 = 0.20\text{ mol}$. Mass of $[\text{O}] = 0.20 \times 16 =$ **3.2 g**.  
- Moles of ethanoic acid = 0.10 mol. Mass of $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} = 0.10 \times 60 =$ **6.0 g**.  
- Moles of water = 0.10 mol. Mass of $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 0.10 \times 18 =$ **1.8 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $4.6 + 3.2 = 7.8$ g; Products = $6.0 + 1.8 = 7.8$ g ✅.

### 4. Dehydration: Converting Ethanol to Ethene

As introduced in Topic 5, heating ethanol with an excess of concentrated sulfuric acid at **170 °C** causes intermolecular elimination of water:

$$\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ 170\ ^\circ\text{C}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_4 \uparrow + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Concentrated sulfuric acid acts strictly as a **catalytic dehydrating agent**. If the temperature is maintained lower (~140 °C), an ether is formed instead.

**Example 6. Dehydration of 11.5 g of Ethanol**

**Problem:** 11.5 g of ethanol undergoes complete dehydration at 170 °C. Calculate the mass and STP volume of ethene gas liberated and the mass of water eliminated.

**Step 1:** Moles of ethanol = $11.5 \div 46 =$ **0.25 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Mole ratio 1 : 1 : 1):**  
- Moles of ethene = 0.25 mol. Mass = $0.25 \times 28 =$ **7.0 g**.  
  $\text{STP volume of ethene} = 0.25 \times 22.4 =$ **5.6 L**.  
- Moles of water = 0.25 mol. Mass = $0.25 \times 18 =$ **4.5 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = 11.5 g; Products = $7.0 + 4.5 = 11.5$ g ✅.

## Part E: Methanol, Denatured Spirit, and the Hazards of Alcohol Abuse

### Methanol (CH₃OH): The Lethal Homologue

**Methanol (methyl alcohol, CH₃OH)** is the simplest alcohol ($n=1$), boiling at 65 °C. To the human senses, it is completely indistinguishable from ethanol: it is a clear, colourless liquid with a very similar solvent scent. 

However, inside the human body, the enzyme **alcohol dehydrogenase** oxidises methanol in the liver into **methanal (formaldehyde, HCHO)**, which rapidly oxidises further into **methanoic acid (formic acid, HCOOH)**:
- Methanal vigorously attacks cellular protoplasm, coagulating intracellular proteins.
- Methanoic acid selectively attacks and destroys the cells of the **optic nerve**, causing irreversible, **permanent blindness**.
- Ingestion of merely **10 mL to 30 mL** leads to catastrophic metabolic acidosis, respiratory failure, coma, and death.

> **Absolute Safety Rule:** Never taste, sniff, or apply unlabelled chemical liquids. The difference of a single –CH₂– unit between ethanol and methanol is literally the difference between an everyday antiseptic and a lethal metabolic poison!

### Denatured Spirit (Industrial Methylated Spirit)

To make ethanol available at low cost for industrial manufacturing, laboratories, and spirit heating lamps without incurring heavy beverage excise taxation, manufacturers deliberately "denature" the spirit.

**Denatured spirit** consists of ethanol deliberately blended with:
- Approximately 5% to 10% **poisonous methanol**.
- Traces of foul-smelling, intensely bitter organic compounds like **pyridine** or bitrex.
- A distinctive **coloured dye** (usually brilliant purple or blue) to provide an unmistakable visual warning that the liquid is toxic and completely unfit for drinking.

### Adulterated and Illicit Liquor

Periodically, tragedy strikes when unscrupulous black-market distillers brew counterfeit or illicit alcoholic beverages using cheap industrial methylated spirit or contaminated distillation equipment. Hundreds of unsuspecting victims suffer immediate blindness, organ failure, and death from **methanol poisoning**. Under no circumstances should uncertified, bootleg, or unsealed liquids ever be consumed.

### Physiological Harm of Alcohol Consumption (Factual Health Summary)

When ingested into the human bloodstream, ethanol acts as a powerful **central nervous system depressant**:
1. **Neurological Impairment:** It slows neural transmission, impairing judgment, emotional inhibition, muscular coordination, and reflexes. This makes driving under the influence of alcohol a leading cause of fatal road accidents.
2. **Liver Pathology:** The liver processes alcohol through metabolic oxidation. Chronic alcohol abuse overwhelms liver enzymes, causing fatty liver deposits, irreversible scarring of hepatic tissue (**liver cirrhosis**), and eventual liver failure.
3. **Adolescent Vulnerability:** The brain and nervous system undergo critical development through adolescence and teenage years. Alcohol consumption during this formative stage causes long-lasting cognitive deficits and carries a severe risk of chemical addiction. Minors must strictly abstain from alcohol.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Ethanol and methanol belong to the same homologous series, so they must be equally safe for skin use."**  
   *Correction:* This is a dangerous and potentially fatal mistake. While they belong to the same homologous series, the human liver metabolises them into entirely different chemical compounds. Ethanol oxidises into relatively harmless ethanoic acid (which enters the body's normal Krebs cycle), whereas methanol oxidises into **formaldehyde and formic acid**, which destroy the optic nerve and cause blindness or death. Methanol must **never** be used in sanitisers or applied to human skin.

2. **Mistake 2: "Ethanol has an '–OH' group in its formula, so it must be a basic metal hydroxide like NaOH."**  
   *Correction:* In sodium hydroxide ($\text{Na}^+\text{OH}^-$), the compound is ionic, dissociating in water to release free hydroxide ions ($\text{OH}^-$), which turn red litmus blue. In ethanol ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$), the oxygen and hydrogen atoms are linked to carbon by **strong covalent bonds**. Ethanol **does not dissociate into hydroxide ions in water**!  
   - It has no effect on red or blue litmus paper (**it is strictly neutral**).  
   - An aqueous solution of ethanol does not conduct electricity (tester bulb does not glow).

| Test | Sodium Hydroxide (NaOH) | Ethanol (C₂H₅OH) |
|---|---|---|
| **Bonding Type** | Ionic lattice ($\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{OH}^-$ ions) | Covalent molecular compound |
| **Ions Formed in Water** | Releases free aqueous $\text{OH}^-$ ions | Zero ions; dissolves as intact neutral molecules |
| **Litmus Action** | Basic: turns red litmus blue | **Neutral:** no colour change on either litmus |
| **Conductivity** | Strong electrolyte (bulb shines brightly) | Non-electrolyte (bulb remains dark) |

## Quick Recap

- **Ethanol (C₂H₅OH):** The second member of the alcohol family ($n=2$); features a non-polar ethyl tail ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{–}$) and a polar hydroxyl head (–OH).
- **Physical Trends:** Clear, colourless liquid, b.p. 78 °C, m.p. −114 °C; completely miscible with water via hydrogen bonding; neutral to litmus; non-electrolyte.
- **Production:**
  - Biochemical: Yeast fermentation of glucose at 30–35 °C: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2$, followed by fractional distillation.
  - Industrial: Catalytic hydration of ethene with steam at 300 °C over phosphoric acid.
- **Chemical Reactions:**
  - Combustion: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (clean blue flame).
  - Reaction with active sodium: $2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{Na} \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow$ (effervescence of hydrogen).
  - Oxidation: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2[\text{O}] \xrightarrow{\text{acidified } \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (orange dichromate turns green).
  - Dehydration: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \xrightarrow{\text{conc. } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4,\ 170\ ^\circ\text{C}} \text{C}_2\text{H}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (ethene formed).
- **Toxicity & Safety:** Methanol (CH₃OH) causes blindness and death; denatured spirit contains foul/toxic additives to prevent drinking. Ethanol is a central nervous system depressant that damages the liver over time.

## Practice Questions

*(All calculations are for paper practice. Atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23. Molar volume of any gas at STP = 22.4 L/mol.)*

1. A school science department purchases 5.0 litres of bulk hand sanitiser formulated with 60% v/v ethanol. Calculate the volume of pure ethanol and the volume of aqueous additives present in the container (assuming ideal additive volumes).
2. 36.0 g of pure glucose undergoes complete anaerobic fermentation: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2$. Determine the mass of ethanol produced, the mass of carbon dioxide evolved, and the volume of CO₂ gas at STP.
3. Calculate the percentage by mass of oxygen in methanol (CH₃OH), ethanol (C₂H₅OH), and propanol (C₃H₇OH). Describe the mathematical trend observed as the carbon chain lengthens.
4. In a teacher demonstration, 3.45 g of clean sodium metal reacts completely with dry absolute ethanol: $2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{Na} \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa} + \text{H}_2$. Calculate the mass of ethanol consumed, the STP volume of hydrogen evolved, and the mass of sodium ethoxide formed.
5. 18.4 g of ethanol is completely oxidised to ethanoic acid: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2[\text{O}] \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. Determine the mass of ethanoic acid formed, the mass of water generated, and the mass of nascent oxygen required. Verify conservation of mass.
6. 34.5 g of ethanol is completely dehydrated by heating with excess concentrated sulfuric acid at 170 °C. Calculate the mass of ethene produced, its volume at STP, and the mass of water eliminated.
7. 13.8 g of ethanol burns completely in air. Calculate the mass of oxygen consumed, the mass of carbon dioxide released, and the mass of water vapour produced.
8. Propanol is the next member in the homologous series after ethanol.  
   (a) Write its molecular formula and draw the complete structural formula of propan-1-ol, confirming the valency of every atom.  
   (b) By how many atomic mass units does its molar mass differ from ethanol?  
   (c) Predict whether propan-1-ol has a higher or lower boiling point than ethanol, stating the chemical principle applied.
9. State whether each statement is True or False. Correct all false statements:  
   (a) Ethanol is a base because its molecular formula terminates in an –OH group.  
   (b) Ethanol mixes with liquid water in all proportions.  
   (c) Yeast enzymes in fermentation achieve optimal activity at 80 °C.  
   (d) Denatured spirit is safe to consume because its main ingredient is ethanol.  
   (e) Methanol and ethanol carry identical health and safety profiles.
10. Two colourless organic liquids both share the molecular formula C₂H₆O. Liquid X reacts vigorously with sodium metal to produce bubbles of hydrogen gas. Liquid Y exhibits no reaction with sodium. Identify compounds X and Y, and name the type of isomerism they illustrate.
11. A student incubates a flask containing glucose solution and active baker's yeast at 80 °C. After 48 hours, no effervescence of gas is observed, and no ethanol has formed. Explain why the biochemical process failed, referencing enzyme kinetics.
12. A student claims: *"Ethanol must be basic because it reacts with active sodium metal to evolve hydrogen gas."* Present two distinct experimental proofs demonstrating that ethanol is neutral, and explain why hydrogen evolution with sodium does not signify acidity or basicity in water.
13. A technician blends 90 mL of pure ethanol with 60 mL of distilled water. Calculate the percentage by volume of ethanol in this mixture. Does this concentration satisfy the effective virucidal range required for antiseptic hand sanitisers (60% to 70% v/v)?
14. Ethanol boils at 78 °C, while water boils at 100 °C. A 50/50 mixture of the two liquids is heated to 85 °C in a distillation apparatus. Explain which compound predominates in the rising vapour and why the condensed distillate is substantially richer in ethanol than the starting boiler liquid.
15. State three mandatory safety rules for storing and using hand sanitiser at home. In one concise sentence, explain why an unlabelled chemical container must never be tasted, smelled, or used.

### Answers

1. 60% v/v in 5.0 L (5,000 mL):  
   - $\text{Volume of ethanol} = 5,000\text{ mL} \times \frac{60}{100} = \mathbf{3,000\text{ mL (3.0 L)}}$.  
   - $\text{Volume of aqueous base} = 5,000 - 3,000 = \mathbf{2,000\text{ mL (2.0 L)}}$ ✅.
2. Molar mass of glucose = 180 g/mol. Moles = $36.0 \div 180 = \mathbf{0.20\text{ mol}}$.  
   From $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2$:  
   - $\text{Moles of ethanol} = 0.20 \times 2 = 0.40\text{ mol}$. Mass = $0.40 \times 46 =$ **18.4 g**.  
   - $\text{Moles of CO}_2 = 0.20 \times 2 = 0.40\text{ mol}$. Mass = $0.40 \times 44 =$ **17.6 g**.  
     $\text{STP volume of CO}_2 = 0.40 \times 22.4 =$ **8.96 L**.  
   Mass check: $18.4 + 17.6 = 36.0$ g ✅.
3. Oxygen mass percentages (each molecule contains 1 oxygen atom = 16 u):  
   - Methanol (CH₃OH, M = 32): $\frac{16}{32} \times 100 =$ **50.0%**.  
   - Ethanol (C₂H₅OH, M = 46): $\frac{16}{46} \times 100 =$ **34.8%**.  
   - Propanol (C₃H₇OH, M = 60): $\frac{16}{60} \times 100 =$ **26.7%**.  
   **Trend:** As the hydrocarbon chain lengthens, the percentage of oxygen by mass **steadily decreases**, because the heavy hydrocarbon portion constitutes a progressively larger fraction of the total molecular mass.
4. Moles of sodium = $3.45\text{ g} \div 23\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.15\text{ mol}}$.  
   - Moles of ethanol consumed = 0.15 mol. Mass = $0.15 \times 46 =$ **6.90 g**.  
   - Moles of sodium ethoxide ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{ONa}$, M = 68) = 0.15 mol. Mass = $0.15 \times 68 =$ **10.20 g**.  
   - Moles of $\text{H}_2 = 0.15 \div 2 = 0.075\text{ mol}$. $\text{STP volume} = 0.075 \times 22.4 =$ **1.68 L** (mass = 0.15 g).  
   Mass check: $3.45 + 6.90 = 10.35$ g; $10.20 + 0.15 = 10.35$ g ✅.
5. Moles of ethanol = $18.4 \div 46 = \mathbf{0.40\text{ mol}}$.  
   - Moles of ethanoic acid = 0.40 mol. Mass = $0.40 \times 60 =$ **24.0 g**.  
   - Moles of water = 0.40 mol. Mass = $0.40 \times 18 =$ **7.2 g**.  
   - Moles of $[\text{O}]$ consumed = $0.40 \times 2 = 0.80\text{ mol}$. Mass = $0.80 \times 16 =$ **12.8 g**.  
   Conservation check: Reactants = $18.4 + 12.8 = 31.2$ g; Products = $24.0 + 7.2 = 31.2$ g ✅.
6. Moles of ethanol = $34.5 \div 46 = \mathbf{0.75\text{ mol}}$.  
   - Moles of ethene = 0.75 mol. Mass = $0.75 \times 28 =$ **21.0 g**.  
     $\text{STP volume} = 0.75 \times 22.4 =$ **16.8 L**.  
   - Moles of water eliminated = 0.75 mol. Mass = $0.75 \times 18 =$ **13.5 g**.  
   Mass check: $21.0 + 13.5 = 34.5$ g ✅.
7. Moles of ethanol = $13.8 \div 46 = \mathbf{0.30\text{ mol}}$.  
   From $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$:  
   - $\text{Mass of O}_2 = (0.30 \times 3)\text{ mol} \times 32\text{ g/mol} = 0.90 \times 32 =$ **28.8 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of CO}_2 = (0.30 \times 2)\text{ mol} \times 44\text{ g/mol} = 0.60 \times 44 =$ **26.4 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of H}_2\text{O} = (0.30 \times 3)\text{ mol} \times 18\text{ g/mol} = 0.90 \times 18 =$ **16.2 g**.  
   Check: $13.8 + 28.8 = 42.6$ g; $26.4 + 16.2 = 42.6$ g ✅.
8. (a) Propan-1-ol molecular formula: C₃H₈O; condensed formula: $\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$.  
   ```
        H   H   H
        |   |   |
   H -- C - C - C -- O -- H
        |   |   |
        H   H   H
   ```
   Valency check: Each C forms 4 bonds; O forms 2 bonds; each H forms 1 bond ✅.  
   (b) Difference in molar mass = $60 - 46 = \mathbf{14\text{ u}}$ (exactly one –CH₂– unit).  
   (c) Its boiling point is **higher** than that of ethanol (97 °C vs 78 °C) because the longer carbon chain increases molecular surface area and strengthens intermolecular London dispersion forces across the homologous series.
9. (a) **False.** Ethanol is a covalent compound that does not dissociate into hydroxide ions in water; it is strictly **neutral**.  
   (b) **True.** The polar –OH group establishes strong hydrogen bonding with water.  
   (c) **False.** The enzymes in yeast denature and deactivate above 40–45 °C; optimal fermentation occurs at **30–35 °C**.  
   (d) **False.** Denatured spirit is deliberately adulterated with toxic poisons (methanol/pyridine) to prevent human consumption.  
   (e) **False.** Methanol is violently toxic, causing blindness and death at low dosages.
10. Liquid X is **ethanol ($\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH}$)**, which contains a terminal –OH group with an active hydrogen that is displaced by sodium. Liquid Y is **methoxymethane (dimethyl ether, $\text{CH}_3\text{OCH}_3$)**, which lacks an –OH group and cannot react with sodium. They illustrate **functional group isomerism**.
11. Yeast is a living biological organism whose catalytic enzymes (such as zymase) consist of complex protein chains. At 80 °C, excessive thermal kinetic energy permanently **denatures the tertiary structure of the enzyme proteins**, destroying their catalytic active sites. As a consequence, biochemical fermentation stops completely.
12. Experimental proof that ethanol is neutral:  
    (1) It causes no colour change on either red or blue litmus paper.  
    (2) An aqueous solution of ethanol does not conduct electricity (tester bulb does not illuminate), proving the absence of free $\text{H}^+$ or $\text{OH}^-$ ions.  
    *Explanation:* Sodium reacts with ethanol because metallic sodium is an exceptionally strong electropositive reducing agent that can displace the weakly acidic proton from the covalent O–H bond, not because the solution contains dissociated aqueous ions.
13. Total solution volume = $90 + 60 = 150$ mL.  
    $$\text{Volume percentage of ethanol} = \frac{90}{150} \times 100 = \mathbf{60\%\text{ v/v}}$$  
    **Yes**, 60% v/v satisfies the established medical threshold (60% to 70% v/v) required to reliably dissolve lipid envelopes and denature microbial proteins.
14. Ethanol has a lower boiling point (78 °C) than water (100 °C) and is therefore significantly **more volatile** (it has a higher equilibrium vapour pressure at 85 °C). Consequently, the vapour escaping the flask contains a far higher mole fraction of ethanol than the liquid below. When this vapour condenses inside the water-cooled condenser, the collected liquid is substantially enriched in ethanol.
15. Safety rules: (1) Keep containers tightly closed and strictly away from cooking gas stoves, candles, and sparks; (2) Allow hands to dry thoroughly after sanitising before striking a match or approaching a stove; (3) Store out of reach of young children.  
    *Unlabelled bottle rule:* An unlabelled container could contain **lethal methanol**, which looks and smells like ethanol but causes permanent blindness and death if ingested or absorbed.
