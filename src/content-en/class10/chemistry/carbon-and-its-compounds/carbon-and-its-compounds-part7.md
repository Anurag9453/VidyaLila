# Topic 7: Ethanoic Acid: Vinegar and Its Esters

In Topic 6, we discovered that when ethanol is oxidised with acidified potassium dichromate, it is converted into ethanoic acid: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2[\text{O}] \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. Now we investigate **ethanoic acid** directly. This is the classic weak organic acid whose chemical equilibrium you encountered in Chapters 1 and 4: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+$. Today, we inspect its molecular architecture: which of its four hydrogen atoms is acidic, how it effervesces with carbonates, how it neutralises alkalis to form salts, and how heating it with alcohols creates sweet, fruit-scented **esters**. Our dual investigation today: where does that sharp sour bite come from, and how does it give birth to exquisite fruity aromas?

## Two Jars, Two Aromas

On a pleasant Sunday afternoon, Mr. Evans took his nephew Noah to visit his grandmother's rural homestead pantry. On a wide wooden shelf by the pantry window rested a large sealed glass jar of **homemade pickled lemons**, bathed in a pale translucent vinegar brine. When Grandmother loosened the airtight clamp lid, a sharp, pungent, unmistakably sour vapour filled the room. Noah wrinkled his nose.

"Uncle, that aroma is remarkably sharp and sour!" Noah remarked. "Grandmother says vinegar prevents the pickles from spoiling for months on end. How does it preserve food?"

As they stepped across the pantry, Grandmother pointed to an old earthenware jar tucked in a cool, dark corner. When she lifted the stone lid, a completely **different aroma** drifted into the air: a delicate, sweet, **fruity fragrance** reminiscent of ripe pears and pineapples. The jar contained artisanal fruit cider vinegar that had aged undisturbed for over two years. Noah paused, comparing the two scents: "Uncle, vinegar smells sharply acidic. How did an old jar of fruit vinegar develop this wonderfully sweet, perfume-like scent?"

Mr. Evans smiled warmly. "The sharp sour bite comes from pure **ethanoic acid**—the natural acid of vinegar. And that sweet, fragrant aroma? That is a chemical offspring: an **ester** formed when ethanoic acid slowly reacts with trace amounts of alcohol over time. Let us explore both stories: first the chemistry of the acid, and then the delightful esters it creates."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Carboxylic acid** | An organic family containing the carboxyl functional group (–COOH) attached to a carbon skeleton | HCOOH, CH₃COOH, C₂H₅COOH |
| **Carboxyl group (–COOH)** | A functional group consisting of a carbonyl group (C=O) linked directly to a hydroxyl group (–OH) on the same carbon | –COOH in ethanoic acid |
| **Ethanoic acid** | The systematic IUPAC name for the two-carbon carboxylic acid, CH₃COOH (common name: acetic acid) | active acid in vinegar |
| **Vinegar** | A 5% to 8% aqueous solution of ethanoic acid used as a culinary condiment and preservative | pickling vinegar |
| **Glacial ethanoic acid** | Highly concentrated, anhydrous (>99.5%) ethanoic acid that freezes into ice-like crystals at ~17 °C | laboratory reagent (corrosive) |
| **Ester** | A sweet-smelling, volatile organic compound formed by the condensation of a carboxylic acid with an alcohol, containing the –COO– ester linkage | ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOC₂H₅ |
| **Esterification** | The reversible acid-catalysed condensation reaction between a carboxylic acid and an alcohol yielding an ester and water | CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O |
| **Hydrolysis** | The chemical cleavage of a chemical bond by water, such as splitting an ester back into its parent acid and alcohol | reversal of esterification |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Organic Acids, Flammable Alcohols, and Catalytic Reagents**
> - **Household culinary vinegar** (5% to 8% aqueous ethanoic acid) is safe for simple tabletop observation, but must **never be splashed into eyes**. Always wash hands thoroughly after handling.
> - **Glacial ethanoic acid** (>99.5% pure) is **dangerously corrosive and flammable**. It causes severe, painful chemical burns on skin and its pungent, choking vapours cause intense respiratory and ocular irritation. It is handled **strictly by certified teachers inside a fume hood**.
> - **Esterification demonstrations** involve three hazardous chemicals simultaneously: flammable ethanol, corrosive glacial ethanoic acid, and **concentrated sulfuric acid** (an aggressive dehydrating agent). This reaction must be conducted **exclusively as a teacher demonstration** using an electric water bath—**never near an open Bunsen flame**.
> - Never attempt to synthesise esters using methanol at home: methanol is violently toxic.
> - Never inhale deep breaths of concentrated acid vapours or esters directly from containers. Always waft vapours gently toward your nose using your hand if instructed by a teacher.

## Part A: Structure of Ethanoic Acid: The Secret of the –COOH Group

Ethanoic acid has the molecular formula **C₂H₄O₂** and the condensed structural formula **CH₃COOH**. It is the second member of the carboxylic acid homologous series ($n=1$ in the formula $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{COOH}$, where $n$ represents the alkyl carbon count).

Inspect its structural formula in Figure 7.1:

```
         H       O
         |       ||
    H -- C ----- C ----- O -- H
         |
         H
```

> **Figure 7.1** – Complete structural formula of ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH). The left-hand methyl carbon forms 4 single bonds (3 C–H and 1 C–C). The right-hand carbonyl carbon forms 4 bonds: 1 C–C single bond, 1 C=O double bond with oxygen, and 1 C–O single bond to the hydroxyl group. Both oxygen atoms satisfy their divalent bonding capacity (valency 2), and every hydrogen atom forms 1 single bond.

Notice the fundamental asymmetry among the four hydrogen atoms:
- **Three hydrogen atoms** are covalently bound directly to carbon in the methyl group ($\text{CH}_3\text{–}$). Because carbon (2.5) and hydrogen (2.1) have very similar electronegativities, these C–H bonds are non-polar and tightly held. They **never dissociate as ions** in aqueous solution.
- **Only one single hydrogen atom** is bonded to the highly electronegative oxygen atom in the –O–H group. Oxygen strongly polarises the O–H covalent bond, allowing this terminal proton to detach in water as a hydronium ion ($\text{H}^+$). 

Therefore, although ethanoic acid contains four hydrogen atoms in its formula, it is strictly **monobasic**: it yields only **one replaceable hydrogen ion per molecule**!

```
   HCOOH                    O
                            ||
                       H -- C ----- O -- H


   CH₃COOH             H    O
                       |    ||
                  H -- C -- C ----- O -- H
                       |
                       H


   C₂H₅COOH            H    H    O
                       |    |    ||
                  H -- C -- C -- C ----- O -- H
                       |    |
                       H    H
```

> **Figure 7.2** – The first three carboxylic acids in the homologous series: methanoic acid (formic acid, HCOOH, $n=0$, molar mass = 46 g/mol), ethanoic acid (acetic acid, CH₃COOH, $n=1$, molar mass = 60 g/mol), and propanoic acid (propionic acid, C₂H₅COOH, $n=2$, molar mass = 74 g/mol). Each successive member increases by exactly one –CH₂– unit (14 u).

### Example 1. Molecular and Empirical Analysis of Ethanoic Acid

**Problem:** Determine: (a) the molar mass of ethanoic acid (CH₃COOH), (b) the mass percentage of carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, and (c) its empirical formula. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Molecular formula = C₂H₄O₂.  
$$\text{Molar mass} = 2(12) + 4(1) + 2(16) = 24 + 4 + 32 = \mathbf{60\text{ g/mol}}$$  
**Step 2 (Mass Percentages):**  
- $\text{Carbon \%} = \frac{24}{60} \times 100 = \mathbf{40.0\%}$  
- $\text{Hydrogen \%} = \frac{4}{60} \times 100 = \mathbf{6.67\%}$  
- $\text{Oxygen \%} = \frac{32}{60} \times 100 = \mathbf{53.33\%}$  
*(Check: $40.0 + 6.67 + 53.33 = 100.00\%$ ✅.)*  
**Step 3 (Empirical Formula):**  
Mole ratio $\text{C} : \text{H} : \text{O} = 2 : 4 : 2 = \mathbf{1 : 2 : 1}$.  
The simplest whole-number empirical formula is **CH₂O** (empirical formula mass = $12 + 2 + 16 = 30$ u; ratio $n = 60 \div 30 = 2$).

## Part B: Physical Properties: Vinegar, Glacial Acid, and a Cold Winter Morning

**Culinary Vinegar:** Commercial table vinegar is an aqueous solution containing **5% to 8% ethanoic acid by volume**. In artisanal food preparation, vinegar is produced by a two-stage biochemical process:
1. Anaerobic fermentation of fruit sugars into ethanol by yeast: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{CO}_2$ (Topic 6).
2. Aerobic oxidation of ethanol into ethanoic acid by *Acetobacter* bacteria: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.

**Physical Constants of Pure Ethanoic Acid:**

| Physical Property | Characteristic Value |
|---|---|
| **Appearance & Odour** | Colourless liquid with a sharp, pungent, vinegar-like odour |
| **Solubility in Water** | Completely miscible in all proportions via hydrogen bonding |
| **Freezing / Melting Point** | **16.6 °C (~17 °C)** |
| **Normal Boiling Point** | **118 °C** |

```
   Temperature (°C)  --->
   
   Below 17 °C               17 °C           118 °C
   --------------------------+---------------+------------>
   Solid ice-like crystals   Liquid phase    Gaseous vapour
   ("Glacial" acetic acid)   (ambient room)  (boiling phase)
```

> **Figure 7.3** – Physical phases of pure ethanoic acid. Because its freezing point is a mild 16.6 °C, pure anhydrous ethanoic acid readily freezes into glistening, ice-like crystals in unheated rooms on cold winter mornings, earning it the classical laboratory name **glacial ethanoic acid**.

**Intermolecular Hydrogen Bonding and Boiling Point:**  
Notice that although ethanoic acid (molar mass = 60 g/mol) has a molar mass close to propanol (60 g/mol) and butane (58 g/mol), its boiling point (**118 °C**) is remarkably high. In liquid and vapour phases, pairs of ethanoic acid molecules link together via two complementary intermolecular hydrogen bonds, forming stable cyclic **dimers** that require substantial thermal kinetic energy to break apart.

**Weak Acid Character:**  
In aqueous solution, ethanoic acid is a **weak acid**: only about 1% to 2% of dissolved molecules ionise into hydronium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) and ethanoate ions ($\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$). It is a weak electrolyte that conducts an electric current poorly compared to strong mineral acids like HCl.

## Part C: Chemical Reactions of the Acid: Carbonates, Bases, and Metals

### 1. Reaction with Carbonates and Bicarbonates: The Kitchen Fizz Experiment

Like all typical acids, ethanoic acid vigorously decomposes metal carbonates and hydrogen carbonates (bicarbonates), liberating **carbon dioxide gas**, water, and forming the soluble salt **sodium ethanoate**:

$$2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 \uparrow$$
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaHCO}_3 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 \uparrow$$

```
                   CO₂ effervescent bubbles rising
                     o   o  o   o
                   o   o   o  o
            +--------------------------+
            |                          |
            |  ~~~~ Foaming froth ~~~~ |
            |  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ |
            |        . . . . .         |   <-- Baking soda (NaHCO₃)
            +--------------------------+
                   Inside shallow tray
```

> **Figure 7.4** – The household effervescence reaction. When dilute vinegar is poured onto baking soda in an open tumbler, rapid bubbling effervescence releases carbon dioxide gas. The gas extinguishes a burning splint and turns clear limewater milky.

**Diagnostic Distinction between Alcohols and Carboxylic Acids:**  
Both ethanol and ethanoic acid are colourless liquids, and both react with active sodium metal to evolve hydrogen. However, **only carboxylic acids effervesce with sodium bicarbonate (baking soda)**. Alcohols have zero reaction with carbonates. This simple effervescence test is the standard chemical method used to distinguish an organic acid from an alcohol!

### Example 2. Reaction with Sodium Carbonate: Calculating CO₂ Evolution

**Problem:** In a teacher demonstration, 15.0 g of pure ethanoic acid reacts completely with sodium carbonate: $2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$. Determine: (a) the mass of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3$ consumed, (b) the volume of CO₂ gas evolved at STP, and (c) the mass of sodium ethanoate formed. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23; molar volume at STP = 22.4 L/mol.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of CH₃COOH = 60 g/mol.  
Moles of ethanoic acid = $15.0\text{ g} \div 60\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.25 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 2 : 1 : 2 : 1 : 1):**  
- $\text{Moles of Na}_2\text{CO}_3 = 0.25 \div 2 = \mathbf{0.125\text{ mol}}$.  
  Molar mass of $\text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 = 2(23) + 12 + 3(16) = 106$ g/mol.  
  $\text{Mass of Na}_2\text{CO}_3 = 0.125\text{ mol} \times 106\text{ g/mol} =$ **13.25 g**.  
- $\text{Moles of CO}_2 = 0.125\text{ mol}$.  
  $\text{Volume of CO}_2\text{ at STP} = 0.125\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} =$ **2.80 L** (mass = $0.125 \times 44 = 5.50$ g).  
- $\text{Moles of CH}_3\text{COONa} = 0.25\text{ mol}$.  
  Molar mass of $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} = 12 + 3 + 12 + 32 + 23 = 82$ g/mol.  
  $\text{Mass of CH}_3\text{COONa} = 0.25\text{ mol} \times 82\text{ g/mol} =$ **20.50 g**.  
- $\text{Mass of H}_2\text{O} = 0.125\text{ mol} \times 18\text{ g/mol} =$ **2.25 g**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:**  
Reactants = $15.00 + 13.25 = 28.25$ g; Products = $20.50 + 2.25 + 5.50 = 28.25$ g ✅.

### 2. Reaction with Bases: Neutralisation

Ethanoic acid reacts smoothly with strong alkalis like sodium hydroxide to form a soluble salt and water:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

Because sodium ethanoate is the salt of a **weak acid** (ethanoic acid) and a **strong base** (sodium hydroxide), its aqueous solution undergoes slight anionic hydrolysis, producing a mildly **basic solution (pH ~ 8.5 to 9.0)**.

### Example 3. Neutralisation with Sodium Hydroxide (For Reading Only)

**Problem:** Calculate the mass of pure ethanoic acid required to completely neutralise 2.0 g of solid sodium hydroxide dissolved in water, and determine the mass of sodium ethanoate formed.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of NaOH = $23 + 16 + 1 = 40$ g/mol.  
Moles of NaOH = $2.0\text{ g} \div 40\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.050 mol**.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1):**  
- Moles of ethanoic acid required = 0.050 mol.  
  Mass of ethanoic acid = $0.050\text{ mol} \times 60\text{ g/mol} =$ **3.0 g**.  
- Moles of sodium ethanoate = 0.050 mol.  
  Mass of $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} = 0.050\text{ mol} \times 82\text{ g/mol} =$ **4.1 g**.  
- Mass of water formed = $0.050 \times 18 =$ **0.9 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $3.0 + 2.0 = 5.0$ g; Products = $4.1 + 0.9 = 5.0$ g ✅.

### 3. Reaction with Active Metals

When active electropositive metals (such as magnesium ribbon or zinc granules) are introduced into dilute ethanoic acid, hydrogen gas bubbles off steadily:

$$2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{Mg} \rightarrow (\text{CH}_3\text{COO})_2\text{Mg} + \text{H}_2 \uparrow \quad (\text{magnesium ethanoate})$$

Because ethanoic acid is weak and poorly dissociated, the rate of hydrogen bubble evolution is noticeably slower and gentler than the violent fizzing observed when magnesium reacts with hydrochloric acid.

### A Quick Comparison: Hydrochloric Acid vs Ethanoic Acid

| Diagnostic Test (Equal Molar Concentrations: 0.1 M) | Dilute Hydrochloric Acid (HCl) | Dilute Ethanoic Acid (CH₃COOH) |
|---|---|---|
| **Acid Classification** | Strong mineral acid | Weak organic carboxylic acid |
| **Extent of Ionisation in Water** | Complete (~100% dissociated into $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$) | Slight (~1.3% dissociated into $\text{H}^+$ and $\text{CH}_3\text{COO}^-$) |
| **Aqueous pH (at 0.1 M)** | $\text{pH} \approx 1$ (intense red on universal indicator) | $\text{pH} \approx 3$ (orange-yellow on universal indicator) |
| **Electrical Conductivity** | High conductivity; tester LED bulb glows brilliantly | Low conductivity; tester LED bulb glows dimly |
| **Reaction with Magnesium Ribbon** | Rapid, vigorous effervescence of hydrogen | Slow, steady stream of hydrogen bubbles |

## Part D: Reaction with Alcohols: The Birth of Fragrant Esters

### Esterification (Teacher Demonstration Only)

When absolute ethanol is gently warmed with glacial ethanoic acid in the presence of a few drops of **concentrated sulfuric acid** as a catalyst, an equilibrium reaction occurs to produce **ethyl ethanoate** and water:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \underset{\text{heat}}{\overset{\text{conc. } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

**Role of Concentrated Sulfuric Acid:**
1. **Acid Catalyst:** Supplies protons to accelerate the slow chemical equilibrium.
2. **Dehydrating Agent:** Absorbs and binds product water molecules, shifting the reversible equilibrium forward according to Le Chatelier's principle to maximize ester yield.

```
                       Test tube (open-mouth, NO stopper)
                            |     |
                            |     |
          +-----------------|-----|-----------------+
          |   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|     |~~~~~~~~~~~~~~   |   <-- Hot water bath
          |   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|     |~~~~~~~~~~~~~~   |
          |   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~|#####|~~~~~~~~~~~~~~   |   <-- ##### = Reaction mixture
          |   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~+-----+~~~~~~~~~~~~~~   |
          +-----------------------------------------+
            [ Electric hot plate ]   NO open Bunsen flame
```

> **Figure 7.5** – Safe experimental setup for esterification (teacher demonstration). The reaction mixture (glacial ethanoic acid, absolute ethanol, and conc. H₂SO₄) is heated in a boiling tube immersed inside a hot water bath powered by an electric hot plate. No open Bunsen flame is permitted because ethanol is highly volatile and flammable.

After heating for 5 to 10 minutes, the teacher pours the warm mixture into a beaker filled with cold water containing a little sodium carbonate (to neutralise unreacted acid). An immiscible, oily liquid layer separates and floats on the surface of the water, releasing a distinctive, **sweet, fruity scent** (resembling pear drops or artificial apple essence). This is **ethyl ethanoate**.

```
   CH₃CO-OH   +   H-OC₂H₅    ---->    CH₃CO-OC₂H₅   +   H₂O
    (acid)         (alcohol)            (ester)          (water)
        \_________/
     Acid –OH + Alcohol –H combine into H₂O
```

> **Figure 7.6** – The mechanism of esterification. The carboxylic acid contributes its hydroxyl (–OH) group, while the alcohol contributes its terminal hydrogen atom, condensing together to form water (H₂O). The remaining acyl fragment ($\text{CH}_3\text{CO–}$) joins with the alkoxy fragment ($\text{–OC}_2\text{H}_5$) to establish the covalent ester linkage (–COO–).

```
         H       O                H      H
         |       ||               |      |
    H -- C ----- C ----- O ------ C ---- C -- H
         |                        |      |
         H                        H      H
```

> **Figure 7.7** – The full structural formula of ethyl ethanoate ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_2\text{CH}_3$). The ester functional group (–COO–) forms the central bridge linking the two alkyl groups together. Every carbon atom maintains 4 bonds, each oxygen forms 2 bonds, and each hydrogen forms 1 bond.

### Example 4. Theoretical Yield of Ethyl Ethanoate

**Problem:** In a teacher demonstration, 12.0 g of ethanoic acid is mixed with 9.2 g of ethanol. Assuming 100% stoichiometric conversion, calculate the theoretical mass of ethyl ethanoate ester and water formed. (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Moles of ethanoic acid = $12.0\text{ g} \div 60\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.20 mol**.  
Moles of ethanol = $9.2\text{ g} \div 46\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.20 mol**.  
The reactants are mixed in an exact 1 : 1 stoichiometric ratio.  
**Step 2:** Molar mass of ethyl ethanoate ($\text{C}_4\text{H}_8\text{O}_2$) = $4(12) + 8(1) + 2(16) = 48 + 8 + 32 = \mathbf{88\text{ g/mol}}$.  
- $\text{Theoretical mass of ester} = 0.20\text{ mol} \times 88\text{ g/mol} =$ **17.6 g**.  
- $\text{Mass of water formed} = 0.20\text{ mol} \times 18\text{ g/mol} =$ **3.6 g**.  

**Mass Check:** Reactants = $12.0 + 9.2 = 21.2$ g; Products = $17.6 + 3.6 = 21.2$ g ✅.  
*(In actual practice, because esterification is a reversible equilibrium, the isolated yield is typically 65% to 70% of this theoretical maximum.)*

### Other Esters: Reactions with Methanol and Propanol

Varying the alcohol partner produces a library of distinct esters, each possessing a characteristic natural fruit aroma:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{methyl ethanoate})$$
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_3\text{H}_7 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad (\text{propyl ethanoate: pear aroma})$$

**Naming Rule for Esters:**
- The first word is derived from the **alcohol** (the alkyl group attached to oxygen, e.g., *methyl*, *ethyl*, *propyl*).
- The second word is derived from the **carboxylic acid**, changing the *-ic acid* suffix to ***-oate*** (e.g., *ethanoate*, *propanoate*).

### Hydrolysis: Reversing the Esterification Reaction

Because esterification is reversible, heating an ester with water in the presence of an acid or alkali hydrolyses it back into its constituent parent molecules:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \xrightarrow{\text{dil. acid, heat}} \text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

When hydrolysis is performed using an aqueous alkali (such as hot dilute sodium hydroxide), the organic acid is isolated as its sodium salt:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH}$$

This alkaline hydrolysis of an ester is called **saponification**—the identical chemical reaction used to manufacture domestic bar soaps from natural vegetable oils and animal fats, as we will explore in Topic 8!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Glacial ethanoic acid is a strong acid because it burns skin and tastes intensely sour."**  
   *Correction:* Never confuse **concentration** with **acid strength**!  
   - **Glacial ethanoic acid** is extremely **concentrated** (>99% pure acid, virtually zero water). It causes painful skin burns through hygroscopic dehydration and protein irritation.  
   - However, **acid strength** is strictly defined by the **degree of ionisation in water ($\alpha$)**. Even when diluted in water, ethanoic acid ionises only ~1.3% into $\text{H}^+$ ions. A 0.1 M solution of ethanoic acid has a pH of ~2.9, whereas a 0.1 M solution of strong HCl has a pH of 1.0. Ethanoic acid is fundamentally a **weak acid**, regardless of whether it is diluted as vinegar or concentrated as glacial acid.

2. **Mistake 2: "Esterification is just another name for neutralisation because an acid reacts to form water."**  
   *Correction:* While both reactions produce water as a byproduct, they represent fundamentally different chemical categories:

| Feature | Neutralisation Reaction | Esterification Reaction |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactants** | Acid + **Base / Alkali (e.g., NaOH)** | Acid + **Alcohol (e.g., C₂H₅OH)** |
| **Nature of Second Reactant** | Basic (releases aqueous $\text{OH}^-$ ions) | **Neutral covalent molecule** (zero $\text{OH}^-$ ions) |
| **Organic Product** | **Ionic salt** (e.g., $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$) | **Covalent ester** (e.g., $\text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5$) |
| **Reaction Rate & Conditions** | Instantaneous, highly exothermic at room temperature | Slow, reversible equilibrium; requires heat and conc. H₂SO₄ catalyst |
| **Aroma of Product** | Inodorous salt solution | **Sweet, fragrant, fruity perfume aroma** |

---

## Quick Recap

- **Ethanoic Acid (CH₃COOH):** A monobasic carboxylic acid containing the functional group –COOH (molar mass = 60 g/mol). Only the single hydrogen atom bonded to oxygen is ionisable.
- **Physical Constants:** Colourless, pungent liquid; b.p. 118 °C; freezes at ~17 °C into ice-like crystals (**glacial acetic acid**). Forms cyclic hydrogen-bonded dimers.
- **Culinary Vinegar:** An aqueous solution of 5% to 8% ethanoic acid.
- **Acidic Reactions:**
  - Carbonates/Bicarbonates: $2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2 \uparrow$ (effervescent diagnostic test).
  - Neutralisation: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (mildly basic salt).
  - Active Metals: Slow hydrogen gas evolution with magnesium.
- **Esterification:** Reversible acid-catalysed condensation between ethanoic acid and ethanol yielding fragrant **ethyl ethanoate**: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_2\text{H}_5 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.
- **Saponification:** Alkaline hydrolysis of an ester with hot NaOH yields an alcohol and the sodium salt of the acid (the chemical basis of soap manufacturing).
- **Practical Uses:** Food preservation, synthetic perfume and fruit flavouring esters, solvent in nail polish removers, and domestic limescale remover.

### Answers

1. Molar masses:  
   - $\text{HCOOH} = 1 + 12 + 32 + 1 = \mathbf{46\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   - $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} = 12 + 3 + 12 + 32 + 1 = \mathbf{60\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   - $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOH} = 24 + 5 + 12 + 32 + 1 = \mathbf{74\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   Difference: $60 - 46 = 14$ u; $74 - 60 = 14$ u. This constant incremental difference corresponds precisely to one **methylene unit (–CH₂–)** ($12 + 2 = 14$ u) ✅.
2. For the member following propanoic acid ($n=3$):  
   - Molecular formula = **C₄H₈O₂**; condensed formula = **C₃H₇COOH** (butanoic acid).  
   - Molar mass = $3(12) + 7(1) + 12 + 32 + 1 = 48 + 8 + 32 = \mathbf{88\text{ g/mol}}$ ($74 + 14 = 88$ u ✅).
3. Propanoic acid structure:
   ```
            H      H      O
            |      |      ||
       H -- C ---- C ---- C ----- O -- H
            |      |
            H      H
   ```
   Valency verification: Carbon-1 forms 3 C–H + 1 C–C = 4 bonds; Carbon-2 forms 2 C–H + 2 C–C = 4 bonds; Carbon-3 forms 1 C–C + 2 (from C=O) + 1 (from C–O) = 4 bonds. Every hydrogen forms 1 single bond ✅. The **single terminal hydrogen bonded to the oxygen atom** is the ionisable acidic hydrogen.
4. Molar mass of propanoic acid = 74 g/mol. Moles = $14.8 \div 74 = \mathbf{0.20\text{ mol}}$.  
   From $2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOH} + \text{Na}_2\text{CO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$:  
   - $\text{Mass of Na}_2\text{CO}_3\text{ (M = 106)} = (0.20 \div 2) \times 106 = 0.10 \times 106 =$ **10.6 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COONa}\text{ (M = 96)} = 0.20 \times 96 =$ **19.2 g**.  
   - $\text{STP volume of CO}_2 = 0.10 \times 22.4 =$ **2.24 L** (mass = 4.4 g).  
   Mass check: $14.8 + 10.6 = 25.4$ g; Products = $19.2 + 1.8\text{ (water)} + 4.4\text{ (CO}_2) = 25.4$ g ✅.
5. Molar mass of HCOOH = 46 g/mol. Moles = $4.6 \div 46 = \mathbf{0.10\text{ mol}}$.  
   - Moles of NaOH required = 0.10 mol. Mass = $0.10 \times 40 =$ **4.0 g**.  
   - Moles of sodium methanoate ($\text{HCOONa}$, M = $1 + 12 + 32 + 23 = 68$ g/mol) = 0.10 mol.  
     Mass = $0.10 \times 68 =$ **6.8 g**.  
   Mass check: Reactants = $4.6 + 4.0 = 8.6$ g; Products = $6.8 + (0.10 \times 18 = 1.8) = 8.6$ g ✅.
6. Ester name: **methyl propanoate** ($\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOCH}_3$).  
   Molar mass = $4(12) + 8(1) + 2(16) = \mathbf{88\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   Moles of propanoic acid = $7.4 \div 74 = 0.10$ mol; Moles of methanol = $3.2 \div 32 = 0.10$ mol.  
   - Theoretical mass of ester = $0.10 \times 88 =$ **8.8 g**.  
   - Theoretical mass of water = $0.10 \times 18 =$ **1.8 g**.  
   Mass check: $7.4 + 3.2 = 10.6$ g; $8.8 + 1.8 = 10.6$ g ✅.
7. Equation: $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{C}_3\text{H}_7\text{OH} \underset{\text{heat}}{\overset{\text{conc. } \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{CH}_3\text{COOC}_3\text{H}_7 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$.  
   Ester IUPAC name: **propyl ethanoate**.  
   Moles of propan-1-ol (M = 60 g/mol) = $6.0 \div 60 = 0.10$ mol; requires 0.10 mol ethanoic acid (6.0 g).  
   Molar mass of propyl ethanoate ($\text{C}_5\text{H}_{10}\text{O}_2$) = $5(12) + 10(1) + 32 = 102$ g/mol.  
   - $\text{Mass of ester} = 0.10 \times 102 =$ **10.2 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of water} = 0.10 \times 18 =$ **1.8 g**.  
   Mass check: Reactants = $6.0 + 6.0 = 12.0$ g; Products = $10.2 + 1.8 = 12.0$ g ✅.
8. (a) $\text{HCOOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{HCOOCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$. Ester name: **methyl methanoate**.  
   (b) Molar mass of $\text{HCOOCH}_3 = 2(12) + 4(1) + 2(16) = 24 + 4 + 32 = \mathbf{60\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   (c) They are **functional group isomers**. No, they will not behave alike: ethanoic acid is an organic acid that rapidly effervesces with sodium bicarbonate, whereas methyl methanoate is a neutral ester that exhibits no reaction with carbonates.
9. Equation: $\text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOCH}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{C}_2\text{H}_5\text{COOH} + \text{CH}_3\text{OH}$.  
   Atom balance check: Reactants: C = 4, H = 10, O = 3; Products: C = 4, H = 10, O = 3 ✅.  
   Products: **propanoic acid** and **methanol**.
10. (a) Mass of ethanoic acid = $300\text{ g} \times 0.06 = \mathbf{18.0\text{ g}}$. Moles = $18.0 \div 60 = \mathbf{0.30\text{ mol}}$.  
    (b) From $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} + \text{NaHCO}_3 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COONa} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$, moles of $\text{NaHCO}_3 = 0.30$ mol.  
    $\text{Mass of NaHCO}_3\text{ (M = 84)} = 0.30 \times 84 = \mathbf{25.2\text{ g}}$.  
    (c) $\text{Volume of CO}_2\text{ at STP} = 0.30 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{6.72\text{ L}}$.
11. - **P = ethanoic acid:** It is an acid, turning blue litmus red and effervescing with sodium bicarbonate.  
    - **Q = ethyl ethanoate:** Being a covalent ester, it is neutral to litmus, does not effervesce with carbonates, possesses a sweet fruity aroma, and is immiscible with water.  
    - **R = ethanol:** A neutral alcohol that has an antiseptic solvent odour and forms extensive hydrogen bonds with water, making it infinitely miscible.
12. (a) At 12 °C (which is below its melting point of 16.6 °C), pure ethanoic acid is a **solid** (glacial crystals). At 25 °C, it is a **liquid**.  
    (b) In order of increasing boiling point: **ethanol (78 °C) < water (100 °C) < ethanoic acid (118 °C)**.
13. *Difference:* 'Concentrated' refers to a high ratio of acid to water (little or no water present), whereas 'strong' refers to the degree of complete ionisation in water. Glacial ethanoic acid is concentrated, but it remains a fundamentally **weak acid** because it ionises only slightly in aqueous solution.  
    *Diagnostic test:* Measuring electrical conductivity or reacting with magnesium ribbon at identical 0.1 M concentrations: hydrochloric acid produces rapid effervescence and bright conductivity, whereas ethanoic acid produces gentle bubbling and faint conductivity.
14. (a) **False.** Ethanol is a neutral alcohol; it does not react with sodium bicarbonate.  
    (b) **False.** Esters are synthesized by reacting a carboxylic acid with an **alcohol** in the presence of an acid catalyst.  
    (c) **True.** Both ethanol and ethanoic acid react with active sodium to evolve hydrogen gas.  
    (d) **False.** Ethyl ethanoate is a **covalent molecular compound**.  
    (e) **False.** Only **one** hydrogen atom (the one attached to oxygen in –COOH) is ionisable.
15. Commercial vinegar bottles typically state 5% to 8% acidity. Acetic acid reacts with the carbonate/bicarbonate ion to liberate gaseous CO₂, creating vigorous bubbling effervescence. If pure neutral water were substituted, zero effervescence would occur because water lacks free acidic hydronium ions to decompose carbonates.
