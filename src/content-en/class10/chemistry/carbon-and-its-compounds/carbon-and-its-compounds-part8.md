# Topic 8: Soaps and Detergents: The Chemistry of Cleansing

In Topic 7, we observed that carboxylic acids condense with alcohols to form sweet-smelling **esters**. Esters exist abundantly in nature: everyday cooking oils and animal fats are also esters, built from glycerol and very long hydrocarbon chains called fatty acids. When these heavy natural esters are cleaved with a hot alkali like sodium hydroxide, they yield the ubiquitous household cleaning agent you use every day: **soap**. In Class 9, you learned that soap forms a sticky, insoluble **scum** in hard water, whereas synthetic detergents lather freely ([water-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part3.md)). At the time, soap's formula was introduced as "too complex to memorise yet," and detergent chemistry was deferred to Class 10. That day has arrived! Today, we uncover **what soap really is**, **how dual-ended molecules assemble into cleansing micelles**, and **why synthetic detergents bypass hard water scum**.

## The School Dormitory and the Greasy Plate

On a Sunday morning, Mr. Jenkins visited his nephew Ethan at his boarding school dormitory. Ethan showed his uncle the ground-floor washroom. 

"Uncle, our water here is pumped directly from a deep underground borehole," Ethan explained, pointing toward the sink basin. "Whenever we wash our hands with bar soap, it hardly lathers at all. Instead, an unsightly **white, curd-like scum** sticks stubbornly to the soap dish, the taps, and the plastic washbasins." Mr. Jenkins examined the basin: indeed, a chalky, greasy ring coated the interior.

Afterwards, they walked to the dining hall kitchen, where student volunteers were washing stainless-steel lunch trays. At first, a student rinsed a tray under running cold tap water: while loose food crumbs washed away, a **slick, oily film** remained bonded to the metal. Next, the student squeezed three drops of **liquid dishwashing detergent** onto a wet sponge and gently wiped the surface. Under the rinse stream, the metal surface squeaked spotless, completely free of oil!

Ethan rubbed his chin thoughtfully. "Uncle, in Class 9 we learned that calcium ions in hard water react with soap to precipitate scum. But two questions still puzzle me: why can't pure water wash away cooking oil on its own? What makes dishwashing liquid lift oil instantly? And why doesn't detergent form scum in hard well water?"

Mr. Jenkins smiled. "All three questions converge on the exact same molecular marvel: the **dual-ended amphiphilic architecture** of soaps and synthetic detergents. Let us take a close look at how these molecular bridges work."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Grease / Oily Dirt** | Hydrophobic, non-polar organic substances consisting of long hydrocarbon chains that repel water | cooking oil, butter, sebum |
| **Fatty acid** | A long-chain monocarboxylic acid derived from natural fats and oils; general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₁COOH ($n \ge 11$) | stearic acid, C₁₇H₃₅COOH |
| **Soap** | The water-soluble sodium or potassium salt of a long-chain fatty acid | sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COONa |
| **Saponification** | The alkaline hydrolysis of a natural fat or vegetable oil using hot sodium hydroxide to yield soap and glycerol | fat + 3NaOH → 3 soap + glycerol |
| **Glycerol (Glycerin)** | A trihydric alcohol, propane-1,2,3-triol ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3$), obtained as a major byproduct of saponification | cosmetic emollient |
| **Hydrophilic** | "Water-loving"; a polar or ionic group that forms strong electrostatic attractions with water molecules | the carboxylate head (–COO⁻) |
| **Hydrophobic** | "Water-fearing"; a non-polar hydrocarbon chain that is repelled by water but dissolves readily in oils and grease | the C₁₇H₃₅– alkyl tail |
| **Micelle** | A spherical sub-microscopic cluster of soap or detergent ions in water, with hydrophobic tails buried inside and hydrophilic heads facing outward | colloidal cleaning cluster |
| **Synthetic detergent** | A manufactured surfactant, typically the sodium salt of a long-chain alkyl sulfate or alkylbenzene sulfonate | sodium lauryl sulfate |
| **Biodegradable** | Capable of being broken down into harmless natural substances by environmental micro-organisms | straight-chain detergents |
| **Scum** | A sticky, insoluble curd formed when soap reacts with calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$) or magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) ions in hard water | calcium stearate precipitate |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Caustic Alkalis and Domestic Cleaning Chemicals**
> - The industrial synthesis of soap requires **concentrated sodium hydroxide (NaOH / caustic soda)**, an aggressively corrosive chemical that causes severe, permanent chemical burns to skin and irreversible blindness if splashed into eyes. **Students must never attempt to make soap with raw caustic soda at home.** Saponification is studied purely on paper or as a sealed teacher demonstration.
> - Laundry detergent powders and dishwashing liquids are formulated with active surfactants and alkaline builders. They **severely irritate the eyes and mucous membranes** and are harmful if ingested. Always handle cleaning agents with dry hands and keep them away from younger children.
> - **Never mix different household cleaning chemicals:** specifically, never blend chlorine bleach with acidic descalers or ammonia-based cleaners, as doing so releases lethal, toxic chlorine and chloramine gases.
> - In all household lather activities, work under adult supervision, wear protective safety goggles, and never taste or consume any test solutions.

## Part A: What Is Grease, and Why Does Water Alone Fail?

The oily residue on a dirty dinner plate, the collar grime on a school shirt, and the greasy film on skin are composed primarily of **grease and oils**. These substances consist of long-chain hydrocarbon molecules. 

Recall from Topic 4 that the electronegativity difference between carbon (2.5) and hydrogen (2.1) is merely 0.4. Consequently, C–C and C–H bonds are virtually **non-polar**. Grease molecules are non-polar and hydrophobic.

Now recall the fundamental chemical rule from Chapter 7: **"Like dissolves like"** ([chemical-bonding-ii-part2.md](../chemical-bonding-ii/chemical-bonding-ii-part2.md)). Water is a bent, highly **polar molecule** capable of forming extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonds. When non-polar oil is poured into polar water, the water molecules attract one another with strong hydrogen bonds, effectively squeezing out the oil molecules. The oil floats as an immiscible, separate layer on the surface. 

Because water molecules cannot interact with non-polar grease, **running water alone flows straight over an oily plate without lifting the oil**. Moreover, everyday airborne dust, soot, and bacteria are glued firmly to surfaces by this sticky grease layer. To wash away the dirt, we must first dislodge the grease.

We require a chemical **mediator**: a molecule whose one end bonds firmly with water, while its other end bonds firmly with grease!

## Part B: What Is a Soap? A Dual-Ended Amphiphilic Molecule

Natural **animal fats** (such as tallow) and **vegetable oils** (such as olive, coconut, or palm oil) are esters formed from glycerol and long-chain carboxylic acids called **fatty acids**. 

When natural fats are hydrolysed with hot sodium hydroxide, the ester bonds cleave, yielding the sodium salts of these fatty acids—**soap**. The most representative soap compound is **sodium stearate**:

$$\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} \quad (\text{sodium stearate})$$

Its parent fatty acid is **stearic acid ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COOH}$)**, which perfectly matches the general formula for saturated carboxylic acids: $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{COOH}$ with $n=17$. Just as ethanoic acid forms the sodium salt $\text{CH}_3\text{COONa}$, stearic acid forms $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}$.

Inspect the extended structural framework of a sodium stearate molecule in Figure 8.1:

```
      H   H         H   H
      |   |         |   |
  H – C – C – ... – C – C – C = O
      |   |         |   |   |
      H   H         H   H   O⁻   Na⁺
```

> **Figure 8.1** – Structural formula of sodium stearate ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}$). From left to right: a terminal methyl group ($\text{CH}_3\text{–}$) followed by 16 repeating methylene groups ($\text{–CH}_2\text{–}$), culminating in an ionic carboxylate head ($\text{–COO}^-\text{Na}^+$). Every carbon atom satisfies tetravalency (4 bonds), each hydrogen has 1 bond, and the ionic bond connects the carboxylate anion to the sodium cation.

Because writing all 17 carbon atoms repeatedly is cumbersome, chemists condense the hydrocarbon tail as **$\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{–}$**. Notice that this tail is simply a 17-carbon alkane chain with one terminal hydrogen removed (heptadecane is $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{36}$).

### Example 1. Molar Mass and Non-Polar Tail of Sodium Stearate

**Problem:** Calculate the molar mass of sodium stearate ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}$). Determine what percentage of its total molecular mass consists of the non-polar hydrocarbon tail ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{–}$). (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16, Na = 23.)

**Step 1:** Count the atoms: $\text{Carbon} = 17 + 1 = 18$; $\text{Hydrogen} = 35$; $\text{Oxygen} = 2$; $\text{Sodium} = 1$.  
**Step 2:** Total molar mass:  
$$\text{Molar mass} = 18(12) + 35(1) + 2(16) + 23 = 216 + 35 + 32 + 23 = \mathbf{306\text{ g/mol}}$$  
**Step 3:** Mass of non-polar tail ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{–}$):  
$$\text{Mass of tail} = 17(12) + 35(1) = 204 + 35 = \mathbf{239\text{ g/mol}}$$  
Mass of ionic head ($\text{–COONa}$): $12 + 32 + 23 = 67$ g/mol ($239 + 67 = 306$ g/mol ✅).  
**Step 4:** Percentage of mass in the hydrocarbon tail:  
$$\text{Percentage tail mass} = \frac{239}{306} \times 100 \approx \mathbf{78.1\%}$$  

**Insight:** Over **78% of the mass** of a soap molecule consists of an oily, non-polar hydrocarbon chain! A soap molecule is essentially a long greasy hydrocarbon tail with an ionic tag pinned to one end.

### Two Ends, Two Distinct Personalities

When dissolved in water, sodium stearate ionises into a stearate anion and a sodium cation:

$$\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} \xrightarrow{\text{water}} \text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO}^- + \text{Na}^+$$

The stearate anion is **amphiphilic** (possessing two contrasting affinities):

```
       Ionic Head                     Hydrocarbon Tail
         –COO⁻                    –C₁₇H₃₅ (Long zigzag chain)
      ( - )~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
   Water-soluble                  Oil-soluble, water-repelling
   (Polar, Hydrophilic)           (Non-polar, Hydrophobic)
```

> **Figure 8.2** – Diagrammatic representation of a soap ion. The polar carboxylate head ($\text{–COO}^-$) carries a negative electrical charge and forms strong dipole-ion attractions with polar water molecules (**hydrophilic**). The extended hydrocarbon tail ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{–}$) is non-polar and insoluble in water, but readily dissolves in non-polar oils and grease (**hydrophobic**).

*(Because soap is the salt of a weak organic fatty acid and a strong base like NaOH, aqueous soap solutions undergo slight hydrolysis, producing a mildly alkaline pH of ~9 to 10. This is why rubbing wet soap onto turmeric-stained fabric turns the stain reddish-brown, as observed in Chapter 1!)*

## Part C: Cleansing Action and Micelle Formation

When soap is added to dirty wash water, its molecules arrange themselves spontaneously at the boundary between water and oil:
- The **hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails** embed themselves directly into the non-polar grease droplet, escaping the polar water environment.
- The **polar hydrophilic heads** project outward into the surrounding water phase.

When a critical concentration of soap is reached (the critical micelle concentration), hundreds of soap ions cluster radially around the grease droplet to form a stable, spherical colloidal assembly called a **micelle**:

```
                   (-)  (-)  (-)
                    ~    ~    ~
                    ~    ~    ~
          (-) ~~~~              ~~~~ (-)
          (-) ~~~~    Grease    ~~~~ (-)
          (-) ~~~~   Droplet    ~~~~ (-)
                    ~    ~    ~
                    ~    ~    ~
                   (-)  (-)  (-)
              Surrounding Water (Polar)
```

> **Figure 8.3** – Cross-section of a cleaning micelle. The hydrophobic tails (~) penetrate and dissolve into the central droplet of grease, while the negatively charged ionic heads (-) coat the outer surface, forming a protective hydrophilic shell in contact with water.

**Colloidal Stability and Electrical Repulsion:**  
Notice that the outer sphere of every single micelle is densely coated with identical **negative electrical charges ($\text{–COO}^-$)**. Like charges repel: when two adjacent micelles drift near each other in the wash water, their negative outer shells repel electrostatically. Consequently, the suspended oil droplets **cannot coalesce into larger globules** or redeposit onto the cleaned fabric. The grease remains trapped as a stable emulsion suspended uniformly in the water!

```
 1. Grease on fabric          2. Soap tails penetrate       3. Mechanical agitation lifts
    in wash water                the grease droplet            micelle into water

  Water ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~       Water ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~       Water ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
    (-)    (-)    (-)            (-)   (-)   (-)                 (-) (-)
     ~      ~      ~              ~     ~     ~                 (-)#####(-)  <-- Emulsified
          #####                    ###########                     (-) (-)       micelle washes
  Fabric =============          Fabric =============          Fabric =============  away
  (Grease stuck to cloth)       (Tails dissolve in oil)       (Fabric left spotless)
```

> **Figure 8.4** – The three stages of detergency. Stage 1: The hydrophobic grease clings stubbornly to fabric fibres. Stage 2: Soap anions orient themselves around the grease, inserting their non-polar tails into the oil while leaving polar heads in water. Stage 3: Mechanical rubbing, scrubbing, or machine agitation provides the kinetic energy to tear the grease droplet off the fibres. The emulsified micelle is carried away cleanly in the rinse water.

## Part D: How Soap Is Made: Saponification (For Reading Only)

In soap manufacturing, natural fats and vegetable oils—which are triglycerides (triesters of glycerol and three fatty acid molecules)—are boiled with concentrated aqueous sodium hydroxide:

$$\text{Fat or Oil (Triglyceride)} + 3\text{NaOH} \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} 3\text{ Soap (Sodium Salt)} + \text{Glycerol}$$

Taking **tristearin** (the primary triglyceride ester in solid beef tallow) as an example:

$$\text{C}_{57}\text{H}_{110}\text{O}_6 + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} + \text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3$$

```
        Glass stirring rod
                 \
                  \
         +---------\--------+
         |   ~~~~~~~\~~~~~~ |   <-- Vegetable oil + NaOH solution
         |  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ |
         +------------------+
      [====  Electric hot plate  ====]
```

> **Figure 8.5** – Saponification (teacher demonstration only). A mixture of natural vegetable oil and concentrated sodium hydroxide is heated gently on an electric hot plate while stirring continuously with a glass rod until the liquid thickens into a creamy paste.

**The Salting-Out Process:**  
Once saponification is complete, the viscous hot mixture contains soap, unreacted water, and dissolved **glycerol**. To precipitate and harvest the solid soap, a saturated solution of **common table salt (sodium chloride, NaCl)** is stirred into the mixture:
- Soap is relatively insoluble in concentrated brine (the common ion effect of $\text{Na}^+$ drives precipitation).
- The solid soap precipitates out and floats to the top of the vat as a thick curd (**salting out**).
- The solid curd is skimmed off, washed, pressed into moulds, and dried into soap bars.
- The underlying aqueous layer containing valuable **glycerol ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3$)** is drawn off, purified by vacuum distillation, and utilised in pharmaceuticals, cosmetics, skin moisturisers, and nitroglycerin explosives.

### Example 2. Stoichiometry of Tristearin Saponification

**Problem:** In a theoretical calculation, 8.90 g of pure tristearin fat ($\text{C}_{57}\text{H}_{110}\text{O}_6$, molar mass = 890 g/mol) undergoes complete saponification with excess sodium hydroxide: $\text{C}_{57}\text{H}_{110}\text{O}_6 + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} + \text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3$. Determine: (a) the mass of solid NaOH consumed, (b) the mass of sodium stearate soap formed, and (c) the mass of glycerol produced. (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23.)

**Step 1:** Moles of tristearin = $8.90\text{ g} \div 890\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.010\text{ mol}}$.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 1 : 3 : 3 : 1):**  
- $\text{Moles of NaOH} = 0.010 \times 3 = 0.030\text{ mol}$.  
  $\text{Mass of NaOH} = 0.030\text{ mol} \times 40\text{ g/mol} =$ **1.20 g**.  
- $\text{Moles of soap} = 0.010 \times 3 = 0.030\text{ mol}$.  
  $\text{Mass of soap} = 0.030\text{ mol} \times 306\text{ g/mol} =$ **9.18 g**.  
- $\text{Moles of glycerol} = 0.010\text{ mol}$.  
  Molar mass of glycerol ($\text{C}_3\text{H}_8\text{O}_3$) = $3(12) + 8(1) + 3(16) = 36 + 8 + 48 = 92$ g/mol.  
  $\text{Mass of glycerol} = 0.010\text{ mol} \times 92\text{ g/mol} =$ **0.92 g**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:**  
Reactants = $8.90 + 1.20 = 10.10$ g; Products = $9.18 + 0.92 = 10.10$ g ✅.

## Part E: Soap in Hard Water: The Real Story Behind Scum

In Class 9 ([water-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part3.md)), we noted that hard water contains dissolved salts of **calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$)** and **magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$)** (such as $\text{CaCl}_2$, $\text{Ca(HCO}_3)_2$, $\text{MgSO}_4$).

When soap is dissolved in hard water, the divalent calcium and magnesium ions instantly react with soluble sodium stearate, displacing the sodium ions to form insoluble **calcium stearate** and **magnesium stearate**:

$$2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} + \text{Ca}^{2+} \rightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca} \downarrow + 2\text{Na}^+$$

```
  ~~~~~~~~~~(–)  Ca²⁺  (–)~~~~~~~~~~
    Tail     Head      Head   Tail
  
  Net Electrical Charge: (-1) + (+2) + (-1) = 0
```

> **Figure 8.6** – The structure of insoluble calcium stearate (scum). Two stearate anions are cross-linked by a single divalent calcium ion ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$). Because the positive and negative charges completely neutralize each other, the molecule possesses zero net electrical charge. With no ionic charge remaining on its heads to interact with water, the precipitate falls out of solution as an insoluble, curdy white **scum**.

**Why Soap Fails in Hard Water:**
1. **Severe Wastage:** Soap cannot form cleaning micelles until **every single calcium and magnesium ion** in the water has been precipitated as scum. Massive amounts of soap are wasted just softening the water.
2. **Fabric Deterioration:** The sticky scum adheres tenaciously to textile fibres, making freshly laundered white fabrics look grey and dull, and causing cloth to feel stiff and scratchy.

### Example 3. Calculating the Wastage of Soap as Insoluble Scum

**Problem:** A wash bucket contains hard tap water containing 1.11 g of dissolved calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$). Determine: (a) the mass of sodium stearate soap wasted in precipitating all the $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ions, and (b) the mass of insoluble scum produced: $2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} + \text{CaCl}_2 \rightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca} + 2\text{NaCl}$. (Ca = 40, Cl = 35.5, C = 12, H = 1, O = 16, Na = 23.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of $\text{CaCl}_2 = 40 + 2(35.5) = 111$ g/mol.  
Moles of $\text{CaCl}_2 = 1.11\text{ g} \div 111\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.010\text{ mol}}$.  
**Step 2 (Stoichiometric mole ratio 2 : 1 : 1 : 2):**  
- Moles of sodium stearate consumed = $0.010 \times 2 = 0.020\text{ mol}$.  
  Mass of soap wasted = $0.020\text{ mol} \times 306\text{ g/mol} =$ **6.12 g**.  
- Molar mass of calcium stearate scum ($(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}$):  
  $\text{Mass} = 2(283) + 40 = 566 + 40 = \mathbf{606\text{ g/mol}}$.  
  $\text{Mass of scum precipitate} = 0.010\text{ mol} \times 606\text{ g/mol} =$ **6.06 g**.  
- $\text{Mass of NaCl formed} = 0.020\text{ mol} \times 58.5\text{ g/mol} =$ **1.17 g**.  

**Mass Conservation Check:** Reactants = $6.12 + 1.11 = 7.23$ g; Products = $6.06 + 1.17 = 7.23$ g ✅.  
*(Notice the dramatic result: merely **1.11 g of dissolved mineral salt consumes and ruins over 6.1 g of expensive soap** before any cleaning can even begin!)*

## Part F: Synthetic Detergents: Dual-Ended with a Different Head

To overcome the fatal limitation of soaps in hard water, chemical engineers developed **synthetic detergents** (often called "soapless soaps"). 

Like soaps, detergent molecules possess a dual-ended amphiphilic architecture: a long, non-polar hydrophobic **hydrocarbon tail** and a charged, polar **hydrophilic head**. However, their hydrophilic head is **not a carboxylate group**:
- In anionic detergents, the polar head is a **sulfate group ($\text{–O–SO}_3^-\text{Na}^+$)** or a **sulfonate group ($\text{–SO}_3^-\text{Na}^+$)**.

Two common commercial examples:
1. **Sodium Lauryl Sulfate ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{–O–SO}_3\text{Na}$):** The active cleansing agent in shampoos, liquid body washes, and toothpastes.
2. **Sodium Alkylbenzene Sulfonate:** The primary surfactant in domestic laundry powders.

```
      H   H         H   H
      |   |         |   |
  H – C – C – ... – C – C – O – SO₃⁻  Na⁺
      |   |         |   |
      H   H         H   H
```

> **Figure 8.7** – Molecular structure of sodium lauryl sulfate ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{OSO}_3\text{Na}$). A 12-carbon non-polar alkyl tail ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{–}$) is bonded to an ionic sulfate head ($\text{–O–SO}_3^-\text{Na}^+$).

### Example 4. Molecular Mass of Sodium Lauryl Sulfate

**Problem:** Calculate the molar mass of sodium lauryl sulfate ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{OSO}_3\text{Na}$), and determine the mass of its non-polar tail ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{–}$) and ionic head ($\text{–OSO}_3\text{Na}$). (H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, S = 32.)

**Step 1:** Molecular formula = C₁₂H₂₅SO₄Na.  
$$\text{Molar mass} = 12(12) + 25(1) + 32 + 4(16) + 23 = 144 + 25 + 32 + 64 + 23 = \mathbf{288\text{ g/mol}}$$  
**Step 2:**  
- $\text{Mass of tail (C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{–)} = 12(12) + 25(1) = 144 + 25 = \mathbf{169\text{ g/mol}}$.  
- $\text{Mass of head (–OSO}_3\text{Na)} = 16 + 32 + 48 + 23 = \mathbf{119\text{ g/mol}}$.  
*(Check: $169 + 119 = 288$ g/mol ✅).*

### Why Do Detergents Function Effectively in Hard Water?

When synthetic detergents are introduced into hard water, the sulfate or sulfonate heads react with calcium and magnesium ions:

$$2\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{OSO}_3^- + \text{Ca}^{2+} \rightarrow (\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{OSO}_3)_2\text{Ca}$$

Unlike calcium carboxylates, **calcium and magnesium alkyl sulfates and sulfonates are completely soluble in water!** 
- **No insoluble scum is precipitated.**
- Zero detergent is wasted neutralizing mineral ions.
- Detergents form rich, thick lather immediately, even in freezing ice water, hard well water, or mineral-rich seawater.

### Biodegradability and Environmental Impact

1. **Chain Branching and Biodegradability:** Early synthetic detergents manufactured in the 1950s contained highly **branched hydrocarbon chains** derived from propylene polymers. Soil and sewage bacteria lack the specific enzymes required to break down branched alkyl chains. These detergents accumulated in aquatic ecosystems, producing persistent, towering mountains of chemical foam on rivers, lakes, and sewage treatment plants. Modern detergents are mandated by law to be **linear alkyl sulfonates (straight-chain)**, which environmental bacteria metabolise and decompose rapidly (**biodegradable**).
2. **Phosphate Builders and Eutrophication:** Many laundry powders historically included sodium tripolyphosphate to soften water. When discharged into waterways, excess phosphates act as powerful agricultural fertilisers for aquatic microscopic plants, triggering explosive **algal blooms**. As dense mats of algae die, aerobic decomposing bacteria consume virtually all dissolved oxygen in the water, causing mass fish mortality and aquatic ecosystem collapse (**eutrophication**). Modern ecological detergents are formulated to be phosphate-free.

## Part G: Soaps vs Synthetic Detergents: A Direct Comparison

| Comparative Property | Soaps | Synthetic Detergents |
|---|---|---|
| **Origin & Feedstock** | Natural renewable fats and vegetable oils boiled with NaOH | Petrochemical hydrocarbons (petroleum refining) |
| **Polar Head Structure** | Carboxylate anion: **–COO⁻Na⁺** | Sulfate or Sulfonate: **–O–SO₃⁻Na⁺** or **–SO₃⁻Na⁺** |
| **Performance in Hard Water** | Forms insoluble sticky **scum**; lathers poorly; heavy wastage | **No scum**; forms rich lather immediately in hard water |
| **Solubility of Calcium Salts** | **Insoluble** ($(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca} \downarrow$) | **Completely soluble** ($(\text{R–SO}_3)_2\text{Ca}$) |
| **Cleansing Mechanism** | Micelle formation with mechanical agitation | Micelle formation with mechanical agitation |
| **Biodegradability** | 100% rapidly biodegradable | Straight-chain variants are biodegradable |
| **Aqueous pH** | Mildly alkaline (pH 9–10); can irritate delicate silk/wool | Neutral or custom-formulated; safe for fine fabrics |

## Try It Yourself: Investigating Lather Formation (With an Adult)

*(Safe tabletop home activity conducted under adult supervision.)*

> **Activity 8.1 (Lather Comparison Test):**
> 1. Obtain three clean, identical 250 mL clear glass bottles or jars fitted with secure screw-on lids. Label them **A**, **B**, and **C**, and fill each with 100 mL of distilled or boiled-and-cooled water.
> 2. **Jar A (Soft Water + Soap):** Add 5 small, identically shaved slivers of standard bath bar soap.
> 3. **Jar B (Hard Water + Soap):** Dissolve 2 generous pinches of Epsom salts (magnesium sulfate, $\text{MgSO}_4$) to create artificial hard water. Add 5 identical slivers of the same bar soap.
> 4. **Jar C (Hard Water + Detergent):** Dissolve 2 pinches of Epsom salts in the water. Add 3 drops of liquid dishwashing detergent.
> 5. Seal all three jars tightly and **shake each vigorously 10 times**. Stand the jars on a flat table and measure the height of the foam head with a ruler.
>
> **Observations:**
> - **Jar A:** Produces a towering column of clear, fluffy lather; the water below remains clear.
> - **Jar B:** Produces virtually zero foam; the water turns cloudy and turbid, filled with curdy white flakes of **insoluble magnesium scum**.
> - **Jar C:** Produces rich, dense foam instantly; no scum precipitates because the magnesium detergent salt remains completely dissolved!

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Soaps and detergents are identical substances, just sold under different brand names, and they perform identically in any water."**  
*Correction:* While both function through dual-ended amphiphilic molecules that assemble into cleansing micelles, their chemical performance depends critically on **the mineral content of the water**:
- In **soft rainwater**, both soaps and detergents lather excellently and clean with high efficiency.
- In **hard groundwater** (rich in dissolved $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ or $\text{Mg}^{2+}$), their behaviour diverges completely. Soap precipitates as greasy, insoluble calcium stearate scum, ruining clothes and wasting soap. In contrast, detergent's calcium salts remain completely dissolved, allowing it to lather profusely and wash away grease cleanly.

| Water Quality | Performance of Bar Soap | Performance of Synthetic Detergent |
|---|---|---|
| **Soft Water (Rainwater)** | Excellent lather; clean rinse | Excellent lather; clean rinse |
| **Hard Water (Well / Borehole)** | **Fails:** curdy scum forms; zero lather | **Succeeds:** rich lather; zero scum |

## Quick Recap

- **Grease & Dirt:** Non-polar hydrocarbons repelled by polar water molecules.
- **Soap:** The sodium salt of a long-chain fatty acid (e.g., sodium stearate, $\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa}$, molar mass = 306 g/mol).
- **Dual-Ended Structure:**
  - Hydrophilic ionic head ($\text{–COO}^-$): polar, dissolves in water.
  - Hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail ($\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{–}$): non-polar, dissolves in grease.
- **Micelle Cleansing:** Tails bury into the grease; heads face outward into the water. Mutual electrical repulsion between negative heads keeps micelles suspended as a colloidal emulsion that washes away with water.
- **Saponification:** Alkaline hydrolysis of natural triglycerides with NaOH: $\text{Fat} + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\text{ Soap} + \text{Glycerol}$. Solid soap is precipitated by adding salt ("salting out").
- **Hard Water Limitation:** Soap forms insoluble scum with calcium and magnesium: $2\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COONa} + \text{Ca}^{2+} \rightarrow (\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca} \downarrow + 2\text{Na}^+$.
- **Synthetic Detergents:** Feature a sulfate or sulfonate head (e.g., sodium lauryl sulfate, $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{OSO}_3\text{Na}$). Their calcium and magnesium salts are **completely water-soluble**, preventing scum and working flawlessly in hard water.
- **Environmental Safety:** Modern detergents use straight-chain hydrocarbons for biodegradability; phosphate builders are phased out to prevent algal blooms (eutrophication).

### Answers

1. Molar mass of palmitic acid ($\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{COOH}$) = $16(12) + 32(1) + 32 = 192 + 32 + 32 = \mathbf{256\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   Moles = $25.6\text{ g} \div 256\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.10\text{ mol}}$.  
   Mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.  
   - $\text{Mass of NaOH} = 0.10\text{ mol} \times 40\text{ g/mol} =$ **4.0 g**.  
   - Molar mass of sodium palmitate ($\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{COONa}$) = $16(12) + 31(1) + 32 + 23 = 192 + 31 + 32 + 23 = \mathbf{278\text{ g/mol}}$.  
     $\text{Mass of soap} = 0.10\text{ mol} \times 278\text{ g/mol} =$ **27.8 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of water} = 0.10 \times 18 = 1.8$ g.  
   Conservation check: Reactants = $25.6 + 4.0 = 29.6$ g; Products = $27.8 + 1.8 = 29.6$ g ✅.
2. Moles of tripalmitin = $40.3\text{ g} \div 806\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.050\text{ mol}}$.  
   From $\text{C}_{51}\text{H}_{98}\text{O}_6 + 3\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 3\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{COONa} + \text{C}_3\text{H}_5(\text{OH})_3$:  
   - $\text{Mass of NaOH} = (0.050 \times 3)\text{ mol} \times 40\text{ g/mol} = 0.15 \times 40 =$ **6.0 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of sodium palmitate} = (0.050 \times 3)\text{ mol} \times 278\text{ g/mol} = 0.15 \times 278 =$ **41.7 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of glycerol (M = 92)} = 0.050\text{ mol} \times 92\text{ g/mol} =$ **4.6 g**.  
   Conservation check: Reactants = $40.3 + 6.0 = 46.3$ g; Products = $41.7 + 4.6 = 46.3$ g ✅.
3. Molar mass of $\text{CaCl}_2 = 111$ g/mol. Moles = $2.22 \div 111 = \mathbf{0.020\text{ mol}}$.  
   Mole ratio is $2\text{ soap} : 1\text{ CaCl}_2 : 1\text{ scum} : 2\text{ NaCl}$.  
   - $\text{Mass of sodium palmitate wasted} = (0.020 \times 2)\text{ mol} \times 278\text{ g/mol} = 0.040 \times 278 =$ **11.12 g**.  
   - Molar mass of calcium palmitate scum ($(\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{COO})_2\text{Ca}$) = $2(255) + 40 = 510 + 40 = \mathbf{550\text{ g/mol}}$.  
     $\text{Mass of scum formed} = 0.020\text{ mol} \times 550\text{ g/mol} =$ **11.0 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of NaCl} = 0.040 \times 58.5 = 2.34$ g.  
   Check: Reactants = $11.12 + 2.22 = 13.34$ g; Products = $11.0 + 2.34 = 13.34$ g ✅.
4. Molar mass of $\text{MgSO}_4 = 24 + 32 + 64 = 120$ g/mol. Moles = $3.60 \div 120 = \mathbf{0.030\text{ mol}}$.  
   - Moles of sodium stearate (M = 306) consumed = $0.030 \times 2 = 0.060$ mol.  
     $\text{Mass of soap wasted} = 0.060\text{ mol} \times 306\text{ g/mol} =$ **18.36 g**.  
   - Molar mass of magnesium stearate scum ($(\text{C}_{17}\text{H}_{35}\text{COO})_2\text{Mg}$) = $2(283) + 24 = 566 + 24 = \mathbf{590\text{ g/mol}}$.  
     $\text{Mass of scum formed} = 0.030\text{ mol} \times 590\text{ g/mol} =$ **17.7 g**.  
   - $\text{Mass of Na}_2\text{SO}_4 = 0.030 \times 142 = 4.26$ g.  
   Check: $18.36 + 3.60 = 21.96$ g; $17.7 + 4.26 = 21.96$ g ✅.
5. (a) Mass of tail ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{25}\text{–}$) = $12(12) + 25(1) = 144 + 25 = \mathbf{169\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   (b) Mass of head ($\text{–OSO}_3\text{Na}$) = $16 + 32 + 3(16) + 23 = 16 + 32 + 48 + 23 = \mathbf{119\text{ g/mol}}$.  
   (c) Total molar mass = $169 + 119 = \mathbf{288\text{ g/mol}}$ ($12 \times 12 + 25 + 32 + 64 + 23 = 288$ g/mol ✅).
6. A 15-carbon alkane conforms to $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2}$ with $n=15$, yielding $\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{32}$ (pentadecane). Removing one hydrogen atom leaves the univalent alkyl radical **$\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{–}$**. In the form $\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+1}\text{COOH}$, palmitic acid is written as **$\text{C}_{15}\text{H}_{31}\text{COOH}$**.
