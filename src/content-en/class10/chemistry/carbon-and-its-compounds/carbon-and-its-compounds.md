# Topic 1: What Makes Carbon Unique: The Four-Handed Element

At the end of Chapter 9, we posed a fascinating question: table sugar, laundry soap, plastic bags, and gasoline look nothing alike, yet all of them are built around a single element—**carbon**. In Chapter 7, you studied covalent bonds and electronegativity, and back in Class 9 you saw that a carbon atom has 4 valence electrons. This opening topic of Chapter 10 starts with one fundamental question: **what makes this one element so extraordinarily versatile?** Today we will explore carbon's "four hands" (tetravalency), why it can link up into millions of compounds (catenation), and how pure carbon itself can appear in forms as dramatically different as a sparkling diamond, soft pencil graphite, and black charcoal.

---

## The Outdoor Charcoal Brazier, a Pencil, and a Diamond Ring

It was a crisp December evening. Out on the open patio, Mr. Miller had lit a small **charcoal brazier** to warm their hands in the chilly air. His niece Chloe was sitting at the patio table finishing her homework in her notebook, pausing every now and then to hold her hands near the warm glow. Picking up a magazine from the table, Mr. Miller pointed to a full-page jeweller's advertisement showing a sparkling, colourless **diamond ring**.

Chloe took a wooden pencil from her pencil case and rubbed its tip across her fingertip, leaving a smooth, silvery-black smudge. Then she looked at the dull, brittle black charcoal in the brazier, and back at the brilliant diamond in the magazine. "Uncle, how can these three things have anything in common? Charcoal is black and crumbly, my pencil tip is black and slippery, and a diamond is crystal-clear and hard enough to cut glass! Yet the caption at the bottom of the ad says, 'A diamond is pure carbon.' That has to be a misprint!"

Mr. Miller laughed. "That is a great question, Chloe—and it's not a misprint at all. The charcoal in the brazier, the graphite in your pencil tip, and the diamond in that ring are all made of **the exact same single element**: carbon. No other element is mixed in to make that difference; the difference comes entirely from **how the carbon atoms are bonded together**. And that same versatility is why carbon also forms the backbone of sugar, soap, gasoline, plastics, and the paper in your notebook. Let's look at what makes carbon so special."

One important detail: notice that Mr. Miller lit the charcoal brazier **outdoors on the open patio**, never inside a closed room. When Chloe asked why, he pointed to the Safety First box below: "Read this before anything else."

## Part A: Carbon's "Four Hands": Tetravalency

In Class 9, we learned that carbon (atomic number Z = 6) has the electronic configuration **2, 4**. It has 4 valence electrons in its outer shell and needs **4 more electrons** to complete a stable octet of 8. An atom can achieve an octet either by gaining/losing electrons (forming ionic bonds) or by **sharing** electrons (forming covalent bonds). For carbon, the ionic route is practically impossible:

- **Losing 4 electrons to form a C⁴⁺ cation:** the tiny carbon nucleus has only 6 protons holding onto its electrons. Stripping away 4 electrons one after another against that strong attraction requires an enormous amount of energy—far more than ordinary chemical reactions can supply.
- **Gaining 4 electrons to form a C⁴⁻ anion:** a nucleus with only 6 protons would find it extremely difficult to hold onto 10 electrons, because of the strong mutual repulsion among the 10 negatively charged electrons crowded into a small atom.

Therefore, carbon takes the **sharing route**: it shares its 4 valence electrons with other atoms to form **4 covalent bonds**. This property of having a valency of 4 is called **tetravalency**. With hydrogen, for instance, carbon forms methane, CH₄ (Class 9). Because the electronegativities of carbon (**2.5**) and hydrogen (**2.1**) differ by only 0.4 (Chapter 6), the C–H bond is **virtually non-polar** (Chapter 7).

```
   Carbon atom (Z = 6)                   Methane (CH₄): 4 covalent bonds

   nucleus: 6 protons                          H
   shell 1: 2 electrons                        I
   shell 2: 4 electrons                   H -- C -- H
   (configuration 2, 4)                        I
   needs 4 more -> forms 4 bonds               H
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Left: carbon's electronic configuration (2, 4), with 4 valence electrons needing 4 more for an octet. Right: in methane (CH₄), carbon forms 4 covalent bonds (valency 4) and each hydrogen forms 1 bond (valency 1), completing every atom's outer shell.

### Example 1. Counting bonds to find a molecular formula

**Problem:** Three carbon atoms are linked in a straight chain by single bonds, and all remaining valencies are satisfied by hydrogen atoms. How many hydrogen atoms are present, and how many covalent bonds are in the molecule altogether?

**Step 1 (valency budget):** Three carbon atoms have a total valency of 3 × 4 = **12**. The 2 C–C single bonds between them use up 2 × 2 = 4 valency units (1 from each carbon per bond). That leaves 12 − 4 = **8** unused valency units, which are filled by **8 hydrogen atoms** (each with valency 1), giving the formula **C₃H₈**.
**Step 2 (total bonds):** 2 C–C bonds + 8 C–H bonds = **10 covalent bonds**. Alternatively, (total valency of all atoms = 12 + 8) ÷ 2 = 20 ÷ 2 = **10 bonds**.

```
        H   H   H
        I   I   I
    H - C - C - C - H
        I   I   I
        H   H   H
```

> **Figure 1.2** – A three-carbon straight chain (propane, C₃H₈): every carbon atom has exactly 4 bonds and every hydrogen atom has 1 bond. The middle carbon is bonded to 2 H atoms and 2 C atoms; each end carbon is bonded to 3 H atoms and 1 C atom.

**Check:** Each carbon has 4 bonds ✅; total H atoms = 3 + 2 + 3 = 8 ✅; total bonds = 10 ✅.

## Part B: Small Size and Strong Bonds: Catenation and Millions of Compounds

Many other elements have valencies of 2, 3, or 4 (such as oxygen, nitrogen, or silicon), yet none of them builds vast families of long-chain molecules like carbon does. Why is carbon the champion of chain-building? Two reasons work together:

1. **A carbon atom is small:** when two carbon atoms share electrons, their nuclei sit close to the shared electron pair, holding it tightly. As a result, the **C–C single bond is exceptionally strong**, making long carbon chains stable instead of falling apart.
2. **Carbon has four hands (valency 4):** even after a carbon atom uses two of its bonds to link with neighbours on the left and right in a chain, it still has **two hands left over** to bond with hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine, or another carbon branch!

This remarkable self-linking ability—atoms of the same element bonding to one another to form **straight chains, branched chains, and closed rings**—is called **catenation**. Silicon (sitting directly below carbon in Group 14) also has 4 valence electrons and can form short chains of a few atoms, but because silicon atoms are larger, the Si–Si bond is much weaker and silicon chains are unstable and reactive. Carbon chains, by contrast, can run to hundreds or thousands of carbon atoms!

```
   Straight chain                 Branched chain                 Closed ring
   (4 carbons, C₄H₁₀)             (4 carbons, C₄H₁₀)             (5 carbons, C₅H₁₀)

    H   H   H   H                   H                             CH₂
    I   I   I   I                   I                            /    \
H - C - C - C - C - H           H - C - H                    CH₂        CH₂
    I   I   I   I                   I                          I          I
    H   H   H   H           H       I       H                CH₂ ------ CH₂
                            I       I       I
                        H - C ----- C ----- C - H
                            I       I       I
                            H       H       H
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Three ways carbon atoms can link up via catenation. Left: a 4-carbon straight chain, C₄H₁₀ (10 H atoms). Middle: a 4-carbon branched chain with the exact same formula, C₄H₁₀ (three CH₃ groups attached to a central CH, giving 3 + 3 + 3 + 1 = 10 H). Right: a 5-carbon ring, C₅H₁₀ (each CH₂ has 2 H bonds and 2 ring C–C bonds = valency 4; 10 H total).

Notice a striking detail in Figure 1.3: the straight chain and the branched chain share the **exact same molecular formula** (C₄H₁₀) yet have **different structures**—we will explore these **isomers** in Topic 3!

### Example 2. Comparing hydrogen counts in a chain vs a ring

**Problem:** A 5-carbon straight-chain alkane has 12 hydrogen atoms (C₅H₁₂). If those 5 carbon atoms are joined into a closed 5-membered ring (with single bonds), how many hydrogen atoms does the ring have, and how does the total number of covalent bonds compare?

**Step 1:** To close an open chain into a ring, the two end carbons must join to form one new C–C bond. To free up a hand on each end carbon, **one H atom is removed from each end** (2 H atoms in total). Thus, the 5-carbon ring has 12 − 2 = **10 H atoms**: **C₅H₁₀**.
**Step 2 (total bonds):** Open chain: (5 × 4 + 12) ÷ 2 = **16 bonds** (4 C–C + 12 C–H). Closed ring: (5 × 4 + 10) ÷ 2 = **15 bonds** (5 C–C + 10 C–H).

**Check:** Ring bonds = 5 C–C + 10 C–H = 15 ✅; chain bonds = 4 C–C + 12 C–H = 16 ✅. Closing the ring adds 1 C–C bond and removes 2 C–H bonds, reducing the total bond count by 1.

### How Many Carbon Compounds Exist—and Why?

Combine catenation with four more possibilities: (a) chains can be of **virtually any length**, (b) branches can attach at **many positions**, (c) carbon atoms can join by **single, double, or triple bonds** (C–C, C=C, C≡C; Topic 5), and (d) carbon forms strong covalent bonds with other elements such as **H, O, N, S, Cl, and Br**. Put all of these together—like LEGO bricks that can click together end-to-end, sideways, in loops, and with different-coloured pieces—and the result is astonishing: **more than ten million (10,000,000+) carbon compounds** are known today, far outnumbering the compounds of all other elements combined!

### Organic vs Inorganic Compounds

Early chemists believed that carbon compounds like sugar, urea, and oils could only be made inside **living organisms** (plants and animals) through a mysterious "vital force," which is why they named them **organic compounds**. In 1828, the German chemist **Friedrich Wöhler** synthesized **urea** (an organic compound found in urine) in his lab from an inorganic salt, disproving the vital-force myth forever. The name **organic chemistry**, however, stuck as the branch of chemistry devoted to carbon compounds.

| Feature | Organic Compounds | Inorganic Compounds (salts, mineral acids, metals) |
|---|---|---|
| Main elements | Carbon and hydrogen (often with O, N, Cl, Br, S) | Any of the elements, often including metals |
| Type of bonding | Almost always covalent | Often ionic (though some are covalent) |
| Combustibility | Most burn in air | Most do not burn |
| Melting & boiling points | Usually relatively low (e.g., wax, sugar) | Usually high for ionic solids (e.g., NaCl) |
| Solubility in water | Most are insoluble in water (with exceptions like sugar and ethanol) | Many ionic salts dissolve in water |
| Electrical conductivity | Usually non-conductors as liquids or solutions | Ionic salts conduct when molten or dissolved in water |

*(Note: A few simple carbon-containing substances—specifically **CO, CO₂, carbonates, and bicarbonates**—are traditionally classified as **inorganic** because their properties closely resemble mineral compounds.)*

## Part C: One Element, Different Forms: Allotropes

Even when carbon bonds **only to itself** as a pure element, its atoms can connect in different three-dimensional patterns, creating materials with completely different physical properties. Different structural forms of the same element are called **allotropes**. Let's compare the three crystalline allotropes of carbon.

### 1. Diamond

In diamond, **every carbon atom is bonded to 4 other carbon atoms** by strong single covalent bonds arranged symmetrically in three-dimensional space (forming a rigid 3D tetrahedral network—what we called a **giant covalent lattice** in Chapter 7). The entire diamond crystal is essentially one giant molecule locked together in every direction.

```
                       C(+3)
                       I
           C(+3) ----- C ----- C(+3)
                       I
                       C(+3)
```

> **Figure 1.4** – A small portion of the diamond lattice flattened onto the page: the central carbon atom forms 4 single covalent bonds to 4 neighbouring carbons (valency 4). Each outer carbon also forms 4 bonds—one shown here and "(+3)" continuing outward into the 3D network.

This rigid 3D network explains all of diamond's famous properties:
- **Hardest known natural substance:** every atom is locked in place by 4 strong covalent bonds in three dimensions.
- **Non-conductor of electricity:** all 4 valence electrons of every carbon atom are localized in C–C single bonds; there are no free mobile electrons to carry current.
- **Extremely high melting point** (over 3500 °C): melting requires breaking a vast network of strong covalent bonds.
- **Colourless and brilliant** when cut (it bends and reflects light strongly, making it prized in jewellery).
- **Uses:** jewellery, glass-cutting tools, rock-drilling bits, and diamond dust for polishing hard gemstones.

### 2. Graphite

In graphite, **every carbon atom is bonded to only 3 neighbouring carbon atoms** in the same plane, forming flat **hexagonal (six-membered) rings** fused together like chicken wire into **flat two-dimensional sheets (layers)**. These flat sheets are stacked one on top of another.

```
                 \             /
                  C ========= C
                 /             \
       ------- C                 C -------
                 \\           //
                  C --------- C
                 /             \
```

> **Figure 1.5** – One hexagon inside a single sheet of graphite: each of the 6 carbon atoms is bonded to 3 neighbours (2 in the ring and 1 extending outward in the sheet). To satisfy carbon's valency of 4, one bond per carbon is drawn as a double bond (=): 2 + 1 + 1 = 4. In reality, those fourth valence electrons are not fixed to one bond—they are delocalised and free to move across the entire sheet!

Because each carbon atom uses only 3 of its 4 valence electrons for single bonds within the sheet, its **fourth valence electron is free to move** along the layer. That makes graphite an **excellent conductor of electricity** (unlike diamond!). Furthermore, while the covalent bonds *within* each flat sheet are very strong, the forces *between* stacked sheets are **very weak**:

```
   Layer 1     o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o     <-- strong covalent bonds within each sheet
               . . . . . . . . . . . .     <-- weak attraction between stacked sheets
   Layer 2     o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o
               . . . . . . . . . . . .
   Layer 3     o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o-o

   When rubbed or pressed, one sheet slides easily over the next  ----->
```

> **Figure 1.6** – Side view of stacked graphite layers: strong covalent bonds (`o-o`) hold carbon atoms together inside each flat sheet, but only weak forces (`.`) hold one sheet to the next, allowing the layers to slide smoothly over one another.

This layered structure explains graphite's properties: **soft, smooth, and slippery; greyish-black with a metallic sheen; a good conductor of electricity; and a very high melting point.**
- **Uses:** **pencil "leads"** (a mixture of graphite and clay—despite the old name "pencil lead," it contains **zero lead (Pb)**!), **electrodes** in electrolysis cells and dry-cell batteries (Chapter 4), and a **dry lubricant** for heavy machinery running at high temperatures where oil would burn.
- Why are pencils graded "H" and "B"? Adding more clay makes a harder lead (**H**) that leaves a light line; adding more graphite makes a softer, blacker lead (**B**) whose layers slide onto paper effortlessly.

### Example 3. Comparing packing density in diamond and graphite

**Problem:** You have 0.35 g of pure carbon as diamond and 0.35 g of pure carbon as graphite. Given that the density of diamond is about 3.5 g/cm³ and the density of graphite is about 2.2 g/cm³, calculate the volume of each sample and find the ratio of their volumes.

**Step 1 (volume = mass ÷ density):** Diamond: 0.35 ÷ 3.5 = **0.10 cm³**. Graphite: 0.35 ÷ 2.2 = **0.159 cm³** (≈ **0.16 cm³**).
**Step 2 (ratio):** Graphite occupies 3.5 ÷ 2.2 ≈ **1.59 times** as much volume as the same mass of diamond.

**Check:** 3.5 × 0.10 = 0.35 g ✅; 2.2 × 0.159 = 0.35 g ✅. Because graphite's flat sheets are spaced relatively far apart, its atoms are packed less densely than in diamond's tight 3D network.

### 3. Fullerene (C₆₀) and Modern Carbon Nanomaterials

In 1985, scientists discovered a third crystalline allotrope of carbon: **buckminsterfullerene, C₆₀**. Unlike diamond and graphite (which are giant networks), C₆₀ consists of discrete **60-carbon molecules** shaped exactly like a **soccer ball (football)**, made of **12 pentagons and 20 hexagons**! Each carbon atom sits at a vertex bonded to 3 neighbours. Here is a neat geometry check: 12 pentagons have 12 × 5 = 60 corners, and 20 hexagons have 20 × 6 = 120 corners; since every carbon atom is shared by 3 faces (1 pentagon and 2 hexagons), the total number of carbon atoms is (60 + 120) ÷ 3 = **60** ✅!

Two related modern carbon materials worth knowing by name: **graphene** is a **single, one-atom-thick sheet** peeled off graphite, and a **carbon nanotube** is a graphene sheet rolled into a microscopic cylinder—both are extraordinarily strong and conduct electricity.

### Example 4. Stoichiometry of C₆₀

**Problem:** What is the molar mass of C₆₀? How many moles of C₆₀ molecules and how many moles of carbon atoms are present in 7.2 g of C₆₀? What mass of CO₂ is formed if 7.2 g of C₆₀ is burned completely (C₆₀ + 60O₂ → 60CO₂)? (C = 12, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of C₆₀ = 60 × 12 = **720** g/mol.
**Step 2:** Moles of C₆₀ = 7.2 ÷ 720 = **0.01 mol**. Moles of carbon atoms = 0.01 × 60 = **0.6 mol**.
**Step 3:** From the equation, 1 mol of C₆₀ gives 60 mol of CO₂, so 0.01 × 60 = **0.6 mol of CO₂** = 0.6 × 44 = **26.4 g**.

**Check:** Atom count: C 60 = 60 ✅, O 120 = 60 × 2 ✅. Conservation of mass: 7.2 g carbon + O₂ (0.6 × 32 = 19.2 g) = 26.4 g ✅.

| Property | Diamond | Graphite | Fullerene (C₆₀) |
|---|---|---|---|

## Part D: Non-Crystalline Forms: Amorphous Carbon

Several familiar forms of carbon lack a regular, long-range crystalline network; they are called **amorphous** forms of carbon (actually consisting of tiny, disordered microscopic fragments of graphite-like sheets):

| Form | How It Forms / Is Made | Key Uses |
|---|---|---|
| **Coal** | Formed underground over millions of years from buried plant matter | Fuel for power plants and industry |
| **Coke** | Made by heating coal strongly in the absence of air; hard, porous, nearly pure carbon | Reducing agent and fuel in blast furnaces (Chapter 3) |
| **Charcoal** | Made by heating wood (or plant material) in a limited supply of air | Fuel; **activated charcoal** adsorbs coloured impurities and odours from water and gases |
| **Lamp-black (soot)** | Fine black powder collected from the incomplete combustion of oil or hydrocarbon fuels | Black printing ink, paints, shoe polish, and reinforcing rubber in car tyres |

When any form of carbon burns in a plentiful supply of oxygen, it forms **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**:

C + O₂ → CO₂

When oxygen is limited, however, incomplete combustion produces deadly **carbon monoxide (CO)**—which is why braziers and charcoal grills must never be used indoors.

## Part E: The Proof: All One Element

How did chemists prove beyond doubt that colourless diamond, black graphite, and charcoal are all made of the **exact same element**? By **burning equal masses of pure samples in oxygen**! In the 1770s, **Antoine Lavoisier** used a giant magnifying lens to focus sunlight onto a diamond inside a sealed jar of oxygen; the diamond vanished and produced **only carbon dioxide gas**. Subsequent experiments showed that burning **equal masses of pure diamond, pure graphite, and pure charcoal** in excess oxygen yields **only CO₂, and in the exact same mass**. If they contained different elements, they would produce different oxides or different masses of CO₂.

```
   Teacher demo (for reading only): burning a pure carbon sample in a stream of oxygen

   oxygen     hard-glass combustion tube (open at both ends)    lime water
   cylinder    :   sample                heated zone           (Ca(OH)₂ solution)
    [  ]----->   =====[ carbon ]=======>=====\
    [  ]                 ^                     \
                  strong burner flame           \__ delivery tube dips into
                                                    lime water
                                              +-------------------+
                                              :  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ :
                                              :   (turns milky)   :
                                              +-------------------+
```

> **Figure 1.7** – Teacher demo showing that carbon burns in oxygen to form CO₂ (for reading only): dry oxygen flows through an open hard-glass combustion tube where the carbon sample is heated strongly. The exiting gas bubbles through lime water, turning it milky and proving CO₂ was formed. Before turning off the burner, the delivery tube is lifted out of the lime water to prevent suck-back.

### Example 5. Equal masses of diamond and graphite give equal masses of CO₂

**Problem:** A 0.12 g sample of pure diamond (0.6 carat; 1 carat = 0.2 g) and a 0.12 g sample of pure graphite are burned separately in excess oxygen (C + O₂ → CO₂). Calculate the mass and STP volume of CO₂ formed, and the mass of oxygen consumed in each case. (C = 12, O = 16; molar volume = 22.4 L at STP.)

**Step 1:** Moles of carbon = 0.12 ÷ 12 = **0.01 mol** (identical for both samples, since both are pure carbon).
**Step 2:** From the 1 : 1 : 1 mole ratio, CO₂ = 0.01 mol × 44 = **0.44 g** (volume = 0.01 × 22.4 = **0.224 L** = 224 mL), and O₂ consumed = 0.01 × 32 = **0.32 g** for each sample.

**Check:** Atom count: C 1 = 1, O 2 = 2 ✅. Mass balance: 0.12 + 0.32 = 0.44 g ✅. **Both give the exact same 0.44 g of CO₂**—conclusive proof that diamond and graphite are allotropes of the same element! Furthermore, under extreme temperature and pressure, graphite can even be converted into synthetic diamond in the lab.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Diamond and graphite must be different elements because one is a clear, super-hard crystal and the other is a soft, black conductor."**
It is easy to assume such radically different materials must be made of different elements. However, an element is defined by the **atomic number (type of atoms)** it contains, not by its outward appearance. Three lines of proof settle it (for reading only):

1. **Identical combustion product and mass:** 0.12 g of pure diamond and 0.12 g of pure graphite burned in oxygen produce **only CO₂**, and **0.44 g of CO₂** in both cases (Example 5).
2. **Same atoms:** 12 g of diamond and 12 g of graphite each contain exactly **1 mole of carbon atoms** (Z = 6).
3. **Interconversion:** subjecting graphite to extreme heat and pressure rearranges its 3-neighbour sheets into diamond's 4-neighbour 3D network without adding or removing any other element.

| Question | Diamond | Graphite |
|---|---|---|
| Which element is it made of? | Pure carbon (Z = 6) | Pure carbon (Z = 6) |
| Product of complete combustion | CO₂ only | CO₂ only |
| Neighbours bonded to each C atom | 4 (3D network) | 3 (flat hexagonal layers) |
| Conducts electricity? | No (no free electrons) | Yes (1 delocalised electron per C) |

**Rule to remember:** **Allotropes are different structural arrangements of the exact same element.**

## Quick Recap

- Carbon has electronic configuration **2, 4** (4 valence electrons, **tetravalent**); because forming C⁴⁺ or C⁴⁻ ions requires too much energy, carbon bonds by sharing electrons (**covalent bonding**). The C–H bond is virtually non-polar (electronegativities 2.5 and 2.1).
- Small atomic size, strong C–C bonds, and a valency of 4 give carbon unrivalled **catenation**—forming **straight chains, branched chains, and rings**, along with single, double, and triple bonds and bonds to H, O, N, Cl, and Br, yielding **millions of organic compounds**.
- **Organic compounds** are carbon compounds (mostly covalent, combustible, low-melting), though CO, CO₂, and carbonates are classed as inorganic.
- **Crystalline allotropes:** **diamond** (each C bonded to 4 C in a 3D giant network; hardest natural substance, insulator, used in jewellery and cutting tools), **graphite** (each C bonded to 3 C in flat hexagonal layers with 1 free electron; soft, slippery conductor, used in pencils, electrodes, and lubricants), and **fullerene C₆₀** (60-carbon soccer-ball molecule of 12 pentagons and 20 hexagons).
- **Amorphous forms:** coal, coke, charcoal, and lamp-black.
- Burning equal masses of pure diamond and graphite gives the exact same mass of CO₂ (C + O₂ → CO₂), proving both are pure carbon. Burning carbon in limited air forms deadly **CO**—never burn charcoal indoors.
- The 3 V pencil-lead LED test shows that graphite conducts electricity.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are for written calculation. Atomic masses: C = 12, O = 16. Molar volume = 22.4 L at STP. Approximate densities: diamond = 3.5 g/cm³, graphite = 2.2 g/cm³.)

1. A carbon atom has electron arrangement 2, 4. Explain in 3 to 4 lines why carbon does not form C⁴⁺ or C⁴⁻ ions easily and so forms covalent bonds.
2. In graphite, each carbon atom is joined to 3 other carbon atoms. How many valencies of each atom are left over after counting one bond to each neighbour? What does this leftover valency do for the properties of graphite?
3. 0.6 g of lamp-black (assume it is pure carbon) is burnt completely: C + O₂ → CO₂. Find the mass of CO₂ and its volume at STP.
4. 0.3 g of diamond and 0.3 g of graphite are burnt separately in excess oxygen. Find the mass of CO₂ from each. What does this show about the two substances?
5. A sample of C₆₀ has a mass of 14.4 g. Find the number of moles of C₆₀, the number of moles of carbon atoms, and the mass of CO₂ obtained on burning it completely (C₆₀ + 60O₂ → 60CO₂).
6. A 0.7 g piece of diamond and a 0.7 g piece of graphite are compared. Find the volume of each and say which one has its atoms packed closer together, and by what factor (ratio).
7. A sample of coke is 90 percent carbon by mass (the rest is non-combustible ash). Find the mass of CO₂ formed when 5.0 g of this coke is burnt completely.
8. In C₆₀, each carbon is joined to 3 other carbons and has no hydrogen. (a) How many carbon-carbon links are there in total? (b) Every carbon needs one more unit of valency: how many double bonds are there? (c) How many single bonds?
9. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Diamond is a good conductor of electricity. (b) The "lead" of a pencil is graphite and clay, not the metal lead. (c) Charcoal, coke, and lamp-black are different elements. (d) C₆₀ has 60 carbon atoms arranged like a football. (e) Graphite and diamond give different products when burnt in excess oxygen.
10. Why is graphite used as a lubricant, while diamond is used on tools that cut glass? Use the arrangement of atoms in each.
11. A student connects a 2 AA cell LED tester (with the 220 ohm resistor, adult nearby) with two wires touching a pencil: the LED glows when the wires touch the two sharpened ends but not when both wires touch the wooden part. Explain. Would a diamond piece in the circuit light the LED?
12. A black solid X, when burnt in oxygen, gives only a gas that turns lime water milky. 0.24 g of X gives 0.88 g of this gas. Show whether X could be pure carbon, and say whether this test can tell if X is graphite, charcoal, or lamp-black.
13. Why is a closed room unsafe for a burning charcoal brazier? Name the gas, and give two reasons why it is dangerous.
14. Why is lamp-black called amorphous but diamond crystalline? Give one use of lamp-black.

### Answers

1. Forming C⁴⁺ requires removing 4 electrons against the strong pull of 6 protons in a small atom, which takes far too much energy. Forming C⁴⁻ requires 6 protons to hold 10 mutually repelling electrons, which is also energetically unfavourable. Therefore, carbon **shares** its 4 valence electrons to form **covalent bonds**.
2. Each carbon atom has a valency of 4; forming one bond to each of its 3 neighbours uses 3 valencies, leaving **1 valency (1 valence electron) left over**. Because this fourth electron is delocalised and free to move along the sheet, graphite is a **good conductor of electricity**.
3. Moles of C = 0.6 ÷ 12 = **0.05 mol**. From the 1 : 1 ratio, CO₂ = 0.05 mol × 44 = **2.2 g**; volume at STP = 0.05 × 22.4 = **1.12 L**. Check: C 1 = 1, O 2 = 2 ✅; O₂ used = 0.05 × 32 = 1.6 g, and 0.6 + 1.6 = 2.2 g ✅.
4. In both cases, moles of C = 0.3 ÷ 12 = **0.025 mol**, producing 0.025 × 44 = **1.1 g of CO₂** (and nothing else). Producing the exact same mass of CO₂ proves both are pure carbon—**allotropes of the same element**. Check: 0.3 + (0.025 × 32 = 0.8) = 1.1 g ✅.
5. Molar mass of C₆₀ = 60 × 12 = **720** g/mol. Moles of C₆₀ = 14.4 ÷ 720 = **0.02 mol**. Moles of C atoms = 0.02 × 60 = **1.2 mol**. Mass of CO₂ = 1.2 mol × 44 = **52.8 g**. Check: C 60 = 60, O 120 = 120 ✅; mass: 14.4 + O₂ (1.2 × 32 = 38.4) = 52.8 g ✅.
6. Volume of diamond = 0.7 ÷ 3.5 = **0.20 cm³**. Volume of graphite = 0.7 ÷ 2.2 = **0.318 ≈ 0.32 cm³**. **Diamond** has its atoms packed closer together, by a factor of 3.5 ÷ 2.2 ≈ **1.59 times**. Check: 3.5 × 0.20 = 0.7 g ✅.
7. Mass of pure carbon = 5.0 × 0.90 = **4.5 g**. Moles of C = 4.5 ÷ 12 = **0.375 mol**. Mass of CO₂ = 0.375 × 44 = **16.5 g**. Check: 0.375 × 44 = 16.5 g ✅.
8. (a) Each of the 60 carbons connects to 3 neighbours: 60 × 3 ÷ 2 = **90 carbon-carbon links** (since each link is shared by 2 atoms). (b) Each carbon needs 1 more valency unit, and each double bond supplies 1 extra unit to 2 carbons: 60 ÷ 2 = **30 double bonds**. (c) Single bonds = 90 − 30 = **60 single bonds**. Check: each carbon has 1 double bond (2) + 2 single bonds (2) = valency 4 ✅.
9. (a) **False** ❌: diamond is a non-conductor because all 4 valence electrons are locked in covalent bonds. (b) **True** ✅: pencil lead is a mixture of graphite and clay, containing zero metallic lead (Pb). (c) **False** ❌: all three are amorphous forms of the same element, carbon. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌: both give only CO₂ when burned in excess oxygen.
10. In graphite, flat sheets of carbon atoms are held together only by weak forces between layers, allowing the layers to slide easily over one another as a lubricant. In diamond, every carbon atom is locked to 4 neighbours in a rigid 3D covalent network, making it hard enough to cut glass.
11. The pencil core is made of graphite, which has mobile delocalised electrons that conduct electricity and complete the circuit. Wood is an electrical insulator, so the circuit stays open. A diamond piece would **not** light the LED because all its valence electrons are localized in C–C single bonds.
12. Moles of CO₂ = 0.88 ÷ 44 = **0.02 mol**, which contains 0.02 × 12 = **0.24 g of carbon**—matching the entire 0.24 g mass of X. Thus **X is pure carbon**. However, combustion alone cannot distinguish whether X is graphite, charcoal, or lamp-black, since equal masses of any pure carbon form give the same mass of CO₂.
13. In a closed room, oxygen is depleted and incomplete combustion of carbon produces **carbon monoxide (CO)**. It is dangerous because (1) it is colourless and odourless, giving no warning, and (2) it binds to haemoglobin in the blood, blocking oxygen transport and causing fatal poisoning.
14. Lamp-black lacks a regular long-range repeating atomic arrangement (amorphous), whereas diamond has a regular repeating 3D lattice of atoms (crystalline). Use of lamp-black: black printing ink, pigments, shoe polish, or tyre rubber reinforcement.
