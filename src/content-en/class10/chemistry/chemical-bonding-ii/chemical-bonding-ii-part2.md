# Topic 2: Polar and Non-polar Molecules: The Game of Shape

In Topic 1 we zoomed in on **a single bond**: two atoms, one shared pair, and an electronegativity difference that tells us which way the pair shifts slightly (δ⁺ and δ⁻). Real substances, however, rarely consist of just one bond—water has two bonds, CO₂ has two, and methane has four. Now the question gets bigger: what about the **entire molecule**? Does every molecule containing polar bonds have a δ⁻ end and a δ⁺ end? The answer lies hidden in something we haven't paid attention to yet: the **shape** of the molecule.

---

## A Plastic Comb and a Stream of Water

Uncle Leo was standing by the bathroom sink one morning with his nephew Sam. Sam ran a dry plastic comb briskly through his dry hair several times, and then, with Uncle Leo watching, opened the faucet just a crack so a **thin, steady stream** of water trickled down. The moment Sam brought the comb near the stream—without touching it—the stream **curved sideways toward the comb**, as if an invisible hand were pulling it.

"Whoa, Uncle Leo, what kind of magic is this? Water isn't a piece of metal, and it didn't even touch the comb, yet it bent right over!" Uncle Leo chuckled. "It's not magic, Sam—it's chemistry. That stream bent because each water molecule has **two distinct ends**: one slightly positive and one slightly negative. Rubbing the comb gave it an electric charge, which attracts one of those ends." Sam immediately asked, "Wait—CO₂ has C=O bonds, and oxygen pulls harder than carbon, so oxygen must be slightly negative there too. Would a stream of liquid CO₂ bend the same way?"

Uncle Leo shook his head with a smile. "Great question. A molecule can have several bonds, but it acts as one whole unit. Think of a tug-of-war: every player pulls with their own strength, but whether the rope moves depends on the **combined pull** of everyone together. And combining pulls isn't just about strength—it's also about **direction**. Today we're going to watch a molecular tug-of-war."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Polar molecule** | A molecule that has a δ⁻ end and a δ⁺ end because the pulls of its individual bonds add up to leave an overall pull to one side | H₂O, NH₃, HCl |
| **Non-polar molecule** | A molecule in which the pulls of its bonds cancel out to zero (or none of its bonds are polar to begin with), leaving no δ⁻ or δ⁺ ends | CO₂, CH₄ |
| **Pull arrow** | An arrow drawn along a bond to show the shift of the shared electron pair: pointing from the δ⁺ atom toward the δ⁻ atom | H → O |
| **Net pull** | The overall pull left after combining all the bond-pull arrows in a molecule, taking both strength and direction into account | In H₂O, a net pull remains toward O |
| **Symmetrical shape** | A molecular shape in which identical outer atoms are arranged evenly around the central atom so that all bond arrows cancel one another out | CO₂, CH₄ |
| **Solvent** | The substance (usually a liquid) in which another substance dissolves | Water, gasoline |
| **Solute** | The substance that dissolves in a solvent | Salt in water |
| **Like dissolves like** | The rule that polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar solvents, while non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents | Wax dissolves in gasoline |

## Part A: Polar Bonds, Non-polar Molecule—How Is That Possible?

In Topic 1, we used electronegativity difference to judge the character of a single bond. Now let's draw a **pull arrow** along every polar bond: the **length** of the arrow shows how strong the pull is (from the electronegativity difference), and its **direction** points from the δ⁺ atom toward the δ⁻ atom (the direction in which the shared pair shifts).

**A molecule with two bonds has two pull arrows.** Combining them means asking: when both pulls act together, how strongly and in what direction do they tug? It works just like a tug-of-war or two kids pulling a toy wagon with ropes:

```
   1) Straight line, opposite directions, equal force (like CO₂)

         <=== A    [ wagon ]    B ===>           wagon does not move: net pull = 0


   2) Angled toward the same side, equal force (like H₂O)

          A                 B
           \               /
            \             /
             \           /
              [  wagon  ]                         wagon moves upward, between A and B
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Combining two equal pulls: when they point in exact opposite directions along a straight line, they cancel out (the wagon stays put); when they angle toward the same side, they combine to produce an overall upward pull. Bond pulls in a molecule combine the exact same way.

This gives us a clear rule:

> **Net pull = the combination of all bond-pull arrows (both strength and direction).**
> If the net pull is **zero**, the molecule is **non-polar**. If a net pull **remains**, the molecule is **polar**, with a δ⁻ end in that direction and a δ⁺ end on the opposite side.

Two bond arrows can cancel each other completely **only if** the molecule's shape points them in opposite directions. That is why molecular shape matters so much: having polar bonds is not enough—we must also check **which way they point**.

**Two-atom molecules are the simplest case:** there is only one bond, and therefore only one pull arrow, with no second arrow to cancel it. So in any two-atom molecule, **if the bond is polar, the molecule is polar**; if the bond is non-polar, the molecule is non-polar. Take HCl: difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, δ⁻ on Cl, δ⁺ on H—a **polar molecule**. The real plot twist begins in molecules with **three or more atoms**.

## Part B: CO₂ and H₂O: Straight vs. Bent

Both molecules have one central atom bonded to two identical outer atoms (two O atoms or two H atoms), and both contain polar bonds—yet their stories are completely different.

### CO₂: Straight (Linear)

Recall from Grade 9: carbon uses its 4 valence electrons to form two double bonds with two oxygen atoms, **O=C=O**. All three atoms lie in a **straight line** (a shape called "linear"—exact bond angles come in higher grades; for now, just use the picture). Carbon has no lone pairs left on it, while each oxygen atom has 2 lone pairs.

Difference between C and O: 3.5 − 2.5 = **1.0**, so each C=O bond is **polar**, with δ⁻ on O and δ⁺ on C. That gives two pull arrows pointing outward toward the two O atoms:

```
        ..                ..
        O  ====  C  ====  O                 (2 lone pairs on each O)
        ..                ..

      δ⁻          δ⁺          δ⁻

      <=== (from C to O)       (from C to O) ===>
              pointing outward, opposite directions, equal length

      Net pull = 0   =>   CO₂ is NON-POLAR
```

> **Figure 2.2** – CO₂: two polar C=O bonds, but because the molecule is linear, the two equal arrows point in opposite directions and cancel out. The central C is δ⁺, but looking at the molecule from the outside, there is no positive end and negative end—both ends are identical (O, δ⁻).

### Example 1. Step-by-Step Check for CO₂

1. Dot diagram: 4 + 6 + 6 = 16 valence electrons. O=C=O has 4 shared pairs (8 e⁻) and 2 lone pairs on each O (4 + 4 = 8 e⁻), total = 8 + 8 = **16** ✅.
2. Difference for each bond: 3.5 − 2.5 = 1.0, polar, arrow pointing from C to O.
3. Shape: straight line, arrows in opposite directions with equal length, so net pull = **0**.
4. Conclusion: bonds are **polar**, but the molecule is **non-polar**.

**Check:** 16 = 16 ✅; two equal and opposite arrows: 1 − 1 = 0 ✅.

### H₂O: Bent (V-Shaped)

Oxygen has 2 shared pairs (two O–H bonds) and **2 lone pairs**. Difference between H and O: 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4**, so each O–H bond is polar with δ⁻ on O. Now look at the shape: because of the lone pairs on oxygen, a water molecule is not straight—it is **bent** (V-shaped). (Why lone pairs cause this bend and what the exact angle is will come in higher grades; for now, use the picture.) Both pull arrows point from H toward O, angling upward toward the same side:

```
                  O        ..  ..                 (2 lone pairs on O, at the top)
                /   \
              /       \
            H           H

         δ⁺               δ⁺     <- H side (bottom, both H atoms)
                 δ⁻              <- O side (top)

      Net pull: toward the same side, upward toward O   =>   H₂O is POLAR
```

> **Figure 2.3** – H₂O: two polar O–H bonds arranged in a bent shape. Both arrows point from H to O, so instead of canceling, they reinforce each other upward. The O end is δ⁻ and the side with the two H atoms forms the δ⁺ end. (Oxygen's lone pairs are indicated schematically by `..`; their exact 3D arrangement comes in higher grades.)

### Example 2. Step-by-Step Check for H₂O

1. Dot diagram: 6 + 1 + 1 = 8 valence electrons. 2 shared pairs (4 e⁻) + 2 lone pairs (4 e⁻) = **8** ✅.
2. Difference for each bond: 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4, polar, arrow pointing from H to O.
3. Bent shape, so the two arrows do not point in opposite directions—like two kids pulling a wagon along a V-shape, the wagon moves forward between them, leaving a **non-zero net pull**.
4. Conclusion: **polar molecule**, with the O end δ⁻ and the H end δ⁺.

**Check:** 8 = 8 ✅; two arrows angled toward the same side cannot cancel (Figure 2.1, case 2) ✅.

**Big takeaway:** Both CO₂ and H₂O have one central atom bonded to two identical outer atoms, and both have polar bonds. The only difference is **shape**—straight versus bent. That's why we call it "The Game of Shape."

## Part C: NH₃ and CH₄: Pyramid vs. Four-Cornered

### NH₃: Pyramid

(Ammonia gas is pungent and toxic—for study only.) Nitrogen has 3 shared pairs and **1 lone pair**. Difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9**, so each N–H bond is polar with its arrow pointing from H to N. Because of the lone pair, NH₃ has a **pyramid** shape: N sits at the top peak, and the three H atoms form a tripod base below. All three arrows slant upward toward N—they cannot cancel, so they **add together** upward.

```
              ..                      NH₃: N at the peak, with 1 lone pair above it
              N
            / | \                     all three arrows point upward from H to N
           /  |  \
          H   H   H                   Net pull: upward toward N

                                      NH₃ is POLAR (N peak is δ⁻, H base is δ⁺)
```

> **Figure 2.4** – The pyramid of NH₃: N on top, three H atoms below. All three bond arrows slant upward toward N, combining into a strong upward net pull. (The real molecule is 3D; on paper we show a sketch.)

### CH₄: Four-Cornered (Tetrahedral by Picture)

Carbon has 4 shared pairs and **no lone pairs**. The C–H difference is 2.5 − 2.1 = **0.4**, which is tiny—each bond is nearly non-polar (our Topic 1 rule of thumb: 0.4 or less is nearly equal sharing). Even if we draw a small pull arrow from H toward C along each bond, all four H atoms are spread **completely evenly** around C in space, so the four arrows cancel one another out completely.

```
             H
             :                         Flat "plus-sign" sketch on paper:
        H -- C -- H                    four equal arrows (H toward C) in opposite pairs
             :                         add up to 0.
             H

     In 3D, the real molecule is a symmetrical four-cornered shape (tetrahedral):
     all four H atoms sit at equal distances from C, spread as far apart as possible.
     The four arrows still cancel out to zero in 3D.

     Net pull = 0   =>   CH₄ is NON-POLAR
```

> **Figure 2.5** – CH₄: four identical bonds spread evenly in all directions. Because the shape is completely symmetrical, the net pull is 0.

### Example 3. Step-by-Step Check for NH₃ and CH₄

NH₃ dot diagram: 5 + 3 = 8 valence electrons; 3 shared pairs (6 e⁻) + 1 lone pair (2 e⁻) = **8** ✅. CH₄: 4 + 4 = 8 valence electrons; 4 shared pairs = **8** ✅.

| Molecule | Bond Difference | Lone Pairs on Central Atom | Shape Picture | Do Arrows Cancel? | Molecule Polarity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| NH₃ | 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 | 1 | Pyramid | No | Polar |
| CH₄ | 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4 | 0 | Four-cornered, symmetrical | Yes | Non-polar |

**Check:** 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 ✅; 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4 ✅.

### Quick Symmetry Check

For the molecules in this chapter, ask three quick questions:

1. Draw the dot diagram. Does the **central atom have any lone pairs**? (H₂O has 2, NH₃ has 1, CO₂ and CH₄ have 0.)
2. Are **all the outer atoms identical**?
3. If the central atom has **no lone pairs** AND **all outer atoms are identical**, the shape is symmetrical and the molecule is **non-polar**. If there is a lone pair on the central atom, or if the outer atoms are not all identical, the arrows usually do not cancel and the molecule is **polar**.

This handy test works for all the molecules in this chapter (such as CO₂ and CH₄); in higher grades you will meet additional shapes and special cases.

### Example 4. Checking Two More Molecules

**Question:** Between HCl and CO₂, which molecule is polar? Both have polar bonds.

- HCl has only one bond and one pull arrow, with nothing to cancel it: **polar**.
- CO₂ has two pull arrows, identical outer atoms, and no lone pair on the central carbon: the arrows cancel out: **non-polar**.

**Check:** HCl has one arrow from H to Cl, leaving an unopposed pull of 0.9 ✅; CO₂ net pull = 0 ✅.

## Part D: The Comb and the Water Stream: Real-World Proof

All these shapes and arrows on paper might feel abstract. Where is the physical proof? Right in the comb and the stream of tap water.

Water is **polar**: its O end is δ⁻ and its H end is δ⁺. When you charge a plastic comb by rubbing it against dry hair or wool, the comb becomes **either negatively or positively charged**. Whichever sign of charge the comb carries, water molecules in the falling stream rotate so that their **oppositely charged end** faces the comb:

```
   If the comb is negative (−):                If the comb is positive (+):

     comb (−)       stream                       comb (+)       stream
        −              δ⁺  δ⁻                       +             δ⁻  δ⁺
        −              H---O  <-- molecule          +             O---H
        −       <---                                +      <---
                H end (δ⁺) turns toward                    O end (δ⁻) turns toward
                the negative comb                          the positive comb

   In both cases, the end facing the comb is attracted toward the comb.
   The stream bends toward the comb whether the comb is (−) or (+).
```

> **Figure 2.6** – Near a charged comb, polar water molecules rotate so their oppositely charged end points toward the comb: the H (δ⁺) end turns toward a (−) comb, while the O (δ⁻) end turns toward a (+) comb. Billions of molecules pulled this way deflect the entire water stream toward the comb.

### Example 5. Thinking About the Bending Stream

**Question:** If H₂O were linear like CO₂, with two equal bond arrows pointing in opposite directions, would the water stream still bend toward the comb?

- In a linear H₂O molecule, the two arrows would cancel, giving a net pull of 0 and a non-polar molecule with no δ⁻ and δ⁺ ends.
- A charged comb cannot attract a non-polar liquid stream (or deflects it negligibly).
- Yet real tap water bends dramatically toward the comb—proving that **a water molecule is bent, not straight**.

**Check:** The stream visibly bends (observed fact) ✅ and arrows in a bent shape cannot cancel (Figure 2.3) ✅. This simple sink experiment is direct visible evidence that water molecules are bent!

### Try It Yourself: Comb and Water Stream (At Home With an Adult)

1. Take a clean, dry plastic comb. Rub it briskly 20 to 30 times through dry hair or against a wool sweater. (This works best on a dry day; humid air or damp hair lets the static charge leak away.)
2. **With an adult present**, open a water tap just enough to produce a **thin, steady trickle**. Use only plain tap water, with no electrical appliances nearby.
3. Hold the charged comb near the stream without touching the water: watch the stream bend sideways toward the comb.
4. Try holding the comb near the stream again after touching the comb with your hand, or rub it again on wool: notice how the amount of bending depends on the static charge on the comb.

In a school lab, a teacher can compare this with a non-polar liquid (whose thin stream falls straight down without bending). **Never** try this at home with gasoline or any flammable or toxic non-polar liquid. Cooking oil is too thick to form a thin trickle easily, and barely responds anyway—so at home, test only plain water.

## Part E: Why Don't Water and Oil Mix? "Like Dissolves Like"

Now for the most practical consequence of molecular polarity: **what dissolves in what**. The golden rule is:

> **Like dissolves like.** Polar and ionic substances dissolve in **polar solvents** (such as water). Non-polar substances dissolve in **non-polar solvents** (such as gasoline or dry-cleaning fluids).

Why? Polar water molecules attract one another strongly—the δ⁺ end of one molecule pulls on the δ⁻ end of its neighbor. When another polar or ionic substance enters water, water molecules **surround and pull on its charged ends**, welcoming it into the mix. A **non-polar** molecule, however, has no charged ends for water to grab onto. Water molecules keep clinging tightly to each other, squeezing the non-polar molecules out until they gather in a separate layer. Simply put: **"where attractions match, substances mix; where they don't, they separate."**

In Grade 9 we saw that salt dissolves in water but not in oil ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)). Now we understand the deeper reason why!

### Try It Yourself: The Oil-and-Water Jar (At Home With an Adult)

(Kitchen materials only, with an adult present. Do not taste anything.)

1. Pour water halfway up a clean glass jar. Gently pour an equal layer of cooking oil on top.
2. Observe: the oil floats on top as a separate layer. Water and oil do not mix.
3. Screw the lid on tightly and shake the jar vigorously. Tiny oil droplets scatter through the water, but within a few minutes they rise and merge back into a separate layer on top.
4. Open the lid and drop in a **pinch of salt** and a **pinch of sugar** (one at a time or together). Watch them **sink right through the oil layer** without dissolving in it, then settle into the bottom water layer and dissolve (stirring gently speeds this up).

```
   After shaking and letting the jar rest:

     +----------------------------+
     :  oil   (non-polar)         :     <- floats on top in a separate layer
     +----------------------------+
     :  water (polar)             :     <- at the bottom; salt and sugar dissolve here
     :  salt and sugar dissolved  :
     +----------------------------+
```

> **Figure 2.7** – The oil-and-water jar: non-polar oil forms a separate top layer, while polar water sits below. Salt (ionic) and sugar (which has polar regions) dissolve in the water layer, not in the oil.

### Example 6. What Will Dissolve? Apply "Like Dissolves Like"

**Question:** If you stir a pinch of salt into cooking oil, will it dissolve? What if you mix oil with water?

- Salt is ionic (Na⁺ and Cl⁻), so it needs a polar solvent. Cooking oil is a non-polar solvent.
- Non-polar oil molecules cannot attract and pull Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions apart, so **salt does not dissolve in oil**.
- Water is polar and oil is non-polar, so **oil does not dissolve in water**.

**Check:** In the jar experiment, oil stayed separate on top while salt dissolved only in the water layer ✅.

### A Few Everyday Connections (One Line Each)

- **Dry-cleaning:** Greasy or oily stains (non-polar) won't wash out with plain water, but a non-polar dry-cleaning solvent dissolves them right away. (Never use flammable or toxic solvents at home—professional dry-cleaners use specialized closed machines.)
- **Grease stains and soap:** Soap lifts oil stains into water because one end of a soap molecule loves water (polar/ionic) while the other end loves oil (non-polar). We will explore the full story of soap in **Chapter 10**.
- **Water as the "universal solvent":** Because water is bent and strongly polar, it dissolves a huge variety of ionic and polar substances—salts, sugars, and many gases—earning it the nickname "universal solvent." The nickname is a bit of an exaggeration, though: non-polar substances like **oil, wax, and gasoline** do not dissolve in water at all, so water dissolves "many things," not literally "everything."

## Part F: How Salt Dissolves in Water: Which End Grabs Which Ion?

In Grade 9 we learned that water molecules pull the ions of salt apart. Now we can say **which end of the water molecule grabs which ion**. In H₂O, the O end is δ⁻ and the H end is δ⁺. Therefore:

- **Na⁺** (a positive ion) is attracted by the **O end (δ⁻)** of water—water molecules cluster around Na⁺ with their oxygen ends pointing inward.
- **Cl⁻** (a negative ion) is attracted by the **H end (δ⁺)** of water—water molecules cluster around Cl⁻ with their hydrogen ends pointing inward.

```
   At the edge of a salt crystal:              After dissolving in solution:

     Na+  Cl-  Na+  Cl-                             (O)                (H)
     Cl-  Na+  Cl-  Na+                              :                  :
                                              (O) : Na+ : (O)    (H) : Cl- : (H)
   Water molecules tug on surface ions:              :                  :
        H₂O  (δ⁻ O end) --> Na+                     (O)                (H)
        H₂O  (δ⁺ H end) --> Cl-                 (O) = H₂O molecule with its δ⁻ O end facing Na+
                                                (H) = H₂O molecule with its δ⁺ H end facing Cl-

   O ends surround Na+, H ends surround Cl-. The surrounded ions break free and move in solution.
```

> **Figure 2.8** – How salt dissolves at the molecular level: the O (δ⁻) end of water attracts Na⁺, while the H (δ⁺) end attracts Cl⁻. Surrounded by water molecules, the ions break away from the crystal and drift freely through the solution. (Schematic view—in reality, more water molecules surround each ion in 3D.)

### Example 7. Match the Ends

**Question:** If Mg²⁺ and Br⁻ ions are dissolved in water, which end of the water molecule points toward Mg²⁺? Which end points toward Br⁻?

- Mg²⁺ is a positive ion, so the oppositely charged δ⁻ end—the **O end**—points toward it.
- Br⁻ is a negative ion, so the oppositely charged δ⁺ end—the **H end**—points toward it.

**Check:** Opposite charges attract (positive attracts δ⁻, negative attracts δ⁺) ✅, following the exact same pattern as Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in Figure 2.8 ✅.

## Part G: Why Does HCl Form Ions in Water, While Sugar Does Not?

In Chapter 4 (Electrolysis, Topic 1) we saw that water molecules pull on HCl strongly enough to split it into H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (ionization), and in Chapter 1 (Topic 2) we learned that H⁺ in water actually exists as H₃O⁺. Now, using polar bonds and polar water molecules, let's see **why** water can pull HCl apart into ions, yet does not split sugar molecules into ions.

HCl is a polar molecule: its H end is δ⁺ and its Cl end is δ⁻ (difference 0.9). When HCl enters water:

1. The **O end (δ⁻)** of water pulls on the **H (δ⁺)** of HCl, while the **H end (δ⁺)** of other water molecules pulls on the **Cl (δ⁻)** of HCl.
2. Chlorine was already hogging the shared electron pair. With water pulling from both sides, Cl **takes the shared pair completely** and becomes a **Cl⁻** ion.
3. Hydrogen, leaving its electron behind with Cl, comes off as a bare **H⁺**, which immediately attaches to the O atom of a water molecule to form **H₃O⁺**.

```
        δ⁺    δ⁻                          the O end of water pulls on the H of HCl
        H --- Cl      +    H₂O    --->     H₃O⁺    +    Cl⁻
        (polar bond)      (polar)          (hydronium)    (chloride ion)

   atom count:   H: 1 + 2 = 3     O: 1 = 1     Cl: 1 = 1        (balanced on both sides)
   charge count: 0 = (+1) + (−1) = 0
```

> **Figure 2.9** – Ionization of HCl in water: polar HCl and polar H₂O interact to form H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Both atoms and electrical charge are balanced on the two sides.

HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻.

### Example 8. Check the Equation

**Question:** Is HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ balanced for both atoms and charge?

- Left side: H = 1 + 2 = 3, O = 1, Cl = 1.
- Right side: H = 3 (in H₃O⁺), O = 1, Cl = 1.
- Charge: left side 0 + 0 = 0; right side (+1) + (−1) = 0.

**Check:** Atoms 3 = 3, 1 = 1, 1 = 1 ✅ and charge 0 = 0 ✅.

(How oxygen's lone pair in H₂O provides "both electrons" to bond with H⁺ is a brand-new bond type coming right up in **Topic 3**.)

### Why Is Sugar Different?

Sugar is also a covalent compound and dissolves readily in water. However, the covalent bonds **inside** a sugar molecule **do not break**: water molecules simply surround intact sugar molecules and mix them into solution (sugar has polar regions that attract water), **without breaking any bond to form ions**. Because no ions are formed, a sugar solution does not conduct electricity. In HCl, by contrast, the H–Cl bond is polar and the H atom is exposed enough that water's oxygen atom pulls H⁺ clean away.

| Substance | Type of Compound | What Happens in Water | Conducts Electricity in Solution? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Salt (NaCl) | Ionic | Already-present ions are surrounded by water and separated (dissociation) | Yes |
| HCl | Polar covalent | Covalent bond breaks to form H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (ionization) | Yes |
| Sugar | Covalent (with polar regions) | Intact molecules disperse in water; no ions form | No |
| Cooking oil | Non-polar covalent | Does not dissolve at all; forms a separate layer | No |

### Example 9. Will the Bulb Light Up?

**Question:** If you stir salt, HCl, sugar, and cooking oil into separate beakers of water, which ones will make an LED conductivity tester glow?

- Salt: free Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions—glows.
- HCl: bond splits in water to give free H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions—glows. (For study only: never handle HCl at home; it is toxic and corrosive.)
- Sugar: dissolves as intact molecules with no ions—does not glow.
- Cooking oil: does not dissolve and forms no ions—does not glow.

**Check:** Wherever free ions exist in solution, electricity flows; everywhere else, it does not ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Any molecule that contains polar bonds is automatically a polar molecule."**
This is false, because whether a whole molecule is polar depends not only on having polar bonds, but also on the molecule's **shape**.

*Demonstration 1 (tug-of-war):* Two equally strong kids pull a wagon in exact opposite directions along a straight line. Each child pulls hard, yet the wagon does not budge. That is exactly what happens in CO₂: each C=O bond is polar (difference 1.0), but because CO₂ is linear, the two equal arrows point in opposite directions: 1 − 1 = 0. The molecule is non-polar.

*Demonstration 2 (comparison with water):* H₂O also has two polar bonds (difference 1.4 each), but its molecule is bent, so the two arrows angle toward the same side and cannot cancel. As a result, H₂O is polar—and a thin stream of water bends toward a charged comb. **Both molecules contain polar bonds, yet one is polar and the other is non-polar**—purely because of shape.

*One more check:* In CH₄, the bonds themselves are barely polar (0.4), and even that tiny pull cancels out completely because of the symmetrical four-cornered shape, giving a net pull of 0.

So whenever you are asked whether a molecule is polar, never stop at "the bond is polar, so the molecule is polar." Draw the pull arrows on the shape and check whether they cancel!

## Quick Recap

- **Bond polarity** is about a single bond. **Molecular polarity** is about what remains after combining the pull arrows of all the bonds in the entire molecule.
- Each polar bond has a pull arrow pointing from δ⁺ to δ⁻. Net pull = combination of all bond arrows (strength and direction).
- If the net pull is **0**, the molecule is non-polar (CO₂, CH₄). If a net pull **remains**, the molecule is polar (H₂O, NH₃, HCl).
- CO₂ is straight (linear): equal, opposite arrows cancel out. H₂O is bent and NH₃ is a pyramid: their arrows angle toward the same side and add up. CH₄ is four-cornered and symmetrical: its arrows cancel out. Exact bond angles come in higher grades.
- In any two-atom molecule, a polar bond automatically means a polar molecule.
- Water (polar) bends toward a charged comb: the H (δ⁺) end turns toward a (−) comb, and the O (δ⁻) end turns toward a (+) comb. This is direct visible proof of water's bent shape.
- **Like dissolves like:** Polar and ionic substances dissolve in water; non-polar substances (oil, wax, gasoline) do not dissolve in water, but dissolve in non-polar solvents. That is why oil and water separate, and why dry-cleaners use non-polar solvents.
- Salt in water: water's O (δ⁻) end surrounds Na⁺, and water's H (δ⁺) end surrounds Cl⁻. HCl in water: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ (ionization). Sugar dissolves in water as intact molecules and does not conduct electricity.
- Water is called the "universal solvent," though it cannot dissolve non-polar substances like oil, wax, or gasoline.

### Answers

1. CCl₄: C has 4 + 4 Cl × 7 = **32** valence electrons. In the dot diagram, 4 Cl atoms surround C; there are 4 shared pairs (8 e⁻) and 3 lone pairs on each Cl (4 × 6 = 24 e⁻): 8 + 24 = **32** ✅. C–Cl difference = 3.0 − 2.5 = **0.5**, with Cl as δ⁻ (a mildly polar bond at the lower edge of the polar covalent row). Because the central C has no lone pairs and all four outer Cl atoms are identical, the four C → Cl arrows spread out evenly in all four directions and cancel out: net pull = 0. **The molecule is non-polar**, even though each bond is slightly polar.
2. CHCl₃: C has 4 + H 1 + 3 Cl × 7 = 26 valence electrons; 4 shared pairs (8 e⁻) + 3 × 3 lone pairs on Cl (18 e⁻) = **26** ✅. C–H: 2.5 − 2.1 = **0.4**; C–Cl: 3.0 − 2.5 = **0.5**. Because the outer atoms are not all identical (1 H and 3 Cl), the bond arrows are not equal in all directions and do not cancel. The three C → Cl arrows pull toward the Cl side, and the small H → C arrow also points in that same general direction, leaving a non-zero net pull: **polar molecule** (Cl side δ⁻, H side δ⁺). Toxic—for study only.
3. Since ethanol mixes completely with water in all proportions, the rule "like dissolves like" tells us that ethanol molecules must be **polar** (or have a strongly polar part), because water is polar and polar substances dissolve in polar solvents.
4. Gasoline (petrol) is non-polar and water is polar, so **gasoline floats on top of the water as a separate layer** without mixing. Wax is also non-polar, so **the wax will dissolve in the gasoline layer**, not in the water layer, by the rule "like dissolves like." (Gasoline is highly flammable—keep this strictly as a thought question.)
5. Wax is non-polar while water is polar, so wax cannot dissolve in plain water and the stain stays put. A non-polar cleaning liquid dissolves the non-polar wax by the rule "like dissolves like."
6. (a) **False** ❌: CO₂ has polar bonds, yet the molecule is non-polar. (b) **False** ❌: HCl is a polar molecule, yet it is covalent. (c) **False** ❌: CO₂ is a non-polar molecule that contains polar C=O bonds. (d) **True** ✅: the H (δ⁺) end turns toward a (−) comb, and the O (δ⁻) end turns toward a (+) comb. (e) **False** ❌: gasoline is non-polar while water is polar; merely being covalent is not enough to make two liquids mix.
7. The stream must be thin so that its mass is small enough for the electrostatic attraction from the comb to visibly deflect it; a thick, heavy stream rushes down too strongly to bend much. It bends for both signs of charge because water molecules rotate their H (δ⁺) end toward a (−) comb and their O (δ⁻) end toward a (+) comb, producing an attraction in both cases.
8. CCl₄ is a **non-polar molecule** (symmetrical shape, arrows cancel out, and each bond has only a 0.5 difference). It has no exposed charged ends for polar water molecules to grab onto, so it neither dissolves in water nor splits into ions, and cannot conduct electricity. In HCl, the H atom is strongly δ⁺ and exposed, allowing water's oxygen atom to pull it away as H₃O⁺. (CCl₄ is toxic—for study only.)
9. In a straight line, the two equal arrows point in opposite directions: 3 − 3 = 0, so the **net pull is zero** (non-polar). If bent, the arrows do not point in opposite directions and cannot cancel, so the **net pull is not zero** (polar molecule).
10. Water is bent and strongly polar, allowing it to surround and dissolve a wide variety of ionic and polar substances (such as salt and sugar)—hence the nickname "universal solvent." However, non-polar substances such as cooking oil, wax, or gasoline do not dissolve in water.
11. Grease and oil are non-polar while water is polar, so water cannot dissolve them. Dry-cleaners use non-polar solvents that dissolve non-polar grease according to the rule "like dissolves like." (Never use flammable or toxic solvents at home.)
12. (a) A polar substance: **dissolves in water** (polar dissolves in polar). (b) An ionic substance: **dissolves in water** (water's O and H ends surround and separate the ions). (c) Gasoline (non-polar): **does not dissolve in water**.
