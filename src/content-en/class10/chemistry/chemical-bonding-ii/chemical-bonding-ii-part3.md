# Topic 3: Coordinate Bond: One Atom Supplies Both Electrons

In Topic 1 we saw that a shared pair of electrons can sit dead center or shift toward one atom, and in Topic 2 we saw how that shapes the polarity of an entire molecule. Every single one of those covalent bonds had one thing in common: **each atom contributed one electron** to make the shared pair (H and Cl gave 1 each; N and H gave 1 each). That "fifty-fifty" contribution was our standard rule for covalent bonding in Grade 9. Now for a new question: can a shared pair form when **both electrons come from the same atom**? And if it can, does that bond behave any differently once formed? Back in Chapter 1, we left a brief note that H⁺ and H₂O join through a "special coordinate bond." Today we settle that account in full.

---

## One Bill, Two Lunch Trays

Uncle David had gone to pick up his niece Hannah after school. Drama club rehearsal had just wrapped up, and Hannah said, "Uncle David, can we stop by the school café? Their grilled cheese sandwiches are amazing." At the counter, two of Hannah's classmates were standing together in line. One had his wallet in his hand; the other had accidentally left his wallet at home. The cashier said, "Two grilled cheese combos—that'll be \$6."

The friend with the wallet **paid the entire \$6 himself**. His friend didn't put in a single cent. Yet both of them received a warm sandwich tray, sat down at the same table, and ate lunch together.

Uncle David leaned over and asked Hannah, "Looking at their two trays on the table right now, can you tell which sandwich was 'paid for by a loan'?" Hannah laughed. "Of course not, Uncle David! Both trays look identical. Regardless of who paid at the register, each friend has a normal lunch in front of them." Uncle David nodded. "That's today's chemistry lesson in a nutshell. In Grade 9, every covalent bond formed like **two friends splitting the bill fifty-fifty**. Occasionally, though, **one atom pays the entire bill**: it supplies both electrons for the shared pair, while the other atom simply **accepts a share in them**. Once the bond is formed, **both atoms share the pair just like any ordinary covalent bond**. This can only happen, however, when one atom has 'spare cash' and the other has an 'empty wallet.'"

Hannah's eyes lit up. "So in chemistry, what counts as 'spare cash,' and what counts as an 'empty wallet'?" "Let's find out," said Uncle David. "And here's the best part: you've already met two famous ions built this way—the **ammonium ion** and the **hydronium ion**."

```
   Splitting the bill:   Friend A  ---- $3 ---->  counter  <---- $3 ----  Friend B
                         (both contribute half)

   One pays all:         Friend A  ---- $6 ---->  counter  <---- $0 ----  Friend B
                         (A has spare money; B has an empty wallet)

   Lunch trays:          A's tray     B's tray     both look identical, with no 'IOU' tag on either
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The lunch-bill analogy: in an ordinary covalent bond both atoms contribute half, whereas in a coordinate bond one atom pays the whole bill. Once at the table, the two trays are indistinguishable—just as once a coordinate bond forms, the electrons carry no tag showing where they came from.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Coordinate bond** | A covalent bond in which **both** electrons of the shared pair come from the same atom | The new N–H bond formed in NH₃ + H⁺ |
| **Donor** | The atom (or molecule) that provides its **lone pair** to become the shared pair (the friend who pays the bill) | N in NH₃, O in H₂O |
| **Acceptor** | The atom or ion that has an **empty space** in its outer shell and accepts a share in both electrons (the friend with the empty wallet) | H⁺ |
| **Lone pair** | A pair of outer-shell electrons on an atom that is not already shared in a bond (Grade 9) | 1 lone pair on N in NH₃ |
| **H⁺ (bare proton)** | A hydrogen atom that has lost its single electron, leaving it with **zero electrons** | In an acidic water solution |
| **Ammonium ion, NH₄⁺** | The +1 ion formed when NH₃ donates its lone pair to H⁺ | NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺ |
| **Hydronium ion, H₃O⁺** | The +1 ion formed when H₂O donates one of its lone pairs to H⁺ | H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺ |
| **Net charge** | The overall electrical balance of all protons and electrons in an ion: (protons) minus (electrons) | +1 for NH₄⁺ |

## Part A: How a Coordinate Bond Differs from an Ordinary Covalent Bond

Recall an ordinary covalent bond from Grade 9: H and Cl each contribute **one electron** to form one shared pair. When we check octets (or duplets), both electrons of that shared pair count toward both atoms.

Now look at a coordinate bond: it is the exact same shared pair of 2 electrons, counted the exact same way. The only difference is that **both electrons originally belonged to just one of the atoms**. To record how the bond formed, we sometimes draw an **arrow (→)** pointing from the **donor to the acceptor**: D → A. The arrow simply records "where the electrons came from." Once formed, the bond behaves like a **normal covalent bond**.

In a dot-and-cross diagram, we mark one atom's electrons with **o** and the other's with **x** (just as in Grade 9). Look at the difference side by side:

```
   Ordinary covalent bond                Coordinate bond

        H x o Cl                            N o o H
        x   electron from H                 o   both electrons from N
        o   electron from Cl                H+ brought zero electrons

   Shared pair: 1 x and 1 o              Shared pair: 2 o and 0 x
   Each atom contributed one             N contributed both alone (N -> H)
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Both bonds contain a shared pair of 2 electrons. The only difference is that the first pair contains one `x` and one `o` (one from each atom), whereas the second pair contains two `o`s (both supplied by the same atom).

### When Can This Happen? Two Required Conditions

Our café-bill story makes it clear that two conditions must be met at the same time:

1. **The donor must have an available lone pair** (spare cash).
2. **The acceptor must have an empty space in its valence shell** (an empty wallet, able to take in a share of two electrons).

If both atoms already have full shells and lone pairs, neither has an empty space to accept a pair. If both atoms have empty spaces and no lone pair, neither has anything to donate. In either case, a coordinate bond **cannot** form.

| Does the donor have a lone pair? | Does the acceptor have an empty space? | Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Yes | Yes | Coordinate bond forms |
| Yes | No | Does not form (no room to accept) |
| No | Yes | Does not form (nothing to donate) |
| No | No | Does not form |

### Example 1. Identify the Donor and Acceptor

**Question:** Among NH₃, H₂O, and H⁺, which can act as a donor and which as an acceptor?

- **NH₃:** Nitrogen has 5 outer electrons. It uses 3 of them to form 3 shared pairs with 3 H atoms, leaving 5 − 3 = 2 unshared electrons = **1 lone pair**. Because it has a lone pair, it can act as a **donor**.
- **H₂O:** Oxygen has 6 outer electrons. It uses 2 of them to form 2 shared pairs with 2 H atoms, leaving 6 − 2 = 4 unshared electrons = **2 lone pairs**. It can also act as a **donor**.
- **H⁺:** A neutral H atom had just 1 electron, and lost it to become H⁺. It now has **0 electrons**, and its first shell can hold up to **2 electrons** (a duplet). Because it has an empty space for a pair, it acts as an **acceptor**.

**Check:** Lone pairs in NH₃ = 1 ✅, in H₂O = 2 ✅, electrons in H⁺ = 1 − 1 = 0 ✅ (nothing to give, room to accept 2).

## Part B: The Ammonium Ion: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺

(Ammonia gas is pungent and toxic—for study only, never inhale or use at home.) What happens when an ammonia molecule (NH₃) meets an H⁺ ion? The N atom has a lone pair, and H⁺ needs 2 electrons to complete its duplet. Nitrogen offers its **entire lone pair** to share with H⁺, turning that lone pair into a shared pair between N and H.

```
         H                                           H
         x                                           x
         o                                           o
   H x o N o o       +     H+     -->        [ H x o N o o H ]+
         o                                           o
         x                                           x
         H                                           H

   NH3: 1 lone pair on N (o o)             NH4+: four shared pairs, zero lone pairs
   H+: zero electrons                      three pairs are (x o) and one pair is (o o)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺. Nitrogen's 5 valence electrons are marked `o`, and each hydrogen's single electron is marked `x`. The left, top, and bottom pairs each consist of one `x` and one `o` (ordinary covalent bonds). The pair on the right consists of **two `o`s**: that is the coordinate bond, where N supplied both electrons. The square brackets and `+` show the overall +1 charge of the ion.

Let's count the electrons. **Before:** In NH₃, N has 3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair = 4 pairs = 8 electrons. H⁺ has 0 electrons. **After:** In NH₄⁺, N has 4 bond pairs and 0 lone pairs, which is still 4 × 2 = 8 electrons. Every H atom now has a share in 2 electrons (a complete duplet).

**Notice:** By sharing N's lone pair, H⁺ completes its duplet (2 electrons, like helium). Meanwhile, N still has 8 electrons around it—its octet is **unchanged**.

In dash notation, we can draw NH₄⁺ in either of two ways:

```
              H                                H
              :                                :
      [ H --- N ---> H ]+              [ H --- N --- H ]+
              :                                :
              H                                H

      with an arrow (showing origin)   all four dashes identical (after formation)
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Dash diagrams of NH₄⁺. On the left, the coordinate bond is drawn as an arrow (→) pointing from donor N to acceptor H; on the right, all four N–H bonds are drawn as identical dashes. Both represent the exact same ion. (Vertical bonds are shown with colons here.)

### Example 2. Electron Count Check for NH₄⁺

**Question:** In NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺, count the total outer electrons to show that no extra electrons appeared out of nowhere, and verify that every atom has a complete octet or duplet.

- NH₃: 5 from N + 3 from the 3 H atoms = **8** valence electrons.
- H⁺: **0** electrons.
- Total valence electrons required in NH₄⁺: 8 + 0 = **8**. Check: 4 shared pairs × 2 = 8 ✅.
- Around N: 8 electrons (octet) ✅. Around each H: 2 electrons (duplet) ✅.

**Check:** 5 + 3 = 8, and 4 pairs × 2 = 8, matching perfectly ✅. Not a single new electron was created; one lone pair simply changed its job from sitting alone on N to being shared between N and H.

### Example 3. Where Did the +1 Charge on NH₄⁺ Come From?

**Question:** NH₃ is electrically neutral, so why does NH₄⁺ carry a +1 charge?

- Count all protons: 7 in N + 4 in the four H atoms = **11 protons**.
- Count all electrons: 8 outer electrons + 2 inner-shell electrons on N = **10 electrons**.
- Net charge = protons minus electrons = 11 − 10 = **+1**.

The +1 charge belongs to the **entire ion**, not just one atom. It comes simply from adding a positively charged proton (H⁺) that brought no electron with it.

**Check:** NH₃ (0) + H⁺ (+1) = +1 ✅. Protons: 7 + 3 = 10 in NH₃, plus 1 from H⁺, giving 11 total ✅.

## Part C: Why Are All Bonds Identical Once Formed?

Here is a crucial point. In NH₄⁺, nitrogen is bonded to **four** hydrogen atoms: three bonds formed the "ordinary" fifty-fifty way, and the fourth formed as a coordinate bond. Is that fourth bond different or weaker? **No.** All four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are completely identical.

Why? Think back to the café lunch trays: one friend paid the whole \$6 bill, yet once both trays are on the table, **nobody can tell which sandwich was paid for by whom**. Electrons work the exact same way: **all electrons are identical**. Once a shared pair sits between N and H, there is no tiny name tag on the electrons saying "originally owned by N." Both electrons are shared between N and H and hold the two atoms together in the exact same way.

Because all four bonds are indistinguishable, no single H atom in NH₄⁺ is special: if NH₄⁺ later loses an H⁺ ion, **any one of the four H atoms** is equally likely to leave, leaving behind an ordinary NH₃ molecule. (Laboratory measurements also confirm that all four N–H bonds have the exact same length and strength; the deeper reason comes in higher grades.)

### Try It Yourself: Colored Paper Tokens (At Home With an Adult)

Write **N** in the middle of a sheet of paper and place 5 **red paper tokens** around it (representing N's 5 valence electrons). For three H atoms, place 3 **blue tokens** (1 for each H). Pair them up to make three N–H shared pairs: each pair has 1 red + 1 blue token. The remaining 2 red tokens sit together as N's lone pair. Now bring in a small slip of paper labeled **H⁺** with **zero tokens**. Slide the 2 red tokens between N and H⁺. Right now, you see three (red + blue) pairs and one (red + red) pair.

Now ask a family member to close their eyes while you cover each of the four pairs with an identical small cup, hiding the colors. When they open their eyes, all they see are four identical cups sitting between N and the four H labels. Can anyone tell which cup hides the coordinate bond? **No.** And since real electrons don't have colors at all, in a real NH₄⁺ ion there is literally no difference between the four bonds! The red and blue colors were just our bookkeeping tool.

## Part D: The Hydronium Ion: H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺

Now let's watch the exact same story play out with water. In H₂O, the oxygen atom has **2 lone pairs**. When an H⁺ ion approaches, oxygen donates one of its lone pairs to share with H⁺.

```
         oo                                          oo
   H x o O o o       +     H+     -->        [ H x o O o o H ]+
         o                                           o
         x                                           x
         H                                           H

   H2O: 2 lone pairs on O                 H3O+: 1 lone pair on O, 3 shared pairs
   (one at top o o, one on right o o)     coordinate pair (o o) is on the right
```

> **Figure 3.5** – H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺. Oxygen's 6 valence electrons are marked `o`, and each hydrogen's electron is marked `x`. In H₂O there are two lone pairs (top and right). The right-hand lone pair is shared with H⁺ to form a coordinate bond. In H₃O⁺, oxygen still has 1 lone pair left at the top, along with three shared pairs (two `x o` pairs and one `o o` pair).

Look closely at the three bonds in Figure 3.5: the left pair (`x o`) and bottom pair (`o x`) formed as ordinary covalent bonds, the right pair (`o o`) formed as a coordinate bond, and one lone pair (`o o`) remains on oxygen.

### Example 4. Electron and Charge Count for H₃O⁺

**Question:** In H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺, verify that O and every H atom have complete octets/duplets, and calculate the net charge of the ion.

- In H₂O, around O: 2 bond pairs + 2 lone pairs = 4 pairs = 8 electrons.
- Oxygen shares one of its lone pairs with H⁺. Now around O: **3 bond pairs** + **1 lone pair** = 4 pairs = **8 electrons** ✅.
- Around every H atom (including the newly attached one): 2 electrons (duplet) ✅.
- Charge: protons = 8 (from O) + 3 (from 3 H) = 11; electrons = 2 (inner shell of O) + 8 (outer shell) = 10; net charge = 11 − 10 = **+1**.

**Check:** H₂O (0) + H⁺ (+1) = +1 ✅. Octet on O: 3 × 2 + 1 × 2 = 8 ✅.

### Connection to Chapter 1: Why "H⁺(aq)" Is Really H₃O⁺

In Chapter 1 (Topic 2), you learned that acids release H⁺ in water, but a bare H⁺ cannot float around alone in water—it immediately latches onto an H₂O molecule to form H₃O⁺, and we promised to explain that "special coordinate bond" in Chapter 7. Now the whole picture is crystal clear:

- **H⁺** has an **empty space** (zero electrons, room for 2). It is extremely **electron-hungry**.
- Every surrounding **H₂O** molecule has **two lone pairs** sitting right on its oxygen atom.
- The instant H⁺ appears in water, a nearby H₂O molecule donates a lone pair and snaps it up through a **coordinate bond**.

That is why whenever chemists write **H⁺(aq)** for an acid in water, what actually exists in the beaker is **H₃O⁺**. In our Chapter 1 equation HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻, that is the very same H₃O⁺ ion (and Topic 2 showed us how polar water molecules pull HCl apart in the first place). Both notations are used: H⁺(aq) is convenient shorthand, while H₃O⁺ shows the true bonded species.

(Never touch strong acids—this is strictly for study.)

## Part E: Before-and-After Comparison Table

Let's line up all four species side by side. Watch what happens to the number of lone pairs and bond pairs on the central atom.

| Species | Central Atom | Bond Pairs | Lone Pairs | Total Outer Electrons Around Central Atom |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NH₃ | N | 3 | 1 | 8 |
| NH₄⁺ | N | 4 | 0 | 8 |
| H₂O | O | 2 | 2 | 8 |
| H₃O⁺ | O | 3 | 1 | 8 |

Three patterns jump right out of this table:

1. **The donor loses one lone pair**, and that exact pair becomes **one new bond pair** (in NH₃, lone pairs drop 1 → 0; in H₂O, 2 → 1).
2. The **total electron count around the central atom stays 8**: the octet is neither broken nor exceeded.
3. The central atom of H₃O⁺ now has the same pair count as NH₃: 3 bond pairs and 1 lone pair (both distribute 8 outer electrons in the same way).

### Example 5. Can NH₄⁺ Accept One More H⁺?

**Question:** If another H⁺ approaches NH₄⁺, can a second coordinate bond form to make NH₅²⁺?

- Look at N in the table: it now has **zero lone pairs** (4 bond pairs, 0 lone pairs). It has nothing left to donate.
- Moreover, N already has 8 outer electrons—its octet is completely full.
- Condition 1 (the donor must have a lone pair) fails immediately. So **no**, NH₄⁺ cannot form a coordinate bond with another H⁺. (In addition, both carry a +1 charge and repel each other.)

**Check:** Condition 1 requires a lone pair on the donor; N in NH₄⁺ has 0 lone pairs ✅ (cannot form).

### Example 6. Two Identical Molecules: Will a Coordinate Bond Form?

**Question:** If two NH₃ molecules bump into each other, will they form a coordinate bond with each other?

- Each N atom has **1 lone pair** (so both could donate).
- However, both N atoms already have complete octets (8 electrons) and **neither has an empty space** to accept a pair.
- Both friends have cash in their wallets, and nobody has an empty wallet.
- **No coordinate bond forms.**

**Check:** Condition 2 (acceptor must have an empty space) fails for both molecules ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "In NH₄⁺, the coordinate bond is weaker or different from the other three N–H bonds."**
Demo: Look at the bond before and after formation. In the café story, one friend paid \$6 and the other paid \$0, yet once both trays are on the table, the two sandwiches are identical—neither tray has an "IOU" stamped on it. Or think of the token activity: once you cover the four electron pairs with identical cups, every cup simply holds 2 shared electrons between N and H. No pair is labeled "half N, half H" or "all from N." Consequently, if NH₄⁺ loses an H⁺ ion, **any one of the four H atoms** can leave, leaving behind the exact same NH₃. A coordinate bond describes only **how the bond was born**; once formed, it is indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond.

**Mistake 2: "H⁺ brings one electron with it into the bond."**
Demo: A neutral H atom has 1 proton and 1 electron, giving a net charge of **0**. The symbol **H⁺** means the hydrogen atom has **lost its only electron**: 1 proton, 0 electrons, net charge **+1**. If H brought an electron with it, it would be a neutral H atom (charge 0), and combining NH₃ (0) with H (0) would give a net charge of **0**, not +1. We know NH₄⁺ has a net charge of **+1** (11 protons, 10 electrons). Therefore, H⁺ **brings zero electrons**—both electrons of the new bond are supplied by N's lone pair, which is why the resulting ion carries a +1 charge.

## Quick Recap

- **Coordinate bond:** A covalent bond in which **both electrons of the shared pair come from a single atom (the donor)**. Written as D → A, with the arrow pointing from donor to acceptor.
- **Two required conditions:** The donor must have a **lone pair**, and the acceptor must have an **empty space** in its valence shell. (Café analogy: one friend has spare money, the other has an empty wallet.)
- **H⁺** has zero electrons (1 proton, 0 electrons); it acts as an **acceptor**.
- NH₃ + H⁺ → **NH₄⁺**: N donates its lone pair to H⁺; in NH₄⁺, N has 4 bond pairs, 0 lone pairs, and 8 outer electrons.
- H₂O + H⁺ → **H₃O⁺**: O donates one lone pair to H⁺; in H₃O⁺, O has 3 bond pairs, 1 lone pair, and 8 outer electrons.
- Both NH₄⁺ and H₃O⁺ carry a net charge of **+1** (11 protons, 10 electrons). The charge belongs to the **entire ion**.
- Once formed, **all four N–H bonds (and all three O–H bonds) are identical**: electrons carry no label showing which atom they came from.
- In acidic water solutions, **H⁺(aq)** actually exists as **H₃O⁺**.
- Neither NH₄⁺ (no lone pair left) nor two NH₃ molecules together (no empty space) can form a coordinate bond.

### Answers

1. Two conditions: (i) the **donor** must have an available **lone pair** (like the friend who has money to pay the whole bill), and (ii) the **acceptor** must have an **empty space** in its valence shell (like the friend with an empty wallet who can receive the meal). If either condition is missing, a coordinate bond cannot form.
2. (a) Both have money, neither has an empty wallet: **no coordinate bond** (or ordinary sharing if each contributes half). (b) One has money, one has an empty wallet: **coordinate bond** (donor and acceptor). (c) Both have empty wallets: **no bond at all**, since neither has electrons to share. (d) Both have money and each pays half: **ordinary shared pair**, where each atom contributes one electron. The "empty wallet" friend in this lesson is **H⁺** (zero electrons).
3. Count protons and electrons: (a) H₂O: protons = 8 + 2 = 10, electrons = 10, net charge = 0. (b) H⁺: proton = 1, electrons = 0, net charge = +1. (c) H₃O⁺: protons = 8 + 3 = 11, electrons = 10, net charge = +1. Check: 0 + (+1) = +1 ✅. (H₂O has 10 electrons = 8 from O + 2 from two H atoms; H⁺ brings 0 electrons, so H₃O⁺ still has 10 electrons.)
4. NH₄⁺: 14 + 4 × 1 = **18 u**. H₃O⁺: 16 + 3 × 1 = **19 u**. H₃O⁺ is heavier by **1 u**, because O (16) is 2 u heavier than N (14) while H₃O⁺ has one fewer H atom: 16 + 3 = 19 versus 14 + 4 = 18. Check: NH₃ (14 + 3 = 17) + H⁺ (1) = 18 ✅; H₂O (16 + 2 = 18) + H⁺ (1) = 19 ✅. Attaching H⁺ adds **1 u** of mass.
5. Valence electrons: 6 (from O) + 3 × 1 (from 3 H) − 1 (for the +1 charge) = **8**. In the dot diagram: O has 3 bond pairs (6 electrons) + 1 lone pair (2 electrons) = **8** ✅. They match, confirming no electrons were lost or invented.
6. No lone pair on its central atom: **NH₄⁺** (0 lone pairs on N). (H⁺ has no electrons at all, so no lone pair either.) Species that have a lone pair: **NH₃** (1), **H₂O** (2), and **H₃O⁺** (1 remaining). Of these, the neutral molecules that readily donate a lone pair to H⁺ are **NH₃** and **H₂O** (H₃O⁺ is already +1 and repels another H⁺, as in Example 5). NH₄⁺ cannot take another H⁺ because N has 0 lone pairs left and its octet of 8 electrons is already full.
7. Missing condition: **an acceptor with an empty space in its valence shell**. In two NH₃ molecules, both N atoms already have full octets (8 electrons) and only want to donate; since neither has an empty space to accept a pair, no coordinate bond forms. You need one donor and one acceptor, such as NH₃ and H⁺.
8. (a) **False** ❌: once formed, all three O–H bonds in H₃O⁺ are completely equal in strength. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌: in NH₃, N has **1** lone pair (O in H₂O has 2). (d) **True** ✅: 4 bond pairs × 2 = 8. (e) **True** ✅. (f) **False** ❌: NH₄⁺ has 7 + 4 = 11 protons and **10** electrons, not 11.
9. A bare H⁺ ion has zero electrons, giving it an **empty place** that makes it strongly electron-hungry. Every surrounding H₂O molecule has **lone pairs** on its oxygen atom. Therefore, H⁺ immediately bonds to a water molecule's lone pair to form H₃O⁺, so H⁺(aq) is really H₃O⁺.
10. Dot-cross diagram: O has 6 electrons (`o`), and the 2 H atoms have 1 electron each (`x`), forming two (`x o`) bond pairs and two (`o o`) lone pairs. One (`o o`) lone pair is shared with H⁺: that (`o o`) pair is the **coordinate pair**. Donor: **O (in H₂O)**. Acceptor: **H⁺**. Arrow direction: **from O to H⁺**. Lone pairs on donor: **2 before, 1 after**. Check: O has 3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair = 8 electrons ✅.
11. Lone pairs on N: drops **from 1 to 0**. Bond pairs on N: rises **from 3 to 4**. What stays the same: the total number of outer electrons around N stays **8** (3 × 2 + 2 = 8 before; 4 × 2 = 8 after) ✅.
12. **The bill** = the pair of electrons supplied entirely by nitrogen (and the story of how the coordinate bond was formed). **The lunch tray** = the finished N–H bonds in NH₄⁺, all four of which are identical with no "IOU" mark on any of them.
