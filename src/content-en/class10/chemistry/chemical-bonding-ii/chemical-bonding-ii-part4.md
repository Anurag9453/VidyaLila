# Topic 4: One Compound, Three Bond Types: OH⁻, NaOH, and NH₄Cl

So far we have studied chemical bonds one type at a time: ionic (give-and-take) and covalent (sharing) in Grade 9, followed by polar covalent (unequal sharing) and coordinate (one atom supplying both electrons) in this chapter. In the real world, however, compounds are often **not built from just one kind of bond**. A single chemical formula can house **two or even three different types of bonds** side by side—like smaller sub-models snapped into a larger model. Today we will learn how to spot and count every bond inside a compound, and identify **which type of bond sits where**.

---

## A Building-Block Model Within a Model

Uncle Nathan was sitting in the living room with his nephew Lucas. Lucas had a big set of interlocking building blocks spread out on the rug and had built a model of a house with a garage. Inside the garage sat a little toy car that Lucas had snapped together tightly from red and white blocks. When Lucas lifted the car out of the garage, the car itself stayed in one solid piece—it was a **tightly joined unit**.

Lucas noticed, "Uncle Nathan, the blocks making up this little car are locked together super tightly, but the car itself just rests lightly against the garage floor." Uncle Nathan nodded. "Exactly. Think of it this way: a **small cluster** (the car) is built from several blocks locked tightly together, and that whole cluster acts as **one piece inside the larger model**. So your setup has **two different kinds of connections**: the tight connections **inside** the car, and a different kind of connection **between** the car and the rest of the building."

"Does that happen inside chemical compounds too?" asked Lucas. Uncle Nathan smiled. "It sure does! In many compounds there is a **small cluster of atoms** held together **on the inside** by strong covalent bonds. That entire cluster carries an electrical charge—making it an **ion**—and it is held to an oppositely charged ion on the outside by an ionic bond. Let's look at some everyday compounds built just like that."

```
   Complete model (one compound)
   =====================================================
      [ small cluster A ]    ======    [ unit B ]
      (red + white blocks              (single yellow block)
       locked tightly inside)
   =====================================================
   Connections INSIDE cluster A         : tight, shared pairs (covalent)
   Connection BETWEEN cluster A & unit B : attraction of opposite charges (ionic)
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The building-block analogy: the connections inside a small cluster of blocks and the connection between two units in the larger model are of two different kinds. Similarly, a single chemical compound can contain both covalent and ionic bonds. (This is only an analogy.)

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Polyatomic ion** | A group of atoms held together by covalent bonds that carries an overall electrical charge and acts as a single ion | OH⁻, NH₄⁺ |
| **Hydroxide ion** | A bonded group of one O and one H atom carrying a −1 charge from 1 extra electron | OH⁻ (in NaOH, KOH) |
| **Ammonium ion** | A bonded group of one N and 4 H atoms carrying a +1 charge (NH₄⁺ from Topic 3) | NH₄⁺ (in NH₄Cl) |
| **Ionic bond (between ions)** | The electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions, whether each ion is a single atom or a polyatomic group | Between Na⁺ and OH⁻ |
| **Bracketed formula** | A formula that places parentheses around a polyatomic ion with a subscript outside whenever that ion appears more than once | Ca(OH)₂: 1 Ca and 2 OH groups |
| **Three bond types in one** | A single compound containing ionic, ordinary covalent, and coordinate bonds all together | NH₄Cl |

## Part A: What Is a Polyatomic Ion?

In Grade 9, every ion we met was a **single atom** that had lost or gained electrons: Na⁺, Cl⁻, Mg²⁺, O²⁻. Those are **monatomic (single-atom) ions**. But there is another important family of ions: **a cluster of atoms bonded together that has gained an extra electron (or lost one)**. The atoms inside the cluster are joined to each other by **covalent bonds**, while the cluster as a whole carries **one overall charge**. We call this a **polyatomic ion** ("poly" = many, "atomic" = of atoms).

To the outside world, this cluster behaves **just like a single ion**: when it combines with another ion or dissolves in water, the whole cluster stays intact instead of breaking apart into individual atoms.

```
   Single-atom ion         Polyatomic ion (cluster of atoms)
   ---------------         ---------------------------------
   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Mg²⁺        OH⁻  (2 atoms: O, H)
   (no bonds inside)       NH₄⁺ (5 atoms: N, 4 H)
                           (covalent bonds inside the cluster)
```

In this topic we focus on two famous polyatomic ions: **OH⁻** and **NH₄⁺**. We already saw how NH₄⁺ forms via a coordinate bond in Topic 3; today we pair OH⁻ and NH₄⁺ with counter-ions to build two classic compounds: **NaOH** and **NH₄Cl**.

---

## Part B: How Is the Hydroxide Ion (OH⁻) Built?

A neutral **O** atom and a neutral **H** atom by themselves do not form a stable neutral molecule of "OH," because **O** (2, 6) needs 2 more electrons to complete its octet while **H** has only 1 electron to share. If H shares its single electron with O, oxygen gets 6 + 1 = 7 outer electrons—still 1 short of 8.

Now suppose **one extra electron** (transferred from some metal atom such as sodium) joins the pair on oxygen. Now oxygen's outer count reaches **8**! That **one extra electron** gives the entire O–H cluster a net charge of **−1**, forming the **hydroxide ion, OH⁻**.

Let's look at its dot-and-cross diagram. We mark oxygen's own 6 valence electrons with **dots (.)**, hydrogen's 1 electron with **x**, and the extra electron from outside with **o**.

```
                ..
          [  o. O .x H  ]⁻
                ..
```

> **Figure 4.2** – The hydroxide ion, OH⁻. Oxygen has its own 6 dots (2 on top, 2 on the bottom, 1 on the left, 1 on the right), hydrogen brings `x`, and the extra electron is marked `o`. Around O sit 4 pairs: top, bottom, left (`o` paired with a dot), and right (a dot paired with hydrogen's `x`, forming the shared pair). Total outer electrons = 6 + 1 + 1 = 8.

Count: O has **3 lone pairs** (6 electrons) and **1 shared pair** (2 electrons) = **8** (octet). H has the 2 electrons of the shared pair = **duplet**. In dash notation we write **[ O–H ]⁻**, with 3 lone pairs on O.

### Example 1. Counting Electrons in OH⁻

**Question:** How many outer-shell electrons are in OH⁻ altogether, and where did each come from?

- Outer electrons of O: 6. Electron of H: 1. Extra electron (causing the −1 charge): 1.
- Total = 6 + 1 + 1 = **8**.
- These 8 electrons form 4 pairs: 3 lone pairs (6 e⁻) + 1 shared pair (2 e⁻).

**Check:** 6 + 2 = 8 around O ✅, 2 around H ✅, and 6 + 1 + 1 = 8 = 6 + 2 ✅.

### Example 2. How Polar Is the O–H Bond Inside OH⁻?

Electronegativity: O = 3.5, H = 2.1. Difference = 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4**. By our Topic 1 rule of thumb, this is a **polar covalent** bond: the shared pair is pulled closer to O, giving O a partial negative character and H a partial positive character (while the whole cluster carries a net −1 charge). Because 1.4 is well below the ionic threshold, **O and H do not split apart into separate ions**—OH⁻ stays together as one tightly bonded unit.

**Check:** 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 ✅, which lies in the "0.5 to 1.7" polar covalent range ✅.

## Part C: NaOH: Ionic and Covalent Together

Now bring a **Na** atom alongside the O–H group. **Na** (2, 8, 1) readily gives up its single outer electron (Chapter 6, Topic 2). It **transfers that 1 electron to the OH group**, supplying the "extra electron" we saw in Part B. Sodium becomes **Na⁺**, and the OH group becomes **OH⁻**. Because the two ions have opposite charges, they attract each other through an ionic bond to form **NaOH**.

```
   Na (2,8,1)       +      O-H  (7 electrons: O has 6 + H has 1)

         Na  ----- 1 electron ----->  [ O-H ]
         Na⁺                            OH⁻
         (2,8: 8 outer e⁻)              (8 outer e⁻, octet)

   Na⁺  :  OH⁻     <-- attraction of opposite charges  (IONIC bond)
            O - H  <-- shared electron pair, polar     (COVALENT bond)
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Structure of NaOH: Na transfers 1 electron to the OH group. An ionic bond holds Na⁺ and OH⁻ together, while a polar covalent bond holds O and H together inside OH⁻. One formula, two different bond types.

### Example 3. Counting the Bonds in NaOH (4-Step Method)

**Step 1 (Split into ions):** NaOH → Na⁺ and OH⁻. Charge balance: (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅.
**Step 2 (Look inside each ion):** Na⁺ is a single atom, so there are no bonds inside it. Inside OH⁻ there is **1 O–H covalent bond**.
**Step 3 (Any coordinate bond?):** O has lone pairs, but H already has a complete duplet and needs no more electrons, and no species has an empty valence space. So there are **0 coordinate bonds**.
**Step 4 (Summary count):**

| Bond Type | Where It Is | Count (per formula unit of NaOH) |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Between Na⁺ and OH⁻ | 1 |
| Covalent (O–H) | Inside OH⁻ | 1 |
| Coordinate | None | 0 |

**Check:** Overall charge = 0 ✅; 8 valence electrons inside OH⁻ complete the octet/duplet ✅. Atom count: 1 Na, 1 O, 1 H ✅. Formula mass: 23 + 16 + 1 = **40**.

### Example 4. Checking Bond Character via Electronegativity

Difference between Na and O: 3.5 − 0.9 = **2.6**, which is huge. That explains why the attraction **between Na⁺ and OH⁻ is ionic** (matching the "very large difference" row from Topic 1). Meanwhile, the difference between O and H is only **1.4**, so the O–H link remains covalent. Two different differences, two different bond types inside the same compound!

**Check:** 3.5 − 0.9 = 2.6 ✅ (very large, ionic) and 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 ✅ (intermediate, polar covalent).

**A quick honest note:** In a solid crystal of NaOH, each Na⁺ ion is surrounded by several OH⁻ ions and each OH⁻ ion is surrounded by several Na⁺ ions in a 3D lattice (just like Grade 9 ionic crystals). Saying "1 ionic bond" is shorthand for **one Na⁺–OH⁻ ionic pairing per formula unit**, not a single isolated pair in the crystal.

## Part D: NH₄Cl: All Three Bond Types in One Compound

Now for the star example of this topic. **NH₄Cl** (ammonium chloride) is a white crystalline salt. Inside a single formula unit of NH₄Cl, **all three** bond types—ionic, ordinary covalent, and coordinate—appear together!

First let's recall how it can be made. Ammonia gas (NH₃) and hydrogen chloride gas (HCl) react to form NH₄Cl: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl. (Both gases are toxic and irritating—for study only, never try at home.) Atom count: N: 1 = 1, H: 3 + 1 = 4 = 4, Cl: 1 = 1 ✅.

In this reaction, NH₃ accepts H⁺ from HCl (Topic 3: N donates its lone pair) to become **NH₄⁺**, while Cl keeps the shared pair from HCl to become **Cl⁻**. The oppositely charged NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions then attract each other through an ionic bond.

### What Is Inside NH₄⁺?

Inside NH₄⁺ there are **4 N–H bonds**: **3 ordinary covalent bonds** (where N and H each contributed 1 electron) and **1 coordinate bond** (formed from N's lone pair and H⁺, Topic 3). The ion carries a +1 charge because H⁺ brought no electron with it. Once formed, all four N–H bonds are identical (Topic 3), but **when counting bond origins** we list them as 3 ordinary covalent + 1 coordinate so we can see **how each bond was made**.

Electron count for NH₄⁺: N has 5 + 4 H atoms have 4 = 9 valence electrons, minus 1 for the +1 charge = **8** outer electrons. Those 8 electrons form **4 shared pairs**, giving N a complete **octet** (8) and each H a complete **duplet** (2).

For Cl⁻: Cl has 7 + 1 gained electron = **8** outer electrons = **4 lone pairs** (a complete octet).

```
                    H
                    :
         [ H ----   N  ---> H ]⁺      Cl⁻  (4 lone pairs)
                    :                 (8 outer electrons)
                    H

   N : H  --->  3 ordinary covalent bonds (dashes --)
   N ---> H :  1 coordinate bond          (arrow -->, from N to H)
   NH₄⁺  :  Cl⁻  :  ionic bond
```

> **Figure 4.4** – NH₄Cl: inside NH₄⁺ sit 4 N–H bonds (3 ordinary covalent, 1 coordinate shown with an arrow), and between NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ sits an ionic bond. Because NH₄⁺ has a +1 charge and Cl⁻ has a −1 charge, NH₄Cl is electrically neutral overall. (The 3D tetrahedral shape of NH₄⁺ is covered in higher grades.)

### Example 5. Counting the Bonds in NH₄Cl

**Step 1:** NH₄Cl → NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻. Charge balance: (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅.
**Step 2:** Inside NH₄⁺ there are 4 N–H bonds in total. Cl⁻ is a single-atom ion with no internal bonds.
**Step 3:** Out of those 4 N–H bonds, 1 is a coordinate bond (N's lone pair donated to H⁺) and the other 3 are ordinary covalent bonds.
**Step 4:** Summary count:

| Bond Type | Where It Is | Count (per formula unit of NH₄Cl) |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Between NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ | 1 |
| Ordinary covalent (N–H) | Inside NH₄⁺ | 3 |
| Coordinate (N → H) | Inside NH₄⁺ | 1 |
| **Total bonds** | | **5** |

**Check:** Inside NH₄⁺, 3 + 1 = 4 bonds for 4 H atoms ✅. Valence electrons: 8 in NH₄⁺ + 8 in Cl⁻ = **16**, matching the neutral atoms: 5 (N) + 4 (four H) + 7 (Cl) = **16** ✅.

### Example 6. Electronegativity of N–H vs. the Ionic Attraction

N–H difference: 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9**, so each N–H bond inside NH₄⁺ is polar covalent (with δ⁻ toward N). Meanwhile, the attraction holding NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ together is between two complete ions—an ionic bond (Cl⁻ is not sharing an electron pair with any single atom of NH₄⁺).

**Check:** 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 ✅, which falls between 0.5 and 1.7 (polar covalent) ✅.

### Example 7. HCl in Water: Another Compound-Like Ion Pair

In Topic 2 we saw that HCl ionizes in water to give **H₃O⁺** and **Cl⁻**. Notice that **H₃O⁺** (hydronium) is also a polyatomic ion, formed when H₂O donates a lone pair to H⁺ (Topic 3). (HCl gas and its solution are corrosive—for study only.) Let's count the bonds in the pair H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻: inside H₃O⁺ there are 3 bonds in total—**2 ordinary covalent O–H bonds** and **1 coordinate O → H bond**—plus the electrostatic attraction to Cl⁻.

Electron count in H₃O⁺: 6 (from O) + 3 (from 3 H) = 9, minus 1 for the +1 charge = **8** valence electrons: 3 shared pairs (6 e⁻) + 1 lone pair (2 e⁻), completing oxygen's octet ✅.

**Check:** 3 bonds = 3 H atoms ✅, 8 outer electrons ✅.

## Part E: A 4-Step Method for Counting Bonds in Any Formula

Let's turn what we've learned into a reliable **four-step recipe**:

1. **Split the formula into ions.** Use charges to separate the positive ion (usually a metal ion or NH₄⁺) from the negative ion (such as Cl⁻ or OH⁻). The total charge must add up to 0.
2. **Look inside each ion.** If an ion is a single atom (Na⁺, Cl⁻), it has 0 internal bonds. If it is a polyatomic cluster (OH⁻, NH₄⁺), count the covalent bonds holding its atoms together (each dash = 1 bond).
3. **Check for coordinate bonds.** Did one atom supply both electrons from a lone pair to an acceptor with an empty space (such as H⁺)? Count 1 coordinate bond in NH₄⁺ and 1 in H₃O⁺.
4. **Write the final tally:** how many ionic, how many ordinary covalent, and how many coordinate bonds.

```
   Formula --->  ions  --->  covalent inside ions  --->  coordinate?  --->  final tally
   NaOH         Na⁺ OH⁻       1 (O-H)                    none                 ionic 1, cov 1, coord 0
   NH₄Cl        NH₄⁺ Cl⁻     4 (N-H)                    1 (N → H)            ionic 1, cov 3, coord 1
```

> **Figure 4.5** – The 4-step bond-counting pipeline: split into ions, count internal covalent bonds, identify any coordinate bond, and write the tally.

### Formulas with Brackets

In **Ca(OH)₂**, the brackets mean **1 Ca²⁺ ion** paired with **2 complete OH⁻ groups** (everything inside the parentheses is doubled). Atom count: 1 Ca, 2 O, 2 H. Charge balance: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅. That is why the parentheses are essential: Ca(OH)₂ and CaOH₂ do not mean the same thing (writing CaOH₂ would mean 1 O and 2 H, which does not represent two hydroxide ions). You will practice this in the questions below.

## Part F: Why Do These Compounds Behave Like Ionic Salts?

Both NaOH and NH₄Cl are built as **ionic crystal lattices**: alternating Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions (or NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions) packed in a regular 3D grid. Because their overall structure is an ionic lattice, their bulk physical properties match those of **ionic compounds** (Grade 9: hard solids that conduct electricity when dissolved in water). NaOH is a hard white solid with a high melting point. (When heated strongly, solid NH₄Cl does not melt normally; instead it breaks back down into NH₃ and HCl vapors that recombine on cooler surfaces—for study only, never heat NH₄Cl at home.) A full comparison table with melting points appears in Topic 5.

Now here is the most important question: **when NaOH or NH₄Cl dissolves in water, which bond breaks?**

```
   NaOH  (in water)  --->   Na⁺   +   OH⁻
   NH₄Cl (in water)  --->   NH₄⁺  +   Cl⁻

   The IONIC bond BREAKS (the ions separate from each other).
   The COVALENT bonds INSIDE each polyatomic ion (O-H, N-H) DO NOT BREAK:
   OH⁻ and NH₄⁺ drift through the water as intact clusters.
```

> **Figure 4.6** – Dissolving in water breaks only the ionic attraction between ions; the covalent and coordinate bonds inside OH⁻ and NH₄⁺ stay completely intact. As a result, the solution contains intact Na⁺, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, and Cl⁻ ions.

That explains why NaOH was classified in Chapter 4 (Electrolysis) as a **strong electrolyte**: it dissociates completely into free Na⁺ and OH⁻ ions in water. NH₄Cl is a soluble salt that likewise dissociates into free NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. And NH₄OH (aqueous ammonia / ammonium hydroxide) was classified as a **weak electrolyte** because only a small fraction of it exists as separated NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ ions at any moment.

## Try It Yourself: Build a Block Model

(At home with an adult. No chemicals—just interlocking building blocks or LEGO bricks.)

Pick two different colors of blocks: **red** (for O) and **white** (for H). Snap them firmly together: that unit is your **OH⁻ cluster**. Stick a small paper label reading **"−1"** onto the cluster. Now take a **yellow** block (for Na) and label it **"+1"**. Place the yellow block lightly next to the red-white cluster (or hold them together with a small magnet): you have built **NaOH**! Notice the difference: the red-and-white blocks are snapped tightly together (covalent), whereas the yellow block is held to the cluster by charge attraction (ionic).

To model **NH₄Cl**, snap 4 **white** blocks (H) around a central **green** block (N), tying a piece of yarn with a paper arrow along one of the four connections to remind yourself it formed as a coordinate bond. Label the whole 5-block cluster **"+1"**. Place a **blue** block (Cl) labeled **"−1"** lightly beside it. Now count the connections: how many tight snaps, how many arrow-tagged snaps, and how many loose ion-to-ion pairings? (Answer: 3 ordinary covalent snaps, 1 coordinate snap, and 1 ionic pairing.)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Any compound containing a metal is purely ionic and contains no covalent bonds anywhere."**
This sounds tempting because our Grade 9 examples—NaCl, MgO, Na₂O—had only single-atom ions and therefore only ionic bonds. But it is **not always true**. Let's prove it in three quick steps:

1. **Look at NaOH.** It contains a metal (Na). Split it into ions: Na⁺ and OH⁻. Inside the OH⁻ ion, oxygen and hydrogen are held together by a **shared electron pair**—a covalent bond (Part B). So NaOH contains **1 ionic bond and 1 covalent bond**, not purely ionic bonding.
2. **Compare with Na₂O** (from Grade 9). Na₂O consists of two Na⁺ ions and one O²⁻ ion. Both Na⁺ and O²⁻ are single-atom ions, so there are **no covalent bonds inside either ion**—Na₂O really is **purely ionic**. Where is the difference? In Na₂O the negative ion O²⁻ is a **single atom**, whereas in NaOH the negative ion OH⁻ is a **polyatomic cluster**.
3. **Rule:** A metal compound is purely ionic only when its negative ion is also a single-atom ion (such as Cl⁻, O²⁻, F⁻, S²⁻). Whenever a compound contains a polyatomic ion (such as OH⁻ or NH₄⁺), there are **covalent bonds inside that ion**, so the compound contains **both ionic and covalent bonds**.

Quick visual tip: whenever you spot parentheses or a multi-atom ion group in a salt or base formula (such as Ca(OH)₂, NaOH, or NH₄Cl), immediately ask yourself: "What bonds are inside that ion?"

## Quick Recap

- **Polyatomic ion** = a cluster of atoms held together by covalent bonds that carries an overall electrical charge and behaves as a single ion: OH⁻, NH₄⁺.
- **OH⁻**: 6 outer electrons from O + 1 from H + 1 extra electron = 8 outer electrons (3 lone pairs + 1 shared pair on O). The O–H electronegativity difference is 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 (polar covalent).
- **NaOH** = Na⁺ + OH⁻: **1 ionic bond + 1 covalent bond**, 0 coordinate bonds. Na–O difference is 2.6 (ionic), while O–H is 1.4 (polar covalent).
- **NH₄Cl** = NH₄⁺ + Cl⁻: **1 ionic bond + 3 ordinary covalent bonds (N–H) + 1 coordinate bond (N → H)**—all three bond types in a single compound.
- **4-step bond count:** split into ions, count internal covalent bonds, check for coordinate bonds, write the tally.
- Parentheses in a formula mean the entire polyatomic ion occurs more than once: Ca(OH)₂ has 1 Ca²⁺ and 2 OH⁻ ions.
- Dissolving in water breaks **only the ionic attraction between ions**; the covalent bonds inside polyatomic ions do not break.
- "Contains a metal = purely ionic" is false whenever a polyatomic ion is present: NaOH has both ionic and covalent bonds, whereas Na₂O is purely ionic.

### Answers

1. KOH → K⁺ + OH⁻, charge balance (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅. **Ionic: 1** (between K⁺ and OH⁻), **ordinary covalent: 1** (O–H inside OH⁻), **coordinate: 0**. Check: 1 K, 1 O, 1 H in the formula ✅.
2. Dot-and-cross diagram: 6 dots on O, `x` from H, and extra electron `o` (just like Figure 4.2). Around O: 3 lone pairs (6 e⁻) + 1 shared pair (2 e⁻) = **8** ✅; around H: 2 ✅; total = 6 + 1 + 1 = 8 ✅. The extra electron was supplied by **K**. After giving away its 1 outer electron, K⁺ has the configuration 2, 8, 8—so **8** electrons in its new outer shell ✅.
3. Ca²⁺ + 2 OH⁻: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = **0** ✅. **Ionic attractions: 2** (one Ca²⁺ paired with two OH⁻ ions), **O–H covalent bonds: 2** (one inside each OH⁻), coordinate: 0. Atoms: 1 Ca, 2 O, 2 H ✅. Calcium gave away its 2 outer electrons, 1 to each OH⁻ group ✅.
4. Inside NH₄⁺: 3 ordinary N–H covalent bonds and 1 coordinate N → H bond. Inside OH⁻: 1 ordinary O–H covalent bond. Between NH₄⁺ and OH⁻: 1 ionic bond. **Ionic: 1, ordinary covalent: 3 + 1 = 4 (three N–H and one O–H), coordinate: 1.** No, the ionic bond is not inside NH₄⁺—it is the attraction **between** the NH₄⁺ ion and the OH⁻ ion. Check: 4 bonds inside NH₄⁺ = 4 H atoms ✅.
5. Charge balance: NH₄⁺ (+1) + charge on NO₃⁻ = 0, so the charge on NO₃⁻ is **−1**. **Ionic bond: 1** per formula unit (between NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻). Inside the NH₄⁺ part there are **4 N–H bonds**: 3 ordinary covalent + 1 coordinate. Check: 3 + 1 = 4 ✅ (bonds inside NO₃⁻ are not counted here).
6. K–O: 3.5 − 0.8 = **2.7**; Ca–O: 3.5 − 1.0 = **2.5**; O–H: 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4**; N–H: 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9**. Decreasing order: **K–O (2.7) > Ca–O (2.5) > O–H (1.4) > N–H (0.9)**. K–O and Ca–O are ionic-like (very large differences), while O–H and N–H are polar covalent. Check: 2.7 > 2.5 > 1.4 > 0.9 ✅.
7. (a) **False** ❌: in OH⁻, oxygen has **3** lone pairs (6 e⁻). (b) **False** ❌: K⁺ and OH⁻ are held together by an **ionic** bond, not a shared pair. (c) **False** ❌: each OH⁻ ion contains an O–H covalent bond, giving 2 covalent bonds per formula unit. (d) **True** ✅: 3 ordinary covalent + 1 coordinate = 4 N–H bonds in all.
8. In solid KOH, K⁺ and OH⁻ ions are locked in a rigid crystal lattice and cannot move, so solid KOH does not conduct electricity. In water, the **ionic bond breaks**, freeing K⁺ and OH⁻ ions to move through the solution. The **O–H covalent bond does not break**—each OH⁻ ion moves as an intact unit. Because free ions carry electrical charge, the solution conducts electricity (Chapter 4).
9. CaOH₂ means 1 Ca, 1 O, and 2 H. Ca(OH)₂ means 1 Ca, **2** O, and 2 H. The brackets show that the entire OH⁻ group is present **twice**, so there are 2 oxygen atoms as well as 2 hydrogen atoms. Without brackets, there is only 1 oxygen atom and the charges do not balance (Ca²⁺ requires two OH⁻ ions). Check: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅.
10. NH₄OH ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻ (the reversible arrow ⇌ shows that only a small fraction splits into ions). The bond that breaks is the **ionic** attraction between NH₄⁺ and OH⁻. **Bonds that stay intact:** the 3 ordinary N–H covalent bonds and 1 coordinate N → H bond inside NH₄⁺, plus the 1 O–H covalent bond inside OH⁻. Check: atoms N = 1, H = 5, O = 1 on both sides ✅, charge (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅.
11. Snap one red block (O) and one white block (H) **firmly** together to represent the OH⁻ group, and tag the pair with **"−1"**. Tag a yellow block (K) with **"+1"** and place it **lightly** (or with a small magnet) next to the red-white group. Tight snap = O–H covalent bond; loose pairing = K⁺–OH⁻ ionic bond. Count: 1 tight snap, 1 loose pairing. Check: KOH has 1 K, 1 O, 1 H ✅.
12. The O–H electronegativity difference is 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4**, which lies in the polar covalent zone well below the ionic cutoff (~2.0). Oxygen pulls the shared pair closer to itself but does not strip it away completely from H, so the O–H bond remains strongly covalent. When KOH dissolves in water, only the K⁺–OH⁻ ionic attraction breaks. Check: 1.4 < 2.0 ✅.
