# Topic 5: Comparing All Bonds: Properties and Their Causes

From Topic 1 to Topic 4 we explored the many faces of chemical bonding: **polar covalent bonds** (a shared pair shifted toward one side), **molecular polarity** (the game of shape and dissolving in water), **coordinate bonds** (one atom supplying both shared electrons), and **three bond types** coexisting inside a single compound. In Grade 9 we compared ionic and covalent compounds in a two-column table; today we take that comparison **much deeper**. Equipped with four bond names, electronegativity differences, and polarity, we will assemble **one master table** and uncover the **underlying cause** behind every physical property: why one solid melts at 801 °C while another substance boils way down at −85 °C, why some covalent compounds dissolve readily in water while others float on top, and why a salt crystal shatters under a hammer.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lattice** | A regular, repeating 3D grid of ions in which every ion is held tightly from all sides by oppositely charged neighbors | A crystal of NaCl |
| **Collection of molecules** | Separate, individual molecules that attract one another only weakly | Methane gas or candle wax |

## Part A: Master Table: Four Bond Types at a Glance

First let's compare the **bonds themselves**: how each bond forms and how we recognize it. (Approximate electronegativity values are the same as before: H 2.1, C 2.5, N 3.0, O 3.5, F 4.0, Cl 3.0, Na 0.9, Mg 1.2.)

| Feature | Ionic | Polar Covalent | Non-polar Covalent | Coordinate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| How it forms | A metal transfers electron(s) to a non-metal | Two non-metals share a pair, but one atom pulls harder | Two identical (or nearly identical) atoms share a pair equally | Both shared electrons come from one atom's lone pair into another's empty space |
| Electronegativity difference (rule of thumb) | Very large, ~2.0 or more (1.7 to 2.0 is a borderline zone, Topic 1) | About 0.5 to 1.7 | 0 to about 0.4 | No separate cutoff; once formed, it is indistinguishable from an ordinary covalent bond |
| Building unit | Crystal lattice of ions | Discrete molecule | Discrete molecule | A link inside a molecule or polyatomic ion |
| In a dot diagram | Oppositely charged ions in brackets | Shared pair shifted toward one atom | Shared pair centered between atoms | Shared pair marked with an arrow from donor to acceptor |
| Example | NaCl, MgO | HCl, O–H in water | H₂, Cl₂, N₂, C–H in CH₄ (nearly) | NH₃ → H⁺ forming NH₄⁺ |

Keep three key reminders in mind:

- The difference cutoffs are **rules of thumb**, not sharp walls: bond character shifts gradually. Even though HF has a difference of 1.9 (right near the ionic border), it is still a molecular substance (Topic 1).
- Calling a bond a **coordinate bond** describes **how it was formed**, not a different kind of glue. As we saw in Topic 3, once NH₄⁺ forms, all four N–H bonds are identical. So when we discuss bulk physical properties, a coordinate bond acts **just like a covalent bond**.
- Physical properties belong to an **entire substance**, not an isolated bond. The properties of NaCl come from its ionic lattice; the properties of HCl come from its polar molecules. Even though NH₄Cl contains all three bond types (Topic 4), its bulk properties are governed by its **ionic lattice** of NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions.

## Part B: Melting and Boiling Points: Why Such a Huge Gap?

Start with one crucial question: when we melt or boil a substance, **what attraction are we actually overcoming?**

- To melt an **ionic compound** (such as NaCl), we must overcome the **strong electrostatic attractions between ions**. In a crystal lattice, every positive ion is gripped tightly from **all directions** by surrounding negative ions, and vice versa.
- To melt or boil a **molecular covalent substance**, we only have to overcome the **weak attractions between neighboring molecules**. The strong covalent bonds **inside** each molecule (such as H–Cl or C–H) **do not break** during melting or boiling!

In Grade 9 we stated simply that ionic compounds have high melting points and covalent compounds have low melting points. Now we see the exact structural reason why.

```
   IONIC (crystal lattice)             COVALENT (collection of molecules)

   +  -  +  -                          CH4    CH4    CH4
   -  +  -  +                            CH4    CH4    CH4
   +  -  +  -
   -  +  -  +                          bonds inside each molecule are strong,
                                       but molecule-to-molecule attraction is weak
   every ion is gripped from all
   sides by oppositely charged ions
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Left: an ionic lattice (4 rows by 4 columns = 16 ions: 8 positive and 8 negative, each surrounded by opposite charges). Right: 6 separate methane molecules attracting one another only weakly. (Schematic sketch: exact ion spacings and 3D molecular shapes are not drawn to scale.)

### One Temperature Ladder: Approximate Melting and Boiling Points

```
   2850 °C   MgO melts           (ionic)
    801 °C   NaCl melts          (ionic)
    186 °C   sugar decomposes    (covalent)
    100 °C   water boils         (covalent)
     78 °C   ethanol boils       (covalent)
     60 °C   wax melts           (covalent)
      0 °C   water melts         (covalent)
    -33 °C   ammonia boils       (covalent)
    -85 °C   HCl boils           (covalent)
   -162 °C   methane boils       (covalent)
```

> **Figure 5.3** – A temperature ladder from hottest to coldest (approximate values). Note that some entries list **melting** points while others list **boiling** points, so don't compare two arbitrary lines mechanically—instead, notice the big picture: ionic solids require **801 °C to 2850 °C just to melt**, whereas many covalent substances **boil into gas** at 100 °C, 78 °C, or even far below 0 °C!

### Example 1. NaCl vs. HCl: Why an 886 °C Gap?

**Question:** NaCl melts (solid to liquid) at about 801 °C, whereas HCl **boils** (liquid to gas) at about −85 °C. How large is the temperature gap between them, and what causes it?

- Gap = 801 − (−85) = **886 °C**.
- NaCl is a 3D ionic lattice of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Melting it requires pulling every ion loose from the strong grip of surrounding oppositely charged ions—which takes a huge amount of thermal energy.
- HCl consists of discrete polar molecules (Topic 1: difference 0.9), and the attraction **between** one HCl molecule and the next is comparatively **weak**. Even at −85 °C, thermal motion is enough to separate the molecules into a gas.
- Meanwhile, the covalent H–Cl bond **inside** each molecule stays completely intact—boiling merely separates HCl molecules from one another.

**Check:** 801 + 85 = 886 ✅. The fact that a covalent substance's **boiling** point sits nearly 900 °C below an ionic solid's **melting** point is dramatic proof of how strongly an ionic lattice holds together ✅.

### Example 2. Why MgO Melts Higher Than NaCl

In Grade 9 we noted that MgO (about 2850 °C) melts much higher than NaCl (about 801 °C) because Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ carry **double charges** (2+ and 2−), whereas Na⁺ and Cl⁻ carry only single charges (1+ and 1−). Larger opposite charges attract each other much more strongly, making the MgO lattice far harder to melt.

**Check:** Difference = 2850 − 801 = **2049 °C**—a massive jump ✅. (Quantitative lattice calculations come in higher grades; charge size explains the trend clearly here.)

### Example 3. Sugar Starts Decomposing Around 186 °C as It Melts

Since sugar is covalent, shouldn't it melt cleanly at a low temperature like candle wax? Actually, sugar doesn't melt until about 186 °C, and even then it doesn't melt cleanly—around **186 °C** it begins to **break down chemically (decompose)** as it melts, turning brown into caramel. A sugar molecule is large and packed with polar O–H groups that hold neighboring sugar molecules together strongly; by the time you heat it enough to pull the molecules apart, some covalent bonds inside the sugar molecule start breaking too.

**Check:** Sugar (~186 °C) requires 186 − 60 = **126 °C** more heat than candle wax (~60 °C) ✅, because sugar molecules have many polar O–H regions while wax molecules are non-polar.

### Example 4. Candle Wax Softens and Melts Around 60 °C

Wax consists of long non-polar molecules that attract one another weakly. As a result, wax softens gradually and melts around **60 °C**. The gentle heat near a candle flame easily melts a pool of liquid wax right below the wick.

**Check:** 60 °C is far below the melting point of an ionic crystal like NaCl (801 °C) ✅.

### Example 5. Water vs. Methane: Both Covalent, Yet 262 °C Apart

Methane (CH₄, C–H difference 0.4, non-polar molecule) boils at about −162 °C. Water (H₂O, O–H difference 1.4, strongly polar molecule) boils at 100 °C. Gap = 100 − (−162) = **262 °C**.

Both are small covalent molecules, so why does water boil 262 °C higher? One key reason is that water molecules are **strongly polar** (Topic 2) and attract one another much more tightly than non-polar methane molecules do (this extra-strong attraction between water molecules has a special name you will learn in higher grades). For now, remember this important lesson: molecular covalent substances span a **wide range** of melting and boiling points depending on how strongly their molecules attract one another.

**Check:** 100 + 162 = 262 ✅.

## Part C: Hardness, Brittleness, Electrical Conductivity, and Reaction Speed

### Hard Yet Brittle: The Ionic Crystal

A crystal of rock salt is hard and rigid, yet if you strike it with a hammer it **shatters cleanly** instead of bending or denting. Look at the lattice to see why: in an undisturbed crystal, every `+` ion sits next to `−` ions. If a hammer blow shoves one layer of ions **just one step sideways**, suddenly `+` aligns directly against `+` and `−` aligns directly against `−`. Like charges **repel fiercely**, pushing the two layers apart and splitting the crystal along a flat face!

```
   Before (stable attraction):     After shifting top row by one step:

   + - + -                           + - + -
   - + - +                         - + - +

   + sits above - (attraction)       + sits above +  (strong repulsion!)
                                     - sits above -  (strong repulsion!)
```

> **Figure 5.4** – Two rows of an ionic crystal. Left: every ion faces an opposite charge and holds tight. Right: sliding the top row one step to the right brings `+` over `+` and `−` over `−` in the middle columns; like-charge repulsion splits the crystal apart (brittleness).

By contrast, a molecular solid like candle wax is **soft**: its neutral molecules can slide past one another when pressed without triggering any like-charge repulsion.

### Electrical Conductivity: Who Conducts, and When?

| Substance | Solid State | Molten (Liquid) State | Dissolved in Water |
|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl (ionic) | Does not conduct (ions locked in place) | Conducts (free mobile ions) | Conducts (free mobile ions) |
| Sugar (covalent) | Does not conduct | Does not conduct (neutral molecules) | Does not conduct (dissolves as intact molecules) |
| HCl (polar covalent) | Does not conduct (molecular) | Does not conduct (neutral molecules) | **Conducts** (reacts with water to form H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, Topic 2) |
| Ethanol (covalent) | Does not conduct | Does not conduct | Does not conduct |

Notice the key distinction here: an **ionic** compound conducts whenever its already-existing ions become **free to move** (melted or dissolved). **HCl**, on the other hand, is a covalent molecule that **forms** ions only when it reacts with water (connecting directly to HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ in Chapter 4), so aqueous HCl conducts electricity even though pure liquid or gaseous HCl does not. Aqueous ammonia (NH₃ in water) forms **only a tiny fraction** of ions (Chapter 4), so it conducts only weakly.

### Speed of Chemical Reactions: A Quick Preview

In **ionic solutions**, the ions are **already separated and free** in the water. The instant you mix two ionic solutions, oppositely charged ions collide and react almost instantaneously—like acid-base neutralization (Chapter 1), where H⁺ and OH⁻ snap together to form water in a split second. Reactions between **covalent substances**, by contrast, are usually **much slower**: existing covalent bonds must first be broken before new bonds can form, which takes time and energy. That is why a wax candle does not burst into flame on its own in room air—it needs a lit match to get started. This contrast will be central when we study **organic chemistry** later on.

## Part D: Solubility in Water

In Topic 2 we learned the golden rule: **like dissolves like**. Polar and ionic substances dissolve in polar water, whereas non-polar substances dissolve in non-polar solvents. That shatters a common myth: "covalent means insoluble in water" is flat-out wrong!

| Substance | Bond / Molecule Type | In Water | Conducts in Water? | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| NaCl | Ionic | Dissolves | Yes | Polar water molecules surround and separate Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (Topic 2) |
| Sugar | Covalent with many polar O–H groups | Dissolves | No | Polar regions attract water molecules, dispersing intact sugar molecules without forming ions |
| Ethanol | Covalent with a polar O–H group | Mixes completely | No | Polar molecule mixes with polar water without forming ions |
| Ammonia (NH₃) | Polar covalent | Dissolves极readily (very soluble) | Weakly | Polar molecule attracts water; a small fraction reacts to form NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ ions |
| HCl | Polar covalent | Dissolves | Yes | Polar water pulls HCl apart into H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions |
| Cooking oil, wax | Non-polar covalent | Does not dissolve | No | Non-polar molecules have no charged ends to attract polar water |
| CCl₄ | Polar bonds, but non-polar molecule | Does not dissolve | No | Symmetrical shape cancels bond pulls, making the whole molecule non-polar (Topic 2) |

**Safety reminder:** Ammonia, HCl, CCl₄, and ethanol are discussed here strictly for study—never prepare, inhale, or experiment with them at home. Ammonia and HCl are toxic and corrosive, CCl₄ is toxic, and ethanol is flammable.

## Part E: The 4-Step Decision Flow

Now let's put everything into a **four-step decision flow** that lets you identify the bonding and predict the properties of any compound you meet:

```
   STEP 1   Find the electronegativity difference of the bond
              difference ~0 to 0.4           non-polar covalent
              difference ~0.5 to 1.7         polar covalent
              difference ~2.0 or above       ionic-like
                        v
   STEP 2   Is it a metal combined with a non-metal?
              yes --> confirms ionic bond      no --> covalent
                        v
   STEP 3   Does the formula contain a polyatomic ion (NH4+, OH-)?
              yes --> count covalent bonds inside the ion too (Topic 4)
                        v
   STEP 4   Did one atom's lone pair supply both shared electrons into an empty space?
              yes --> coordinate bond (donor --> acceptor)
```

> **Figure 5.5** – The 4-step decision flow. Remember that Step 1's numbers are **rules of thumb**: borderline cases (such as 1.7 to 2.0 in Topic 1) sit on a gradual spectrum rather than a brick wall.

### Example 6. NaCl

- Step 1: Na = 0.9, Cl = 3.0. Difference = 3.0 − 0.9 = **2.1** (very large).
- Step 2: Na is a metal, Cl is a non-metal—confirms ionic.
- Step 3: No polyatomic ion. Step 4: No lone-pair donation.
- **Conclusion:** Ionic lattice. Predicts **high melting point** (801 °C), hard and brittle crystal, soluble in water, and conducts electricity when melted or dissolved.

**Check:** 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1 ✅.

### Example 7. HCl

- Step 1: H = 2.1, Cl = 3.0. Difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9** (polar covalent).
- Step 2: Both are non-metals—covalent. Steps 3 and 4: No.
- **Conclusion:** Polar covalent molecule. Predicts a **gas** at room temperature (boils at −85 °C) that ionizes in water to form a conducting solution.

**Check:** 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 ✅, squarely in the polar covalent zone.

### Example 8. CH₄

- Step 1: C = 2.5, H = 2.1. Difference = **0.4** (nearly non-polar, right at the rule-of-thumb border).
- Step 2: Both are non-metals. Steps 3 and 4: No.
- **Conclusion:** Non-polar covalent molecule. Predicts a **gas** (boils at −162 °C) that does not dissolve in water.

**Check:** 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4 ✅.

### Example 9. Ammonia and Step 4 (Coordinate Bond)

- Step 1: N = 3.0, H = 2.1. Difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9** (polar covalent). Ammonia boils at −33 °C.
- Step 4: Nitrogen has **1 lone pair**. When an H⁺ ion (with an empty valence space) approaches, N donates that lone pair to share both electrons: a **coordinate bond**.

```
   H₃N:   +   H⁺    --->    [ H₃N → H ]⁺
   1 lone pair on N         lone pair becomes a shared pair; arrow from donor to acceptor
```

> **Figure 5.6** – Step 4 in action (recalling Topic 3): ammonia + H⁺. Electron count: NH₃ has 5 + 3 = 8 outer electrons (3 bond pairs + 1 lone pair). NH₄⁺ also has 8 outer electrons (4 bond pairs) because H⁺ brings 0 electrons, and the +1 charge belongs to the whole ion.

**Check:** NH₄⁺ has 5 + 4 − 1 = 8 outer electrons = 4 pairs ✅. Lone pairs on N drop from 1 before to 0 after ✅.

### Example 10. MgO

- Step 1: Mg = 1.2, O = 3.5. Difference = 3.5 − 1.2 = **2.3** (very large).
- Step 2: Metal + non-metal—ionic. **Conclusion:** Lattice of Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions, melting at about 2850 °C.

**Check:** 3.5 − 1.2 = 2.3 ✅.

## Part G: Properties Come from Structure, and Structure Comes from Bonds

Here is the whole lesson in a single sentence: **chemical bonds determine how strongly particles hold onto one another, and that particle-to-particle grip controls the bulk properties we see and touch.** An ionic lattice has a powerful grip in all directions (high melting point, hard, brittle). Separate covalent molecules have a much gentler grip on one another (lower melting/boiling points, soft solids or gases/liquids). Polar molecules mix with polar water; non-polar molecules do not.

Two additional bonding structures are worth **knowing by name only** for future grades:

- **Metallic bond:** The special bonding in metals that lets outer electrons flow freely, making metals shiny and good conductors of electricity and heat.
- **Giant covalent solid:** Substances such as diamond and quartz sand (silica) in which covalent bonds link atoms continuously into one giant 3D network rather than separate molecules—making them extremely hard with very high melting points.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "All covalent compounds are gases or liquids with very low melting points."**
This is only half true. Demo: Table sugar is covalent, yet it is a **crystalline solid** at room temperature that doesn't melt (and decompose) until about **186 °C**; candle wax is also a covalent **solid** that melts around **60 °C**. Neither is a gas or liquid at room temperature! And in one more sentence: **giant covalent solids** like diamond and quartz sand are covalent yet have extraordinarily high melting points (covered in higher grades). The accurate rule is: "Most **molecular** covalent compounds have much lower melting and boiling points than ionic compounds, though some are solids at room temperature."

**Mistake 2: "No covalent compound ever dissolves in water."**
Demo: Think back to our five grocery glasses. Sugar (covalent) dissolved completely, ethanol (covalent) mixes with water in all proportions, ammonia (covalent) is extremely soluble in water, and HCl (covalent) dissolves and ionizes in water. Meanwhile, cooking oil, wax, and CCl₄ (also covalent) do **not** dissolve in water. Whether a covalent substance dissolves in water depends on **molecular polarity**, not on the label "covalent": polar covalent molecules dissolve in polar water, while non-polar covalent molecules do not ("like dissolves like").

## Quick Recap

- **Four bond types:** Ionic (electron transfer), polar covalent (unequally shared pair), non-polar covalent (equally shared pair), and coordinate (both shared electrons supplied by one atom).
- **Rule-of-thumb differences:** ~0 to 0.4 non-polar covalent, ~0.5 to 1.7 polar covalent, ~2.0 or above ionic-like. Bond character changes gradually, not across sharp walls.
- Bulk properties flow from **particle structure**: a 3D ionic lattice (strong ion-ion attraction) versus a collection of discrete molecules (weak molecule-molecule attraction).
- Approximate temperatures: MgO melts at 2850 °C, NaCl melts at 801 °C, sugar melts/decomposes at 186 °C, wax melts at 60 °C, water melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C, ethanol boils at 78 °C, ammonia boils at −33 °C, HCl boils at −85 °C, methane boils at −162 °C.
- Ionic solids: hard, brittle (shifting a layer brings like charges together, causing repulsion), conduct electricity when melted or dissolved. Molecular covalent substances: soft solids, liquids, or gases; usually non-conductors, **except** polar covalent acids like HCl that ionize in water.
- Water solubility: ionic and polar covalent substances (salt, sugar, ethanol, ammonia, HCl) dissolve in water; non-polar covalent substances (oil, wax, CCl₄) do not.
- **4-step decision flow:** (1) electronegativity difference, (2) metal + non-metal check, (3) polyatomic ion check, (4) lone-pair coordinate donation check.

### Answers

1. From strongest particle attraction to weakest: **MgO** (ionic, 2850 °C) > **ethanol** (78 °C) > **ammonia** (−33 °C) > **methane** (−162 °C). In MgO we must overcome the powerful electrostatic attraction of an **ionic lattice** (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻). In the other three, we only overcome **weak molecule-to-molecule attractions** (the covalent bonds inside the molecules do not break), and those intermolecular attractions grow weaker from ethanol to ammonia to methane. Check: 2850 > 78 > −33 > −162 ✅.
2. CH₃–CH₂–OH contains 9 atoms linked by 8 covalent bonds: **5 C–H bonds, 1 C–C bond, 1 C–O bond, and 1 O–H bond** (5 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 8 ✅). Differences: C–C = 2.5 − 2.5 = **0.0** (non-polar), C–H = 2.5 − 2.1 = **0.4** (nearly non-polar), C–O = 3.5 − 2.5 = **1.0** (polar), O–H = 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4** (polar). Check: 0.0, 0.4, 1.0, 1.4 ✅.
3. Gap = −33 − (−162) = **129 °C**. No, it does not prove the internal covalent bond is stronger: boiling does not break any bonds inside the molecule, it only separates molecules from one another. Because ammonia molecules are polar (N–H difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9), they attract each other more strongly than non-polar methane molecules do, raising ammonia's boiling point to −33 °C. Check: 162 − 33 = 129 ✅.
4. (a) **False** ❌: once the ion has formed, the coordinate bond is identical in strength to the other covalent bonds (Topic 3). (b) **False** ❌: an aqueous solution of HCl conducts electricity because HCl ionizes in water to form H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (Topic 2). (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌: polar covalent compounds such as sugar, ethanol, and ammonia dissolve readily in water. (e) **True** ✅.
5. The prediction is **wrong**. Ethanol molecules are polar (containing a polar O–H group with difference 1.4), and water is also polar: by "like dissolves like," ethanol mixes completely with water. Being covalent does not make a substance insoluble in water—being non-polar (like cooking oil) does.
6. In solid NaCl, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are locked in a rigid lattice and cannot move, whereas melting or dissolving frees the ions to move and carry current. Ethanol consists of neutral molecules that never form ions in any state, so it never conducts. Pure HCl gas consists of neutral molecules, but in water it reacts to form free H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, allowing its aqueous solution to conduct.
7. In an NaCl crystal lattice, `+` ions sit next to `−` ions; when a hammer blow shifts one row of ions sideways, `+` aligns with `+` and `−` aligns with `−`, and the sudden repulsion shatters the crystal (brittle). In wax, neutral molecules are held together only weakly and slide past one another without any like-charge repulsion, so the wax simply dents.
8. **H₂O:** O–H difference = 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4**, polar covalent (both non-metals in Step 2); Steps 3 and 4 do not apply to an isolated H₂O molecule. **LiCl:** Li–Cl difference = 3.0 − 1.0 = **2.0**, in the ionic row; Step 2 confirms Li is a metal and Cl is a non-metal, giving an ionic bond (Li⁺ and Cl⁻); Steps 3 and 4 do not apply. Check: 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 ✅ and 3.0 − 1.0 = 2.0 ✅.
9. Gap = 100 − 78 = **22 °C**. Even though both consist of covalent molecules, molecules of different substances differ in size, shape, and polarity, so the strength of attraction between neighboring molecules differs, giving them different boiling points. Check: 100 − 78 = 22 ✅.
10. In ionic solutions the reacting ions are **already free** in solution and combine the instant they collide (such as acid-base neutralization, H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, Chapter 1). In covalent liquids, existing covalent bonds must first be broken before new bonds can form, which is slower—for example, a wax candle needs a match flame to start burning in air.
11. **Wax:** does not dissolve (non-polar covalent). **Ammonia solution:** dissolves (polar covalent NH₃ molecules mix with polar water). **NaCl:** dissolves (ionic; polar water molecules surround and separate the ions). **Cooking oil:** does not dissolve (non-polar covalent). Only the wax and oil fail to dissolve in water.
12. In NH₄Cl, the 3 ordinary covalent N–H bonds and 1 coordinate N → H bond are tucked **inside** the NH₄⁺ ion and stay intact. Outside, the complete NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are packed together in a 3D **ionic lattice** by opposite-charge attraction. Because melting or dissolving involves breaking that outer ionic lattice to free NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ ions, the bulk properties of NH₄Cl (hard crystal, water-soluble, conducting solution) are those of an ionic salt ✅.
