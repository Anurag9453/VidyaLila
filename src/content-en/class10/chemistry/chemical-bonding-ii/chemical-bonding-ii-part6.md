## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Master method** | The four-question checklist used to solve any "what kind of bond and why" problem | Part A |
| **Rule of thumb** | A practical guideline that works reliably in most cases, while allowing for gradual transitions near the boundaries | Difference 0.5 to 1.7: polar covalent |
| **Borderline bond** | A bond whose electronegativity difference sits near the boundary between two categories, so a single rigid label doesn't tell the whole story | Al–Cl, difference 1.5 |
| **Donor** | The atom whose lone pair supplies both electrons for a coordinate bond | O in H₃O⁺ |
| **Acceptor** | The atom or ion with an empty valence space that accepts a share in both electrons of a coordinate bond | H⁺ in H₃O⁺ |

## Part A: The Master Method: Four Questions

In Grade 9 we used a 3-step method (metal vs. non-metal, bond type, electron count). This chapter deepened every step: now we also check whether a shared pair is pulled **equally or unequally**, whether **polyatomic ions** carry internal covalent bonds, and whether any bond formed as a **coordinate bond**. Ask these four questions in order:

**Step 1: What is the electronegativity difference?** Subtract the smaller electronegativity from the larger one (whenever values are given). Rule of thumb: **0.4 or less** → non-polar covalent; **about 0.5 to 1.7** → polar covalent; **around 2.0 or higher** → ionic. Remember that these numbers are a **rule of thumb**, not a brick wall—bond character changes gradually.

**Step 2: Metal or non-metal?** Two non-metals share electrons (covalent). A metal and a non-metal transfer electrons (ionic). This step works even when electronegativity numbers are not given, and serves as a cross-check on Step 1.

**Step 3: Does the formula contain a polyatomic ion?** If a polyatomic ion (such as NH₄⁺ or OH⁻) is present, there is an **ionic bond outside** between the ions and **covalent bonds inside** the polyatomic ion (Topic 4).

**Step 4: Did one atom supply both shared electrons?** If one atom donated a lone pair into another species' empty valence space, mark a **coordinate bond** with an arrow pointing from donor to acceptor (Topic 3).

```
   given formula
         v
   Step 1: EN difference?       <= 0.4: non-polar    0.5 to 1.7: polar    very large: ionic
         v
   Step 2: metal + non-metal?   yes: ionic           no: covalent
         v
   Step 3: polyatomic ion?      yes: ionic outside, covalent inside the ion
         v
   Step 4: lone-pair donation?  yes: coordinate bond, arrow D -> A
         v
   final answer: bond type(s) + reason + electron-count check
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Flowchart of the 4-step master method. Always finish your answer with the reason and an electron-count check.

**One golden rule for every electron check:** Keep **total valence electrons** and **octet counts** clearly distinct. **Total valence electrons** = the sum of outer electrons brought by each atom (adjusted for any ionic charge), where each electron is drawn and counted **only once**. **Octet (or duplet) count** is checked around one atom at a time by counting its lone-pair electrons plus **both** electrons of every shared pair attached to it, because a shared pair counts toward both bonded atoms.

## Part B: Bond Bingo Rounds (Worked Puzzles)

### Round 1. CaF₂: An Enormous Difference

**Clue:** "A metal and a non-metal with an enormous electronegativity difference."

- Step 1: Ca = 1.0, F = 4.0. Difference = 4.0 − 1.0 = **3.0** (huge).
- Step 2: Ca is a metal, F is a non-metal → electron transfer (ionic).
- Calcium has 2 outer electrons and each fluorine atom needs 1 electron to complete its octet. So Ca transfers **1 electron to each of two F atoms**, forming **Ca²⁺ and 2 F⁻**.

```
   Before:        ..                          ..
                : F .        x Ca x         . F :
                  ..                          ..
               (F: 7 e⁻)    (Ca: 2 e⁻)     (F: 7 e⁻)

   After:         ..                         ..
              [ : F .x ]⁻     Ca²⁺      [ x. F : ]⁻
                  ..                         ..
```

> **Figure 6.3** – CaF₂: Ca (`x`) transfers its 2 outer electrons, one to each F atom. Each F⁻ ion now has 8 outer electrons (4 pairs), and Ca²⁺ also has 8 electrons in its new outer shell.

**Check:** F⁻: 7 + 1 = 8 ✅. Ca²⁺: 20 − 2 = 18 electrons left, arranged as 2, 8, 8 ✅. Charge balance: (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅. With a difference of 3.0, this is unmistakably ionic.

### Round 2. H₂S: Two Non-metals with a Small Difference

**Clue:** "Two non-metals whose electronegativity difference is 0.4."

- Step 2: Both S and H are non-metals → **electron sharing (covalent)**.
- Step 1: S = 2.5, H = 2.1. Difference = 2.5 − 2.1 = **0.4**, sitting right on the rule-of-thumb border (nearly non-polar). Sulfur pulls the shared pairs **slightly** closer to itself, giving S a very slight δ⁻ and each H a very slight δ⁺.
- Dot diagram: S has 6 outer electrons and each of the two H atoms has 1. Since S needs 2 more electrons for an octet, it **shares one pair with each H atom**.

```
            ..
     H x  . S .  x H
            ..
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Dot-and-cross diagram of H₂S: S has 2 lone pairs (`..` at top and bottom) and shares one pair with each H atom. (This diagram shows electron pairing; the bent 3D shape and exact angle of H₂S are covered in higher grades.)

**Check:** Total valence electrons = 6 + 1 + 1 = **8**, and in the diagram there are 6 dots from S + 2 crosses from H = 8 ✅. Octet check: around S = 2 lone pairs (4 e⁻) + 2 shared pairs (4 e⁻) = 8 ✅; around each H = 2 ✅. (H₂S is a toxic gas with a rotten-egg odor—for study only.)

### Round 3. H₃O⁺: Coordinate Bond in Action

**Clue:** "Formed when an acid dissolves in water; one bond has both electrons supplied by the same atom."

- In water (H₂O), the oxygen atom has **2 lone pairs**. A bare H⁺ ion has zero electrons—just an empty valence space.
- Step 4: Oxygen donates one of its lone pairs (**donor**) and H⁺ accepts a share in it (**acceptor**), forming a coordinate bond with the arrow pointing **from O to H⁺**.

```
          ..                               ..
     H -- O -- H     +   H⁺    --->   H -- O -- H
          ..                               v
                                           H
```

> **Figure 6.5** – H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺. The bottom lone pair on O becomes a coordinate bond (vertical arrow) to H⁺, with O supplying both shared electrons. Once formed, all three O–H bonds are identical (Topic 3).

**Check (total outer electrons):** 6 (from O) + 3 (from three H) = 9, minus 1 for the **+1** charge = **8**. In the diagram, O has 4 pairs around it (1 lone pair + 3 bond pairs) = 8 electrons ✅. Equation balance: H₂O + H⁺ → H₃O⁺ has H: 2 + 1 = 3 ✅, O: 1 = 1 ✅, charge: 0 + (+1) = +1 ✅. This is the true species behind "H⁺(aq)" from Chapter 1 ([acids-bases-and-salts-part2.md](../acids-bases-and-salts/acids-bases-and-salts-part2.md)).

### Round 4. NH₄Cl: All Three Bond Types in One Formula

**Clue:** "A single compound containing ionic, ordinary covalent, and coordinate bonds all together."

Start with Step 3: NH₄Cl splits into **NH₄⁺** and **Cl⁻**.

| Location | Bond Type | Count |
|---|---|---|
| Between NH₄⁺ and Cl⁻ | Ionic | 1 |
| Inside NH₄⁺, three N–H links | Ordinary covalent (each H contributed 1 electron) | 3 |
| Inside NH₄⁺, one N → H link | Coordinate (N's lone pair donated to H⁺) | 1 |

**Check:** Inside NH₄⁺ there are 3 + 1 = 4 bonds (giving N an octet of 4 pairs = 8 electrons). Total outer electrons in NH₄⁺: 5 (from N) + 4 (from 4 H) = 9, minus 1 for the +1 charge = **8** ✅. In Cl⁻: 7 + 1 = 8 (4 lone pairs) ✅. Overall charge: (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅.

### Round 5. Ranking Six Bonds by Polarity

**Question:** Arrange Cl–Cl, S–H, H–Cl, Al–Cl, Mg–Cl, and Ca–F in order from least polar to most polar/ionic, and state the zone for each.

Given values: Cl 3.0, S 2.5, H 2.1, Al 1.5, Mg 1.2, Ca 1.0, F 4.0.

```
   Cl-Cl   0.0   (no bar)
   S-H     0.4   ####
   H-Cl    0.9   #########
   Al-Cl   1.5   ###############
   Mg-Cl   1.8   ##################
   Ca-F    3.0   ##############################
```

> **Figure 6.6** – Electronegativity differences across six bonds (each `#` = 0.1). The larger the difference, the more strongly the shared pair shifts to one side—until in Ca–F electron transfer is virtually complete.

- **Cl–Cl (0.0):** non-polar covalent. **S–H (0.4):** right on the border, nearly non-polar. **H–Cl (0.9)** and **Al–Cl (1.5):** in the polar covalent zone. **Mg–Cl (1.8):** in the 1.7–2.0 borderline zone approaching ionic (Topic 1); since Mg is a metal (Step 2), MgCl₂ behaves as an ionic compound. **Ca–F (3.0):** strongly ionic.
- Order: **Cl–Cl < S–H < H–Cl < Al–Cl < Mg–Cl < Ca–F**.

**Check:** 3.0 − 3.0 = 0.0, 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4, 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5, 3.0 − 1.2 = 1.8, 4.0 − 1.0 = 3.0, and 0.0 < 0.4 < 0.9 < 1.5 < 1.8 < 3.0 ✅.

### Round 6. AlCl₃: When Step 1 and Step 2 Pull in Different Directions

**Clue:** "A metal combined with a non-metal, yet the electronegativity difference is only 1.5."

- Step 2 says: Al is a metal and Cl is a non-metal → electron transfer (ionic).
- Step 1 says: 3.0 − 1.5 = **1.5**, which falls inside the 0.5 to 1.7 polar covalent zone!

Here our two indicators point in different directions. This is a classic **borderline bond**. The honest scientific answer is that the Al–Cl bond is neither purely ionic nor purely ordinary covalent—it has significant covalent character despite involving a metal. At the Grade 10 level, simply spotting that the two steps give mixed signals and stating both reasons is a complete, top-marks answer! (The deeper reason why small, highly charged metal ions like Al³⁺ pull electrons back toward themselves is covered in higher grades.)

**Check:** 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5, which sits 0.2 below the 1.7 cutoff ✅.

### Round 7. Which Ones Dissolve in Water?

**Clue:** "Three separate glasses of water: add MgCl₂ to the first, ethanol to the second, and candle wax to the third."

- **MgCl₂:** Ionic (Round 5, difference 1.8). Polar water molecules (Topic 2) orient their δ⁻ oxygen ends toward Mg²⁺ and their δ⁺ hydrogen ends toward Cl⁻, pulling the ions into solution. **Dissolves.**
- **Ethanol:** Polar covalent molecule (with a polar O–H group); mixes completely with polar water. **Dissolves.**
- **Candle wax:** Non-polar covalent; by "like dissolves like" (Topic 2), it **does not dissolve** in polar water.

One honest note: solubility rules are also rules of thumb—a few ionic crystals (such as CaF₂, the mineral fluorite) pack so tightly in their lattice that even water barely dissolves them (covered in higher grades).

**Check:** Ionic and polar dissolve in water, non-polar does not: MgCl₂ ✅, ethanol ✅, wax ❌ (insoluble) ✅.

### Round 8. Match the Substance to Its Melting or Boiling Point

**Clue:** "Four substances—MgO, NaCl, HCl, and candle wax—and four temperatures: (a) boils at about −85 °C, (b) melts at about 60 °C, (c) melts at about 801 °C, (d) melts at about 2850 °C."

- HCl: small covalent molecules with weak attractions between molecules → **(a) −85 °C**.
- Candle wax: larger non-polar covalent molecules, soft solid with a low melting point → **(b) 60 °C**.
- NaCl: ionic lattice of singly charged Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions → **(c) 801 °C**.
- MgO: ionic lattice of doubly charged Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ ions with even stronger attraction → **(d) 2850 °C**.

**Check:** −85 < 60 < 801 < 2850, with covalent substances at the low end, ionic compounds at the high end, and doubly charged MgO highest of all ✅.

### Round 9. Aqueous HCl vs. Aqueous Sugar in a Conductivity Tester

**Clue:** "Two beakers with a conductivity tester: one holds HCl dissolved in water, the other holds sugar dissolved in water. One lights the bulb; the other does not."

- **HCl in water:** The H–Cl bond is polar (difference 0.9). Polar water molecules pull on H and Cl strongly enough to split the covalent bond (**ionization**). The H⁺ attaches to a lone pair on water's oxygen atom to form **H₃O⁺**, leaving **Cl⁻**.
- Equation: **HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻** (the full version of HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ from our electrolysis chapter, [electrolysis.md](../electrolysis/electrolysis.md)).
- Because the solution contains **free mobile ions**, the tester bulb **lights up**.
- **Sugar in water:** Although sugar molecules have polar regions that let them dissolve in water, water **does not break** any bonds inside the sugar molecule—sugar dissolves as intact neutral molecules. With no free ions, the bulb **does not light up**.

**Check:** H: 1 + 2 = 3 ✅, O: 1 = 1 ✅, Cl: 1 = 1 ✅, charge: 0 = (+1) + (−1) ✅. (HCl is a corrosive acid—for study only. At home, only ever test table salt and sugar solutions with adult supervision, and never taste any test solution.)

```
   Aqueous HCl solution                     Aqueous sugar solution

   H₃O⁺  Cl⁻  H₃O⁺  Cl⁻                    sugar molecule  (intact, unsplit)
   Cl⁻  H₃O⁺  Cl⁻  H₃O⁺                    sugar molecule  (intact, unsplit)
   (free mobile ions carry charge)          (zero ions present)

   bulb: GLOWS                              bulb: DOES NOT GLOW
```

> **Figure 6.7** – Side-by-side comparison of the two solutions: aqueous HCl contains free H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions that carry electric current, whereas aqueous sugar contains only intact neutral molecules.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Double-counting shared electrons when drawing a dot diagram."**
When finding the **total** number of dots to draw, count each valence electron **only once**; when checking **octets**, a shared pair counts toward both bonded atoms. Demo: In H₂S, total valence electrons = 6 + 1 + 1 = **8**. If a student draws 6 dots on S and then draws 2 extra electrons for each bond separately, the diagram ends up showing 6 + 2 + 2 = **10** electrons—2 more than actually exist! Always draw the exact total (8 electrons), with the 2 shared pairs sitting between S and the two H atoms so they count for both.

**Mistake 2: "Forgetting to adjust the electron count for an ion's charge."**
Demo: In H₃O⁺, adding neutral valence electrons gives 6 (from O) + 3 (from three H) = 9 electrons—an odd number that cannot form pairs for an octet. Because the ion carries a **+1** charge, it has **one electron fewer**: 9 − 1 = **8** electrons (4 pairs). Conversely, for a negative ion you **add** electrons equal to the negative charge (e.g., Cl⁻: 7 + 1 = 8).

**Mistake 3: "Drawing the coordinate bond arrow backward."**
The coordinate arrow always points **from the donor to the acceptor**. Demo: In NH₃ + H⁺, drawing H⁺ → N would claim that H⁺ donated a pair of electrons to nitrogen—which is impossible because H⁺ has zero electrons! Since both electrons come from nitrogen's lone pair, the arrow must point **N → H⁺**.

**Mistake 4: "Putting δ⁻ on the atom with the smaller electronegativity."**
It is the exact opposite: whichever atom pulls the shared electron pair more strongly (larger electronegativity) becomes **δ⁻**. Demo: In H₂S, S = 2.5 and H = 2.1. Because 2.5 > 2.1, sulfur pulls the pair closer and carries **δ⁻**, while hydrogen carries **δ⁺**. Remember the playground seesaw from Topic 1: the balance point shifts toward the heavier (stronger-pulling) side.

**Mistake 5: "Thinking MgCl₂ contains a Cl–Cl covalent bond because the formula says Cl₂."**
In an ionic formula like MgCl₂, the subscript `2` simply means there are **two separate Cl⁻ ions** balancing one Mg²⁺ ion. Demo: MgCl₂ = Mg²⁺ + 2 Cl⁻, with charge balance (+2) + 2 × (−1) = 0 ✅. The two Cl⁻ ions are not bonded to each other (in fact, two negative ions repel each other!); each Cl⁻ ion is attracted to Mg²⁺. Elemental chlorine gas (Cl₂) has a Cl–Cl covalent bond, but MgCl₂ does not.

## Quick Recap

- **4-step master method:** (1) electronegativity difference, (2) metal vs. non-metal, (3) polyatomic ion check, (4) lone-pair coordinate donation. Always include the reason and an electron-count check.
- Rule-of-thumb cutoffs: ≤ 0.4 non-polar covalent, 0.5 to 1.7 polar covalent, ~2.0+ ionic. These are gradual zones, not rigid walls.
- For **borderline** cases (such as Al–Cl at 1.5), state both indicators honestly rather than forcing a one-word label.
- **δ⁻** always goes on the more electronegative atom. A **coordinate arrow** always points from **donor to acceptor**.
- Total valence electrons = sum of outer electrons ∓ ionic charge (each electron drawn once). Octet checks count shared pairs for both bonded atoms.
- Aqueous **HCl** conducts electricity because it ionizes into H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻; aqueous **sugar** does not conduct because it dissolves as intact neutral molecules.
- In ionic formulas like MgCl₂, the subscript `2` counts separate Cl⁻ ions—there is no Cl–Cl bond.

Clues: (a) an ionic solid that conducts when melted; (b) a gas whose central atom has one lone pair, three single bonds and all bonds with difference 0.0; (c) a gas whose polar molecules form H₃O⁺ and I⁻ in water; (d) a liquid with two C=S double bonds and no lone pair on the central atom. Match them to KI, HI, PH₃ and CS₂.

### Answers

1. (a) C = 2.5, S = 2.5, difference = **0.0**, so the C=S bond is **non-polar**. (b) In S=C=S: each S has 2 lone pairs (4 e⁻) and shares 2 pairs with C (4 e⁻) = 8; C shares 4 pairs in all (8 e⁻) = 8. Total valence electrons = 4 + 6 + 6 = **16**. In the diagram: 4 lone pairs (2 on each S) = 8 e⁻, plus 4 shared pairs = 8 e⁻, total = 16 ✅. Because each bond has a difference of 0.0, both bonds and the molecule are non-polar.
2. Since iodine is more electronegative than H (2.1) but less electronegative than Br (2.8), I lies between 2.1 and 2.8. Therefore the H–I difference lies between 0 and 2.8 − 2.1 = 0.7, meaning it is **smaller than 0.7**. Because I is more electronegative than H, **iodine (I)** carries **δ⁻**. Check: for any value of I between 2.1 and 2.8, I − 2.1 < 0.7 ✅.
3. Step 2: K is a Group 1 metal and I is a Group 17 non-metal, so KI has an **ionic bond** (K⁺ and I⁻) without needing iodine's exact electronegativity number. (Cross-check with Step 1: K = 0.8 is very low, guaranteeing a large difference.) When melted, KI's ions become **free to move**, so molten KI **conducts electricity**, whereas molten wax consists of neutral molecules and does not conduct.
4. Total valence electrons: 4 (from Si) + 4 × 1 (from 4 H) = **8** electrons = 4 shared pairs. Si forms 4 single bonds, one with each H. Octet/duplet check: Si has 4 shared pairs × 2 = 8 (octet) ✅; each H has 1 shared pair = 2 (duplet) ✅. All 8 electrons are drawn once.
5. Difference = 3.0 − 1.5 = **1.5**, with **Cl** carrying **δ⁻** (since 3.0 > 1.5). Because 1.5 lies between 0.5 and 1.7, it falls in the **polar covalent zone** (making BeCl₂ a classic borderline case like AlCl₃). Check: 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5 ✅. (Why Be is stable with fewer than 8 electrons around it is covered in higher grades.)
6. (a) PH₃ + H⁺ → PH₄⁺: H atoms: 3 + 1 = 4 ✅, P: 1 = 1 ✅, charge: 0 + (+1) = +1 ✅. (b) **Donor: P** (supplies its lone pair); **acceptor: H⁺** (empty valence space); arrow points **P → H⁺**. (c) Outer electrons: 5 (from P) + 4 (from 4 H) − 1 (for the +1 charge) = 8 electrons = 4 bond pairs × 2 = 8 around P ✅.
7. In one formula unit of PH₄I: **1 ionic bond** (between PH₄⁺ and I⁻), **3 ordinary covalent P–H bonds** (inside PH₄⁺), and **1 coordinate P → H bond** (inside PH₄⁺). Check: 3 + 1 = 4 bonds inside PH₄⁺ ✅, and charge (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅.
8. P–H difference = 2.1 − 2.1 = **0.0**, so the P–H bonds themselves are non-polar (no δ⁺/δ⁻ bond arrows). Thus PH₃ is **nearly non-polar** (a tiny polarity remains from P's lone pair, covered in higher grades). In NH₃, each N–H bond has a difference of 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9, so the three polar bond arrows in the pyramid add up to make NH₃ strongly polar—far more polar than PH₃. Check: 2.1 − 2.1 = 0.0 vs. 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 ✅.
9. **KI** dissolves in water: it is ionic, and polar water molecules surround and separate K⁺ and I⁻ ions. **CS₂** is non-polar (Q1), so by "like dissolves like" it does not dissolve in polar water. Conductivity tester: the **KI solution** lights the bulb because it contains free K⁺ and I⁻ ions; CS₂ neither dissolves nor forms ions.
10. Atom and charge check for HI + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + I⁻: H: 1 + 2 = 3 ✅, I: 1 = 1 ✅, O: 1 = 1 ✅, charge: 0 + 0 = (+1) + (−1) = 0 ✅. Because the solution contains free mobile H₃O⁺ and I⁻ ions, the tester bulb **will glow**—just like aqueous HCl.
11. (a) **False** ❌: KI is made of monatomic K⁺ and I⁻ ions with only ionic bonding. (b) **False** ❌: the C–S difference is 2.5 − 2.5 = 0.0 (non-polar). (c) **False** ❌: Cl (3.0) is more electronegative than Be (1.5), so Cl carries δ⁻ and Be carries δ⁺. (d) **False** ❌: once PH₄⁺ forms, all four P–H bonds are identical. (e) **True** ✅ (silicon is often called a metalloid, and the Si–H bond is a shared-pair covalent bond).
12. Total valence electrons in SiH₄ = 4 (from Si) + 4 × 1 (from 4 H) = **8**, not 10. The student confused the octet check (where a shared pair counts for both atoms) with the total number of valence electrons available to draw (where each electron is drawn only once).
13. A coordinate bond arrow must point **from donor to acceptor**. H⁺ has zero electrons and cannot donate anything; phosphorus donates its lone pair, so the arrow must point **P → H⁺** (or **PH₃ → H⁺**).
14. (a) **KI**: ionic solid whose ions move freely when melted. (b) **PH₃**: central P has 1 lone pair, 3 single bonds, and P–H difference = 0.0. (c) **HI**: polar gas that ionizes in water to give H₃O⁺ and I⁻. (d) **CS₂**: liquid with two C=S double bonds and no lone pair on the central C atom. Every clue matches a unique square ✅.
