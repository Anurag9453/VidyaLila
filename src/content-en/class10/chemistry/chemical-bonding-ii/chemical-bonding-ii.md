# Topic 1: Polar Covalent Bond: Unequal, Yet Still Shared

In Grade 9, we sorted chemical bonds into two neat boxes: **ionic** (complete transfer of electrons) and **covalent** (sharing a pair of electrons). Then, in Chapter 6 Topic 4, we met electronegativity, and one small question was left hanging: if two atoms share an electron pair, but **their pull is not equal**, where does the pair sit? We noted there that the name and effect of this unequal sharing would appear in **Chapter 7**. Today is that day: a bond that is genuinely covalent (electrons are shared) yet unequal (one atom pulls harder). Let's start at a neighborhood playground seesaw.

---

## The Balance Point of a Seesaw

It was a breezy Sunday afternoon. Uncle Marcus had taken his niece Clara to the neighborhood park. Near the corner stood a long wooden seesaw with two kids sitting at opposite ends: a tall, heavier older boy on one side and a smaller, lighter girl on the other. Naturally, the seesaw tipped right down—the heavier child sank to the ground while the lighter child stayed up in the air.

Clara laughed, "Uncle Marcus, that was obviously going to happen!" Uncle Marcus smiled. "True. Now think: if you could slide the plank across the central pivot to make this seesaw **balance**, where would you place the pivot?" Clara thought for a moment. "If I keep it in the middle, the heavier kid will always drop down. I'd have to shift the pivot **closer to the heavier kid**."

"Spot on!" said Uncle Marcus. "That means the **balance point** of the two kids is neither dead center nor all the way under one child—it sits **closer to the heavier child**. Now picture two atoms sharing a pair of electrons between them. If the two atoms are identical, the shared pair stays right in the middle. But if one atom has a **stronger pull** (like the heavier child on the seesaw), the 'average position' of the shared pair shifts toward that stronger-pulling atom. Even so, the pair is **still shared**—neither atom has ripped it away completely."

Clara nodded slowly. "So the bond isn't completely equal, and it isn't completely one-sided either. It's somewhere in between." "Exactly," Uncle Marcus replied. "That in-between bond is called a **polar covalent bond**."

```
   1) Both children equally heavy            2) One child heavier

        A ------- ^ -------- A                    A ----- ^ ------------ B
               pivot in the middle                     pivot shifted toward B
   (balance point in the center)             (balance point closer to the heavier one)

   The position of a shared pair works the same way: centered when both atoms pull equally,
   and shifted toward whichever atom pulls more strongly.
```

> **Figure 1.1** – The balance point of a seesaw shifts toward the heavier child. Similarly, the "average position" of a shared electron pair shifts toward the more electronegative atom. (This is only an analogy: an electron is not a child sitting on a plank, and even when shifted toward one side, the shared pair still remains between both atoms.)

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-polar covalent bond** | A covalent bond in which the shared electron pair stays at or nearly at the exact center (both atoms pull equally or nearly equally) | In F–F, or in C–H (very small difference) |
| **Polar covalent bond** | A covalent bond in which the shared electron pair shifts toward the more electronegative atom, while still remaining shared | H–Cl, H–F |
| **δ⁺ (delta plus)** | A **slight** positive charge on an atom (much less than a full unit charge), because the shared pair spends less time near it | H in HCl |
| **δ⁻ (delta minus)** | A **slight** negative charge on an atom, because the shared pair spends more time closer to it | Cl in HCl |
| **Partial charge** | Not a full ionic charge, only a small fraction of a charge: δ⁺ or δ⁻ | δ⁺ and δ⁻ |
| **Electronegativity difference** | The larger electronegativity value minus the smaller one for the two bonded atoms (Chapter 6, Topic 4); always a positive number | H–Cl: 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 |

## Part A: When the Pull Is Equal: Non-polar Covalent Bond

First, let's look at the case where there is no tilt at all: **both atoms are identical**. In Grade 9 we looked at H₂ and Cl₂; today let's take F₂ (fluorine, a toxic gas—for study only, never handle it). You drew its dot diagram during Grade 9 practice; here we quickly recall it and move ahead.

Each F atom has 7 outer electrons (2, 7). It needs 1 more electron to complete its octet, so two F atoms share one pair. In the diagram below, `o` marks the electrons of the left F and `x` marks the electrons of the right F.

```
           oo    xx
        oo F o x F xx          F-F
           oo    xx
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Dot diagram of F₂: the shared pair "o x" sits right in the middle, and each F atom has three lone pairs (6 electrons). Since both sides are F atoms, the pull is identical and the pair stays centered.

**Count:** Each F has 3 lone pairs (6 electrons) + 1 shared pair (2 electrons) = **8**. Total valence electrons = 7 + 7 = 14, and in the diagram 6 + 6 + 2 = 14 ✅.

### Example 1. The difference in F₂

F = 4.0 and F = 4.0. Difference = 4.0 − 4.0 = **0.0**. The pull is completely equal, the pair sits dead center, and there is neither δ⁺ nor δ⁻. This is a **non-polar covalent bond**.

**Check:** Two atoms of the exact same element always have a difference of 0.0 ✅.

**Rule of thumb:** Whenever a bond is between **two atoms of the same element**, it is non-polar. The same holds true in bromine (Br₂, a hazardous, toxic liquid—for study only) and oxygen (O₂), as well as N₂ from Grade 9: all have a difference of 0.0.

Note carefully: "non-polar covalent" does **not** mean "there is no bond" or "there is no sharing." Sharing is 100% present—the pull on the shared pair is simply equal.

## Part B: When the Pull Is Unequal: Polar Covalent Bond

Now let's take two different atoms: H and Cl. In Grade 9 we saw the dot diagram of HCl (H needs 1 more electron, Cl needs 1 more). It is the exact same diagram, except now we view it **through the lens of electron pull**. (HCl gas and its aqueous solution are toxic and corrosive—for study only.)

H = 2.1 and Cl = 3.0. Since Cl pulls more strongly, the shared pair shifts closer to Cl.

```
              xx
        H o x Cl xx          shared pair shifted toward Cl
              xx
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Dot diagram of HCl: 1 electron from H (o) and 1 electron from Cl (x) form the shared pair; Cl also has 3 lone pairs (6 electrons). Count: H has 2 (duplet), Cl has 6 + 2 = 8 (octet) ✅.

**Count:** Total valence electrons = 1 + 7 = 8, and in the diagram 6 lone-pair electrons + 2 shared = 8 ✅.

Because the shared pair spends **more time closer to Cl**:

- Cl acquires a slight **negative** charge: **δ⁻**.
- H acquires a slight **positive** charge: **δ⁺**, because its shared electron now sits farther away toward Cl.

We represent this with partial charge symbols and an **arrow** pointing from δ⁺ toward δ⁻ (the direction in which the shared pair is pulled):

```
        δ+             δ-
        H ------------ Cl          dash diagram
        +------------>             arrow: pull toward Cl
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Partial charges in HCl. The arrow points from H (δ⁺) to Cl (δ⁻), showing that the shared pair is pulled toward Cl. Notice the small "+" tail on the arrow at the H end.

### Example 2. HCl: How strong is the tilt?

H = 2.1, Cl = 3.0. Difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9**. This represents a moderate tilt—neither 0 (equal sharing) nor huge (ionic transfer). Because Cl pulls harder, **Cl is δ⁻ and H is δ⁺**. The bond is **polar covalent**.

**Check:** 3.0 > 2.1 ✅ and 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 ✅.

### What δ Means: Not a Full Charge!

An essential point: in HCl, the chlorine atom carries **δ⁻**, **not Cl⁻**. Writing Cl⁻ means chlorine has permanently taken a full extra electron (as in NaCl). Here, Cl has merely pulled the shared pair **a bit closer** to itself while the pair **still belongs to both atoms**. That is why HCl is a **molecule** (H–Cl joined together), not a crystal made of separate ions.

We do not write a numerical value for δ here (measuring its exact magnitude comes in higher grades). For now, we only need the **sign** (which atom is δ⁺ and which is δ⁻) and the **relative size** of the electronegativity difference.

### Which End Is Negative? (3 Steps)

1. Look up the electronegativity of both bonded atoms from the table (Chapter 6, Topic 4).
2. The atom with the **larger** number gets **δ⁻** (it pulls the shared pair closer).
3. The atom with the **smaller** number gets **δ⁺**. Draw the arrow pointing from δ⁺ to δ⁻.

### Example 3. HF: A Very Strong Tilt

H = 2.1 and F = 4.0. Difference = 4.0 − 2.1 = **1.9**. Because F pulls much harder, **F is δ⁻ and H is δ⁺**. This bond is tilted far more strongly than HCl (0.9)—fluorine tugs on the shared pair even more fiercely than chlorine does. HF is a hazardous, toxic, corrosive gas/liquid (for study only). You have already seen its dot diagram in Grade 9.

**Check:** 4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9 ✅ and 1.9 > 0.9 ✅.

## Part C: The Referee: How Electronegativity Difference Tells Bond Character

Chapter 6 introduced the **electronegativity difference** as a referee. Now let's turn that referee into a handy reference table. Keep in mind: this is a **rule of thumb**, not a brick wall. Bonds never jump abruptly from one box to another—their character changes **gradually** along a continuous scale.

| Electronegativity Difference | Position of Shared Pair | Bond Type | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 to about 0.4 | Nearly in the middle | Non-polar covalent | F–F (0.0), C–H (0.4) |
| About 0.5 to 1.7 | Clearly shifted to one side | Polar covalent | H–Cl (0.9), O–H (1.4) |
| Around 1.7 to 2.0 | Heavily shifted (borderline zone) | Strongly polar, approaching ionic | H–F (1.9) |
| About 2.0 or greater | Almost completely on one side | Ionic | Na–Cl (2.1), Mg–O (2.3) |

```
   diff:   0            0.4        0.9        1.9    2.1
           :............:..........:..........:......:.............
           F-F          C-H        H-Cl       H-F    Na-Cl
           equal        nearly     clearly    heavily almost
                        equal      tilted     tilted  complete transfer
   <--- non-polar ---><----- polar covalent ------><---- ionic --->
```

> **Figure 1.5** – The bonding spectrum: as the electronegativity difference increases, a bond shifts from non-polar covalent to polar covalent, and finally to ionic. The boundary numbers (0.4, 1.7) are rules of thumb, not sharp walls. That is why H–F (1.9) is still a covalent molecule despite sitting right near the ionic boundary, while Na–Cl (2.1) is just past it.

Notice also how our Grade 9 rule ("metal + non-metal = ionic, two non-metals = covalent") is really a **rough version** of this table: metals have low electronegativities (Chapter 6), so pairing a metal with a non-metal gives a large difference and an ionic bond. But this table is **more precise**: even between two non-metals, the difference can range anywhere from 0 up to 1.9.

### Example 4. C–H: Nearly Equal

In every C–H bond of CH₄, C = 2.5 and H = 2.1. Difference = 2.5 − 2.1 = **0.4**. This sits right at the upper edge of the first row. Carbon pulls slightly harder, but the tilt is so tiny that we usually treat a C–H bond as **nearly non-polar**. (We will return to CH₄ in Topic 2 to see what happens to the whole molecule.)

**Check:** 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4 ✅ and 0.4 falls in the first row ✅.

### Example 5. Na–Cl: A Very Large Difference

Na = 0.9, Cl = 3.0. Difference = 3.0 − 0.9 = **2.1**. Because this difference is so large, Cl pulls the electron pair almost completely over to itself, leaving Na as an electron donor: **Na⁺ and Cl⁻**. This is the "give-and-take" story from Grade 9, now backed up by numbers. Here we do not use δ symbols—this is an ionic bond with **full** unit charges.

**Check:** 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1 ✅ and 2.1 falls in the "about 2.0 or greater" row ✅.

### Example 6. Ranking Three Bonds

**Question:** Among HCl (0.9), HF (1.9), and C–H (0.4), in which bond is the shared pair shifted most strongly to one side? Write them in increasing order.

- The larger the difference, the farther the shared pair shifts toward one atom.
- Order: **C–H (0.4) < H–Cl (0.9) < H–F (1.9)**.

**Check:** 0.4 < 0.9 < 1.9 ✅.

## Part D: Find the Atoms First, Then Draw the Arrow

Whether a bond is polar depends on the size of the difference; **which way the arrow points** depends on comparing the two electronegativity numbers. Remember from Chapter 6 that electronegativity is always a **comparison**: the exact same element can be δ⁺ with one partner and δ⁻ with another. So inspect both numbers fresh for every bond instead of memorizing a fixed label like "H is always positive." In all the bonds in this topic, H (2.1) happened to have the weaker pull, but with other partners later on, that can change.

### Example 7. Which Atom Is δ⁺ and Which Is δ⁻?

**Question:** In a C–Cl bond, which atom is δ⁺ and which is δ⁻? What is the difference, and which row of the table does it fall into?

- C = 2.5, Cl = 3.0. Since Cl is larger, **Cl is δ⁻** and **C is δ⁺**.
- Difference = 3.0 − 2.5 = **0.5**.
- This sits right at the **start** of the second row: polar covalent, though only mildly polar.

**Check:** 3.0 − 2.5 = 0.5 ✅. (The C–Cl bond occurs in compounds such as CCl₄; CCl₄ is toxic—for study only.)

See that? The differences for C–H (0.4) and C–Cl (0.5) are barely 0.1 apart, yet the table's dividing line puts them in two different rows. That is why we emphasize that the boundary is a **rule of thumb**—both bonds have only a gentle tilt and behave quite similarly.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "A bond between two different atoms is always polar."**
Truth: Whether a bond is polar depends on the **electronegativity difference**, not merely on the two atoms being different elements. Demo: P (2.1) and H (2.1) are different elements, yet their difference is **0.0**—the P–H bonds in PH₃ (phosphine, a toxic gas—for study only) are completely non-polar. C (2.5) and H (2.1) have a difference of only **0.4**—nearly non-polar. S (2.5) and H (2.1) also differ by just 0.4. Now increase the difference: H–Cl (0.9) and H–F (1.9) are clearly polar. Having "two different atoms" does not make a bond polar; a **difference in pull** does.

**Mistake 2: "Polar covalent means the same thing as ionic."**
Truth: In a polar covalent bond, the electron pair is **tilted toward one side** but **still shared**; in an ionic bond, the pair has been transferred almost completely to one atom. Demo: Compare HCl (difference 0.9, polar covalent) with NaCl (difference 2.1, ionic). HCl consists of discrete molecules and boils at about **−85 °C** (making it a gas at room temperature), whereas NaCl is an ionic crystal that melts at about **801 °C**. Different bond character, vastly different properties. And remember: even HF (1.9), which sits right near the ionic boundary, is still a molecular substance rather than an ionic crystal. A polar covalent bond is a **covalent** bond—just a tilted one.

## Quick Recap

- **Non-polar covalent bond:** The shared pair stays at or near the center (difference 0 to about 0.4). Two atoms of the same element (F₂, Br₂, O₂, N₂) always have a difference of 0.0.
- **Polar covalent bond:** The shared pair shifts toward the more electronegative atom (difference about 0.5 to 1.7) while remaining shared. The more electronegative atom becomes **δ⁻**, the less electronegative atom becomes **δ⁺**, and the arrow points from δ⁺ to δ⁻.
- δ means a **partial** (slight) charge, not a full unit charge. Cl⁻ and δ⁻ are completely different things.
- When the difference is about 2.0 or more, the bond is ionic-like (Na–Cl 2.1, Mg–O 2.3). All these cutoffs are **rules of thumb**: bond character changes gradually, which is why H–F (1.9) is strongly polar covalent right near the ionic border.
- A bond between two different elements is not automatically polar: C–H (0.4) and P–H (0.0) are nearly non-polar.
- Polar covalent and ionic are not the same: HCl boils at about −85 °C, whereas NaCl melts at about 801 °C.

(c) In a polar covalent bond the shared pair stays shared. (d) A bond with a difference of 0.0 is non-polar. (e) Polar covalent bonds are ionic bonds.
7. Li–O has a difference of 2.5. Why do we not draw δ⁺ and δ⁻ on it? What do we draw instead?
8. Why is the bond in O₂ non-polar although the molecule has a double bond?
9. For the bond between N and Cl, find the difference and say which atom is δ⁻. Explain your answer in a sentence.
10. A student draws the arrow for the Br–Cl bond pointing from Cl to Br. Is it correct? Explain with the values.
11. Al–O has a difference of 2.0 and S–Cl has 0.5. Which bond is closer to ionic? Does a difference of 0.5 make the S–Cl bond ionic?
12. In the seesaw story, what does the pivot (balance point) stand for, and what does a very heavy child on one end stand for?
13. Two bonds X–Y and X–Z have differences 1.1 and 0.2. Which bond is more polar and which pair is nearer to the middle?

### Answers

1. C = 2.5, F = 4.0. Difference = 4.0 − 2.5 = **1.5**. Because F is larger, **F is δ⁻ and C is δ⁺**. Since 1.5 lies between 0.5 and 1.7, the bond is **polar covalent** (with a strong tilt). Check: 4.0 − 2.5 = 1.5 ✅.
2. Br has 7 + 7 = 14 valence electrons. Diagram: each Br has 3 lone pairs (6) + 1 shared pair (2) = 8, and total electrons = 6 + 6 + 2 = 14 ✅. Both atoms are identical (2.8 and 2.8), so the difference is 0.0 and the pair stays centered: **non-polar**.
3. (a) P–S: 2.5 − 2.1 = 0.4, **non-polar (nearly)**. (b) S–O: 3.5 − 2.5 = 1.0, **polar covalent**. (c) Ca–Cl: 3.0 − 1.0 = 2.0, **ionic-like** (around 2.0: the ionic row). (d) O=O: 3.5 − 3.5 = 0.0, **non-polar**. Check: 0.4, 1.0, 2.0, 0.0 ✅.
4. C–N: N (3.0) > C (2.5), so **N is δ⁻**, difference = 0.5. N–F: F (4.0) > N (3.0), so **F is δ⁻** and here N is **δ⁺**, difference = 1.0. This shows that nitrogen can be δ⁻ with one partner and δ⁺ with another—electronegativity is always a comparison (Part D). The shared pair is pulled more strongly to one side in N–F. Check: 0.5 < 1.0 ✅.
5. S–Cl: 3.0 − 2.5 = 0.5; P–Br: 2.8 − 2.1 = 0.7; N–F: 4.0 − 3.0 = 1.0. Order: **S–Cl (0.5) < P–Br (0.7) < N–F (1.0)**. Check: 0.5 < 0.7 < 1.0 ✅.
6. (a) **False** ❌: P–H (0.0) and C–H (0.4) are between different atoms yet are non-polar or nearly non-polar. (b) **False** ❌: δ⁻ is only a slight negative charge; a full electron has not been transferred. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌: in polar covalent bonds the pair remains shared, whereas in ionic bonds it is transferred almost completely to one atom.
7. The difference between Li (1.0) and O (3.5) is very large, so O pulls the electrons almost completely to itself. Instead of partial charges, we write **full ionic charges**: Li⁺ and O²⁻ (in Li₂O). It is an ionic bond. Check: 3.5 − 1.0 = 2.5 ✅.
8. Both atoms in O=O are identical (3.5 and 3.5), giving a difference of 0.0. A double bond simply means two shared pairs, and both pairs are pulled equally by the two oxygen atoms, so there is no tilt. Bond multiplicity (single, double) and bond polarity are two separate things.
9. N = 3.0 and Cl = 3.0. Difference = 3.0 − 3.0 = **0.0**. Even though they are two different elements, their pull is equal, so **neither atom is δ⁻** (and neither is δ⁺)—the bond is non-polar.
10. **Incorrect.** Br = 2.8 and Cl = 3.0: Cl pulls more strongly, so Cl is δ⁻ and Br is δ⁺. The arrow must point from δ⁺ (Br) to δ⁻ (Cl), that is, **from Br to Cl**. (The difference is only 0.2, so the tilt is tiny and the bond is nearly non-polar.) Check: 3.0 > 2.8 ✅, 3.0 − 2.8 = 0.2 ✅.
11. Al–O has a difference of 2.0, while S–Cl has 0.5. **Al–O is much closer to ionic**. No, S–Cl is not ionic: 0.5 sits right at the low end of the polar covalent row, so the pair remains shared with only a slight shift toward Cl.
12. Pivot (balance point) = average position of the shared electron pair. Very heavy child = a strongly electronegative atom that pulls the shared pair close to itself. The closer the pivot shifts toward the heavier side, the more polar the bond.
13. X–Y has a difference of 1.1, while X–Z has 0.2. **X–Y is more polar**, whereas the shared pair in X–Z stays much closer to the middle. Check: 1.1 > 0.2 ✅.
