# Topic 2: How Electrolysis Happens: Cells, Electrodes, and Ions

In the previous topic, we discovered that substances releasing **freely mobile ions** when molten or dissolved in water conduct electricity. Crucially, unlike the quiet flow of current through a copper wire, electrical conduction through an electrolyte causes a **chemical change**. But what is that chemical change? Which substances form at which electrode? How does attaching the two terminals of a battery trigger this transformation?

Today, we take apart the entire electrolytic engine: the electrolytic cell, its electrodes, the migration of ions, the flow of electrons through external wires, and the modern **electron view of oxidation and reduction**—completing the picture we first introduced in introductory chemistry.

---

## The Mystery of the Two Nails

During the annual school Science Open House, tenth-grader Leo was touring the chemistry laboratory with his uncle, Mr. Foster, an engineer with a passion for practical science. At one station, the chemistry teacher, Dr. Bennett, dissolved two spoonfuls of table salt in a beaker of water. He then suspended **two clean iron nails** in the solution, wiring one nail to the **positive (+) terminal** of a 9 V battery and the other to the **negative (−) terminal**.

Dr. Bennett began with a clear safety note: *"This is strictly a teacher demonstration. We are using a harmless 9 V battery in a well-ventilated laboratory, and no student should touch the wires or the beaker. Never attempt to replicate this using household wall sockets or mains power."*

As soon as the circuit was connected, **streams of tiny gas bubbles** began nucleating on both nails. The nail connected to the negative terminal was bubbling vigorously, while the other nail produced only a few bubbles. Within minutes, the solution around the second nail began turning a cloudy greenish-brown.

Leo's eyes widened in amazement. *"Uncle Foster, where did those bubbles come from? All we had in the glass was tap water and table salt!"*

Mr. Foster smiled. *"Those bubbles are gases, Leo. Forming a gas where none existed before means a brand-new chemical substance has formed—a **chemical change**. The electric battery is tearing water and dissolved salt molecules apart. That process is called **electrolysis**. Let's examine the machinery of an electrolytic cell, and then we will return to this demonstration with complete understanding."*

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain Language) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electrolysis** | The chemical decomposition of an electrolyte caused by the passage of a direct electric current | Splitting molten $\text{PbBr}_2$ into $\text{Pb}$ and $\text{Br}_2$ |
| **Electrolytic cell** | An apparatus consisting of an electrolyte, two electrodes, and a DC power source | The beaker containing salt solution, nails, and battery |
| **Electrode** | A conductive rod, strip, or plate through which electric current enters or leaves the electrolyte | Graphite rod, platinum foil, iron nail |
| **Anode (+)** | The electrode connected to the **positive** terminal of the battery; where oxidation occurs | Where anions surrender electrons |
| **Cathode (−)** | The electrode connected to the **negative** terminal of the battery; where reduction occurs | Where cations accept electrons |
| **Cation** | A positively charged ion (an atom or group that has lost electrons) | $\text{Pb}^{2+}$, $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{H}^+$ |
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion (an atom or group that has gained electrons) | $\text{Br}^-$, $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{OH}^-$ |
| **Oxidation** | The **loss** of electrons by an atom, molecule, or ion | $2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$ |
| **Reduction** | The **gain** of electrons by an atom, molecule, or ion | $\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb}$ |
| **Half-equation** | An equation showing the chemical reaction and electron transfer occurring at a single electrode | $\text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Na}$ |
| **Inert electrode** | An electrode that does not participate chemically in the reaction; serves only as an electron conductor | Graphite rod, platinum foil |
| **Active electrode** | An electrode that participates chemically in the reaction (can dissolve into ions) | Copper, silver, nickel, iron |

## Part A: The Electrolytic Cell and the Journey of Ions

An electrolytic cell requires just **three essential components**:

1. **Electrolyte:** A molten ionic compound or an aqueous solution containing mobile ions (from Topic 1).
2. **Two Electrodes:** Conductive rods or plates immersed in the electrolyte.
3. **DC Power Source (Battery):** A device that supplies direct current, acting as an electron pump.

> [!TIP]
> **Remembering Electrode Polarities:**
> In an electrolytic cell:
> - The electrode wired to the **positive (+)** terminal of the battery is the **Anode (+)**.
> - The electrode wired to the **negative (−)** terminal of the battery is the **Cathode (−)**.
>
> **Remembering Ion Destinations:**
> Opposite charges attract!
> - **Cations (+)** migrate toward the negatively charged **Cathode (−)** (*CAT-ions seek the CAT-hode*).
> - **Anions (−)** migrate toward the positively charged **Anode (+)** (*AN-ions seek the AN-ode*).

Now follow the journey of charges around the entire circuit:

- **Inside the external metal wires:** Conduction occurs purely via **electrons**. The battery pumps electrons out of its negative terminal into the cathode, making it electron-rich (negatively charged). At the same time, the battery pulls electrons from the anode into its positive terminal, making the anode electron-deficient (positively charged).
- **Inside the electrolyte:** **No electrons swim through the liquid!** Current inside the electrolyte is carried entirely by physical **ions** migrating in opposite directions: cations migrate toward the cathode, and anions migrate toward the anode.

```
      Electron path (external wires only): Anode ---> Battery ---> Cathode

        +--------------[ (+)  BATTERY  (-) ]-------------+
        :                                                :
        ^ e⁻                                         e⁻  v
        :                                                :
        :                                                :
   +----#------------------------------------------------#----+
   :    #  ANODE (+)                       CATHODE (-)   #    :
   :    #                                                #    :
   :    #  <-- X⁻                                M⁺ -->  #    :
   :    #  <-- X⁻       ELECTROLYTE              M⁺ -->  #    :
   :    #  <-- X⁻                                M⁺ -->  #    :
   :    #                                                #    :
   :    #                                                #    :
   +----------------------------------------------------------+
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Schematic of an electrolytic cell: the battery drives electrons through the external circuit from anode to cathode. In the electrolyte, cations ($\text{M}^+$) migrate toward the cathode (−), while anions ($\text{X}^-$) migrate toward the anode (+). Electron exchange at the electrode surfaces completes the continuous electric loop.

Notice how the entire system forms an uninterrupted relay loop:
1. Electrons travel through external wires from anode to cathode.
2. At the cathode surface, incoming electrons are handed off to cations.
3. Ions migrate across the electrolyte.
4. At the anode surface, anions surrender electrons to the anode.
5. Those surrendered electrons flow through the external wire back toward the battery.

If the reaction at either electrode stalls, the entire circuit is broken and current ceases immediately.

## Part B: What Happens at the Electrodes: Reduction and Oxidation

What actually happens when an ion touches an electrode surface?

**At the Cathode (−):**
The cathode is crowded with electrons pumped from the battery. When positive cations arrive, they **accept electrons** to become neutral atoms:

$$\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb}$$
$$\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$$
$$\text{Ag}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Ag}$$
$$2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$$

The **gain of electrons is Reduction**. Therefore:
> **Reduction always occurs at the Cathode.**

**At the Anode (+):**
The anode is electron-deficient. When negative anions arrive, they **surrender their excess electrons** to become neutral atoms or molecules:

$$2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$$
$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$$
$$4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 + 4\text{e}^-$$

The **loss of electrons is Oxidation**. Therefore:
> **Oxidation always occurs at the Anode.**

Two universal mnemonics keep these rules crystal clear:
- **OIL RIG:** **O**xidation **I**s **L**oss (of electrons), **R**eduction **I**s **G**ain.
- **AN OX, RED CAT:** **AN**ode = **OX**idation; **RED**uction = **CAT**hode.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Conservation of Electrons:**
> Electrons can neither be created nor destroyed. The total number of electrons surrendered at the anode must **exactly equal** the total number of electrons accepted at the cathode!

---

### The Class 9 View vs. The Electron View

In introductory chemistry, oxidation was defined as the *addition of oxygen* (or removal of hydrogen), and reduction was defined as the *removal of oxygen* (or addition of hydrogen) ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part5.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part5.md)).

While that definition works for simple combustion reactions, it leaves major gaps. For example, when magnesium burns in oxygen to form $\text{MgO}$, magnesium gains oxygen (oxidation)—but what happened to the oxygen?
The modern **electron view** resolves this completely:

$$\text{Mg} \rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \quad (\text{loss of electrons: Oxidation})$$
$$\text{O}_2 + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{O}^{2-} \quad (\text{gain of electrons: Reduction})$$

Magnesium lost electrons (it was oxidised); oxygen gained electrons (it was reduced). Oxygen acted as the oxidising agent because it took electrons from magnesium!

Furthermore, in electrolysis, many reactions involve neither oxygen nor hydrogen. For instance:

$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$$

No oxygen or hydrogen is present, yet bromide ions undeniably surrender electrons. Under the electron definition, this is unequivocally **oxidation**. The electron view is broader, universal, and fundamental to all electrochemistry.

## Part C: Inert vs. Active Electrodes

Electrodes fall into two major categories depending on their chemical reactivity:

1. **Inert Electrodes (Graphite, Platinum):**
   Inert electrodes do not react chemically with the electrolyte, nor do they dissolve. They act solely as conductive surfaces for electron transfer.
   - Graphite is widely used industrially because it is cheap, heat-resistant, and conducts electricity well despite being a non-metal.
   - Platinum is highly inert and used for precision laboratory work.

2. **Active Electrodes (Copper, Silver, Nickel, Iron):**
   Active electrodes are made of metals that can participate directly in the electrode reaction. When an active metal is used as the **anode**, the metal atoms themselves tend to lose electrons and dissolve into solution as cations:

$$\text{Cu (solid anode)} \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$$

Active electrodes play a central role in electroplating (Topic 4) and metal refining (Topic 5). For all standard baseline cells, **electrodes are assumed to be inert unless stated otherwise.**

## Part D: Molten Lead Bromide: Putting It All Together

To study electrolysis without the competing ions of water, we examine a pure molten salt: **Lead(II) bromide ($\text{PbBr}_2$)**.

Solid lead bromide is a white crystalline powder. When heated above its melting point ($\approx 373\ ^\circ\text{C}$), its crystal lattice collapses, liberating mobile $\text{Pb}^{2+}$ cations and $\text{Br}^-$ anions.

> [!WARNING]
> **Safety & Toxicity Warning:**
> Lead and its compounds are cumulative heavy-metal toxins. Bromine is an intensely toxic, corrosive element: a dense red-brown liquid at room temperature that vaporises into dense red-brown fumes, severely burning skin, eyes, and mucous membranes. The electrolysis of molten lead bromide is **strictly a theoretical study topic** or performed solely by a trained instructor inside a certified fume hood. It must never be attempted at home or in an unventilated classroom.

In an inert silica crucible, molten $\text{PbBr}_2$ is electrolysed using two graphite rods connected to a DC power supply:

```
       (+) to battery                     (-) to battery
             :                                     :
    Br₂  ^   #                                     #
  (red-      #                                     #
   brown     #                                     #
   vapour)   #                                     #
         +---#-------------------------------------#----+
         :   #                                     #    :
         :   #  <-- Br⁻               Pb²⁺ -->     #    :
         :   #  ANODE (+)         CATHODE (-)      #    :
         :   #                                     #    :
         :   #        Molten PbBr₂                 #    :
         :                             Pb (molten,     :
         :                             dense pool)     :
         +----------------------------------------------+
                    ^  ^  ^   Heat (fume cupboard demo)
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide: at the graphite anode (+), bromide ions lose electrons, releasing pungent red-brown bromine vapor ($\text{Br}_2$). At the graphite cathode (−), lead ions gain electrons, forming silvery molten lead metal ($\text{Pb}$) that pools at the bottom of the crucible.

**At the Cathode (−) [Reduction]:**
Lead cations migrate to the negative cathode, where each ion absorbs 2 electrons:

$$\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb (molten)}$$

Molten lead is a silvery-grey liquid. Because lead is very dense ($\approx 11.3\text{ g/cm}^3$), it collects as a liquid puddle at the bottom of the crucible.

**At the Anode (+) [Oxidation]:**
Bromide anions migrate to the positive anode, where each ion surrenders an electron. Two neutral bromine atoms pair up to form a diatomic molecule:

$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$$

Dense, reddish-brown fumes of bromine gas bubble off and rise from the anode.

**Overall Cell Equation:**
Combine the two half-reactions (electrons cancel: 2 accepted, 2 surrendered):

$$\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb} + \text{Br}_2$$
$$\text{PbBr}_2\text{ (liquid)} \xrightarrow{\text{electricity}} \text{Pb (liquid)} + \text{Br}_2\text{ (gas)}$$

*(Verification: 1 Pb atom, 2 Br atoms on each side ✅; charge: $0 = 0$ ✅; mass conserved ✅)*

## Part E: Connecting Back to Metallurgy

With the mechanism of electrolysis now clear, the industrial extraction reactions from metallurgy fall neatly into place:

| Molten Electrolyte | Cathode Reaction (Reduction) | Anode Reaction (Oxidation) | Overall Decomposition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molten $\text{NaCl}$ (Downs Cell) | $\text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Na}$ | $2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$ | $2\text{NaCl} \rightarrow 2\text{Na} + \text{Cl}_2$ |
| Molten $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ in cryolite (Hall-Héroult) | $\text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Al}$ | $2\text{O}^{2-} \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + 4\text{e}^-$ | $2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow 4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2$ |

In every instance, **metals deposit at the cathode** and **non-metals evolve at the anode**.

---

### Why Does Metal Always Deposit at the Cathode?

Metals are electropositive elements—their atoms readily lose valence electrons to form **positive cations** ($\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{Pb}^{2+}$, $\text{Cu}^{2+}$). Hydrogen behaves similarly, forming $\text{H}^+$.

Because opposite charges attract, these positive cations are drawn inexorably toward the **negative electrode (the cathode)**. Upon contact, they gain electrons (reduction) and revert to neutral metal atoms. Non-metals form negative anions ($\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{Br}^-$, $\text{O}^{2-}$), which are attracted to the **positive electrode (the anode)**, where they lose electrons (oxidation).

## Part F: Revisiting the Two Nails Demo: Now with Full Understanding

Returning to Dr. Bennett's demonstration at Open House, we can now explain every detail:

The electrolyte was an aqueous solution of sodium chloride. In water, four distinct ion species exist:
- Cations: $\text{Na}^+$ (from salt) and $\text{H}^+$ (from water auto-ionisation).
- Anions: $\text{Cl}^-$ (from salt) and $\text{OH}^-$ (from water).

**At the Cathode (− nail):**
Both $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{H}^+$ migrate toward the cathode. Hydrogen ions accept electrons far more easily than sodium ions do (a principle called *selective discharge*, explored in Topic 3):

$$2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{ (gas)}$$

This explains the vigorous stream of **hydrogen gas bubbles** at the negative nail!

**At the Anode (+ nail):**
The positive nail is made of iron—an **active electrode**. While anions ($\text{Cl}^-$ and $\text{OH}^-$) migrate toward it, it is far easier for the metallic iron nail itself to oxidise and dissolve:

$$\text{Fe (solid nail)} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$$

As $\text{Fe}^{2+}$ ions enter the solution, they encounter $\text{OH}^-$ ions to form iron(II) hydroxide, $\text{Fe(OH)}_2$, which oxidises in air to form greenish-brown hydrated iron oxides. This explains the cloudy discoloration and why bubble formation was sluggish at the anode nail!

```
              [ 9 V BATTERY ]
              (+)         (-)
               :           :
               :           :
           +---#-----------#---+
           :   #           #   :
           :   #           #   :
           :   #  o       o#o  :
           :   #           #  o:
           :   # o       o o#o :
           :   #           #o  :
           +-------------------+
          ANODE (+)    CATHODE (-)
          Iron nail    Iron nail
          few bubbles  vigorous bubbles
          (nail dissolves) (H₂ gas)
```

> **Figure 2.3** – The two-nail demonstration explained: the cathode (−) nail evolves a vigorous stream of hydrogen gas bubbles ($2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$). The anode (+) nail oxidises directly ($\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$), dissolving into the water and producing a cloudy greenish-brown iron precipitate.

Dr. Bennett concluded the demonstration: *"Never ignite or inhale gas bubbles produced during electrolysis. In our next topic, we will discover why $\text{H}^+$ discharged instead of $\text{Na}^+$, unlocking the universal rules of **selective discharge**."*

## Part G: Solved Examples

*(Atomic masses: $\text{H} = 1$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{Mg} = 24$, $\text{Cl} = 35.5$, $\text{Ni} = 59$, $\text{Cu} = 63.5$, $\text{Zn} = 65$, $\text{Br} = 80$, $\text{Pb} = 207$)*

**Solved Example 1 (Formulating Half-Equations and Overall Cell Reaction).** Write the balanced cathode and anode half-equations for the electrolysis of molten lead(II) bromide ($\text{PbBr}_2$). Verify electron balance and deduce the overall cell equation.

*Solution:*
- At cathode (−) [Reduction]:
$$\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb}$$
- At anode (+) [Oxidation]:
$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$$
- Balancing electrons: The cathode consumes 2 electrons; the anode releases 2 electrons. The electron counts match perfectly ($1 : 1$).
- Overall equation:
$$\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb} + \text{Br}_2 \quad \text{or} \quad \text{PbBr}_2\text{ (l)} \rightarrow \text{Pb (l)} + \text{Br}_2\text{ (g)}$$
*(Verification: Atoms: 1 Pb, 2 Br on both sides ✅; Charge: $0 = 0$ ✅; Mass: $207 + 160 = 367\text{ g/mol}$ on both sides ✅)*

---

**Solved Example 2 (Stoichiometry of Electron Flow in Electrolysis).** During the electrolysis of molten $\text{PbBr}_2$, exactly $0.5\text{ mol}$ of lead metal is deposited at the cathode.
(a) How many moles of electrons passed through the circuit?
(b) What mass of lead metal was collected?
(c) What mass of bromine gas was liberated at the anode? Show that total mass is conserved.

*Solution:*
(a) From the cathode half-equation, $\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Pb}$, depositing $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Pb}$ requires $2\text{ mol}$ of electrons.
$$\text{Moles of electrons} = 0.5\text{ mol Pb} \times 2 = \mathbf{1.0\text{ mol of electrons}}$$

(b) Mass of lead formed:
$$\text{Mass of Pb} = 0.5\text{ mol} \times 207\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{103.5\text{ g}}$$

(c) At the anode, $2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$, liberating $1\text{ mol}$ of $\text{Br}_2$ releases $2\text{ mol}$ of electrons. Thus, $1.0\text{ mol}$ of electrons yields:
$$\text{Moles of Br}_2 = \frac{1.0\text{ mol } \text{e}^-}{2} = 0.5\text{ mol Br}_2$$
$$\text{Mass of Br}_2 = 0.5\text{ mol} \times (2 \times 80\text{ g/mol}) = 0.5 \times 160 = \mathbf{80.0\text{ g}}$$

*(Conservation of Mass Check: Total mass decomposed = $0.5\text{ mol PbBr}_2 \times 367\text{ g/mol} = 183.5\text{ g}$. Mass of products = $103.5\text{ g (Pb)} + 80.0\text{ g (Br}_2) = 183.5\text{ g}$ ✅)*

---

**Solved Example 3 (Interpreting Redox from the Electron Perspective).** The combustion of magnesium in oxygen is represented by $2\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{MgO}$. Break this reaction into half-reactions, identify the oxidised and reduced species, and state the oxidising and reducing agents.

*Solution:*
- Magnesium half-reaction:
$$2\text{Mg} \rightarrow 2\text{Mg}^{2+} + 4\text{e}^-$$
Each magnesium atom loses 2 electrons $\rightarrow$ **Oxidation**.
- Oxygen half-reaction:
$$\text{O}_2 + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{O}^{2-}$$
Each oxygen atom gains 2 electrons $\rightarrow$ **Reduction**.
- **Conclusions:**
  - $\text{Mg}$ is **oxidised** (electron donor); it is the **reducing agent**.
  - $\text{O}_2$ is **reduced** (electron acceptor); it is the **oxidising agent**.
*(Check: Electrons lost = 4; Electrons gained = 4; Net charge on both sides = 0 ✅)*

---

**Solved Example 4 (Direct Electron Transfer vs. Electrolysis).** When a strip of zinc metal is dipped into blue copper(II) sulfate solution, the displacement reaction $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$ occurs spontaneously.
(a) Write the individual oxidation and reduction half-equations.
(b) How does electron transfer in this beaker reaction differ from that in an electrolytic cell?

*Solution:*
(a)
- Oxidation: $\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$ (Zn loses electrons)
- Reduction: $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$ (Cu²⁺ gains electrons)

(b) In the simple beaker displacement reaction, electrons are transferred **directly** at the zinc surface where copper ions collide with zinc atoms; no wire is present and no electrical work is harnessed. In an electrolytic cell, oxidation and reduction are **physically separated** at two different electrodes, forcing electrons to travel through an external metal circuit to accomplish work.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that the cathode must be positive because cations are positive.**
   *Correction:* Remember that opposite charges attract! Cations are positively charged, so they are pulled toward the **negatively charged electrode**. In an electrolytic cell, the **cathode is negative** (connected to the battery's negative terminal).

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking that electrons flow directly through the liquid electrolyte.**
   *Correction:* Free electrons cannot survive or travel through liquid water or molten salts! Electrons travel strictly through the **external metallic wires**. Inside the electrolyte, current is carried entirely by physical, migrating **cations and anions**. At the electrodes, electron transfer occurs like a relay race: the cathode hands electrons to cations, and anions hand electrons back to the anode.

3. **Mistake 3: Defining oxidation strictly as the addition of oxygen.**
   *Correction:* In electrochemistry, oxidation is defined universally as the **loss of electrons** ($\text{OIL}$). Reactions like $2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$ involve zero oxygen, yet they are classic oxidation reactions. Always remember: $\text{OIL RIG}$ (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain).

4. **Mistake 4: Believing the anode in an electrolytic cell is negative.**
   *Correction:* In an electrolytic cell, the anode is connected to the **positive terminal** of the external power supply, making the **anode positive**. Negative anions ($\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{Br}^-$) are attracted to it. (Do not confuse an electrolytic cell with a galvanic battery, where chemical energy produces power; galvanic cells are covered in advanced chemistry.)

## Quick Recap

- **Electrolysis:** Chemical decomposition of an electrolyte caused by the passage of direct electric current.
- **Cell Structure:** Consists of an electrolyte, two electrodes, and a DC power source. The electrode wired to battery $(+)$ is the **anode (+)**; the electrode wired to battery $(-)$ is the **cathode (−)**.
- **Migration:** Cations (+) migrate toward the cathode (−); anions (−) migrate toward the anode (+).
- **Conduction Modes:** Metallic conduction in external wires (via free electrons); electrolytic conduction in the solution (via mobile ions).
- **Reactions at Electrodes:**
  - **Cathode (−):** Reduction (gain of electrons): $\text{M}^{n+} + n\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{M}$.
  - **Anode (+):** Oxidation (loss of electrons): $2\text{X}^- \rightarrow \text{X}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$.
- **Mnemonics:** $\text{OIL RIG}$ (Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain); $\text{AN OX, RED CAT}$ (Anode = Oxidation, Reduction = Cathode).
- **Electrodes:** Inert electrodes (graphite, platinum) do not react; active electrodes (copper, silver, iron) can oxidise and dissolve into the electrolyte.
- **Metal Deposition:** Metals and hydrogen form cations and therefore **always discharge at the cathode**.
- **Molten $\text{PbBr}_2$:** Yields dense molten lead at the cathode and dense reddish-brown bromine vapor at the anode. Both are toxic: **theoretical study only**.

### Answers

1. **Electrolysis** is the chemical decomposition of an electrolyte brought about by the passage of direct electric current. The three essential components are: (1) the electrolyte, (2) two electrodes, and (3) a DC power source (battery). The electrode joined to the battery's positive terminal is the **anode**.

2. (a) $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ cations migrate to the **cathode (−)**; $\text{Cl}^-$ anions migrate to the **anode (+)**.
   (b)
   - Cathode (−): $\text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Zn}$ (**Reduction**)
   - Anode (+): $2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$ (**Oxidation**)
   (c) Overall equation: $\text{ZnCl}_2\text{ (l)} \rightarrow \text{Zn (l)} + \text{Cl}_2\text{ (g)}$.
   *(Check: Zn 1, Cl 2 on each side; net charge $0 = 0$; 2 electrons transferred ✅)*

3. (a) Moles of zinc = $\frac{195\text{ g}}{65\text{ g/mol}} = \mathbf{3.0\text{ mol Zn}}$.
   (b) Each mole of $\text{Zn}$ requires $2\text{ mol}$ of electrons ($\text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Zn}$).
   $$\text{Moles of electrons} = 3.0 \times 2 = \mathbf{6.0\text{ mol electrons}}$$
   (c) At the anode, $2\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$. $6.0\text{ mol}$ of electrons liberates $\frac{6.0}{2} = 3.0\text{ mol Cl}_2$.
   $$\text{Mass of Cl}_2 = 3.0\text{ mol} \times 71\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{213\text{ g}}$$
   *(Conservation check: Mass of $\text{ZnCl}_2$ decomposed = $3.0 \times (65 + 71) = 408\text{ g}$. Mass of products = $195\text{ g} + 213\text{ g} = 408\text{ g}$ ✅)*

4. (a)
   - Cathode: $\text{K}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{K}$ (Multiply by 2 for electron balance: $2\text{K}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{K}$)
   - Anode: $2\text{I}^- \rightarrow \text{I}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$
   - Overall cell equation: $2\text{KI} \rightarrow 2\text{K} + \text{I}_2$
   (b) Moles of potassium = $\frac{78\text{ g}}{39\text{ g/mol}} = 2.0\text{ mol K}$. Since each $\text{K}^+$ requires $1\text{ e}^-$, **$2.0\text{ mol of electrons}$** passed. At the anode, $2\text{ mol e}^-$ liberates $1.0\text{ mol of I}_2$:
   $$\text{Mass of I}_2 = 1.0\text{ mol} \times 254\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{254\text{ g}}$$
   *(Conservation check: $2\text{ mol KI} = 2 \times 166 = 332\text{ g}$; products = $78 + 254 = 332\text{ g}$ ✅)*

5. (a) $\text{Ni}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Ni}$: **Reduction** (gain of 2 electrons).
   (b) $\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$: **Oxidation** (loss of 2 electrons).
   (c) $2\text{I}^- \rightarrow \text{I}_2 + 2\text{e}^-$: **Oxidation** (loss of 2 electrons).
   (d) $\text{Ag}^+ + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Ag}$: **Reduction** (gain of 1 electron).
   (e) $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{Cl}^-$: **Reduction** (gain of 2 electrons).

6. (a)
   - Oxidation: $\text{Fe} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$
   - Reduction: $\text{Ni}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Ni}$
   (b) $\text{Fe}$ is **oxidised** (loses electrons); $\text{Ni}^{2+}$ is **reduced** (gains electrons).
   (c) Neither oxygen nor hydrogen participates in this reaction. Thus, the classical oxygen/hydrogen definition is incapable of analyzing it, making the electron definition necessary and comprehensive.

7. (a) Deposition half-equation: $\text{M}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{M}$.
   $2\text{ mol of electrons}$ deposits $1\text{ mol of M}$.
   With $1.0\text{ mol of electrons}$, moles of M deposited = $\frac{1.0}{2} = 0.5\text{ mol}$.
   $$\text{Molar mass of M} = \frac{29.5\text{ g}}{0.5\text{ mol}} = 59.0\text{ g/mol}$$
   Comparing with the given masses, the metal is **Nickel ($\text{Ni}$, molar mass $59\text{ g/mol}$)**.
   (b) Metal M is deposited at the **cathode (−)**, because positive metal cations ($\text{M}^{2+}$) are electrostatically attracted to the negative electrode to gain electrons.

8. A well-drawn diagram must match Figure 2.1:
   - DC source clearly showing long line (+) and short thick line (−).
   - Wire from (+) terminal connected to the left graphite rod, labelled **Anode (+)**.
   - Wire from (−) terminal connected to the right graphite rod, labelled **Cathode (−)**.
   - Electron flow arrows in external wires pointing from anode to battery, and from battery to cathode.

   (b) **False.** Electrons do not travel through the electrolyte; conduction in the electrolyte is by migrating ions. Electrons travel exclusively through the external wires.
   (c) **True.**
   (d) **True.**
   (e) **True.**

10. In solid crystalline $\text{ZnCl}_2$, the $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions are locked in fixed positions within a rigid electrostatic lattice and cannot move; hence, solid crystals are electrical insulators. Upon melting, thermal energy overcomes the electrostatic lattice forces, allowing the ions to move freely to carry electric current.

11. Metal atoms lose electrons to form positively charged cations ($\text{M}^{n+}$). In an electric field, positive ions are attracted exclusively to the negatively charged electrode—the cathode. At the cathode, they acquire electrons (reduction) to become neutral metal atoms. Anions migrate to the anode, so metals cannot form at the anode.

12. (i) **Toxicity:** Lead compounds are severe neurotoxins, and bromine vapor released at the anode is dense, highly corrosive, and suffocatingly toxic.

   *Safe alternative:* Study the process theoretically using diagrams, animated simulations, or observe a professionally staged teacher demonstration in a certified fume cupboard.
