# Topic 1: Electrolytes and Non-Electrolytes: What Conducts Electricity?

Throughout Chapter 3, we repeatedly encountered the phrase: "and now, **electrolysis** takes place." Sodium, magnesium, and aluminium were extracted from their ores this way, and blister copper was refined into high-purity metal using electric current. Yet each time, we deferred a fundamental question: how does electricity actually travel through a **liquid**? In a solid copper wire, moving electrons do the job easily enough—but what happens inside a liquid? Some liquids conduct electricity brilliantly, others conduct very poorly, and many do not conduct at all. To understand how an electrolytic cell works, which ions discharge first, how electroplating coats jewelry, and how massive chlor-alkali factories operate, we must first answer one essential question: **what gives a liquid the power to conduct an electric current?**

---

## The Water Heater Switch and an Intriguing Question

In Dr. Bennett's bathroom, right beside the wall switch for the electric water heater, there is a small, clear warning label: **"Do not touch with wet hands."**

During summer break, his teenage niece Maya was visiting. Stepping out of the bathroom one morning, she noticed the sign and asked, "Uncle Bennett, that warning is everywhere—in homes, swimming pool locker rooms, and science labs. But why are water and electricity treated as sworn enemies? In physics class, we learned that **pure water is a very poor conductor of electricity**. If water itself hardly conducts, why is everyone so terrified of wet hands near a switch?"

Dr. Bennett smiled. "What you learned in physics is absolutely true, and the warning label is equally true. Both statements are correct at the same time. The secret lies in a single word: **pure**. The tap water flowing through household pipes looks crystal clear, but it is **not pure distilled water**. As it journeys through reservoirs, soil, and underground pipes, it dissolves tiny amounts of mineral salts. It is those dissolved salts that turn water into an **electric pathway**."

Maya's eyes widened. "So it's not the water molecules conducting electricity—it's the dissolved minerals inside?"

"Precisely," said Dr. Bennett. "That property defines an **electrolyte**. That is our topic today. And let's make one rule crystal clear from the start: that label is an absolute **safety warning**, not an invitation to experiment. Mains electricity (120 V or 230 V) carries deadly current, and water must never be brought anywhere near mains sockets or appliances."

Maya nodded. "What about the small battery tester we built in ninth grade? Is that safe?"

"Completely safe," Dr. Bennett assured her. "A tester powered by **two AA cells (3 volts)** cannot shock anyone and has **zero connection to mains power**. In our home lab, under supervision, that low-voltage tester will let us investigate an entire lineup of everyday liquids. Let's first establish the key vocabulary, and then test some liquids."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain Language) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electrolyte** | A substance that conducts electricity when molten or dissolved in water, due to the presence of freely mobile ions | Acids, alkalis, soluble salts |
| **Non-electrolyte** | A substance that does not conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water, because it contains no ions | Sugar, glucose, ethanol, urea |
| **Ion** | An electrically charged particle (an atom or group of atoms that has lost or gained electrons) | Na⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻ |
| **Cation, Anion** | A positively charged ion; a negatively charged ion | Na⁺ cation; Cl⁻ anion |
| **Free ions** | Ions that are mobile and free to move throughout a solution or molten liquid | Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions drifting in saltwater |
| **Strong electrolyte** | An electrolyte that dissociates or ionises **almost completely** into ions in aqueous solution | HCl, NaOH, NaCl |
| **Weak electrolyte** | An electrolyte that ionises **only slightly** in aqueous solution, leaving most molecules intact | CH₃COOH, H₂CO₃, NH₄OH |
| **Ionisation** | The process in which a neutral covalent molecule reacts with water to form ions where none existed before | HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻ |
| **Dissociation** | The separation of pre-existing ions from an ionic solid lattice when it dissolves in water or melts | NaCl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ |
| **Metallic conduction** | Electrical conduction in metals through the flow of mobile delocalised electrons | Current flowing in a copper wire |
| **Electrolytic conduction** | Electrical conduction in a liquid or solution through the migration of mobile ions | Current flowing in a sodium chloride solution |

## Part A: What Is Needed to Conduct Electricity?

In introductory physics, you learned that an electric current is the **flow of electric charge** ([electricity.md](../../../class9/physics/electricity/electricity.md)). In chemical bonding, you discovered that solid ionic crystals cannot conduct electricity because their ions are locked tightly inside a rigid lattice, but they conduct readily when melted or dissolved in water ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)).

The fundamental rule can be stated in a single sentence:

> **To conduct an electric current, a substance must contain mobile charge carriers—either free electrons (in metals) or freely mobile ions (in liquids).**

We can capture the electrical conductivity of any liquid in a simple formula:

> **Electrical conductivity of a liquid ∝ Concentration of freely mobile ions (and their net charges).**

If a liquid contains no mobile ions, it cannot complete an electrical circuit. If it contains few mobile ions, it carries only a trickle of current. If it is packed with mobile ions, it carries a robust current with ease.

## Part B: Activity 1.1: Testing Everyday Liquids with a Conductivity Tester

> **Home / School Lab Activity with Adult Supervision.** This investigation uses strictly 2 AA batteries (3 volts). Never connect any wire, probe, or liquid to household mains electricity, sockets, or wall adapters.

**Safety Guidelines:**
- The tester uses **only 2 AA cells (total 3 V)**, a small LED, and a **220 Ω resistor in series**. The resistor protects the LED from burnout (explained in Solved Example 5).
- **Never taste any test liquid** (not even lemon juice or vinegar: observe only the LED and probe tips). Clearly label every cup.
- **Never test flammable liquids (gasoline, lighter fluid, methylated spirits, rubbing alcohol).** Never test household bleach, drain cleaner, or concentrated acids or alkalis. Stick strictly to the safe domestic liquids listed below.
- Ensure the two bare probe wires **do not touch each other directly** during testing. After each test, rinse both probes thoroughly with clean water and dry them with a paper towel.
- Dip the probes into each liquid for **only a few seconds**. If tiny bubbles form on the metal tips, that is normal, but do not leave the current running indefinitely.
- Wash your hands thoroughly after finishing.

**Materials:** 2 AA batteries with holder, 1 small LED (e.g. red or green), 1 resistor (220 Ω), insulated copper connecting wires, 7 small clear glass cups or beakers, distilled water, tap water, freshly squeezed lemon juice, white vinegar, club soda (freshly opened), baking soda (sodium bicarbonate), granulated sugar, measuring spoons, labels.

6. **Tap water:** ½ cup of regular household tap water.
7. **Distilled water:** ½ cup of pure distilled water (or deionised water).

## Part C: Three Classes of Substances

Our experimental findings divide chemical substances into three distinct classes:

1. **Strong Electrolytes:** Substances that release a large concentration of freely mobile ions in solution, allowing a heavy current to flow (LED glows brightly). Soluble salts like baking soda (NaHCO₃) and table salt (NaCl), as well as strong mineral acids and alkalis, belong here.
2. **Weak Electrolytes:** Substances that produce only a low concentration of mobile ions in solution (LED glows dimly or faintly). Weak acids (acetic acid, citric acid, carbonic acid) and weak bases (ammonium hydroxide) belong here.
3. **Non-Electrolytes:** Substances whose aqueous solutions or molten liquids contain no ions at all (LED remains completely dark). Molecular compounds such as sugar, glucose, ethanol, and urea belong here.

---

### Strong and Weak: A Single Question: "How Many Molecules Ionise?"

The difference between a strong and a weak electrolyte boils down to a single question: **what fraction of the dissolved compound splits into ions?**

- **Strong Electrolyte:** Dissociates or ionises **almost completely** (close to 100%) in aqueous solution. Almost no intact neutral molecules remain. Examples: strong acids (HCl, H₂SO₄, HNO₃), strong alkalis (NaOH, KOH), and soluble ionic salts (NaCl, CuSO₄, NaHCO₃).
- **Weak Electrolyte:** Ionises only to a **very small degree** (typically 1% to 5% or less) in water; the vast majority of dissolved molecules remain un-ionised. Examples: acetic acid (CH₃COOH), carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), and ammonium hydroxide (NH₄OH). In chemical equations, a reversible equilibrium arrow (⇌) is used to show that molecules split into ions while ions simultaneously recombine into molecules:

$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Do not confuse "strong vs. weak" with "concentrated vs. dilute"!**
> Concentration refers to the **amount of solute dissolved per unit volume**. Degree of ionisation refers to the **innate nature of the substance**. A dilute 0.001 M solution of hydrochloric acid is still a **strong electrolyte** because every single dissolved HCl molecule splits into H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Conversely, pure glacial acetic acid (extremely concentrated) is still a **weak electrolyte** because only a minuscule fraction of its molecules ionises into ions.

```
   Strong electrolyte                   Weak electrolyte
   (10 molecules of HCl)                (10 molecules of CH₃COOH = HAc)

   H⁺  Cl⁻  H⁺  Cl⁻                    HAc  HAc  HAc
   Cl⁻ H⁺   Cl⁻ H⁺                     HAc  HAc  HAc
   H⁺  Cl⁻  H⁺  Cl⁻                    HAc  HAc  HAc
   Cl⁻ H⁺   Cl⁻ H⁺                     H⁺   Ac⁻
   H⁺  Cl⁻  H⁺  Cl⁻

   10 H⁺ + 10 Cl⁻ = 20 free ions       9 intact HAc + 1 H⁺ + 1 Ac⁻ = 2 free ions
   Complete ionisation: Bright LED     Partial ionisation: Dim LED
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Conceptual model comparing strong and weak electrolytes: in the left beaker, all 10 HCl molecules have ionised into 10 H⁺ and 10 Cl⁻ (20 mobile ions in total), lighting the LED brightly. In the right beaker, only 1 out of 10 acetic acid molecules (HAc) has ionised into H⁺ and Ac⁻ (just 2 mobile ions), while 9 molecules remain neutral and uncharged. In reality, vinegar ionises to less than 1%, making the contrast even sharper!

### Ionisation and Dissociation: Where Did the Ions Come From?

Chemists draw a clear distinction between the origins of ions:

**1. Dissociation (Ionic Compounds):**
In an ionic compound like solid sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) or copper(II) sulfate ($\text{CuSO}_4$), ions **already exist** inside the crystal lattice, held by strong electrostatic forces. When dissolved in water or melted by heat, the crystal structure breaks down, setting the pre-existing ions free:

$$\text{NaCl} \xrightarrow{\text{water}} \text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \quad (\text{dissociation})$$
$$\text{NaOH} \xrightarrow{\text{water}} \text{Na}^+ + \text{OH}^-$$
$$\text{CuSO}_4 \xrightarrow{\text{water}} \text{Cu}^{2+} + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$$

**2. Ionisation (Polar Covalent Compounds):**
A pure compound like hydrogen chloride gas ($\text{HCl}$) is a polar covalent molecular substance—it consists of neutral $\text{H}-\text{Cl}$ molecules sharing an electron pair. **No ions exist in the pure gas.** However, when dissolved in polar water, the partial charges on water molecules pull the $\text{H}-\text{Cl}$ bond apart, generating ions that did not exist beforehand:

$$\text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \quad (\text{ionisation})$$
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+ \quad (\text{partial ionisation})$$
$$\text{NH}_4\text{OH} \rightleftharpoons \text{NH}_4^+ + \text{OH}^-$$

Notice three vital facts:
1. **Dissociation liberates pre-existing ions; ionisation creates new ions from covalent molecules.**
2. **Neither process requires an electric battery.** The solvent (water) or heat energy frees or forms the ions before any current is applied. An electric battery merely directs their movement!
3. **Every solution remains electrically neutral overall.** In any electrolyte solution, the total positive charge carried by all cations equals the total negative charge carried by all anions:

$$\text{Total positive charge} = \text{Total negative charge}$$

## Part D: Which Substances Are Electrolytes? A Comparative Table

| Chemical Category | Strong Electrolytes | Weak Electrolytes | Non-Electrolytes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Acids** | Hydrochloric acid ($\text{HCl}$), Sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), Nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) | Acetic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$), Carbonic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$), Citric acid | *(No common acids)* |
| **Alkalis / Bases** | Sodium hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$), Potassium hydroxide ($\text{KOH}$) | Ammonium hydroxide ($\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$), Calcium hydroxide ($\text{Ca(OH)}_2$) | *(No ionic alkalis)* |
| **Salts** | $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{CuSO}_4$, $\text{K}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{NaHCO}_3$, $\text{AgNO}_3$ | Mercuric chloride ($\text{HgCl}_2$), Lead acetate (slightly ionised) | *(No typical salts)* |
| **Organic & Covalent Compounds** | *(None)* | *(None)* | Cane sugar (sucrose), Glucose, Ethanol, Urea, Kerosene, Glycerol |
| **Water** | *(None)* | Pure distilled water (extremely weak: self-ionises negligibly) | *(Treated practically as non-conducting)* |

> [!NOTE]
> **Does pure distilled water conduct zero electricity?**
> Not quite zero, but very close. Water undergoes a minute auto-ionisation:
> $$\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+ + \text{OH}^-$$
> At 25 °C, only about 1 in every 550,000,000 water molecules splits into ions ($\text{[H}^+\text{]} = 10^{-7}\text{ mol/L}$, corresponding to $\text{pH} = 7$). That minuscule ion concentration cannot pass enough current to illuminate our LED tester. For practical high school purposes, distilled water behaves as a non-electrolyte.

## Part E: Metallic vs Electrolytic Conduction: Not the Same Thing

Both a copper wire and a beaker of saltwater conduct electricity, but their underlying mechanisms are entirely different:

| Feature | Metallic Conduction (Electronic) | Electrolytic Conduction (Ionic) |
|---|---|---|
| **Charge carriers** | **Free delocalised electrons** | **Freely mobile ions** (cations and anions) |
| **What moves** | Only electrons drift; metal atomic nuclei remain fixed in lattice | Cations migrate toward cathode; anions migrate toward anode |
| **Chemical change** | **No chemical change occurs**; the metal wire remains chemically identical | **Chemical change occurs**; substances are discharged and formed at electrodes |
| **Effect of heating** | Conductivity **decreases** (lattice vibrations scatter electron flow) | Conductivity **increases** (viscosity drops; ions move faster) |
| **Matter transported?** | **No physical transfer of matter** along the conductor | **Physical transfer of ions** toward the respective electrodes |
| **Typical examples** | Copper wire, aluminium cables, iron nail, graphite rod | Molten $\text{NaCl}$, aqueous $\text{CuSO}_4$, dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ |

```
  A. Metallic Wire Conductor            B. Electrolytic Solution Conductor

  (−) ------------------ (+)           (−) cathode       anode (+)
                                        :                      :
     +    +    +    +    +              :   ←  Na⁺   Cl⁻  →    :
  e⁻→  e⁻→  e⁻→  e⁻→  e⁻→               :   ←  Na⁺   Cl⁻  →    :
     +    +    +    +    +              :   ←  Na⁺   Cl⁻  →    :

  + = fixed metal cation core           Cations (+) drift to negative electrode;
      (stationary in lattice)           anions (−) drift to positive electrode;
  e⁻ = free electron (mobile)           ions migrate through the solution.
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Contrasting the two conduction modes: **(A)** In a solid metal wire, positive atomic cores remain fixed in their lattice positions while mobile valence electrons drift from the negative terminal to the positive terminal. **(B)** In an electrolytic solution, no free electrons swim through the water; instead, physical cations ($\text{Na}^+$) migrate toward the negative electrode and physical anions ($\text{Cl}^-$) migrate toward the positive electrode.

*(Special Note: Graphite is a non-metal, yet it has delocalised electrons between its hexagonal carbon sheets. It conducts electricity via metallic conduction without chemical decomposition, making it an excellent inert electrode for electrolysis!)*

## Part F: Solved Examples

**Solved Example 1 (Predicting Conductivity from Solutes).** Predict the behavior of an LED tester when immersed in four separate beakers containing: (a) a spoonful of baking soda in water; (b) a spoonful of sugar in water; (c) a teaspoon of white vinegar in ½ cup of water; (d) pure distilled water.

*Solution:*
- **(a) Baking soda ($\text{NaHCO}_3$):** An ionic salt that dissociates into $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{HCO}_3^-$ ions. Rich in mobile ions → **bright LED glow** (strong electrolyte).
- **(b) Sugar ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$):** A covalent molecular solid that dissolves as neutral molecules without forming ions → **LED remains completely dark** (non-electrolyte).
- **(c) Vinegar ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$):** A weak organic acid that ionises only slightly ($\approx 1\%$) → **LED glows dimly** (weak electrolyte).
- **(d) Distilled water:** High-purity $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ with negligible auto-ionisation ($\approx 10^{-7}\text{ M}$) → **LED remains completely dark** (non-electrolyte in practice).

---

**Solved Example 2 (Counting Dissociated Ions and Verifying Electrical Neutrality).** A sample containing 100 formula units of sodium sulfate ($\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4$) dissolves completely in water.
(a) How many sodium ions ($\text{Na}^+$) and sulfate ions ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) are liberated?
(b) What is the total number of ions produced?
(c) Demonstrate that the resulting solution is electrically neutral.

*Solution:*
(a) Write the balanced dissociation equation:
$$\text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow 2\text{Na}^+ + \text{SO}_4^{2-}$$
Each formula unit releases 2 $\text{Na}^+$ cations and 1 $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ anion.
- Number of $\text{Na}^+$ ions = $100 \times 2 = \mathbf{200}$
- Number of $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ ions = $100 \times 1 = \mathbf{100}$

(b) Total number of mobile ions = $200 + 100 = \mathbf{300\text{ ions}}$.

(c) Net electrical charge check:
- Total positive charge = $200 \times (+1) = +200$ elementary charges
- Total negative charge = $100 \times (-2) = -200$ elementary charges
- Net charge = $+200 + (-200) = 0$.
The solution is strictly electrically neutral! *(Check: $200 + 100 = 300$ ions ✅; $+200 - 200 = 0$ ✅)*

---

**Solved Example 3 (Writing Ionisation Equations and Comparing Arrows).** Write the equation for the formation of ions when nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$) and acetic acid ($\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$) dissolve in water. Explain why their reaction arrows differ.

*Solution:*
- For nitric acid (a strong mineral acid):
$$\text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{H}^+ + \text{NO}_3^-$$
- For acetic acid (a weak organic acid):
$$\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{CH}_3\text{COO}^- + \text{H}^+$$
- **Reason for arrow difference:** Nitric acid ionises virtually 100%, so a single forward arrow ($\rightarrow$) indicates complete, irreversible ionisation. Acetic acid ionises only partially, reaching dynamic equilibrium where ions recombine into neutral molecules at the same rate they form; hence, an equilibrium arrow ($\rightleftharpoons$) is mandatory.

**Solved Example 4 (Quantitative Comparison from Figure 1.2).** Compare the total number of free ions present in the two beakers of Figure 1.2. How many times greater is the ion population in the strong electrolyte beaker than in the weak electrolyte beaker?

*Solution:*
- In the strong electrolyte beaker (10 HCl molecules, 100% ionisation):
$$\text{Total ions} = 10\text{ H}^+ + 10\text{ Cl}^- = 20\text{ ions}$$
- In the weak electrolyte beaker (10 acetic acid molecules, 10% ionisation in model):
$$\text{Total ions} = 1\text{ H}^+ + 1\text{ CH}_3\text{COO}^- = 2\text{ ions}$$
- Ratio of ions:
$$\frac{20}{2} = 10\text{ times}$$
The strong electrolyte solution provides 10 times as many charge carriers, explaining why its LED shines far more brightly under identical testing conditions.

**Solved Example 5 (Why the Current-Limiting Resistor is Essential).** A standard red indicator LED has an operating voltage drop of approximately 2.0 V and a safe operating current of about 15–20 mA. Two fresh 1.5 V AA cells deliver 3.0 V. If a 220 Ω resistor is placed in series, calculate the expected circuit current when the probes are immersed in a concentrated salt solution of negligible resistance. What would happen without this resistor?

*Solution:*
- Voltage drop across the resistor:
$$V_R = V_{\text{battery}} - V_{\text{LED}} = 3.0\text{ V} - 2.0\text{ V} = 1.0\text{ V}$$
- Circuit current using Ohm's Law:
$$I = \frac{V_R}{R} = \frac{1.0\text{ V}}{220\ \Omega} \approx 0.00455\text{ A} \approx 4.5\text{ mA}$$
This current of ~4.5 mA is well within the safe operational range of the LED, producing a clear glow without thermal damage.
- **Without the resistor:** When the probes dip into concentrated saltwater, the solution resistance is extremely low (~a few ohms). The voltage across the bare LED would surge toward 3.0 V, driving an uncontrolled surge of current that would instantly burn out the tiny semiconductor chip inside the LED!

**Solved Example 6 (Identifying an Unknown Liquid).** An unlabelled beaker of clear liquid is connected to the LED tester, and the LED stays completely dark. A teaspoon of solid sodium chloride is added and stirred until dissolved; the LED immediately shines with brilliant brightness. Can we conclude with certainty that the original clear liquid was pure distilled water?

*Solution:*
- The initial darkness proves only that the clear liquid contained **virtually no mobile ions** (it acted as a non-electrolyte).
- Adding sodium chloride provided freely mobile $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, completing the circuit.
- However, the initial liquid could have been distilled water, or it could have been pure ethanol, a 10% sugar solution, or liquid glycerol—all of which are clear non-electrolytic liquids that dissolve salt.
- **Conclusion:** We cannot conclude that the original liquid was distilled water based on conductivity alone; further tests (such as boiling point, density, or odor) are required to confirm its identity.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that any substance that dissolves in water is automatically an electrolyte.**
   *Correction:* Solubility does not equal conductivity! Table sugar (sucrose) dissolves abundantly in water, forming a thick syrup, but because sucrose molecules remain intact and uncharged ($\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$), the syrup contains zero ions and cannot conduct electricity. To be an electrolyte, a solute must produce **freely mobile ions**, not merely dissolve.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking that ions are created only when the electric battery is switched on.**
   *Correction:* In both molten salts and aqueous solutions, mobile ions are already present before the battery is ever attached! In ionic salts, water breaks the crystal lattice; in polar covalent acids, water causes ionisation upon dissolving. The battery does not manufacture ions—it merely creates an electric field that causes existing cations and anions to drift in opposite directions.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing a "strong electrolyte" with a "concentrated solution."**
   *Correction:* "Strong" describes the **extent of ionisation** (a chemical property of the substance), whereas "concentrated" describes the **amount of solute per unit volume**. A 0.001 M solution of hydrochloric acid is extremely dilute, yet it is a 100% strong electrolyte. Pure glacial acetic acid is nearly 100% solute (extremely concentrated), yet it remains a weak electrolyte because only a tiny fraction of its molecules ionises.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing water is a perfect insulator and cannot shock you.**
   *Correction:* While high-purity distilled water has a negligible ion concentration, natural water (tap water, shower water, rainwater) is saturated with dissolved minerals and salts. These free ions make tap water a hazardous conductor of electricity. That is why wet hands near a household mains switch (120 V / 230 V) pose an acute shock hazard!

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming metallic conduction and electrolytic conduction operate by the same mechanism.**
   *Correction:* In solid metals, conduction is carried out strictly by **free delocalised electrons** drifting past fixed metal cations, with **no chemical decomposition** and no physical transfer of matter. In electrolytes, conduction is carried out by **mobile cations and anions** migrating physically through the liquid, resulting in **chemical reactions** at the electrode surfaces. Furthermore, heating a metal decreases its conductivity, whereas heating an electrolyte increases its conductivity!

## Quick Recap

- **Electrolyte:** A compound that conducts electricity in the molten state or in aqueous solution due to the migration of freely mobile ions (acids, alkalis, and soluble salts).
- **Non-Electrolyte:** A substance that does not conduct electricity in the liquid or dissolved state because it consists of neutral molecules with no ions (sugar, glucose, ethanol, glycerol, urea).
- **Electrical Conductivity:** Directly proportional to the concentration of mobile ions in the liquid. Distilled water is virtually non-conducting ($\approx 1$ ion pair in 550 million molecules); tap water conducts slightly due to dissolved mineral ions.
- **Strong vs. Weak Electrolyte:** Strong electrolytes (e.g., $\text{HCl}$, $\text{NaOH}$, $\text{NaCl}$) ionise or dissociate almost completely ($100\%$, single arrow $\rightarrow$). Weak electrolytes (e.g., $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$, $\text{NH}_4\text{OH}$) ionise only partially, establishing dynamic equilibrium ($\rightleftharpoons$). This is an intrinsic property, independent of concentration.
- **Dissociation vs. Ionisation:** Dissociation separates pre-existing ions from an ionic solid lattice ($\text{NaCl} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$). Ionisation generates new ions from polar covalent molecules upon reaction with water ($\text{HCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$). Neither requires an external electric current.
- **Metallic vs. Electrolytic Conduction:** Metals conduct via mobile electrons without chemical change (conductivity drops on heating). Electrolytes conduct via migrating cations and anions with chemical decomposition at electrodes (conductivity rises on heating).
- **Safety in Testing:** Conductivity testers must strictly use low-voltage dry cells ($\le 3\text{ V}$) with a current-limiting resistor. Mains power must never be used with liquids.
