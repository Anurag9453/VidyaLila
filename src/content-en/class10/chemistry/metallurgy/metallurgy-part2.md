# Topic 2: Cleaning the Ore: Concentration

In Topic 1, we saw that raw ore dug out of a mine is always a mixture of the valuable metal-bearing mineral and unwanted **gangue** (soil, sand, and barren rock). In our five-step flow of metallurgy, the step immediately after mining is **concentration**—cleaning the raw ore to remove as much gangue as possible while keeping the valuable mineral. Today we will explore the different ways engineers concentrate ores, and see how each method exploits a specific **physical or chemical difference** between the mineral and the gangue. It is the exact same principle you learned in Class 9: first find how the components differ, then pick the method that uses that difference.

---

## Panning by the Riverbank

During a family trip, Mr. Bennett took his niece Maya to a shallow gravel bar along a mountain stream where a local geological heritage guide was demonstrating traditional gold panning. Several people stood ankle-deep in the clear water, each holding a wide, shallow pan with sloped sides. They scooped up river sand and gravel, dipped the pan just beneath the water, swirled it in a gentle **circular motion**, tilted the rim slightly, and let the water wash the top layer of sand away. Within seconds, the pile of sand in the pan grew smaller and smaller.

"Uncle Dan, what are they doing? Isn't that just ordinary creek sand?" Maya asked. Mr. Bennett smiled. "They're hunting for **tiny specks of native gold**. Streams that flow past gold-bearing rocks carry tiny flakes of gold mixed with huge amounts of sand." Maya leaned in as the guide tilted his pan one last time. Down in the bottom crease of the dark pan gleamed three **tiny, bright yellow flecks**—all the lighter sand had washed away.

"Wait, Uncle Dan—he separated the gold from the sand without a magnet or a filter!" "Exactly," said Mr. Bennett. "This method relies on a **difference in density**. Gold ismore than **seven times denser** than ordinary sand. When you swirl water in the pan, the light sand gets swept over the rim while the heavy gold sinks straight to the bottom. And that is just one way to clean an ore. Depending on what kind of ore a mine produces, engineers choose a matching cleaning method. Let's look at all five of them."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Concentration (ore dressing)** | Removing gangue from raw ore to increase the proportion of the valuable mineral | Separating sand and rock from mined ore |
| **Concentrate** | The enriched ore left after most of the gangue has been removed | The mineral-rich froth skimmed off a flotation tank |
| **Hydraulic washing (gravity separation)** | Using a stream of water to wash away lighter gangue while heavier ore particles settle | Panning for gold; washing haematite |
| **Panning** | Swirling sand and water in a shallow pan to separate dense particles from light sand | Washing river sand for gold flakes |
| **Magnetic separation** | Separating magnetic components from non-magnetic components using a magnet | Separating magnetite from quartz sand |
| **Froth flotation** | Using rising air bubbles in an oily water bath to carry sulphide ore particles up into a surface foam | Sulphide ores: ZnS, PbS, CuFeS₂ |
| **Frothing agent / pine oil** | An oil added to stabilise bubbles into a lasting froth and coat sulphide ore particles | Pine oil |
| **Leaching** | Dissolving the valuable mineral in a suitable chemical solution while leaving the gangue undissolved | Treating bauxite with hot sodium hydroxide |
| **Red mud** | The insoluble reddish-brown waste left behind after leaching bauxite | Residue rich in Fe₂O₃ |

## Part A: The Rule of Separation: "Spot the Difference"

In Topic 1 we wrote our basic equation for mined rock: **raw ore = mineral + gangue**. The sole job of concentration is to strip away the bulk of the gangue. **No free metal is produced yet**—in the concentrate, the metal is still chemically combined (as an oxide, sulphide, or carbonate). We concentrate the ore first so that we do not waste huge amounts of fuel, electricity, and furnace space heating tons of worthless rock in the expensive steps that follow.

Just as in Class 9 Topic 4, we always ask one guiding question: **which property is different between the ore mineral and the gangue?** Five methods, five differences:

| Method | Property Difference Used | Typical Ores Concentrated |
|---|---|---|
| Hand-picking | Visible colour, lustre, and large lump size | Coarse ores with distinct appearance in small batches |
| Hydraulic washing | Density (heavy vs. light) | Native gold; heavy oxide ores like haematite, cassiterite |
| Magnetic separation | Magnetic attraction | Magnetite; (also separating magnetic wolframite from tin ore, by name only) |
| Froth flotation | Wetted by oil vs. wetted by water | Sulphide ores: ZnS, PbS, CuFeS₂ |
| Leaching | Solubility in a specific chemical reagent | Bauxite (using NaOH); gold and silver ores (by name only) |

## Part B: By Hand and by a Stream of Water

### 1. Hand-picking

When the mined ore is broken into **fairly large chunks** and the mineral looks **visibly different in colour or lustre** from the barren rock, workers standing alongside a slow conveyor belt can simply **pick out the waste rock by hand**. In Class 9 you saw the same idea when picking small stones out of dry lentils. It works on the simplest principle—look carefully and sort—but it is practical only for **small-scale operations** or **coarse lumps with obvious visual contrast**.

### 2. Hydraulic Washing (Gravity Separation)

When the ore mineral and the gangue have a **large difference in density**, a rushing stream of water does the sorting. Finely crushed ore is fed onto a **gently sloping, vibrating table with shallow grooves**, and a brisk stream of water flows across it. **The lighter gangue particles are swept away by the water, while the denser mineral grains settle into the grooves.**

Compare the approximate densities (in g/cm³):

| Substance | Density (approx., g/cm³) |
|---|---|
| Quartz (SiO₂, ordinary sand/gangue) | 2.65 |
| Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) | 5.2 |
| Haematite (Fe₂O₃) | 5.3 |
| Cassiterite (tin ore, SnO₂) | 7.0 |
| Galena (PbS) | 7.5 |
| Gold (Au) | 19.3 |

Notice how much denser gold is than quartz sand: 19.3 ÷ 2.65 ≈ **7.3 times denser**. That is why gold flakes stay pinned to the bottom crease of a pan while the sand washes away. Even between quartz (2.65) and haematite (5.3), the ore is roughly twice as dense as the gangue, making hydraulic washing effective for heavy oxide ores like haematite and cassiterite (SnO₂).

```
        lighter sand washes over the rim with the water
                  ~~~~>  ~~~~>
         ______________________________
        /  ~    ~    ~    ~    ~    ~   \      <- water
       /   . .  . .  . .  . .  . .      \     <- light sand (SiO₂)
      /     .  .   .   .   .   .   .     \
     /______________________________* * *_\    <- heavy grains (gold) at the bottom
          the pan is tilted slightly and swirled in circles
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Panning (a small-scale version of hydraulic washing): swirling water in a tilted pan washes the lighter sand grains (`.`) over the edge while the much denser particles (`*`) settle at the bottom corner.

> **Activity 2.1 (At home with an adult): Try Your Own Panning.**
> Outdoors (on a patio, balcony, or in a garden), take a wide, shallow basin or pan. Put two or three handfuls of clean, dry sand into it and mix in **5 or 6 small steel nuts or bolts** (small, dense objects—do not use glass beads, as glass has nearly the same density as sand). Add enough water to submerge the sand, then gently swirl the pan in circles while tilting one edge slightly. Watch how the lighter sand washes out over the rim with the water while the heavy steel nuts stay trapped at the bottom. When finished, empty the wet sand into the garden (never down a household drain, as sand can clog pipes) and wash your hands. Keep fingers away from eyes and mouth.

## Part C: By Magnet (Magnetic Separation)

In Class 9 you separated iron filings from sand using a magnet. Industrial **magnetic separation** scales that exact idea up to process tons of crushed rock. When either the ore mineral **or** the gangue is attracted to a magnet while the other is not, the crushed ore is fed onto a moving conveyor belt that passes over a **magnetic roller**.

- **Magnetite** (Fe₃O₄) is strongly magnetic, whereas its usual gangue (mainly silica sand) is non-magnetic. Passing crushed magnetite ore over a magnetic pulley separates it cleanly.
- **Haematite** (Fe₂O₃) is also an iron ore, but it is only weakly magnetic; separating it requires high-intensity magnets, often combined with hydraulic washing.
- Sometimes the **reverse** happens: **tin ore** (cassiterite, SnO₂) is non-magnetic, but it often occurs mixed with a magnetic impurity called **wolframite** (a tungsten mineral). Here, the magnetic roller pulls away the magnetic impurity and leaves the non-magnetic tin ore behind (you only need to know this example by name).

```
        crushed ore (magnetite + gangue)
                   :
                   v
        =================================( M )   <- magnetic roller
           moving conveyor belt ----->     :  \
                                           :    \   gangue (non-magnetic):
      magnetite (magnetic): clings to      :      \  flies straight off the roller
      the roller briefly, then drops       :        \ into the far bin
                                           v          v
                                     +-----------+ +----------+
                                     : MAGNETITE : :  GANGUE  :
                                     +-----------+ +----------+
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Magnetic separation: the front roller of the conveyor belt contains a magnet. Non-magnetic gangue flies forward off the belt into the outer bin, while magnetic ore clings to the roller a little longer and drops into the inner bin directly underneath.

> **Activity 2.2 (At home with an adult): Magnetic Sample Sorting.**
> You already separated iron nails from sand in Class 9. This time, mix a little dry sand on a paper plate with **three different metals**: steel paperclips, tiny balls of crumpled aluminium foil, and short snippets of copper wire (cut safely by an adult). Wrap a magnet in a thin plastic bag or sheet of paper and sweep it just above the mixture: **only the steel paperclips jump up to the magnet**; the aluminium foil and copper wire stay behind with the sand. Test a steel nail and a piece of aluminium foil one by one with the magnet: steel sticks, aluminium does not. This shows why magnetic separation works only when the ore (or its specific impurity) is magnetic. Keep magnets away from electronics, credit cards, and young children.

## Part D: Froth Flotation (The Foam Method)

For **sulphide ores**—such as **zinc blende (ZnS), galena (PbS), and copper pyrites (CuFeS₂)**—engineers use a remarkably clever surface-chemistry trick. Sulphide minerals have a special surface property: their particles are **preferentially wetted by oil rather than water**, whereas gangue particles (silica sand and clay) are **wetted by water** rather than oil. **Froth flotation** is built entirely on this contrast.

How the process works (for reading and understanding only—this is carried out in industrial flotation cells):

1. The raw ore is ground into a **fine powder**.
2. The powder is mixed with **water** in a large tank to form a slurry.
3. A small amount of **pine oil** (or a similar collector/frothing agent) is added; the oil coats the sulphide ore particles and helps create stable bubbles.
4. A rotating paddle agitates the mixture while **compressed air** is blown in from the bottom.
5. The oil-coated **sulphide ore particles** attach to the rising air bubbles and float to the top in a thick layer of **froth**. The water-wetted **gangue particles** sink to the bottom of the tank.
6. The mineral-rich froth is skimmed off the top and dried: that is the **concentrate**.

```
                 rising bubbles carry ore grains into the froth
                      O o O o O o O o
                    O o *O o *O o O *o O
        ============================== +--> froth skimmed off (concentrate)
        :  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  :
        :  ~ *o ~  ~ *o ~  ~ *o ~  ~   :        * = sulphide ore grain
        :  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  ~  :        o = air bubble
        :  #  ~  #  ~  #  ~  ~  #  ~   :       # = gangue grain
        :_#__#__#___#__#__#__#___#__#_:
              ^     ^     ^     ^
              compressed air blown in from below
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Froth flotation: finely ground ore, water, and pine oil are churned with rising air bubbles. Oil-wetted sulphide ore grains (`*`) cling to air bubbles (`o`) and rise into the surface froth, while water-wetted gangue grains (`#`) sink to the bottom.

Notice two important details here: first, the ore must be **ground into a fine powder** so the individual mineral grains are light enough for air bubbles to lift. Second, the method works because the **surface wettability** (affinity for oil vs. water) of sulphide ores and silicate gangue is opposite. This process allows mining plants to separate thousands of tons of zinc, lead, and copper sulphide ores every day.

## Part E: Leaching (Dissolving, or Sorting with a Chemical)

Sometimes the mineral and the gangue have **no big difference in density**, neither is magnetic, and neither is a sulphide that floats on oily bubbles. In such cases, chemists use a **chemical solvent** that **dissolves the valuable ore mineral while leaving the gangue undissolved**. This process is called **leaching**. Filtering the mixture then separates the dissolved mineral (in the liquid filtrate) from the solid gangue (left on the filter).

### Bauxite and Sodium Hydroxide (The Amphoteric Story from Chapter 2)

The main ore of aluminium is **bauxite** (Al₂O₃·2H₂O). Raw bauxite is mixed with gangue consisting mainly of **iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃)**—which gives bauxite its reddish-brown colour—and silica. Recall from Chapter 2 ([metals-and-non-metals-part2.md](../metals-and-non-metals/metals-and-non-metals-part2.md)) that **Al₂O₃ is amphoteric**: it reacts not only with acids, but also with strong bases like sodium hydroxide (NaOH). That gives us a neat way to clean bauxite: digest the crushed ore in hot, **concentrated** sodium hydroxide solution.

Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O

The sodium aluminate (NaAlO₂) formed is **soluble in water**. In contrast, **Fe₂O₃ is a basic oxide**, not amphoteric: it does not react with NaOH and **does not dissolve**. When the mixture is filtered, this **insoluble reddish-brown waste** stays behind on the filter as **red mud**, while the clear solution of sodium aluminate passes through.

Hot concentrated sodium hydroxide solution is **strongly corrosive and hazardous**: this process is **for study only** and is carried out in industrial plants by trained operators wearing full protective gear. The complete story of aluminium—how pure Al₂O₃ is recovered from the solution and electrolysed—will be covered in **Topic 5**.

### Leaching of Gold: By Name Only, and a Poison Warning

Low-grade gold and silver ores are sometimes concentrated in industry by leaching with a dilute **cyanide solution**. **Cyanide compounds are deadly poisons—even a tiny amount can be fatal.** Consequently, this operation is performed only inside strictly monitored industrial facilities, and **for students you only need to know that gold can be concentrated by chemical leaching**: no equations and no laboratory activities are ever done at school level.

## Part F: Solved Examples

**Solved Example 1 (Choosing the right method).** Below are three ores and their gangue. State which concentration method works best for each.

| Ore | Gangue | Given Property Difference |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc blende (ZnS) | Quartz (SiO₂) | ZnS is wetted by pine oil; quartz is wetted by water |
| Magnetite (Fe₃O₄) | Silica sand | Magnetite is attracted to a magnet; silica sand is not |
| Cassiterite (SnO₂, density 7.0) | Quartz (density 2.65) | Large difference in density (about 2.6 times) |

- **ZnS and quartz:** The difference is oil-wettability vs. water-wettability, so use **froth flotation**.
- **Fe₃O₄ and silica sand:** The difference is magnetic attraction, so use **magnetic separation**.
- **SnO₂ and quartz:** The difference is density (7.0 ÷ 2.65 ≈ 2.64), so use **hydraulic washing**.

**Solved Example 2 (How much gangue is removed by flotation?).** A 1500 kg batch of raw galena ore contains 8% PbS and the rest gangue. After froth flotation, the concentrate contains 64% PbS. Assuming all the PbS is recovered in the concentrate, find the mass of the concentrate and the mass of gangue removed.

- Mass of PbS = 8% of 1500 = **120 kg**. Total initial gangue = 1500 − 120 = **1380 kg**.
- Since PbS makes up 64% of the concentrate, mass of concentrate = 120 ÷ 0.64 = **187.5 kg**.
- Gangue remaining in the concentrate = 187.5 − 120 = **67.5 kg**.
- **Gangue removed** from the raw ore = 1380 − 67.5 = **1312.5 kg**.
- Transporting the concentrate instead of the raw ore means hauling **1500 ÷ 187.5 = 8 times** less mass!
- *(Check: 120 + 67.5 = 187.5, and 120 ÷ 187.5 = 0.64 ✅; 1312.5 + 187.5 = 1500 ✅)*

Notice that even after concentration, the concentrate is **64% PbS**, not 100%: a small amount of gangue still tags along and will be dealt with in later stages.

**Solved Example 3 (Magnetic separation math).** A 500 kg sample of crushed ore contains 60% magnetite. A magnetic separator picks up all the magnetite, along with 10 kg of gangue that gets trapped with the magnetic grains. Find the mass of the concentrate and the percentage of magnetite in it.

- Mass of magnetite = 60% of 500 = **300 kg**. Total gangue = 200 kg.
- Mass of concentrate = 300 + 10 = **310 kg**.
- Percentage of magnetite in concentrate = 300 ÷ 310 × 100 ≈ **96.8%**.
- Gangue separated out = 200 − 10 = **190 kg**.
- *(Check: 190 + 310 = 500 ✅)*

**Solved Example 4 (Identify the method).** A student reads this description of an ore-cleaning step: "The finely ground ore is heated with concentrated NaOH solution; the useful oxide dissolves into the liquid while a reddish-brown solid is filtered off." Which concentration method is being used?

**Leaching.** A chemical reagent selectively **dissolved** the ore mineral while leaving the gangue behind as an insoluble solid. This is the bauxite story: amphoteric Al₂O₃ dissolved, while basic Fe₂O₃ remained as red mud.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **"After concentration, we have obtained the pure metal."** Incorrect. Look at Solved Example 2: the flotation concentrate is 64% PbS. PbS is a **sulphide compound**, not metallic lead (Pb). Although lead makes up 207 ÷ (207 + 32) = 207 ÷ 239 ≈ 86.6% of pure PbS by mass, those lead atoms are still **chemically bonded to sulphur**. Extracting the free metal requires further chemical steps (Topics 3 and 4).
- **"Froth flotation works for every kind of ore."** Incorrect. It is designed primarily for **sulphide ores**, whose surfaces cling to oil. Quartz sand is wetted by water, not oil—if you put quartz in a flotation tank, it sinks to the bottom instead of rising with the froth. Every ore requires a method matched to its own properties.
- **"A magnet can separate any metallic ore from gangue."** Incorrect. A magnet attracts only magnetic minerals (such as iron's Fe₃O₄). As you saw in Activity 2.2, a steel paperclip jumps to a magnet, but aluminium foil and copper wire do not. Similarly, zinc blende (ZnS) and galena (PbS) cannot be separated using a magnet.
- **"In hydraulic washing, the lighter particles settle to the bottom."** Backwards! In panning and gravity separation, the **denser** particles (like gold or haematite) stay at the bottom while the **lighter** sand and clay wash away with the flowing water (Figure 2.1).
- **"In leaching, the chemical dissolves the gangue and leaves the ore behind."** Usually the exact opposite! In the leaching of bauxite, the **ore mineral (Al₂O₃) dissolves** into the NaOH solution while the gangue (Fe₂O₃ in red mud) stays behind on the filter.

## Quick Recap

- **Concentration** = removing gangue from raw ore. The metal is still in compound form inside the concentrate.
- Each method relies on a **property difference**: hand-picking (colour/appearance of large lumps), hydraulic washing (density), magnetic separation (magnetism), froth flotation (wetting by oil vs. water, for sulphide ores), and leaching (selective chemical solubility).
- Gold is more than **7 times** denser than quartz sand—that is why panning works.
- Magnetite is strongly magnetic; haematite is weakly magnetic. Magnetic wolframite in tin ore is known by name only.
- **Froth flotation:** finely powdered ore + water + pine oil + rising air bubbles. Sulphide ore rises in the froth; gangue sinks. Used for zinc blende, galena, and copper pyrites.
- **Leaching of bauxite:** Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O; insoluble Fe₂O₃ and other impurities form **red mud** (thanks to Al₂O₃ being amphoteric).
- Cyanide leaching of gold is **by name only**—cyanide is extremely poisonous.
- Panning and magnet sorting can be modelled safely at home with an adult; industrial flotation, hot NaOH digestion, and cyanide leaching are **for reading only**.

**What's next?** We now have a cleaned **concentrate**—rich in PbS, ZnS, ZnCO₃, or hydrated Al₂O₃·2H₂O. However, the metal is still locked up as a **sulphide, carbonate, or hydrated oxide**, whereas the next major stage (reduction) works best on a dry **metal oxide**. In Topic 3, we will see how concentrates are **heated to convert them into oxides**—sometimes in **plenty of air (roasting)** and sometimes in **limited or no air (calcination)**—and how plants deal with a toxic, choking gas released during roasting.
