# Topic 3: Heating to Form an Oxide: Roasting and Calcination

In the previous topic, we **cleaned** the raw ore by removing most of the gangue, leaving us with **concentrated ore**—a heap rich in compounds such as ZnS, PbS, or ZnCO₃. Yet the metal is still **chemically locked inside a compound**. In the five-step flow of metallurgy (mining, concentration, **conversion to oxide**, reduction, refining), we now reach Stage 3: **heating the concentrated ore to turn it into a metal oxide**. Why an oxide? Because the reduction methods we will use in Topic 4 work far more easily on **oxides** than on sulphides or carbonates. Today we will compare the two ways of heating an ore—**roasting** (with plenty of air) and **calcination** (with limited or no air)—and see what modern factories do with a **pungent, toxic gas** produced during roasting.

---

## Fool's Gold and a Sharp, Choking Smell

At the school science fair, Mr. Rivera walked into the chemistry hall with his nephew Lucas. Outside one demonstration booth stood a bold sign: **"Is This Real Gold?"** On the counter lay glittering, brassy-yellow cubes of rock with sharp, mirror-flat faces. Lucas's eyes went wide.

"Uncle Leo, look at all that gold! Whoever found that rock must be a millionaire!"

Ms. Bennett, the chemistry teacher running the booth, smiled. "That isn't gold at all, Lucas. Prospectors call it **fool's gold**, and its chemical name is **iron pyrites** (FeS₂). It looks remarkably like gold, but chemically it is completely different." She picked up a pair of tongs. "Wait right here—I'll show you why it isn't gold. Everyone take **three steps back**, please, and keep the area near the exhaust hood clear."

Inside the glass **fume hood** (equipped with an extractor fan), Ms. Bennett placed a **tiny pinch** of powdered pyrites into a hard-glass test tube and heated it over a burner. Within moments, the brassy powder turned dull reddish-brown, and a **sharp, choking, acrid smell**—like a hundred刚-struck match heads all at once—wafted briefly near the hood sash before the fan pulled it away. Lucas wrinkled his nose and stepped back.

"Whoa, what is that smell?" he coughed. "Real gold wouldn't do that when heated, would it?"

"Not at all," Mr. Rivera said. "Real gold doesn't react with air even at red heat, just as you learned in Chapter 2. This mineral changed colour and gave off a gas because it contains **sulphur**. When heated in air, the sulphur combined with oxygen to form **sulphur dioxide** gas—the exact same gas you studied in Class 9 when learning about acid rain. And here is the fascinating part: **what Ms. Bennett just did in a tiny test tube is done on a massive scale inside metallurgical plants**, not on fool's gold, but on sulphide ores of zinc, lead, and copper."

Lucas was still rubbing his nose. "If a tiny pinch smells that strong, how do people breathe near a giant factory?"

"That is one of our big questions for today," Mr. Rivera replied. "Let's see **why metallurgists heat ores**, the two different ways they do it, and how factories capture and use that choking gas."

> **Safety Warning:** Heating iron pyrites is **strictly a teacher demonstration inside a working fume hood**. Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) is a **toxic, irritating gas** that burns the eyes, throat, and lungs. Students must **never** heat any sulphide ore or mineral at home or in an open laboratory.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Roasting** | Heating a concentrated ore strongly in an **excess of air** (oxygen) below its melting point to convert it into a metal oxide | Converting ZnS to ZnO |
| **Calcination** | Heating a carbonate or hydrated ore strongly in **limited or no air** below its melting point so it decomposes into a metal oxide and a volatile gas | Converting ZnCO₃ to ZnO |
| **Sulphide ore** | An ore in which the metal is combined with sulphur | ZnS, PbS, Cu₂S |
| **Carbonate ore** | An ore in which the metal is combined with the carbonate group (CO₃) | ZnCO₃, MgCO₃ |
| **Hydrated ore** | An ore that contains chemically bound water molecules (water of crystallisation) | Bauxite, Al₂O₃·2H₂O |
| **Sulphur dioxide (SO₂)** | A colourless, choking, toxic gas formed when sulphur or a sulphide burns in air | Gas released during roasting |
| **Furnace** | An enclosed industrial chamber lined with heat-resistant bricks for heating ores to high temperatures | Roasting furnace |

## Part A: What Did Pyrites Do? An Equation

In Ms. Bennett's fume-hood demonstration, iron pyrites (FeS₂) reacted with oxygen from the air. The products were a **reddish-brown solid oxide** (iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃) and that **choking gas** (SO₂):

4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂

Atom count (Check): Fe: 4 = 4; S: 8 = 8; O: 22 = 6 + 16 = 22 ✅.

Notice that two things happened at once: the sulphur combined with oxygen to escape as a gas, and the iron combined with oxygen to remain behind as a solid oxide. Neither could happen **without a supply of air**. This is the exact chemistry of **roasting**.

**How to tell real gold from fool's gold without heating:** Real gold is **soft and malleable** (it dents and flattens under a hammer), whereas iron pyrites is **hard and brittle** (it shatters into powder when struck). Geologists also use a simple, completely safe test called the **streak test**.

### Safe Activity: The Streak Test (In the school lab with a teacher)

Your teacher takes the rough, **unglazed back of a white ceramic tile**. When a piece of iron pyrites is rubbed firmly across the unglazed porcelain, it leaves a **greenish-black powder trail** on the tile. Real gold leaves a **bright golden-yellow streak**. This powder trail is called the mineral's **streak**. **What you observe:** even though both minerals look brassy yellow in the hand, their streaks are completely different. Do this in a well-ventilated room, do not blow on or inhale the powder, and wash your hands afterwards. Never heat the pyrites.

## Part B: Two Methods, One Goal: Forming the Oxide

Concentrated ores that are not already dry oxides usually fall into three categories: **sulphides**, **carbonates**, and **hydrated oxides**. In all three cases, our goal is to obtain the dry **metal oxide**, but the method depends on the type of ore.

### 1. Roasting: Sulphide Ore + Excess Air

A finely powdered **sulphide ore** is heated strongly in a furnace with a **plentiful, continuous supply of air (oxygen)**. The sulphur reacts with oxygen and escapes as SO₂ gas, while the metal combines with oxygen to form the metal oxide:

2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂   (zinc blende to zinc oxide)

2PbS + 3O₂ → 2PbO + 2SO₂   (galena to lead(II) oxide)

2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂   (copper(I) sulphide to copper(I) oxide)

2HgS + 3O₂ → 2HgO + 2SO₂   (cinnabar to mercury(II) oxide; **for reading only**—mercury and its compounds are highly toxic)

Atom count (Check): ZnS: Zn 2 = 2, S 2 = 2, O 6 = 2 + 4 = 6 ✅. PbS: Pb 2 = 2, S 2 = 2, O 6 = 2 + 4 = 6 ✅. Cu₂S: Cu 4 = 4, S 2 = 2, O 6 = 2 + 4 = 6 ✅. HgS: Hg 2 = 2, S 2 = 2, O 6 = 2 + 4 = 6 ✅.

Notice that **all four equations follow the exact same pattern**: sulphide + O₂ → metal oxide + SO₂. Once you understand one, you can write the others immediately. (A small practical detail: if the air supply or temperature is not carefully controlled, part of the sulphide can sometimes oxidise into a metal sulphate such as ZnSO₄ instead—one reason engineers monitor furnace temperature and airflow closely.)

Roasting brings two extra benefits: first, **volatile impurities** present in the ore (such as free sulphur or arsenic compounds) are oxidised and driven off as gases. Second, the burning of sulphides is **exothermic** (it releases heat), so once roasting starts in a furnace, the reaction helps sustain its own high temperature with very little extra fuel.

### 2. Calcination: Carbonate or Hydrated Ore + Limited or No Air

When a **carbonate ore** or a **hydrated oxide ore** is heated strongly, it **breaks down (decomposes)** on its own: a carbonate releases **carbon dioxide (CO₂)** gas, and a hydrated ore releases **water vapour (steam, H₂O)**. Behind remains the dry **metal oxide**. Because no outside oxygen is needed for this breakdown, the heating is done in **limited air or in the absence of air**:

ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂   (calamine to zinc oxide)

Al₂O₃·2H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 2H₂O   (hydrated bauxite to anhydrous alumina and steam)

CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂   (limestone to quicklime—**the exact same lime-kiln reaction from Class 9**, [chemical-changes-and-reactions-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part2.md), now applied to metallurgy!)

Atom count (Check): ZnCO₃: Zn 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ✅. Al₂O₃·2H₂O: Al 2 = 2, O 5 = 3 + 2 = 5, H 4 = 4 ✅. CaCO₃: Ca 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ✅.

Chemically, calcination is simply **thermal decomposition** (Class 9): heat breaks a single compound into simpler ones. In metallurgy, we use it specifically to drive off CO₂ or water of crystallisation and leave behind a porous metal oxide.

### Teacher Demonstration: A Look at Calcination (For observation only)

In a well-ventilated laboratory (or fume hood), your teacher heats a small sample of **green copper(II) carbonate** (CuCO₃) in a hard-glass test tube fitted with a delivery tube that dips into a second tube of **clear lime water**. **Before** turning off the burner at the end, the teacher lifts the delivery tube out of the lime water so cold liquid cannot suck back into the hot glass tube.

CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂

**What you observe:** The bright green powder turns **black** (forming black copper(II) oxide, CuO), and the bubbles passing through the lime water turn it **milky**, proving that CO₂ gas was released (Atom count: Cu 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 ✅). Because copper compounds are harmful if swallowed or inhaled, students observe from a safe distance wearing safety goggles, do not touch the powders, and wash hands after class.

## Part C: Roasting and Calcination: Side by Side

| Feature | Roasting | Calcination |
|---|---|---|
| Type of ore used | Sulphide ores (ZnS, PbS, Cu₂S) | Carbonate and hydrated ores (ZnCO₃, Al₂O₃·2H₂O) |
| Air supply | **Excess air** (oxygen is required as a reactant) | **Limited or no air** (oxygen is not required) |
| What happens chemically | Sulphur burns in oxygen; metal forms its oxide | The ore decomposes by heat, driving off gas or steam |
| Gas released | **SO₂** (toxic, sharp choking smell) | **CO₂** or **water vapour (H₂O)** (odourless, non-choking) |
| Type of reaction | Oxidation (reaction with O₂) | Thermal decomposition (breaking down by heat) |
| Solid product left behind | Metal oxide | Metal oxide |
| Main purpose | Convert sulphide to oxide; drive off volatile impurities | Convert carbonate/hydrate to oxide; drive off CO₂ or moisture |

```
   ROASTING                                  CALCINATION
   (sulphide ore)                            (carbonate or hydrated ore)

   +------------------------+                +------------------------+
   :                        :                :                        :
   :   ZnS  (powder)        :                :   ZnCO₃  (crushed)     :
   :                        :                :                        :
   :   heated strongly      :                :   heated strongly      :
   +------------------------+                +------------------------+
        ^   ^   ^                                   (air: LIMITED / NONE)
        :   :   :
     air (O₂) IN EXCESS                      heat supplied from outside
        :   :   :                                   :
        v   v   v                                   v
   ZnO  +  SO₂ gas                           ZnO  +  CO₂ gas
   (toxic, choking smell)                    (odourless, turns lime water milky)
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Comparing roasting and calcination: in roasting, **excess air (O₂)** is blown in and toxic SO₂ gas is given off; in calcination, **little or no air** is used and CO₂ (or steam) is driven off. Both processes leave behind a **metal oxide**.

> **Quick Check:** If heating an ore in air produces a sharp, choking gas that smells like burning matches, the ore is a **sulphide** and the process is **roasting**. ✅ Because sulphur is burning in oxygen to form SO₂.

## Part D: What Does the Furnace Look Like Inside?

Industrial roasting is carried out inside large furnaces. In its simplest traditional form (a reverberatory-style hearth furnace), finely powdered ore is spread in a **thin layer across a flat floor (the hearth)**. **Hot gases** sweep across the top, **air (O₂)** is blown in over the bed of ore, and the resulting **SO₂ gas** exits through an overhead **flue (chimney)** leading to a gas-treatment plant. Many modern plants use a **fluidised-bed roaster**, where air is blown upward through the perforated floor so briskly that the hot ore powder swirls and bubbles like a boiling liquid, giving every grain instant contact with oxygen. (You only need to know this by name.)

```
               powdered ore from hopper
                      :
                      v
            +----------------------+
   hot      :  ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~   :     SO₂ gas out
   gases -> :  thin layer of ore   : --->  through flue
            :  =================   :     (treated next: Part F)
            :   HEARTH (floor)     :
            +----------------------+
                  ^   ^   ^   ^
                  :   :   :   :
              air (O₂) blown in
                      :
                      v
            roasted ore (metal oxide)
              withdrawn at the bottom
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Simplified diagram of a roasting furnace: powdered ore drops from a hopper onto the hearth in a thin bed, hot gases and air pass over it, SO₂ gas exits through the flue for capture, and the solid metal oxide is collected below. (Real industrial furnaces are much larger and automated.)

For students, industrial furnaces are **for reading only**: ores are never roasted outside properly engineered facilities equipped with gas scrubbers.

## Part E: Why Must We Convert to an Oxide First?

There are three clear reasons why metallurgists convert sulphides and carbonates into oxides before reduction:

1. **Oxides are much easier to reduce.** As we will see in Topic 4, cheap reducing agents like carbon (coke) and carbon monoxide readily strip oxygen away from a **metal oxide**. They do not reduce metal sulphides effectively (carbon and sulphur do not form a stable, easily removed gas the way carbon and oxygen form CO and CO₂). Thus, zinc blende (ZnS) is always roasted to **ZnO** before reduction with carbon.
2. **Volatile impurities are removed.** During roasting, sulphur escapes as gas along with volatile impurities like arsenic and antimony oxides; during calcination, CO₂ and bound water are driven off. Removing these leaves behind a **cleaner, more metal-rich oxide**.
3. **The solid becomes porous.** As bubbles of SO₂, CO₂, or steam escape from the heated lumps, they leave behind a network of tiny pores, making the resulting metal oxide **porous and crumbly** so reducing gases can penetrate it easily in Stage 4.

Remember one crucial point: **forming the oxide does not mean we have obtained the free metal yet.** Inside ZnO or PbO, the metal is **still chemically bonded to oxygen**. Removing that oxygen is the job of Topic 4.

Also recall from Chapter 2 that ZnO and Al₂O₃ are **amphoteric** oxides (they react with both acids and bases). In Topic 5, pure anhydrous **Al₂O₃** obtained by calcination will be the exact feed for extracting aluminium metal.

## Part F: What Should Be Done with SO₂? Three Paths

Roasting sulphide ores generates **enormous quantities of SO₂ gas**. As you will calculate in Example 1 below, roasting 970 kg of ZnS produces **640 kg of SO₂**. A large zinc or copper smelter can produce hundreds of tonnes of SO₂ every single day. Venting this gas straight into the atmosphere is **unacceptable** because:

- SO₂ is **toxic and choking**: it irritates and damages the eyes, throat, and lungs (Class 9: atmospheric pollution).
- In the atmosphere, SO₂ oxidises to SO₃ and dissolves in rainwater to form **sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)**, falling as **acid rain** that corrodes limestone buildings and statues, acidifies lakes, and harms forests and crops ([atmospheric-pollution-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/atmospheric-pollution/atmospheric-pollution-part2.md)):

2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃,   SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄

Atom count (Check): S: 2 = 2, O: 4 + 2 = 6 = 6 ✅; S: 1 = 1, O: 3 + 1 = 4 = 4, H: 2 = 2 ✅.

Modern metallurgical plants handle SO₂ using three main measures:

1. **Trapping the gas in scrubbers:** Exhaust gases are passed through wet **scrubbers** containing slaked lime or crushed limestone (CaCO₃), which reacts with SO₂ to lock it up as a **solid salt** (calcium sulphite, CaSO₃, or calcium sulphate, CaSO₄) so it cannot escape into the air—the exact same scrubber chemistry you saw in Class 9 power plants: SO₂ + CaCO₃ → CaSO₃ + CO₂ (Atom count: S 1 = 1, Ca 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 2 + 3 = 5 = 3 + 2 = 5 ✅).
2. **Turning a pollutant into a valuable product:** Better yet, smelters pipe the captured SO₂ directly into an on-site **sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄) plant**. Sulphuric acid is one of the world's most important **industrial chemicals**, used to manufacture fertilisers, car batteries, detergents, and dyes. You will study its full manufacture in **Chapter 9**; for now, appreciate the green-engineering principle: turning a dangerous waste gas into a useful resource.
3. **Continuous monitoring and emission controls:** Chimneys are fitted with sensors that monitor SO₂ levels continuously to ensure emissions stay within strict legal limits.

```
                    SO₂ gas (from roasting furnace)
                          :
         +----------------+----------------+
         :                :                :
         v                v                v
   Vented straight    Captured in a      Piped to an acid plant
   into the air       lime scrubber      to make sulphuric acid
   (WRONG)            (GOOD)             (BEST: waste to wealth)
         :                :                :
         v                v                v
   Harmful to lungs;  Trapped as solid   Produces H₂SO₄
   causes acid rain   salts (CaSO₃/      (for fertilisers,
   that damages       CaSO₄); air        batteries, industry)
   lakes & stone      stays clean
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Three possible fates for SO₂ from roasting: venting it into the open air (causes respiratory harm and acid rain—unacceptable), trapping it in a limestone scrubber, or converting it into valuable sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄).

> **Quick Check:** "Calcination also releases large amounts of sulphur dioxide that must be scrubbed." ❌ False—calcination heats carbonate or hydrated ores, releasing CO₂ or steam (H₂O), not SO₂. SO₂ comes from roasting sulphide ores.

## Part G: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Roasting of Zinc Blende

**Question:** 970 kg of pure ZnS is completely roasted in air (2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂). What mass of ZnO and what mass of SO₂ are formed, and what mass of oxygen is consumed? (Zn = 65, S = 32, O = 16.)

**Solution:**
- Formula masses: ZnS = 65 + 32 = **97**; ZnO = 65 + 16 = **81**; SO₂ = 32 + 32 = **64**; O₂ = **32**.
- From the balanced equation: 2 × 97 = **194** parts ZnS : 3 × 32 = **96** parts O₂ : 2 × 81 = **162** parts ZnO : 2 × 64 = **128** parts SO₂.
- Scaling factor: 970 ÷ 194 = **5**. Therefore: ZnO = 5 × 162 = **810 kg**; SO₂ = 5 × 128 = **640 kg**; O₂ used = 5 × 96 = **480 kg**.

Check conservation of mass: 970 + 480 = 1450 kg; 810 + 640 = 1450 kg ✅.

**Takeaway:** 970 kg of ZnS yields **810 kg** of ZnO and **640 kg** of SO₂. Notice something interesting: the solid product (810 kg of ZnO) weighs **less** than the starting solid (970 kg of ZnS) even though oxygen was added! That is because each heavier sulphur atom (mass 32) was replaced by a lighter oxygen atom (mass 16) as the sulphur escaped as SO₂ gas.

### Example 2. Galena and Sulphuric Acid Recovery

**Question:** 478 kg of pure galena (PbS) is roasted (2PbS + 3O₂ → 2PbO + 2SO₂). (a) What mass of PbO is formed? (b) What mass of SO₂ is released? (c) If all the SO₂ is converted into sulphuric acid (every 64 kg of SO₂ yields 98 kg of H₂SO₄), what mass of H₂SO₄ is produced? (Pb = 207, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Solution:**
- Formula masses: PbS = 207 + 32 = **239**; PbO = 207 + 16 = **223**; SO₂ = **64**.
- Equation mass ratio: 2 × 239 = **478** (PbS) : 2 × 223 = **446** (PbO) : 2 × 64 = **128** (SO₂). Here 478 kg matches the equation numbers with a scaling factor of 1.
- (a) Mass of PbO = **446 kg**. (b) Mass of SO₂ = **128 kg**.
- (c) Scaling factor for acid = 128 ÷ 64 = 2, so mass of H₂SO₄ = 2 × 98 = **196 kg** (since H₂SO₄ = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98).

Check conservation of mass in roasting: O₂ used = 3 × 32 = 96 kg; 478 + 96 = 574 kg, and 446 + 128 = 574 kg ✅.

**Takeaway:** 478 kg of galena gives 446 kg of lead(II) oxide **plus** 196 kg of valuable sulphuric acid—turning a hazardous pollutant into a useful product. (Lead and its compounds are toxic; this is purely a paper calculation.)

### Example 3. Calcination of Calamine

**Question:** 250 kg of pure calamine (ZnCO₃) is calcined (ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂). What mass of ZnO is obtained and what mass of CO₂ is driven off? (Zn = 65, C = 12, O = 16.)

**Solution:** Formula masses: ZnCO₃ = 65 + 12 + 48 = **125**; ZnO = **81**; CO₂ = 12 + 32 = **44**. Mass ratio = 125 : 81 : 44. Scaling factor = 250 ÷ 125 = **2**. Thus, ZnO = 2 × 81 = **162 kg**; CO₂ = 2 × 44 = **88 kg**.

Check: 162 + 88 = 250 kg ✅.

**Takeaway:** 250 kg of calamine yields **162 kg** of ZnO. Notice that the ZnO obtained by roasting ZnS (Example 1) and the ZnO obtained by calcining ZnCO₃ (Example 3) are the **exact same compound**—they simply took different paths to get there.

### Example 4. Driving Water Out of a Hydrated Ore

**Question:** 207 kg of pure hydrated alumina (Al₂O₃·2H₂O) is calcined. What mass of anhydrous Al₂O₃ is obtained, what mass of steam escapes, and by what percentage does the mass of the solid decrease? (Al = 27, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Solution:** Formula masses: Al₂O₃·2H₂O = 102 + 36 = **138**; Al₂O₃ = **102**; 2H₂O = **36**. Scaling factor = 207 ÷ 138 = **1.5**. Mass of Al₂O₃ = 1.5 × 102 = **153 kg**; mass of steam = 1.5 × 36 = **54 kg**.

Check: 153 + 54 = 207 kg ✅. Percentage loss in solid mass = 36 ÷ 138 × 100 ≈ **26.1%**.

**Takeaway:** 207 kg of hydrated bauxite mineral loses 54 kg of water vapour to leave **153 kg** of dry Al₂O₃—a **26.1%** reduction in solid mass.

### Example 5. Roasting or Calcination? Reasoning Task

**Question:** Four concentrated ores are brought to a plant: (a) PbS, (b) ZnCO₃, (c) Cu₂S, (d) Al₂O₃·2H₂O. State whether each should undergo roasting or calcination, and give a one-line reason.

**Solution:**

| Ore | Type of Ore | Process | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| PbS | Sulphide | Roasting | Needs oxygen to burn off sulphur as SO₂ and form PbO |
| ZnCO₃ | Carbonate | Calcination | Decomposes on heating to release CO₂; no oxygen needed |
| Cu₂S | Sulphide | Roasting | Follows the standard sulphide pattern: sulphide + O₂ → oxide + SO₂ |
| Al₂O₃·2H₂O | Hydrated oxide | Calcination | Heat drives off bound water as steam |

**Takeaway:** A quick rule of thumb: if the formula is a **sulphide (S)**, use **roasting**; if it is a **carbonate (CO₃)** or **hydrated (·nH₂O)**, use **calcination**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Roasting and calcination are just two words for the same thing."**
Incorrect. Compare what happens when two zinc ores are heated: heat **ZnS** in air and you get a sharp, choking gas (SO₂). Heat **ZnCO₃** and you get an odourless gas that turns lime water milky (CO₂). Two different ores, two different gases, two distinct processes: in **roasting**, oxygen from the air reacts with the ore to oxidise it; in **calcination**, heat alone decomposes the ore in limited or no air.

**Mistake 2: "Calcination also requires a strong blast of air."**
Incorrect. Calcination requires **heat, not oxygen**. In the teacher demonstration earlier, green copper carbonate decomposed inside a narrow test tube where the escaping CO₂ quickly pushed out the air, yet CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂ proceeded smoothly because thermal decomposition does not consume oxygen.

**Mistake 3: "Since oxygen is added during roasting, the solid product must weigh more than the starting sulphide ore."**
Incorrect—it usually weighs **less**! Look at Example 1: 194 kg of ZnS roasted in air leaves 162 kg of solid ZnO, a loss of 32 kg. Why? Because each sulphur atom (atomic mass 32) that leaves as SO₂ gas is replaced by a lighter oxygen atom (atomic mass 16). In Class 9 you saw that burning a **pure metal** in air increases the solid mass, but roasting a **sulphide ore** swaps heavy sulphur for lighter oxygen.

**Mistake 4: "The sky is huge, so releasing SO₂ up a tall chimney makes it disappear harmlessly."**
Incorrect. In Example 1, a single 970 kg batch of ZnS released 640 kg of SO₂. If a plant processes 100 such batches in a month, that is **64 tonnes** of toxic SO₂ entering the atmosphere, where it turns into sulphuric acid and falls as acid rain on farms, forests, and lakes hundreds of kilometres downwind. Tall chimneys only spread pollution further; scrubbers and acid plants actually stop it.

**Mistake 5: "Once roasting or calcination is finished, we have the pure metal."**
Incorrect. Look at the ZnO left after roasting or calcination: it is a white **powder** (used in calamine lotion and sunscreens), not shiny metallic zinc. To get the actual metal, we still have to strip away the oxygen—and that is the job of **reduction** (Topic 4).

## Quick Recap

- Concentrated sulphide, carbonate, and hydrated ores are first converted into **metal oxides** because oxides are much easier to reduce.
- **Roasting:** sulphide ore + **excess air** → metal oxide + **SO₂**. Key equations: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂; 2PbS + 3O₂ → 2PbO + 2SO₂; 2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂.
- **Calcination:** carbonate or hydrated ore + **limited or no air** → metal oxide + CO₂ (or steam). Key equations: ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂; Al₂O₃·2H₂O → Al₂O₃ + 2H₂O; CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂.
- Quick rule: **S** (sulphide) → roasting; **CO₃** or **·nH₂O** → calcination.
- SO₂ is **toxic** and causes **acid rain**: smelters capture it in **lime scrubbers** or convert it into **sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)** (Chapter 9).
- Industrial furnace operations are **for reading only**; students never roast sulphide ores.
- Mass relationships: 970 kg ZnS → 810 kg ZnO + 640 kg SO₂; 250 kg ZnCO₃ → 162 kg ZnO + 88 kg CO₂.

## Practice Questions

1. Define roasting and calcination. Write one balanced chemical equation for each.

2. State whether roasting or calcination is the appropriate step for each ore, with a one-line reason: (a) FeS₂, (b) MgCO₃, (c) Cu₂S, (d) PbCO₃, (e) malachite, Cu₂CO₃(OH)₂ (the same basic carbonate written as CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂ in Topic 1), (f) HgS (for reading only—mercury and its compounds are poisonous).

3. 318 kg of copper(I) sulphide, Cu₂S, is roasted in air (2Cu₂S + 3O₂ → 2Cu₂O + 2SO₂). Find the mass of Cu₂O formed, the mass of SO₂ released, and the mass of oxygen consumed. Check that total mass is conserved. (Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16.)

4. 42 kg of pure magnesite, MgCO₃, is calcined (MgCO₃ → MgO + CO₂). Find the mass of MgO and the mass of CO₂ formed. By what percentage does the mass of the solid decrease? (Mg = 24, C = 12, O = 16.)

5. 60 kg of iron pyrites, FeS₂, is roasted completely (4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂). Find the mass of Fe₂O₃ formed, the mass of SO₂ released, and the mass of oxygen required. Verify conservation of mass. (Fe = 56, S = 32, O = 16.)

6. 22.1 kg of malachite, Cu₂CO₃(OH)₂, is calcined (Cu₂CO₃(OH)₂ → 2CuO + CO₂ + H₂O). Find the mass of CuO, of CO₂, and of water vapour formed. Verify the total mass. (Cu = 63.5, C = 12, O = 16, H = 1.)

7. Calculate the mass percentage of sulphur in FeS₂ and in Cu₂S. Which of the two releases more SO₂ per kilogram of pure mineral when roasted, and what does this mean for a factory designing its gas-treatment plant?

8. 32 kg of sulphur dioxide captured from a roasting plant is converted completely into sulphuric acid (every 64 kg of SO₂ yields 98 kg of H₂SO₄). What mass of H₂SO₄ is produced?

9. Three unknown ore samples X, Y, and Z are heated strongly in separate test tubes by a teacher in a fume hood. Clues: a sulphide gives off a choking gas; a carbonate gives off an odourless gas that turns lime water milky; a hydrated oxide gives off only droplets of water that condense near the cool mouth of the tube. Sample X gives a choking gas, Sample Y turns lime water milky, and Sample Z gives only water droplets. Identify the type of each ore and state whether roasting or calcination is used for it.

10. Give two reasons why metallurgists convert a sulphide or carbonate ore into an oxide before reducing it.

11. Why must sulphur dioxide from a roasting furnace never be vented straight into the air? Name two ways a modern smelter deals with it.

12. Draw a comparison table contrasting roasting and calcination across at least four features (type of ore, air supply, gas released, type of chemical reaction).

13. State whether True or False, and correct the false statements: (a) Calcination is carried out in a plentiful supply of air. (b) Roasting a sulphide ore releases SO₂ gas. (c) After roasting, the solid left in the furnace is the pure metal. (d) Sulphuric acid can be manufactured from the SO₂ captured during roasting.

14. At the science fair, the teacher heated iron pyrites inside a fume hood with an extractor fan. Why should a student never try heating a sulphide mineral at home or on an open lab bench?

### Answers

1. **Roasting:** Heating a concentrated sulphide ore strongly in an excess of air below its melting point to convert it into a metal oxide; e.g., 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂ (Zn 2 = 2, S 2 = 2, O 6 = 6 ✅). **Calcination:** Heating a concentrated carbonate or hydrated ore strongly in limited or no air to drive off CO₂ or water and leave the metal oxide; e.g., ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂ (Zn 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 ✅).

2. (a) FeS₂: **roasting** (sulphide ore; oxygen is needed to oxidise sulphur to SO₂). (b) MgCO₃: **calcination** (carbonate ore; decomposes by heat to release CO₂). (c) Cu₂S: **roasting** (sulphide ore). (d) PbCO₃: **calcination** (carbonate ore; note that lead compounds are toxic, so for reading only). (e) Malachite: **calcination** (basic carbonate containing CO₃ and OH groups; heat drives off both CO₂ and H₂O). (f) HgS: **roasting** (sulphide ore; for reading only).

3. Formula masses: Cu₂S = 2 × 63.5 + 32 = **159**; Cu₂O = 127 + 16 = **143**; SO₂ = **64**. From equation: 2 × 159 = 318 (Cu₂S) : 96 (O₂) : 2 × 143 = 286 (Cu₂O) : 128 (SO₂). Since 318 kg gives a scaling factor of 1: **Cu₂O = 286 kg, SO₂ = 128 kg, O₂ = 96 kg**. Check: 318 + 96 = 414 kg; 286 + 128 = 414 kg ✅.

4. Formula masses: MgCO₃ = 24 + 12 + 48 = **84**; MgO = **40**; CO₂ = **44**. Scaling factor = 42 ÷ 84 = **0.5**. **MgO = 20 kg, CO₂ = 22 kg** (20 + 22 = 42 kg ✅). Percentage decrease in solid mass = 22 ÷ 42 × 100 ≈ **52.4%** (same as 44 ÷ 84 × 100).

5. Formula masses: FeS₂ = 56 + 64 = **120**; Fe₂O₃ = 112 + 48 = **160**; SO₂ = **64**; O₂ = 32. Equation masses: 4 × 120 = 480 : 11 × 32 = 352 : 2 × 160 = 320 : 8 × 64 = 512. Scaling factor = 60 ÷ 480 = **0.125**. **Fe₂O₃ = 0.125 × 320 = 40 kg; SO₂ = 0.125 × 512 = 64 kg; O₂ = 0.125 × 352 = 44 kg.** Check: 60 + 44 = 104 kg; 40 + 64 = 104 kg ✅.

6. Formula masses: Cu₂CO₃(OH)₂ = 127 + 12 + 48 + 34 = **221**; 2CuO = 2 × 79.5 = **159**; CO₂ = **44**; H₂O = **18**. Scaling factor = 22.1 ÷ 221 = **0.1**. **CuO = 15.9 kg, CO₂ = 4.4 kg, H₂O = 1.8 kg.** Check: 15.9 + 4.4 + 1.8 = 22.1 kg ✅.

7. In FeS₂: 64 ÷ 120 × 100 ≈ **53.3% S**. In Cu₂S: 32 ÷ 159 × 100 ≈ **20.1% S**. Because every 32 g of sulphur produces 64 g of SO₂ (double the mass), 1 kg of FeS₂ produces about 1.07 kg of SO₂ (512 ÷ 480), whereas 1 kg of Cu₂S produces about 0.40 kg of SO₂ (128 ÷ 318). **FeS₂ releases far more SO₂ per kilogram**, meaning a plant roasting it would require a **much larger gas-scrubbing and sulphuric-acid recovery system**.

8. Scaling factor = 32 ÷ 64 = **0.5**. Mass of H₂SO₄ = 0.5 × 98 = **49 kg**.

9. **Sample X:** Choking gas → **sulphide ore**, processed by **roasting** (heating in excess air). **Sample Y:** Turns lime water milky (CO₂) → **carbonate ore**, processed by **calcination**. **Sample Z:** Only water droplets → **hydrated oxide ore**, processed by **calcination**.

10. (i) Metal oxides are much easier and cheaper to reduce using carbon or carbon monoxide than sulphides or carbonates. (ii) Roasting and calcination drive off sulphur, CO₂, moisture, and volatile impurities, leaving a cleaner, porous oxide that reacts readily in the reduction stage.

11. SO₂ is a toxic, choking gas that damages the respiratory system and reacts with atmospheric oxygen and rainwater to form sulphuric acid (**acid rain**). A modern smelter can (a) trap it in a **lime/limestone scrubber** as solid CaSO₃/CaSO₄, or (b) pipe it to an acid plant to manufacture **sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)**.

12. Comparison table:

| Feature | Roasting | Calcination |
|---|---|---|
| Type of ore | Sulphide | Carbonate or hydrated oxide |
| Air supply | Excess air (O₂) | Limited or no air |
| Gas released | SO₂ | CO₂ or water vapour |
| Reaction type | Oxidation | Thermal decomposition |

13. (a) **False** ❌: Calcination is done in limited or no air. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌: Roasting leaves behind a **metal oxide**, not the pure metal (reduction is still required). (d) **True** ✅.

14. Heating iron pyrites releases **sulphur dioxide (SO₂)**, a toxic and choking gas that can severely irritate the eyes, throat, and lungs and trigger asthma attacks. Without an industrial or laboratory fume hood to pull the gas away safely, anyone nearby will inhale it. Heating sulphide minerals is strictly a fume-hood teacher demonstration or an enclosed industrial process.
