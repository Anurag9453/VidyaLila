# Topic 4: From Oxide to Metal: Reduction

In Topic 3, we converted our concentrated ores into **metal oxides** (by roasting sulphides and calcining carbonates or hydrates). Yet an oxide is still not a metal: ZnO is a white powder, and Fe₂O₃ is a reddish-brown solid. To liberate the free metal, we must **strip the oxygen away** from the oxide. In Class 9, we learned the name for removing oxygen from a compound: **reduction**. Today's central question is: **which reduction method works for which metal?** The answer comes straight from your old friend, the reactivity series.

---

## A Morning at the Steel Plant

For her school science project on "How Iron Is Made," Chloe visited a steelworks visitor gallery on Saturday morning with her uncle, Mr. Rivera. Standing safely behind thick heat-shielded glass in their hard hats and safety goggles, they looked out at a towering, reddish-brown steel cylinder rising like a giant chimney against the sky. "That is the **blast furnace**," the plant guide explained. "It runs twenty-four hours a day, all year round, without stopping."

Chloe watched an inclined conveyor belt carry black lumps, reddish-brown ore, and pale grey stones up to the very top of the tower. Down at the base of the furnace, a blindingly bright **orange-white river of molten iron** poured along a clay-lined channel, while a second notch slightly higher up tapped off a duller, lighter stream of glowing liquid. "Uncle Leo, what is that second stream?" Chloe asked. Mr. Rivera smiled: "That's one of the best questions in metallurgy. And here's another: inside the ore, the iron is tightly bonded to oxygen. What actually pulls that oxygen away?"

The guide pointed to a diagram on the wall: "From the top we charge **iron ore, coke, and limestone**; near the bottom we blow in a **blast of hot air**. The real work inside is done by a **gas**." Chloe opened her notebook: "I want to write down the whole story of what happens from top to bottom." "We will," said Mr. Rivera, "not just for iron, but for metals at every level of the reactivity ladder."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reduction** | Removing oxygen from a metal oxide to obtain the free metal | Extracting Fe from Fe₂O₃ |
| **Reducing agent** | A substance that takes oxygen away from another substance | C, CO, Al |
| **Coke** | A hard, porous, nearly pure form of carbon made by heating coal in the absence of air | Fuel and source of reducing agent in a blast furnace |
| **Blast furnace** | A tall, vertical shaft furnace used to extract iron, into which a blast of hot air is blown near the base | Iron-making furnace in a steel plant |
| **Flux** | A substance added to the furnace charge to react with high-melting gangue and turn it into an easily melted liquid | CaO (formed from limestone) |
| **Slag** | The low-density molten compound formed when flux reacts with gangue; it floats on top of the molten metal | Calcium silicate, CaSiO₃ |
| **Pig iron** | Crude iron tapped from a blast furnace, containing about 4% carbon along with small amounts of other impurities | Cast blocks of crude iron |
| **Auto-reduction (self-reduction)** | A process in which part of a roasted sulphide ore reduces the remaining sulphide upon heating without any external reducing agent | Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂ |
| **Aluminothermy** | Reducing a metal oxide using aluminium powder as the reducing agent | Thermite reaction |
| **Electrolysis** | Passing an electric current through a molten (or dissolved) ionic compound to decompose it into its elements | Electrolysis of molten NaCl |

## Part A: What Reduction Is in One Sentence (and What Can Act as a Reducing Agent)

Recall from Class 9 ([oxidation and reduction](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part5.md)) that when a substance **loses oxygen**, it is **reduced**, and the substance that takes that oxygen away is the **reducing agent** (which gets oxidised in the process). Extracting a metal from its oxide is therefore a **reduction** step. In industrial metallurgy, three main tools are used to reduce metal oxides:

- **Carbon (coke) and carbon monoxide (CO):** Cheap and abundant, effective for **moderately reactive** metals in the middle of the series.
- **A more reactive metal** (such as aluminium): A displacement reaction like the thermite process—more expensive than carbon, but ideal for certain high-melting oxides where carbon doesn't work well.
- **Electricity (electrolysis):** The most powerful "reducing agent" of all, because the negative electrode supplies electrons directly to metal ions. Required for **highly reactive** metals at the top of the series.

(Hydrogen gas can also reduce certain metal oxides in a laboratory tube, as you saw in Class 9, but on an industrial scale it is expensive and explosive, so it is used only for specialised metals like tungsten.)

Let's see how the reactivity series divides metals into three clear extraction groups.

```
   REACTIVITY SERIES (from top to bottom: decreasing reactivity)

   GROUP 1: HIGHLY REACTIVE        K  Na  Ca  Mg  Al
   Hold on to oxygen very tightly
        ==> Method: ELECTROLYSIS of the molten chloride or oxide

   GROUP 2: MODERATELY REACTIVE    Zn  Fe  Sn  Pb
   Hold on to oxygen with moderate strength
        ==> Method: Form the oxide (Topic 3), then REDUCE
            with CARBON or CARBON MONOXIDE

   GROUP 3: LOW REACTIVITY         Cu  Hg  Ag  Au
   Hold on to oxygen weakly
        ==> Method: Heating alone (or auto-reduction),
            or found native in the earth (Ag, Au)
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The three groups of the reactivity series and the reduction method matched to each. The higher a metal sits on the ladder, the more powerful the method required to free it (electricity at the top, simple heating at the bottom).

## Part B: Group 3, Low-Reactivity Metals: Heating Alone Is Enough

At the bottom of the reactivity series, metal oxides are so **thermally unstable** that **heating alone** is enough to decompose them into the free metal and oxygen gas. No external reducing agent is required.

**Mercury** (for reading only): The ore of mercury is cinnabar (HgS). First it is roasted in air to form mercury(II) oxide (HgO), and on further strong heating (~300 °C), HgO decomposes directly into liquid mercury and oxygen:

2HgO → 2Hg + O₂

Atom count: Hg: 2 = 2, O: 2 = 2 ✅.

> **Safety Warning:** Mercury and its vapour are **highly toxic** to the nervous system and kidneys. This process is **strictly for reading only**; students must never touch or heat mercury or its compounds. Industrial extraction is carried out inside sealed, negatively pressured condensers by trained specialists.

**Copper** has a particularly clever twist. Because copper's main ores are sulphides (such as copper pyrites, CuFeS₂, which is converted to copper(I) sulphide, Cu₂S), roasting in air converts **part** of the Cu₂S into copper(I) oxide (Cu₂O) (Topic 3). Once enough Cu₂O has formed, the air supply is shut off and the furnace temperature is raised. The remaining unreacted Cu₂S and the newly formed Cu₂O **react with each other**, reducing the copper without any carbon at all:

Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂

Atom count: Cu: (2 + 4) = 6 = 6, S: 1 = 1, O: 2 = 2 ✅. This is called **auto-reduction** (or self-reduction): the sulphur in Cu₂S takes the oxygen from Cu₂O to form SO₂ gas, leaving behind molten metallic copper. (As the molten copper solidifies, dissolved SO₂ bubbles out of the surface, raising blisters—so this crude metal is called "blister copper" and is later purified by electrolytic refining in Topic 6.)

**Gold** (and often **silver**) are so unreactive that they occur in the **free (native) state** in the earth and do not need chemical reduction from an oxide.

## Part C: Group 2, Moderately Reactive Metals: Reduction with Carbon

Oxides of moderately reactive metals—**Zn, Fe, Sn, and Pb**—cannot be decomposed by heat alone, but their oxygen can be stripped away by heating them with **carbon (coke)** or **carbon monoxide (CO)**. Carbon acts just like a more reactive element pulling oxygen away from the metal oxide.

For zinc, after zinc blende (ZnS) is roasted to ZnO (Topic 3: 2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂), the oxide is heated strongly with coke:

ZnO + C → Zn + CO

Atom count: Zn: 1 = 1, O: 1 = 1, C: 1 = 1 ✅. Similarly, for lead(II) oxide obtained by roasting galena:

PbO + C → Pb + CO

Atom count: Pb: 1 = 1, O: 1 = 1, C: 1 = 1 ✅. (Lead and its compounds are toxic: for reading only.) And for iron(III) oxide in a blast furnace:

Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

Atom count: Fe: 2 = 2, C: 3 = 3, O: (3 + 3) = 6 = (3 × 2) = 6 ✅. Here the primary reducing agent is **carbon monoxide (CO)** gas, which takes oxygen from Fe₂O₃ to become CO₂.

Similarly, tin(IV) oxide (cassiterite) is reduced with carbon: SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO (Atom count: Sn: 1 = 1, O: 2 = 2, C: 2 = 2 ✅).

> **Activity 4.1 (Teacher demonstration only): Reducing CuO with Carbon.** In a well-ventilated lab or fume hood, your teacher mixes black copper(II) oxide (CuO) powder with dry charcoal powder (carbon) in a **hard-glass** test tube and heats it strongly (2CuO + C → 2Cu + CO₂; Atom count: Cu: 2 = 2, O: 2 = 2, C: 1 = 1 ✅), bubbling the escaping gas through lime water, which turns **milky**. (A small amount of toxic CO can also form, which is why good ventilation or a fume hood is used, and the teacher removes the delivery tube from the lime water before turning off the flame to prevent suck-back.) Once the tube cools completely, reddish-brown specks of metallic copper can be seen inside. Students observe from a safe distance wearing safety goggles.

However, carbon **cannot** reduce the oxides of **Group 1** metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al)—let's see why in Part E, right after we tour the blast furnace.

## Part D: The Blast Furnace: Extracting Iron (For Study Only)

Iron is the backbone of modern construction and engineering. Its principal ore is haematite, Fe₂O₃ (which is 70% iron by mass when pure: 112 ÷ 160 = 0.70), along with magnetite, Fe₃O₄. Iron is extracted in a giant vertical shaft called a **blast furnace**—a tall steel tower lined inside with heat-resistant (refractory) firebricks. **Three solid raw materials (the "charge") are fed in at the top:**

1. **Iron ore** (mainly Fe₂O₃, mixed with gangue that is mostly silica sand, SiO₂).
2. **Coke** (nearly pure carbon): serves both as the fuel and as the source of the reducing gas CO.
3. **Limestone** (CaCO₃): acts as the **flux** to remove the silica gangue.

Near the base of the furnace, a powerful **blast of pre-heated air** is blown in through nozzles (called tuyeres).

```
              iron ore + coke + limestone
                        :
                        v
                  +-----------+
   waste gas <-- :           :   ZONE 1 (top): approx. 200 to 400 °C
   (CO, CO₂, N₂)  :  drying   :   charge dries; moisture is driven off
                 /             \
                /  ZONE 2       \ approx. 400 to 900 °C
               /   Fe₂O₃ + 3CO   \ CO reduces Fe₂O₃ to iron:
              /    ---> 2Fe + 3CO₂\ limestone decomposes: CaCO₃ ---> CaO + CO₂
             /                     \
            /  ZONE 3               \ approx. 900 to 1500 °C
           /   CO₂ + C ---> 2CO      \ rising CO₂ reacts with coke to form CO;
          /                           \ spongy iron begins to melt
         /  ZONE 4 (bottom, hottest)   \ approx. 1500 to 2000 °C
        /   C + O₂ ---> CO₂             \ coke burns in hot air blast, supplying heat;
       /    CaO + SiO₂ ---> CaSiO₃       \ flux reacts with gangue to form liquid slag
        \                               /
 hot ===>\                           / <== hot air blast
 air      :  SLAG (lighter, floats)   :  <-- slag tap hole
 blast    :-------------------------:
          :  MOLTEN IRON (denser)     :  <-- iron tap hole
          :_________________________:
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Cross-section of a blast furnace showing the main temperature zones. Iron ore, coke, and limestone are fed in at the top; hot air is blasted in near the bottom, just above the hearth. Waste gases exit near the top. At the bottom, denser molten iron collects at the base while lighter molten slag floats on top of it, and each is tapped off through its own outlet. (Temperatures are approximate and vary slightly between furnaces.)

### Step-by-Step Chemistry Inside the Blast Furnace

1. **At the bottom—coke burns in the hot air blast:** Oxygen in the incoming hot air reacts vigorously with hot coke, releasing intense heat that keeps the bottom of the furnace near 1500–2000 °C:

   C + O₂ → CO₂

   Atom count: C: 1 = 1, O: 2 = 2 ✅.
2. **In the middle zone—rising CO₂ reacts with red-hot coke:** As the hot CO₂ gas rises through the overlying bed of white-hot coke, it is reduced to **carbon monoxide (CO)**, the main reducing agent in the furnace:

   CO₂ + C → 2CO

   Atom count: C: (1 + 1) = 2 = 2, O: 2 = 2 ✅.
3. **In the upper-middle zone—CO reduces the iron ore:** The rising carbon monoxide gas permeates the porous lumps of haematite and strips away their oxygen:

   Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

   This is the exact reduction equation we balanced in Part C. The iron formed trickles down into the hotter lower zones and melts.
4. **The job of limestone (flux and slag formation):** In the hot middle zone, limestone thermally decomposes (calcination):

   CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

   Atom count: Ca: 1 = 1, C: 1 = 1, O: 3 = (1 + 2) = 3 ✅. Here, quicklime (**CaO**) is a **basic oxide**, whereas the main gangue in iron ore—silica (**SiO₂**)—is an **acidic oxide** (recall acid-base oxides from Class 9 and Chapter 2). At high temperature, the basic flux reacts with the acidic gangue:

   CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃

   Atom count: Ca: 1 = 1, Si: 1 = 1, O: (1 + 2) = 3 = 3 ✅. This molten calcium silicate (**CaSiO₃**) is the **slag**—the second glowing stream Chloe saw at the steel plant!

**Why is slag formation so important, and why does it float?** Pure silica sand has a very high melting point (~1710 °C) and would clog the furnace as a sticky solid if left alone. By reacting it with CaO (the **flux**), engineers turn the gangue into liquid **slag** (CaSiO₃) that melts easily and flows smoothly. Because molten slag is **less dense** than molten iron, it **floats as a protective blanket** right on top of the liquid iron—preventing the incoming hot air blast from re-oxidising the freshly made iron! The slag and the iron are then tapped off through separate holes at different heights.

**What happens to the cooled slag?** Far from being useless waste, solidified blast-furnace slag is crushed and used for **road building** and manufacturing **slag cement**.

### Pig Iron and What Comes Next

The molten iron tapped from the bottom of the blast furnace is called **pig iron**. It is not pure iron: it contains about **4% dissolved carbon** along with small amounts of silicon, phosphorus, and sulphur. Because of that 4% carbon, pig iron is **hard but brittle** (it cracks if hammered), so most of it is taken straight to a steelmaking furnace where oxygen is blown through it to burn off most of the excess carbon and turn it into tough, flexible **steel** (Topic 6).

## Part E: Group 1, Highly Reactive Metals: Why Carbon Cannot Reduce Them

Now for a crucial question: if carbon (coke) is so cheap and works so well for iron and zinc, why don't we use carbon to reduce **Al₂O₃, MgO, or Na₂O**?

**The answer from the reactivity series:** Recall the thermite reaction from Chapter 2 ([metals-and-non-metals-part4.md](../metals-and-non-metals/metals-and-non-metals-part4.md)): aluminium strips oxygen away from Fe₂O₃ because Al is much more reactive than Fe. In general, **the more reactive a metal is, the stronger its chemical bond with oxygen**. Metals at the top of the series—**K, Na, Ca, Mg, and Al**—have a greater affinity for oxygen than carbon does. Carbon's pull on oxygen is strong enough to take oxygen from ZnO, Fe₂O₃, and PbO, **but not strong enough** to pull oxygen away from Al₂O₃, MgO, or CaO (at ordinary furnace temperatures, you can think of carbon as sitting between Al and Zn on the reactivity ladder). Therefore, heating Al₂O₃ or MgO with coke simply does not reduce them.

To extract these top-group metals, we need a reducing method stronger than carbon. Since no cheaper, even more reactive metal is available in bulk, we use **electricity (electrolysis)**.

### A First Look at Electrolysis (Full Story in Chapter 4)

In Class 9 ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)), you learned that a **molten ionic compound conducts electricity** because its positive and negative ions are free to move. In electrolytic extraction, a direct electric current is passed through the **molten** chloride or oxide of the reactive metal:

- At the **cathode (negative electrode)**: positive metal ions (cations) gain electrons (reduction) and deposit as the pure metal.
- At the **anode (positive electrode)**: negative non-metal ions (anions) lose electrons (oxidation) and escape as a gas.

**Extracting sodium from molten sodium chloride (NaCl)** (for reading only—molten salt at ~600–800 °C, reactive liquid sodium, and toxic chlorine gas are strictly industrial):

- At the cathode (−): Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na
- At the anode (+): 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻
- Overall reaction: 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂

Atom count: Na: 2 = 2, Cl: 2 = 2 ✅. Charge balance: net 0 on both sides ✅. (Chlorine gas is **toxic** and is collected in sealed pipes for industrial use.)

**Extracting magnesium from molten magnesium chloride (MgCl₂)** (for reading only): Similarly, Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg at the cathode and 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ at the anode; overall: MgCl₂ → Mg + Cl₂ (Atom count: Mg: 1 = 1, Cl: 2 = 2 ✅).

**Aluminium** is extracted in the same way by electrolysing **molten Al₂O₃** dissolved in cryolite—a story so important that it gets its own full lesson in **Topic 5**.

(The complete principles of electrolysis—how electrolytes work, how ions migrate, and what happens at electrodes in different solutions—will be covered thoroughly in **Chapter 4: Electrolysis**. Here we focus on why Group 1 metals require electrolysis.)

**Why must the NaCl be molten rather than dissolved in water?** If you electrolyse an **aqueous solution** of NaCl in water, you do not get metallic sodium at the cathode (even if sodium formed, it would react violently with the water immediately, as you saw in Chapter 2!). Instead, hydrogen gas is released from the water. Therefore, reactive metals must be extracted from their **anhydrous molten salts**, which requires high temperatures and a large amount of electrical energy.

## Part F: The Middle Path: Reduction by a More Reactive Metal (Aluminothermy)

For certain metals—such as **manganese (Mn)** and **chromium (Cr)**—reduction with carbon does not work cleanly (carbon reacts with the metal to form brittle carbides or requires impractically high temperatures), while full electrolysis would be too expensive. For these oxides, metallurgists use a **more reactive metal—aluminium powder**—as the reducing agent. This is the exact **aluminothermy (thermite)** family of reactions you met in Chapter 2:

Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe   (used to weld cracked railway tracks on-site)

In the exact same way, aluminium reduces manganese(IV) oxide and chromium(III) oxide:

- 3MnO₂ + 4Al → 3Mn + 2Al₂O₃   (extracting manganese). Atom count: Mn: 3 = 3, O: 6 = 6, Al: 4 = 4 ✅.
- Cr₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Cr   (extracting chromium). Atom count: Cr: 2 = 2, O: 3 = 3, Al: 2 = 2 ✅.

These reactions are **intensely exothermic**—they release so much heat that the produced metal (Fe, Mn, or Cr) comes out **white-hot and molten**. Because of this extreme heat, aluminothermic reactions are carried out only by trained professionals using refractory crucibles and heat shields; for students they are **for study only**. (Because aluminium itself takes a lot of electricity to make, aluminothermy is reserved for special applications like producing carbon-free Mn and Cr or field-welding rails.)

## Part G: Putting It All Together: Which Method for Which Metal

| Metal | Position in Series | Typical Ore / Feed | Extraction / Reduction Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| K, Na, Ca, Mg | Top (very reactive) | Chlorides (e.g., NaCl, MgCl₂) | Electrolysis of the molten chloride |
| Al | Top (very reactive) | Al₂O₃ (from bauxite) | Electrolysis of Al₂O₃ dissolved in molten cryolite (Topic 5) |
| Zn | Middle | ZnS or ZnCO₃ → ZnO | Roast or calcine to ZnO, then reduce with carbon (coke) |
| Fe | Middle | Fe₂O₃ (haematite) | Reduce with CO and coke in a blast furnace |
| Pb | Middle | PbS → PbO | Roast to PbO, then reduce with carbon |
| Sn | Middle | SnO₂ (cassiterite) | Reduce with carbon |
| Mn, Cr | Middle (high-melting / carbide-forming) | MnO₂, Cr₂O₃ | Reduce with aluminium powder (aluminothermy) |
| Cu | Bottom | Cu₂S / CuFeS₂ | Partial roasting followed by auto-reduction |
| Hg | Bottom | HgS (cinnabar) | Roasting / heating alone (for study only—toxic) |
| Ag, Au | Very bottom | Often found native (free) | Gravity/physical separation; no reduction needed when native |

Everything fits into one logical pattern: **a metal's position in the reactivity series dictates how we extract it**. We can summarise the decision in a simple flowchart:

```

                               :

                               :

        :                      :                      :
        v                      v                      v
       TOP                   MIDDLE                 BOTTOM
 (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al)    (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb)       (Cu, Hg, Ag, Au)
        :                      :                      :
        v                      v                      v

  the molten chloride    oxide (roast/calcine), auto-reduction), or
  or molten oxide        then REDUCE with       recover native metal
```

## Part H: Solved Examples (Furnace Stoichiometry on Paper)

Atomic masses: C = 12, O = 16, Fe = 56, Zn = 65, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, Si = 28.

### Solved Example 1: Iron from Haematite in a Blast Furnace

**Question:** A 1000 kg charge of haematite ore contains 80% Fe₂O₃ (the rest being gangue). In the blast furnace, Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Calculate the mass of pure iron produced, the mass of CO consumed, and the mass of CO₂ formed. If the tapped pig iron contains 96% iron and 4% carbon/impurities, what is the total mass of pig iron obtained?

**Solution:**
1. Mass of pure Fe₂O₃ = 1000 × 0.80 = 800 kg. Molar mass of Fe₂O₃ = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 = 160 g/mol. Therefore, 800 kg = 800 ÷ 160 = **5 kmol** (kilomoles).
2. From the equation: 1 mol Fe₂O₃ + 3 mol CO → 2 mol Fe + 3 mol CO₂. Thus:
   - Iron (Fe) = 2 × 5 = 10 kmol = 10 × 56 = **560 kg**.
   - Carbon monoxide (CO) = 3 × 5 = 15 kmol × 28 = **420 kg**.
   - Carbon dioxide (CO₂) = 15 kmol × 44 = **660 kg**.
3. Check conservation of mass: 800 + 420 = 1220 kg on the left; 560 + 660 = 1220 kg on the right ✅.
4. Since pig iron is 96% Fe, total mass of pig iron = 560 ÷ 0.96 ≈ **583 kg** (the remaining ~23 kg is dissolved carbon and minor impurities).

**Takeaway:** 1 tonne of this haematite ore yields 0.56 tonnes of pure iron (or about 0.583 tonnes of pig iron).

### Solved Example 2: Zinc from Zinc Oxide

**Question:** 405 kg of ZnO is reduced with coke: ZnO + C → Zn + CO. Find the mass of zinc obtained, the mass of carbon consumed, and the mass of CO formed.

**Solution:** Molar mass of ZnO = 65 + 16 = 81. Number of kilomoles = 405 ÷ 81 = **5 kmol**. Using the 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 mole ratio: Zn = 5 × 65 = **325 kg**, C = 5 × 12 = **60 kg**, CO = 5 × 28 = **140 kg**. Check conservation of mass: 405 + 60 = 465 kg; 325 + 140 = 465 kg ✅.

| Fe | Group 2 (Middle) | Reduce Fe₂O₃ with CO in a blast furnace |
| Mg | Group 1 (Top) | Electrolysis of molten MgCl₂ |

### Solved Example 4: Why Can't Carbon Reduce Al₂O₃?

**Question:** A classmate asks: "Since we use cheap coke (carbon) to reduce Fe₂O₃ in a blast furnace, why don't we just heat bauxite's Al₂O₃ with coke to make aluminium?" Explain in three clear steps.

**Solution (Three steps):**
1. From the thermite reaction (Chapter 2), we know that aluminium pulls oxygen away from Fe₂O₃—meaning aluminium forms a **much stronger bond with oxygen** than iron does.
2. Carbon's affinity for oxygen is strong enough to strip oxygen from middle-group oxides like Fe₂O₃ and ZnO, **but weaker than aluminium's hold on oxygen**.
3. Therefore, carbon cannot pull oxygen away from Al₂O₃ at furnace temperatures. Extracting aluminium requires a much stronger method: **electrolysis** of molten Al₂O₃.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "If I can balance the equation on paper, carbon will reduce any metal oxide."**
Suppose a student writes and balances: Al₂O₃ + 3C → 2Al + 3CO. The atoms balance on paper, **but balancing an equation does not mean the reaction actually happens in a furnace!** Because Al sits high above Fe and Zn in the reactivity series, Al holds onto oxygen far too tightly for carbon to reduce it at ordinary furnace temperatures. Top-group metals (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) require **electrolysis**.

**Mistake 2: "Blast-furnace slag is just useless waste."**
If you ask why limestone is even added to the furnace, remember two vital jobs: inside the furnace, the flux (CaO from limestone) turns high-melting silica gangue (SiO₂) into liquid **slag (CaSiO₃)** so it can flow out without clogging the furnace, and the floating layer of slag **protects the molten iron underneath from being re-oxidised** by the incoming air blast. Outside the furnace, cooled slag is a valuable raw material for **road construction and cement manufacture**.

**Mistake 3: "In the blast furnace, solid lumps of coke directly reduce most of the solid Fe₂O₃ lumps."**
In reality, solid-against-solid contact is very limited. The main reducing agent in the blast furnace is **carbon monoxide gas (CO)**, which forms via C + O₂ → CO₂ followed by CO₂ + C → 2CO, and then flows into every pore of the iron ore lumps (Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂).

**Mistake 4: "Pig iron from the blast furnace is 100% pure iron."**
Pig iron contains about **4% dissolved carbon** (in 500 kg of pig iron, 500 × 0.04 = 20 kg is carbon and 480 kg is iron). That 4% carbon makes pig iron **brittle**—if you hit it hard with a hammer, it can crack rather than bend. Most pig iron is therefore refined further to reduce its carbon content and make **steel** (Topic 6).

## Quick Recap

- **Reduction:** Removing oxygen from a metal oxide—the decisive step that liberates the metal.
- The reduction method is governed by the metal's **position in the reactivity series**.
- **Bottom group (Cu, Hg, Ag, Au):** Heating alone, auto-reduction, or found native. Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂ (auto-reduction); 2HgO → 2Hg + O₂ (for study only—mercury is toxic).
- **Middle group (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb):** Convert to oxide, then reduce with carbon or CO: ZnO + C → Zn + CO; PbO + C → Pb + CO; Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂.
- **Blast furnace for iron:** Charge of iron ore + coke + limestone at the top; hot air blast near the bottom. Key equations: C + O₂ → CO₂; CO₂ + C → 2CO; Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂; CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂; CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃ (slag). Lighter slag floats on top of denser molten iron. Pig iron contains ~4% carbon.
- **Top group (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al):** Cannot be reduced by carbon; extracted by **electrolysis** of the molten chloride or oxide—e.g., 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂ (Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na at the cathode; 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻ at the anode).
- **Aluminothermy (reduction with Al):** Used for oxides like MnO₂ and Cr₂O₃ (and welding rails with Fe₂O₃).
- Industrial furnaces, molten metals, molten salts, and toxic substances (mercury, lead, chlorine) are strictly **for study only**.

## Practice Questions

1. 464 kg of magnetite, Fe₃O₄, is reduced by carbon monoxide in a furnace: Fe₃O₄ + 4CO → 3Fe + 4CO₂. Find the mass of iron obtained, the mass of CO consumed, and the mass of CO₂ formed. Verify conservation of mass. (Fe = 56, C = 12, O = 16.)
2. 669 kg of lead(II) oxide, PbO, is reduced with coke: PbO + C → Pb + CO. Find the mass of lead obtained, the mass of carbon needed, and the mass of CO formed. Verify conservation of mass. (Pb = 207, C = 12, O = 16.)
3. In an aluminothermic reaction, 3MnO₂ + 4Al → 3Mn + 2Al₂O₃, 261 g of MnO₂ is reacted completely. Find the mass of manganese formed, the mass of aluminium required, and the mass of Al₂O₃ formed. Verify conservation of mass. (Mn = 55, Al = 27, O = 16.)
4. Molten magnesium chloride (190 kg) is electrolysed: MgCl₂ → Mg + Cl₂. Find the mass of magnesium and the mass of chlorine produced. Write the half-equation at each electrode, and explain why chlorine gas must be handled in a sealed system. (Mg = 24, Cl = 35.5.)
5. 755 kg of tin(IV) oxide, SnO₂, is reduced with carbon: SnO₂ + 2C → Sn + 2CO. Find the mass of tin obtained, the mass of carbon required, and the mass of CO formed. Verify conservation of mass. (Sn = 119, C = 12, O = 16.)
6. In a blast furnace, 180 kg of silica gangue, SiO₂, must be converted into slag using CaO from limestone: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ and CaO + SiO₂ → CaSiO₃. Find the mass of pure limestone (CaCO₃) required and the mass of slag (CaSiO₃) formed. (Ca = 40, Si = 28, C = 12, O = 16.)
7. Molten sodium chloride (351 g) is electrolysed: 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂. Find the mass of sodium and the mass of chlorine produced, and check that the masses add up. (Na = 23, Cl = 35.5.)
8. Copper is extracted by auto-reduction: Cu₂S + 2Cu₂O → 6Cu + SO₂. If 477 g of Cu₂S reacts completely, find the mass of Cu₂O needed, the mass of copper obtained, and the mass of SO₂ formed. Verify conservation of mass. (Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16.)
9. Choose the appropriate extraction method (heating alone, reduction with carbon/CO after roasting or calcination, aluminothermy, or electrolysis of a molten compound) for each metal and give a one-line reason based on its position in the reactivity series: calcium, tin, chromium, mercury, potassium, lead.
10. Three metals P, Q, and R are studied. Clues: (i) a more reactive metal can reduce the oxide of a less reactive metal; (ii) P reduces the oxide of Q; (iii) Q reduces the oxide of R; (iv) carbon can reduce the oxide of R, but cannot reduce the oxides of P or Q. Arrange P, Q, and R in decreasing order of reactivity, and state which of them require an extraction method stronger than carbon reduction.
11. A 750 kg batch of pig iron contains 4% carbon. Find the mass of carbon and the mass of iron in it. Why is pig iron not used directly for making hammers or structural beams?
12. State True or False and correct the false statements: (a) Slag is denser than molten iron and sinks to the bottom of the blast furnace. (b) Carbon reduces the oxides of metals at the top of the reactivity series. (c) Mercury(II) oxide decomposes into mercury and oxygen simply on heating. (d) In the blast furnace, the main reducing agent for Fe₂O₃ is carbon monoxide gas. (e) Electrolysis of molten NaCl produces sodium metal at the anode.
13. Why is hot air blown in near the bottom of the blast furnace rather than from the top? Give two reasons based on the furnace reactions (heat and CO formation). Also explain why NaCl must be molten, rather than solid, for electrolysis to work.

### Answers

1. Molar mass of Fe₃O₄ = 3 × 56 + 4 × 16 = 232. Moles = 464 ÷ 232 = **2 kmol**. From 1 mol Fe₃O₄ + 4 mol CO → 3 mol Fe + 4 mol CO₂: Fe = 6 kmol × 56 = **336 kg**; CO = 8 kmol × 28 = **224 kg**; CO₂ = 8 kmol × 44 = **352 kg**. Check: 464 + 224 = 688 kg; 336 + 352 = 688 kg ✅.
2. Molar mass of PbO = 207 + 16 = 223. Moles = 669 ÷ 223 = **3 kmol**. Ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 gives: Pb = 3 × 207 = **621 kg**; C = 3 × 12 = **36 kg**; CO = 3 × 28 = **84 kg**. Check: 669 + 36 = 705 kg; 621 + 84 = 705 kg ✅.
3. Molar mass of MnO₂ = 55 + 32 = 87. Moles = 261 ÷ 87 = **3 mol**. For 3 mol MnO₂ we need 4 mol Al and get 3 mol Mn and 2 mol Al₂O₃: Mn = 3 × 55 = **165 g**; Al = 4 × 27 = **108 g**; Al₂O₃ = 2 × 102 = **204 g**. Check: 261 + 108 = 369 g; 165 + 204 = 369 g ✅.
4. Molar mass of MgCl₂ = 24 + 71 = 95. Moles = 190 ÷ 95 = **2 kmol**. Mg = 2 × 24 = **48 kg**; Cl₂ = 2 × 71 = **142 kg**. Check: 48 + 142 = 190 kg ✅. Cathode: Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Mg. Anode: 2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻. Chlorine is a **toxic, choking gas** that damages the lungs, so it must be captured in a closed system.
5. Molar mass of SnO₂ = 119 + 32 = 151. Moles = 755 ÷ 151 = **5 kmol**. Sn = 5 × 119 = **595 kg**; C = 2 × 5 = 10 kmol × 12 = **120 kg**; CO = 10 kmol × 28 = **280 kg**. Check: 755 + 120 = 875 kg; 595 + 280 = 875 kg ✅.
6. Molar mass of SiO₂ = 28 + 32 = 60. Moles = 180 ÷ 60 = **3 kmol**. Since 1 mol SiO₂ requires 1 mol CaO, which comes from 1 mol CaCO₃: mass of CaCO₃ = 3 kmol × 100 = **300 kg** (producing 3 × 56 = 168 kg CaO). Mass of slag CaSiO₃ = 3 kmol × 116 = **348 kg**. Check: 180 kg SiO₂ + 168 kg CaO = 348 kg CaSiO₃ ✅.
7. Molar mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5. Moles = 351 ÷ 58.5 = **6 mol**. From 2NaCl → 2Na + Cl₂: Na = 6 × 23 = **138 g**; Cl₂ = 3 mol × 71 = **213 g**. Check: 138 + 213 = 351 g ✅.
8. Molar mass of Cu₂S = 2 × 63.5 + 32 = 159. Moles = 477 ÷ 159 = **3 mol**. Since 1 mol Cu₂S reacts with 2 mol Cu₂O, we need 6 mol Cu₂O (molar mass 143): mass of Cu₂O = 6 × 143 = **858 g**. Copper formed = 18 mol × 63.5 = **1143 g**. SO₂ formed = 3 mol × 64 = **192 g**. Check: 477 + 858 = 1335 g; 1143 + 192 = 1335 g ✅.
9. **Ca:** Group 1 (top of series), holds oxygen too tightly for carbon → electrolysis of molten chloride. **Sn:** Group 2 (middle) → reduce SnO₂ with carbon. **Cr:** Oxide reduced by aluminium powder (aluminothermy): Cr₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Cr. **Hg:** Group 3 (bottom) → roast/heat alone to decompose HgO into Hg and O₂ (for study only). **K:** Group 1 (top) → electrolysis of molten chloride. **Pb:** Group 2 (middle) → roast PbS to PbO, then reduce with carbon.
10. From (ii), P > Q; from (iii), Q > R. Therefore, the decreasing order of reactivity is **P > Q > R**. From (iv), since carbon cannot reduce the oxides of **P and Q**, both P and Q require a method stronger than carbon (such as electrolysis).
11. Mass of carbon = 750 × 0.04 = **30 kg**. Mass of iron = 750 − 30 = **720 kg** (96%). Check: 30 + 720 = 750 kg ✅. With 4% carbon, pig iron is **brittle** and shatters under impact, so its carbon content must be reduced to make tough steel.
12. (a) **False** ❌: Slag is **less dense** than molten iron and **floats on top** of it. (b) **False** ❌: Carbon reduces only middle-group oxides, not top-group oxides like Al₂O₃ or MgO. (c) **True** ✅ (for study only). (d) **True** ✅: CO gas is the primary reducing agent in the blast furnace. (e) **False** ❌: Sodium forms at the **cathode** (Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na); chlorine gas forms at the anode.
13. Blowing hot air in at the bottom burns coke (C + O₂ → CO₂) right where **maximum heat** is needed to keep the iron and slag molten at the base of the furnace. Second, as that hot CO₂ rises through the overlying bed of red-hot coke, it forms **CO gas** (CO₂ + C → 2CO), which then rises through the descending iron ore and reduces it. For NaCl electrolysis: in **solid** NaCl, ions are locked in a rigid crystal lattice and cannot move to carry current; when **molten**, the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are free to move to the electrodes (Class 9, Chemical Bonding Topic 4).
