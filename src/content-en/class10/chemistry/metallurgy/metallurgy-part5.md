# Topic 5: The Story of Aluminium: The Baeyer and Hall-Héroult Processes

In the previous topic, we saw that how we extract a metal from its oxide depends on its **position in the reactivity series**: low-reactivity metals by simple heating, moderately reactive metals by reduction with carbon. However, metals sitting near the **top of the series** (Na, Mg, Al) hold on to oxygen so tightly that carbon cannot strip their oxygen away. For them, only **electrolysis** is powerful enough. Today we will follow the complete industrial journey of the most widely used metal in that top group: **aluminium**, tracing it from reddish-brown bauxite rock all the way to a shiny beverage can. There are two major stages: first **purifying bauxite into white alumina (the Baeyer process)**, and then **electrolysing molten alumina to obtain aluminium metal (the Hall-Héroult process)**. Along the way, we will answer a practical everyday question: why is recycling an aluminium can such a big deal for saving energy?

## The Return of the Soda Can

At the school community fair, a tall green recycling bin stood beside the refreshment stand with a bold sign: **"DROP EMPTY ALUMINIUM CANS HERE!"** Ms. Bennett was walking past with her niece Maya, who tossed her empty sparkling-water can into the slot and paused.

"Aunt Claire, what actually happens to this can after it gets recycled? And how do they make aluminium in the first place? It's feather-light, super shiny, and it never rusts like steel."

Ms. Bennett smiled. "Maya, aluminium has one of the best stories in chemistry. In the earth's crust, aluminium is actually the **most abundant metal** of all—making up about 8% of the crust by mass! Yet only a hundred and fifty years ago, pure aluminium was considered more precious than silver, and emperors set out aluminium cutlery for their most honoured guests."

"More precious than silver?! If there's so much of it in the ground, why was it so rare and expensive?"

"Because aluminium is **never found free** in nature. It is always locked up tightly with oxygen as **Al₂O₃**, and the aluminium–oxygen bond is so strong that heating it with carbon in a furnace does nothing at all. Remember our reactivity ladder from Topic 4: aluminium sits high near the top, so the only way to free it on a large scale is with electricity—and a huge amount of electricity at that."

"So tossing this can into the recycling bin really does save all that electricity!" Maya said, her eyes widening.

"Wait till you see the exact numbers," Ms. Bennett said. "First, let's see how engineers turn reddish-brown bauxite clay into a gleaming metal—and then we'll calculate just how much power your single recycled can saves."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bauxite** | The principal ore of aluminium, a reddish-brown rock containing hydrated alumina (Al₂O₃·2H₂O) mixed with impurities such as Fe₂O₃ and SiO₂ | Mined in Australia, Guinea, Jamaica, India |
| **Alumina** | Pure anhydrous aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃, a white powder | Feedstock for the Hall-Héroult electrolysis cell |
| **Baeyer process** | The chemical leaching process that purifies raw bauxite into pure alumina (Al₂O₃) using hot concentrated NaOH | Stage 1 of aluminium extraction |
| **Red mud** | The insoluble reddish-brown waste (mainly Fe₂O₃ and other impurities) filtered off during the Baeyer process | Gangue residue from bauxite |
| **Seeding** | Adding tiny crystals of pure Al(OH)₃ to the cooled, diluted sodium aluminate solution to trigger precipitation of fresh Al(OH)₃ | Middle step of the Baeyer process |
| **Cryolite** | Sodium hexafluoroaluminate, Na₃AlF₆, a fluoride mineral that melts and dissolves alumina at a much lower temperature | Solvent bath in the Hall-Héroult cell |
| **Hall-Héroult process** | The industrial electrolysis of alumina dissolved in molten cryolite to produce liquid aluminium | Stage 2 of aluminium extraction |
| **Cathode / Anode** | Cathode (−): electrode where cations gain electrons (reduction); Anode (+): electrode where anions lose electrons (oxidation) | Carbon tank lining (cathode) and suspended carbon blocks (anodes) |

## Part A: The Baeyer Process: From Bauxite to Pure Alumina (For Study Only)

Raw bauxite cannot be fed straight into an electrolysis cell because it contains impurities like **iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃) and silica (SiO₂)**. If Fe₂O₃ were left in the bath during electrolysis, iron would deposit right alongside the aluminium and ruin the metal's purity and lightness. Therefore, bauxite is first purified chemically. This is the exact **leaching** step we previewed in Topic 2, built around the **amphoteric** behaviour of Al₂O₃ from Chapter 2.

> **Safety Note:** Hot, concentrated sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution under pressure is intensely corrosive and causes severe chemical burns and blindness on contact. The entire industrial process below is **for study only** and is never performed in a school lab.

**Step 1: Digestion in hot, concentrated NaOH.** Finely ground bauxite is heated under pressure with hot, concentrated sodium hydroxide solution. Because **Al₂O₃ is amphoteric** (reacting with both acids and strong bases, as seen in Chapter 2), it reacts with NaOH and dissolves:

Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O

(The water of crystallisation in bauxite, 2H₂O, simply joins the water of the solution.) The **sodium aluminate (NaAlO₂)** formed dissolves completely in water. In contrast, **Fe₂O₃ is a basic oxide**, not amphoteric: it does not react with NaOH and remains undissolved as a reddish-brown solid.

**Step 2: Filtration.** The hot mixture is filtered. The dissolved NaAlO₂ passes through into the clear liquid **filtrate**, while the insoluble **red mud** (Fe₂O₃ and other insoluble impurities) stays behind on the filter.

**Step 3: Dilution, cooling, and seeding (precipitation).** The clear filtrate is diluted with water, cooled, and **seeded** with a small amount of freshly prepared **Al(OH)₃ crystals**. The dissolved sodium aluminate hydrolyses to form a thick white precipitate of pure **aluminium hydroxide, Al(OH)₃**:

NaAlO₂ + 2H₂O → Al(OH)₃ + NaOH

Notice an important bonus: **sodium hydroxide (NaOH) is regenerated** in the liquid! It is concentrated and recycled back to Step 1 to digest the next batch of bauxite.

**Step 4: Filtration and calcination.** The white Al(OH)₃ precipitate is filtered off, washed, and heated strongly (~1000–1200 °C) in a kiln (calcination, Topic 3). It loses water vapour to leave behind pure, snow-white **alumina (Al₂O₃)**:

2Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂O

```
   Crushed bauxite ore (Al₂O₃·2H₂O + Fe₂O₃, SiO₂ ...)
        :
        v
   +------------------------------------------+
   : 1. Digest with hot, concentrated NaOH    :
   :    Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH -> 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O        :
   +------------------------------------------+
        :
        v
   +------------------------------------------+
   : 2. Filter the mixture                    : ---> Red mud removed
   :    (NaAlO₂ in filtrate; Fe₂O₃ on filter) :      (Fe₂O₃ & insolubles)
   +------------------------------------------+

        v
   +------------------------------------------+

   +------------------------------------------+

```

## Part B: The Hall-Héroult Process: From Alumina to Aluminium (For Study Only)

Now we have pure white Al₂O₃. Because **carbon cannot reduce Al₂O₃** (Topic 4), the only practical way to extract the metal is **electrolysis**. For decades, chemists struggled to do this economically until **1886**, when two 22-year-old inventors working independently on opposite sides of the Atlantic—**Charles Martin Hall in the USA** and **Paul Héroult in France**—discovered the exact same solution in the same year! In their honour, it is called the **Hall-Héroult process**.

> **Safety Note:** Molten fluoride salts at ~950 °C, molten aluminium, and massive electric currents are industrial hazards: this process is **strictly for study only**.

### Why Not Just Melt Pure Alumina and Electrolyse It?

Pure alumina (Al₂O₃) has an extremely high melting point of **about 2072 °C**. Keeping a giant tank of pure Al₂O₃ molten above 2072 °C would consume a ruinous amount of energy and rapidly destroy furnace linings. Yet as you learned in Class 9 ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)), **solid** ionic compounds cannot conduct electricity because their ions are locked in place; the alumina **must be liquid (molten or dissolved)** so its Al³⁺ and O²⁻ ions are free to move. How can we get mobile Al³⁺ and O²⁻ ions without heating the cell to 2072 °C?

### The Magic of Cryolite

Hall and Héroult solved the puzzle using **cryolite, Na₃AlF₆** (often mixed with a little fluorspar, CaF₂). Pure alumina **dissolves readily in molten cryolite**, very much like table salt dissolving in warm water! Using molten cryolite as a solvent gives three huge advantages:

1. **Drops the operating temperature dramatically:** The mixture melts and operates at **about 950 °C instead of 2072 °C**, saving vast amounts of heat and electrical energy.
2. **Improves electrical conductivity:** Molten cryolite is rich in mobile ions and conducts electricity much better than pure molten alumina alone.
3. **Allows continuous operation:** Dissolved Al₂O₃ splits into mobile Al³⁺ and O²⁻ ions throughout the bath, and fresh alumina powder can be fed in periodically as aluminium is tapped off.

Note carefully: cryolite is **not** the ore being reduced to make aluminium; it serves as the **molten solvent bath**.

### Inside the Hall-Héroult Cell

The electrolytic cell consists of a large, shallow **rectangular steel tank**. The inside walls and floor of the tank are lined with thick blocks of **carbon (graphite)**, and this entire **carbon lining serves as the cathode (−)**. Suspended from above and dipping into the molten bath are heavy **carbon (graphite) rods or blocks**, which serve as the **anodes (+)**. Inside the tank sits the molten bath of cryolite with alumina dissolved in it at ~950 °C.

```
           (+)         (+)         (+)       <- positive power busbar
           ##          ##          ##        <- carbon blocks = ANODES (+)
   +-------##----------##----------##-------+  <- outer steel tank
   :# =====##==========##==========##===== #:
   :# ~~~~~##~~~~~~~~~~##~~~~~~~~~~##~~~~~ #:
   :# ~~ molten cryolite + dissolved Al₂O₃ #:
   :# ~~ at ~950 °C; O₂ formed at anodes   #:
   :# ~~ reacts with hot carbon to form CO₂#:
   :# ------------------------------------ #:
   :# molten aluminium (denser, at bottom) #:  ---> tapped off periodically
   :########################################:
   +----------------------------------------+
     # = carbon: suspended blocks are ANODES (+); tank lining is CATHODE (−)
     = = crust of solid alumina on top (insulates heat)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Cross-section of a Hall-Héroult cell (schematic, not to scale): the steel tank is lined with carbon (`#`), which acts as the cathode (−); thick carbon blocks dipping from above act as the anodes (+). Between them is the molten bath of cryolite and dissolved alumina at ~950 °C. Denser molten aluminium sinks to the bottom carbon floor and is tapped off periodically.

### What Happens at the Electrodes? (A First Look)

In the molten cryolite bath, dissolved alumina dissociates into mobile ions: **Al³⁺** and **O²⁻**. When direct current is switched on:

- **At the cathode (carbon tank lining, −):** Positive aluminium ions (Al³⁺) are attracted to the negative lining and gain electrons to form liquid aluminium metal:
  **Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al**
  Because molten aluminium is slightly **denser** than the molten cryolite bath at 950 °C, it sinks to the floor of the cell and collects as a silvery liquid pool, which is siphoned or **tapped off** from the bottom.
- **At the anode (suspended carbon blocks, +):** Negative oxide ions (O²⁻) are attracted to the positive carbon blocks and lose electrons to form oxygen gas:
  **2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻**

To balance the electrons transferred between cathode and anode: producing 4 Al atoms requires 4 × 3 = 12 electrons, and oxidising 6 O²⁻ ions into 3 O₂ molecules releases 3 × 4 = 12 electrons. Combining the two half-reactions gives the overall electrolysis reaction:

4Al³⁺ + 6O²⁻ → 4Al + 3O₂,   or simply   **2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂**.

Atom-count check (Check): Al: 4 = 4, O: 6 = 6 ✅. Electron balance: 4 × 3 = 12 electrons gained at the cathode = 3 × 4 = 12 electrons lost at the anode ✅.

(The deeper rules of electrolysis—electrolytes, ion migration, and selective discharge—will be studied in full in our next chapter, **Electrolysis**. For now, remember that aluminium metal forms at the **cathode**.)

### Why Do the Carbon Anodes Burn Away and Need Replacing?

At the operating temperature of **~950 °C**, the hot oxygen gas (O₂) liberated right on the surface of the positive carbon anodes immediately reacts with the **carbon anodes themselves**, burning them into carbon dioxide (along with some carbon monoxide):

C + O₂ → CO₂

Because of this reaction, the suspended **carbon anode blocks are gradually eaten away** and must be lowered and **replaced every few weeks**. If we combine the electrolysis equation with the burning of the carbon anodes (where 3 O₂ molecules consume 3 C atoms), the net overall cell equation is:

**2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂**

Check: Al: 4 = 4, O: 6 = 3 × 2 = 6, C: 3 = 3 ✅. Mass check: 2 × 102 + 3 × 12 = 204 + 36 = 240, and 4 × 27 + 3 × 44 = 108 + 132 = 240 ✅.

> **Quick Check:** In the Hall-Héroult cell, molten aluminium collects at the carbon-lined cathode on the floor, while oxygen released at the suspended carbon anodes burns the anodes into CO₂. ✅

## Part C: So Much Electricity, So Much Scrap: The Math of Recycling

Extracting primary aluminium from alumina takes an enormous amount of electrical energy—typically **about 13 to 15 kWh of electricity for every 1 kilogram of aluminium produced** (in calculations below, we will use a representative value of **14 kWh/kg**). That is why aluminium smelters are almost always built near large hydroelectric or power stations!

In contrast, **recycling scrap aluminium** (such as used beverage cans, old window frames, and foil) by simply melting it down takes **only about 5% of the energy** needed to make new aluminium from bauxite!

```
   Electrical energy needed to produce 1 kg of aluminium (kWh)

   From bauxite : ########################################  14 kWh
   From scrap   : ##                                          0.7 kWh

   (Each # represents 0.35 kWh; 5% of 14 kWh = 0.7 kWh)
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Comparing the energy required to produce 1 kg of primary aluminium from bauxite (~14 kWh) versus 1 kg of recycled aluminium from scrap (~0.7 kWh). Recycling saves roughly **95% of the energy**!

Why is the saving so dramatic? Because in an empty soda can, the aluminium atoms have **already been freed from oxygen**: you skip mining, skip the Baeyer process, and skip the electricity-hungry Hall-Héroult electrolysis completely! You simply shred and melt the metal at ~660 °C and cast it into fresh sheets. That is why the green recycling bin at Maya's school fair is really an **energy-saving bank**.

## Part E: Solved Examples (Quantitative Calculations)

Atomic masses: Al = 27, O = 16, C = 12, H = 1. Formula mass of Al₂O₃ = 2 × 27 + 3 × 16 = 102; mass of 2Al in it = 54. All mass-to-mass calculations follow directly from balanced equation ratios.

**Example 1 (Aluminium from alumina):** What mass of aluminium is obtained from 306 tonnes of pure alumina (Al₂O₃)?

- Equation: 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂. Mass ratio: 2 × 102 = 204 parts Al₂O₃ → 4 × 27 = 108 parts Al (which simplifies to 102 parts Al₂O₃ → 54 parts Al).
- Scaling factor: 306 ÷ 102 = 3. Therefore, mass of Al = 3 × 54 = **162 tonnes**.

**Check:** 162 ÷ 306 × 100 = 52.94% ✅ (matches the mass percentage of Al in Al₂O₃: 54 ÷ 102 = 52.94%).

**Example 2 (Alumina and bauxite needed for 1 tonne of aluminium):** What mass of pure alumina is needed to produce 1 tonne of aluminium? And what mass of pure hydrated bauxite mineral (Al₂O₃·2H₂O) does that correspond to?

- Since 54 parts of Al require 102 parts of Al₂O₃, 1 tonne of Al requires 102 ÷ 54 = **1.889 tonnes** of alumina (approx.).
- Formula mass of Al₂O₃·2H₂O = 102 + 2 × 18 = 138. Since 102 parts of alumina come from 138 parts of pure Al₂O₃·2H₂O, mass of pure bauxite mineral = 1.889 × 138 ÷ 102 = **2.556 tonnes** (approx.).
- *(In real life, because raw mined bauxite also contains red-mud gangue like Fe₂O₃ and SiO₂, roughly 4 to 5 tonnes of raw bauxite ore are mined per tonne of aluminium.)*

**Check:** 2.556 × 102 ÷ 138 = 1.889 ✅; 1.889 × 54 ÷ 102 = 1.000 ✅.

**Example 3 (Carbon anode consumption):** Using the overall equation 2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂, calculate the theoretical minimum mass of carbon anode burnt and the mass of CO₂ produced for every 1 tonne of aluminium extracted.

- Mass ratio from equation: 4Al = 108 parts ↔ 3C = 36 parts ↔ 3CO₂ = 132 parts.
- For 1 tonne of Al, mass of carbon burnt = 36 ÷ 108 = **0.333 tonnes = 333 kg** (approx.).
- Mass of CO₂ formed = 132 ÷ 108 = **1.222 tonnes** (approx.).

**Check:** Conservation of mass in equation: 2 × 102 + 36 = 240, and 108 + 132 = 240 ✅. Note: **333 kg of carbon per tonne of Al is the theoretical minimum** when only CO₂ is formed. In actual smelters, about **400 to 450 kg** of carbon anode is consumed per tonne of Al because some carbon escapes as CO and the hot tops of the anodes also oxidise slightly in air.

**Example 4 (Energy saved by recycling a single soda can):** Suppose an empty aluminium can has a mass of 15 g. Taking 14 kWh/kg to make primary aluminium from bauxite and 5% of that to recycle scrap, how much electrical energy is saved by recycling one can?

- Mass of 1 can = 15 g = 0.015 kg.
- Energy for a new can from bauxite = 0.015 × 14 = **0.21 kWh**.
- Energy for a recycled can (5%) = 0.21 × 0.05 = **0.0105 kWh**.
- **Energy saved** = 0.21 − 0.0105 = **0.1995 kWh** (roughly **0.20 kWh**)—enough electricity to keep a 10 W LED study lamp shining for **nearly 20 hours** from recycling just **one** can!

**Check:** 10 W = 0.01 kW, so 0.1995 kWh ÷ 0.01 kW = 19.95 hours ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Cryolite is the ore from which aluminium is extracted."** Incorrect: **bauxite** is the ore of aluminium, and **alumina (Al₂O₃)** is the compound electrolysed. Look at the electrolysis equation: 2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂. Cryolite (Na₃AlF₆) does not get consumed to make the aluminium; it acts as the **molten solvent** that dissolves alumina at ~950 °C, just as water dissolves salt without being salt itself.

**Mistake 2: "The Baeyer process produces metallic aluminium."** Incorrect: the final product of the Baeyer process is **pure white alumina powder (Al₂O₃)**, not aluminium metal. In all four steps of Part A (NaAlO₂ → Al(OH)₃ → Al₂O₃), aluminium remains in the +3 oxidation state bonded to oxygen. Metallic aluminium is produced only in the **Hall-Héroult electrolysis cell** at the cathode (Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al).

**Mistake 3: "The carbon anodes in the Hall-Héroult cell last forever."** Incorrect: the suspended carbon anodes are **steadily burnt away** by the hot oxygen gas liberated at their surface (C + O₂ → CO₂). As Example 3 showed, producing 1 tonne of aluminium burns away at least 333 kg of carbon (and over 400 kg in practice), so the anode blocks must be replaced regularly.

**Mistake 4: "Recycling aluminium uses zero energy."** It uses energy—collecting, sorting, shredding, and melting scrap at 660 °C takes about 0.7 kWh/kg—but that is only **about 5%** of the ~14 kWh/kg needed to extract new aluminium from ore, saving roughly **95%** of the energy.

## Quick Recap

- Because aluminium sits near the top of the reactivity series, its oxide cannot be reduced by carbon; it is extracted by **electrolysis** (Topic 4).
- **Baeyer process (purifying bauxite to alumina):** Digest crushed bauxite in hot concentrated NaOH (amphoteric Al₂O₃ dissolves as NaAlO₂), filter off insoluble **red mud** (Fe₂O₃, etc.), dilute/cool/seed the filtrate to precipitate pure Al(OH)₃ (regenerating NaOH for reuse), and calcine Al(OH)₃ to obtain pure white **Al₂O₃**.
- **Hall-Héroult process (electrolysing alumina):** Dissolve pure Al₂O₃ (m.p. ~2072 °C) in **molten cryolite (Na₃AlF₆)** so the bath operates at **~950 °C** and conducts well. Carbon tank lining = **cathode (−)**; suspended carbon blocks = **anodes (+)**.
- Electrode reactions: Cathode: **Al³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Al** (molten Al sinks to the floor); Anode: **2O²⁻ → O₂ + 4e⁻**, followed by hot oxygen burning the carbon anodes: **C + O₂ → CO₂**.
- Overall equations: **2Al₂O₃ → 4Al + 3O₂**, and including anode consumption: **2Al₂O₃ + 3C → 4Al + 3CO₂**.
- Primary aluminium requires ~13–15 kWh/kg of electricity; recycling scrap requires only **~5%** of that energy (~95% energy saving).
- Stoichiometry shortcut: every **102 parts of Al₂O₃** contain **54 parts of Al**.
- Hot concentrated NaOH and molten fluoride baths at 950 °C are industrial processes **for study only**.

(iii) Alumina dissolves to provide mobile Al³⁺ and O²⁻ ions throughout the bath for smooth, continuous electrolysis.
