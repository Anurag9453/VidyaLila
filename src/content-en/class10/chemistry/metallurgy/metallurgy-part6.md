# Topic 6: Refining and Alloys: Purifying Metals and Blending Them for Strength

Across the first five topics, we traced the long journey from raw ore in the ground to extracted metal: concentration, roasting or calcination, reduction, and—for top-group metals like aluminium—electrolysis. However, the crude metal obtained right after reduction is almost always **slightly impure**: it retains traces of unreduced gangue, dissolved gases, carbon, or other metals that were present in the ore. The final stage of extraction is **refining** (purifying the crude metal). Once we have a pure metal, a surprising new question arises: is a 100% pure metal always the best choice for everyday use? Often the answer is no! For most engineering and household uses, a carefully blended mixture of a metal with one or more other elements—an **alloy**—outperforms the pure metal by far. In this topic we will explore both sides of the story: how we purify metals, and why we blend them.

## The Marching Band, the Brass Horns, and the Bronze Bell

During the town's autumn festival parade, **Mr. Rivera** walked along the main square with his nephew **Noah**. Leading the procession was a lively marching band: trumpets, trombones, and tubas gleamed like polished gold in the afternoon sun. As the parade passed the historic town hall tower, the heavy tower bell struck the hour—*BONG!*—and its deep, clear note echoed across the square for several seconds.

Noah looked up. "Uncle Leo, why do those horns shine like gold? And why does that tower bell ring for so long?"

Mr. Rivera smiled. "Those horns are made of **brass**—a blend of copper and zinc. And that tower bell is cast from **bronze**—a blend of copper and tin. Even the gold wedding band on my finger is **18-carat gold**—a blend of gold with a little copper and silver!"

"A blend? Why not make the horns out of pure copper, or the ring out of 100% pure gold?" Noah asked.

"That is the exact question at the heart of today's lesson," Mr. Rivera replied. "First, let's see why some uses—like electrical wiring—demand **ultra-pure** metal and how we refine it. Then we'll see why instruments, bells, bridges, and jewellery need **alloys**."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Refining** | Purifying a crude extracted metal to remove remaining impurities | Electrolytic refining of copper |
| **Anode** | The electrode connected to the positive (+) terminal of the direct-current power supply | Thick block of impure copper in a refining cell |
| **Cathode** | The electrode connected to the negative (−) terminal of the direct-current power supply | Thin starter sheet of pure copper in a refining cell |
| **Electrolyte** | An ionic solution (or molten salt) that conducts electricity and in which the electrodes are immersed | Acidified aqueous copper(II) sulphate solution |
| **Anode mud (slime)** | Insoluble impurities (including precious metals like gold, silver, and platinum) that settle beneath the anode during electrolytic refining | Valuable by-product of copper refining |
| **Distillation** | Purifying a low-boiling metal by boiling it into vapour and condensing the pure vapour | Refining zinc and mercury |
| **Liquation** | Purifying a low-melting metal by heating it on a sloping hearth so the pure metal melts and flows away from higher-melting impurities | Refining tin and lead |
| **Zone refining** | Passing a narrow moving heater ring along a rod so impurities travel with the molten zone to one end | Producing ultra-pure silicon and germanium for computer chips |
| **Alloy** | A homogeneous mixture (solid solution) of a metal with one or more other metals or non-metals, prepared by melting them together | Steel, brass, bronze, duralumin |
| **Carat** | A measure of gold purity defined as the number of parts of pure gold by mass out of 24 parts of alloy | 22-carat gold = 22 parts gold and 2 parts other metals |

## Part A: Why Refine Metals, and Which Methods Are Used?

Crude metal coming out of a reduction furnace still contains small amounts of impurities. For some rough castings a little impurity may not matter, but for many applications extreme purity is essential. For example, copper used in **electrical wiring** must be **more than 99.9% pure**—even a tiny trace of arsenic or iron impurity cuts copper's electrical conductivity dramatically. And silicon used for **computer microchips** must be refined until less than one impurity atom remains in a billion silicon atoms!

Just like ore concentration in Topic 2, the choice of refining method depends on **which physical or chemical property differs** between the metal and its impurities:

| Refining Method | Property Difference Used | Typical Metals Refined | How It Works in One Line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrolytic refining | Selective electrochemical dissolution and deposition | Copper, zinc, silver, gold | Impure metal is made the anode; pure metal deposits on the cathode (Part B) |
| Distillation | Metal has a much lower boiling point than its impurities | Zinc (b.p. ~907 °C), mercury (b.p. ~357 °C) | Crude metal is boiled into vapour and condensed in a separate receiver, leaving non-volatile impurities behind |
| Liquation | Metal has a much lower melting point than its impurities | Tin (m.p. ~232 °C), lead (m.p. ~327 °C) | Crude metal is heated on a gently sloping furnace floor; the low-melting metal melts and runs down into a basin while solid impurities remain behind |
| Zone refining | Impurities are more soluble in the molten liquid than in the solid crystal | Ultra-pure silicon and germanium (for semiconductors/chips) | A narrow ring heater moves slowly along a solid rod, sweeping impurities inside the travelling molten zone to one end |

> **How zone refining works in brief:** A circular induction heater is placed around one end of a rod of impure silicon, melting a narrow slice (zone) of the rod. As the heater slowly moves along the length of the rod, pure silicon crystallises behind the moving zone while the impurities stay dissolved in the advancing liquid slice. After several passes, virtually all impurities are swept to the far tip of the rod, which is simply sawn off and discarded.

> **Safety Note:** Mercury and lead are **toxic heavy metals** whose vapours and dust are hazardous to health. Distillation of mercury and liquation of lead are industrial processes **strictly for study only**.

## Part B: Electrolytic Refining of Copper (A First Look)

The most widely used industrial method for purifying crude copper ("blister copper" from Topic 4) is **electrolytic refining**. It uses an electric current to dissolve copper from an impure block and plate it out as pure copper onto a starter sheet. The full electrochemical theory (electrolytes and selective discharge of ions) will be covered in **Chapter 4: Electrolysis**; here we focus on how the refining tank works (**for study only**—copper(II) sulphate solution is harmful if swallowed and acidic).

**Cell set-up:**
- **Anode (+):** A thick cast slab of **impure copper**.
- **Cathode (−):** A thin starter sheet of **pure copper**.
- **Electrolyte:** An aqueous solution of **copper(II) sulphate (CuSO₄)** acidified with a little dilute sulphuric acid (the acid boosts electrical conductivity and prevents hydrolysis).

```
                direct-current supply
       (+) o------[ battery ]------o (-)
           :                   :
+----------:-------------------:-------------+
:      +---:---+           +---:---+         :
:      :       :   Cu²⁺    :       :         :
:      : ANODE :  ------>  :CATHODE:         :
:      :impure :   Cu²⁺    : pure  :         :
:      : copper:  ------>  : copper:         :
:      :(thick :           :(thin  :         :
:      : slab) :           : sheet):         :
:      +-------+           +-------+         :
:  CuSO₄ solution (acidified with H₂SO₄)     :
:                                            :
:     . . . . .  <-- anode mud (Au, Ag, Pt)  :
+--------------------------------------------+
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Electrolytic refining cell for copper. As current flows, the thick impure copper anode (+) gradually dissolves as Cu²⁺ ions into the solution; an equal number of Cu²⁺ ions migrate to the thin pure copper cathode (−) and plate out as pure copper metal. Unreactive precious-metal impurities settle below the anode as **anode mud**.

**Electrode reactions (a first look):**

- **At the anode (+):** **Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻** (copper atoms in the impure slab lose electrons and dissolve into the solution as Cu²⁺ ions; the anode gradually grows thinner).
- **At the cathode (−):** **Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu** (Cu²⁺ ions from the solution gain electrons at the cathode surface and deposit as pure copper atoms; the cathode gradually grows thicker).

Check charge and atom balance: in both half-equations, net charge is 0 = (+2) + (−2) ✅, and Cu atoms are 1 = 1 ✅. Notice the net effect: **copper is transferred atom-by-atom from the impure anode to the pure cathode**, while the concentration of Cu²⁺ in the electrolyte stays essentially constant!

**What happens to the impurities in the anode?**
- **Less reactive metals (gold, silver, platinum)** sitting below copper in the reactivity series do not oxidise or dissolve at the anode voltage. As the copper around them dissolves away, these tiny particles of precious metal simply fall to the floor of the tank right underneath the anode as **anode mud (anode slime)**. Far from being waste, recovering gold, silver, and platinum from anode mud often pays for a large share of the electricity used in the refinery!
- **More reactive metals (zinc, iron, nickel)** sitting above copper in the reactivity series dissolve from the anode into the solution as ions (Zn²⁺, Fe²⁺), but at the operating voltage they stay dissolved in the electrolyte and do not plate out onto the cathode (you will learn why in Chapter 4).

The copper collected at the cathode is **99.95% to 99.99% pure**—ideal for electrical cables and electronics.

## Part C: What Is an Alloy and Why Do We Make Alloys?

Recall from Class 9 ([is-matter-around-us-pure-part6.md](../../../class9/chemistry/is-matter-around-us-pure/is-matter-around-us-pure-part6.md)) that an **alloy is a homogeneous mixture** (usually a solid solution), not a chemical compound: it is made by melting the primary metal, dissolving definite proportions of other metals (or non-metals like carbon) in the liquid, and cooling it to solidify. Because its composition can be varied over a range, an alloy is classified as a mixture.

**Why do engineers prefer alloys over pure metals?**

| Advantage of Alloying | How It Helps | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|
| Greater hardness and tensile strength | Atoms of different sizes lock crystal layers against slipping (Part D) | Structural steel is far stronger than pure iron; bronze is much harder than pure copper |
| Resistance to corrosion and rusting | Alloying elements form a tough, self-healing protective surface film | Stainless steel cutlery and sinks do not rust like ordinary iron |
| Lower melting point | Blending disrupts regular crystal packing, lowering the melting temperature | Solder melts at a lower temperature than either pure tin or pure lead |
| Improved appearance or acoustic resonance | Changes colour, lustre, or elastic ringing quality | Brass looks like bright gold; bronze bells ring with a long, clear tone |
| Tailored engineering properties | Combines lightness with high strength | Duralumin is nearly as light as pure aluminium but strong enough for airliner frames |

One important trade-off: **alloys generally have lower electrical conductivity than pure metals**. That is why electrical wiring inside walls is always made of refined **pure copper**, never brass or bronze.

Look closely at **steel** in Figure 6.2: adding just **0.1% to 1.5% carbon** transforms soft pure iron into the world's most versatile structural material! Low-carbon ("mild") steel (~0.1–0.25% C) is tough and easily shaped into car bodies and girders; high-carbon steel (~0.8–1.5% C) is extra hard and holds a sharp edge for cutting tools and springs. Remember from Topic 4 that blast-furnace pig iron starts with ~4% carbon (making it brittle), so steelmaking works by burning off most of that carbon down to the desired 0.1–1.5% range.

## Part D: Why Is an Alloy Harder Than the Pure Metal? (An Atomic Picture)

In a **pure metal**, every atom is the **exact same size**. The atoms pack together in neat, flat, regular rows and layers—like a stack of identical marbles or smooth playing cards. When you hammer or pull on a pure metal, one flat layer of atoms **slides (slips) easily** over the layer beneath it. That is why pure iron, pure copper, and pure 24-carat gold are comparatively **soft and malleable**.

In an **alloy**, we introduce atoms of a second element that have a **different atomic radius** (either larger or smaller than the host metal atoms). These differently sized atoms wedge into the crystal lattice and **jolt the flat layers out of alignment**, acting like keys or speed bumps that **jam the layers and stop them from sliding past one another easily**. Because the layers cannot slip without a much larger force, the alloy is **much harder and stronger** than the pure metal!

```
PURE METAL                       ALLOY
(all atoms identical size, o)    (contains larger/different atoms, O)

  ----> layer slides easily        ----> X layer is jammed from sliding
 o o o o o o o o                  o o o O o o o o
 o o o o o o o o                  o o o o o O o o
 o o o o o o o o                  o O o o o o o o

Smooth flat rows slip easily     Different-sized atoms block slip
```

> **Figure 6.3** – Atomic model comparing a pure metal (24 identical atoms `o`) and an alloy (21 host atoms `o` and 3 larger foreign atoms `O`). In the pure metal, uniform layers slide over each other easily (softer). In the alloy, the differently sized atoms distort the layers and block them from slipping (harder and stronger). Remember: this is a physical packing effect—the alloy is still a mixture, not a chemical compound.

## Part E: Calculating Gold Carats and Hallmarks

Pure gold is so soft that a ring made of 100% pure gold would easily bend out of shape or scratch in daily wear. To make gold durable enough for jewellery, goldsmiths alloy it with small amounts of **copper or silver**. The purity of gold is expressed in **carats**, where pure gold is defined as **24 carats** (meaning 24 parts out of 24 are gold):

- **24-carat gold:** 24 parts gold out of 24 = **100% pure gold** (used for investment coins and bullion bars, too soft for most everyday rings).
- **22-carat gold:** 22 parts gold and 2 parts alloying metal (Cu/Ag) = 22 ÷ 24 × 100 = **91.67% gold**.
- **18-carat gold:** 18 parts gold and 6 parts alloying metal = 18 ÷ 24 × 100 = **75.0% gold**.
- **14-carat gold:** 14 parts gold and 10 parts alloying metal = 14 ÷ 24 × 100 = **58.33% gold**.

Official jewellery carries a tiny laser-etched or stamped **fineness hallmark** that states the gold content in **parts per 1000**:
- 22-carat gold is stamped **916** (916 parts gold per 1000, or 91.6%).
- 18-carat gold is stamped **750** (750 parts gold per 1000, or 75.0%).
- 14-carat gold is stamped **585** (585 parts gold per 1000, or 58.5%).

**Activity 6.1: Reading a Jewellery Hallmark (At home with an adult).** With an adult's permission and supervision, use a magnifying glass (or a smartphone macro camera) to look inside the band of a gold ring or clasp of a necklace for a tiny three-digit number such as **916**, **750**, or **585**. Never scratch, bend, or apply any chemical to jewellery. Divide the stamped number by 10 to find the percentage of gold, and multiply `(number ÷ 1000) × 24` to confirm its carat rating!

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Composition of a Brass Trumpet

**Question:** A 250 g brass horn mouthpiece assembly contains 70% copper and 30% zinc by mass. Find the mass of each metal in it.

**Solution:**
- Mass of copper = 250 × 70 ÷ 100 = **175 g**.
- Mass of zinc = 250 × 30 ÷ 100 = **75 g**.

**Check:** 175 + 75 = 250 g ✅ and 70% + 30% = 100% ✅.

---

### Example 2. Gold Content in a 22-Carat Ring

**Question:** A 22-carat gold ring has a mass of 10 g. What mass of pure gold and what mass of alloying metal (such as copper or silver) does it contain?

**Solution:**
- Percentage of gold = 22 ÷ 24 × 100 = 91.67%.
- Mass of pure gold = 10 × 22 ÷ 24 = **9.17 g** (approx.).
- Mass of alloying metal = 10 − 9.17 = **0.83 g** (approx., i.e., 2 ÷ 24 × 10 = 0.83 g).

**Check:** 9.17 + 0.83 = 10.00 g ✅. The exact same 22/24 ratio applies to any 22-carat item regardless of its total weight.

---

### Example 3. Mass Balance in Electrolytic Copper Refining

**Question:** An impure copper anode slab in a refinery has a mass of 40 kg and contains 97.5% copper (the rest being impurities). Assuming the entire anode is consumed and all its copper plates onto the cathode, what mass of pure copper is gained by the cathode, and what total mass of impurities is removed (into the anode mud and solution)?

**Solution:**
- Mass of pure copper deposited on cathode = 40 × 97.5 ÷ 100 = **39 kg**.
- Total mass of impurities removed = 40 − 39 = **1 kg** (2.5%): unreactive metals (Au, Ag, Pt) settle as **anode mud**, while more reactive metals (Zn, Fe) stay dissolved in the electrolyte.

**Check:** 39 + 1 = 40 kg ✅, and 97.5% + 2.5% = 100% ✅. Every Cu atom oxidised at the anode (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻) corresponds to one Cu²⁺ ion reduced at the cathode (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu).

---

### Example 4. Carbon in Structural Steel

**Question:** A 2000 kg batch of high-carbon tool steel contains 0.8% carbon, the rest being iron. Find the mass of carbon, the mass of iron, and the percentage of iron in the steel.

**Solution:**
- Mass of carbon = 2000 × 0.8 ÷ 100 = **16 kg**.
- Mass of iron = 2000 − 16 = **1984 kg**.
- Percentage of iron = 100 − 0.8 = **99.2%**.

**Check:** 16 + 1984 = 2000 kg ✅. Just 16 kg of carbon atoms wedged into 1984 kg of iron transforms soft iron into hard tool steel!

---

### Example 5. Mixture or Compound? (Evidence from Gold Alloys)

**Question:** In Class 9 you saw that brass is a mixture because its copper-to-zinc ratio can vary. Now use 22-carat gold and 18-carat gold (both made of gold and copper) to prove that a gold alloy is a **mixture** and not a chemical compound.

**Solution:** In a true chemical compound, the elements are always combined in a **fixed ratio by mass** (for example, water always has H : O = 1 : 8 by mass). In 22-carat gold, the mass ratio of gold to copper is 22 : 2 = **11 : 1**; in 18-carat gold, the mass ratio is 18 : 6 = **3 : 1**. Because the mass ratio can be varied freely while still forming a uniform gold alloy, the alloy is a **homogeneous mixture**, not a compound.

**Check:** 22 ÷ 2 = 11 whereas 18 ÷ 6 = 3; the ratios are completely different ✅.

### Example 6. Paper Alloy-Formulation Task

**Task:** An instrument maker needs 2 kg (2000 g) of cartridge brass containing 63% copper and 37% zinc, but currently has only 1 kg (1000 g) of pure copper in stock.

**(a)** What mass of copper and what mass of zinc are required to make the full 2 kg of brass?
- Copper needed = 2000 × 63 ÷ 100 = **1260 g** (1.26 kg).
- Zinc needed = 2000 − 1260 = **740 g**.

**(b)** Using only the 1000 g of copper available, what is the maximum mass of 63%-copper brass that can be made, and how much zinc should be melted with it?
- Since 1000 g of copper must represent 63% of the total alloy mass: maximum brass = 1000 ÷ 0.63 = **1587 g** (approx. 1.59 kg).
- Mass of zinc to add = 1587 − 1000 = **587 g** (approx.).

**Check:** (a) 1260 + 740 = 2000 g ✅. (b) 1000 ÷ 1587 × 100 = 63.0% ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "An alloy is a chemical compound formed between two metals."**
   As Example 5 showed, gold alloys can have a gold-to-copper mass ratio of 11 : 1 (22-carat) or 3 : 1 (18-carat), and brass can range from 60% to 70% copper. In a compound like H₂O, the mass ratio is fixed forever (1 : 8). Because alloys have variable composition and are made by physical melting and mixing, **an alloy is a homogeneous mixture**.
2. **Mistake 2: "24-carat gold is the best choice for everyday rings and chains."**
   100% pure (24-carat) gold is so soft that thin rings, clasps, and stone settings easily bend or wear away. Alloying it to **22-carat (91.67% gold)** or **18-carat (75% gold)** with a little copper or silver jams the crystal layers (Figure 6.3) so the jewellery holds its shape for decades.
3. **Mistake 3: "Anode mud in copper refining is worthless dirt that gets thrown away."**
   In Example 3, the 1 kg of impurities removed included **anode mud**—which contains unreactive precious metals like **gold, silver, and platinum** that were present in trace amounts in the copper ore! Refineries carefully collect and process every gram of anode mud to recover these valuable metals.
4. **Mistake 4: "In electrolytic refining, it doesn't matter which electrode is connected to (+) and which to (−)."**
   Suppose you accidentally connected the thin **pure** copper sheet to the positive (+) terminal (making it the anode) and the thick **impure** block to the negative (−) terminal. Then your pure copper sheet would dissolve (Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻) and plate onto the impure lump—the exact opposite of what you want! Always remember: **Impure = Anode (+); Pure = Cathode (−)**.
5. **Mistake 5: "The melting point of an alloy is always the average of the melting points of its component metals."**
   Solder proves this wrong! Pure tin melts at ~232 °C and pure lead melts at ~327 °C, yet a 60 : 40 tin–lead solder alloy melts at **~183–190 °C**—**lower than either pure metal!** That low melting point is why solder can join delicate electronic components without overheating them.
6. **Mistake 6: "Since alloys are stronger than pure metals, they must also be better electrical conductors."**
   The exact opposite is true: foreign atoms that jam crystal slip also scatter moving electrons, so **alloys have lower electrical conductivity** than pure metals. That is why household electrical wiring is made of **99.9%+ pure electrolytic copper**, never brass.

## Quick Recap

- Crude metals extracted by reduction still contain impurities and are **refined** when high purity is required.
- Refining methods match the property difference: **electrolytic refining** (copper, silver, gold), **distillation** (low-boiling zinc and mercury), **liquation** (low-melting tin and lead), and **zone refining** (ultra-pure silicon and germanium for microchips).
- **Electrolytic refining of copper:** Impure copper = **anode (+)**; thin pure copper sheet = **cathode (−)**; acidified CuSO₄ solution = **electrolyte**. Anode: Cu → Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻; Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. Unreactive gold, silver, and platinum fall to the bottom as valuable **anode mud**. (Full electrolysis theory in Chapter 4.)
- **An alloy is a homogeneous mixture**, not a compound (its composition can vary). Benefits: greater hardness/strength, corrosion resistance, lower melting point, or improved colour/sonority.
- Key alloys: **steel** (Fe + 0.1–1.5% C), **stainless steel** (Fe + Cr + Ni), **brass** (Cu + Zn), **bronze** (Cu + Sn), **solder** (Sn + Pb, or modern lead-free Sn/Ag/Cu), **duralumin** (Al + Cu, Mg, Mn), **amalgam** (alloy of mercury).
- Alloys are harder than pure metals because **atoms of different sizes block crystal layers from sliding** over one another.
- **24-carat gold** = 100% pure; **22-carat gold** = 22 ÷ 24 × 100 = **91.67% gold** (hallmark **916**); **18-carat** = **75% gold** (hallmark **750**).
- Mercury and lead are toxic heavy metals: their refining and alloys are **for study only**.

7. Carat rating = (375 ÷ 1000) × 24 = **9-carat gold**. Mass of pure gold in 8 g = 8 × 375 ÷ 1000 = **3 g** (37.5% of 8 g = 3 g ✅).

8. (a) **Distillation** (e.g., zinc or mercury). (b) **Zone refining** (silicon, germanium). (c) **Liquation** (e.g., tin or lead). (d) **Electrolytic refining** (e.g., copper).

Because gold is much less reactive than copper, it does not dissolve at the anode and settles to the bottom of the tank as valuable **anode mud**.

10. Moles of Cu deposited = 19.05 ÷ 63.5 = 0.3 mol. Since each Cu²⁺ ion requires 2 electrons (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), moles of electrons needed = 0.3 × 2 = **0.6 mol of electrons**. Check: 0.3 × 63.5 = 19.05 g ✅.

11. (a) **False** ❌: Bronze is an **alloy (homogeneous mixture)** of copper and tin whose ratio can vary (e.g., 88 : 12 or 90 : 10). 12. Because solder melts at ~190 °C—well below the melting points of pure tin (232 °C) and pure lead (327 °C)—a soldering iron can melt it quickly at a lower temperature without heat-damaging sensitive microchips and plastic insulation on the circuit board.

13. As shown in Figure 6.3, in pure iron all atoms are the same size, so flat crystal layers slide over one another easily. Carbon atoms have a much smaller atomic radius than iron atoms; wedging even 1% carbon by mass into the iron lattice disrupts the smooth planes and locks the layers against slipping, making steel dramatically harder and stronger than pure iron.
