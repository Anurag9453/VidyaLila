# Topic 7: Preventing Corrosion and Chapter Summary

Across the first six topics, we mined ores from the earth, concentrated them, roasted or calcined them into oxides, reduced them to metals, refined them to high purity, and blended them into strong alloys. Yet one big practical challenge remains: once we have invested all that energy to turn an oxide ore into a pure metal, **how long will the metal last?** Leave an unprotected piece of iron out in the damp air, and nature slowly reverses our metallurgy—turning the shiny metal right back into a crumbly reddish-brown oxide: **rust**. In this final topic, we will look inside the chemistry of corrosion, compare the smartest ways to stop it (especially when a coating gets scratched!), uncover the scientific secret behind a famous 1,600-year-old iron pillar, and bring the entire chapter together in a master summary.

## The 1,600-Year-Old Iron Pillar That Refuses to Rust Away

During a holiday trip to Delhi, **Mr. Bennett** took his niece **Chloe** to visit the historic Qutub complex. Standing in an open courtyard was a sleek, dark iron column about **7 metres tall**, surrounded by a low protective fence. A tour guide was telling a group of visitors: "This Iron Pillar was forged around **1,600 years ago**, and after sixteen centuries of open sun and monsoon rains, it is still standing strong!"

A tourist standing nearby whispered to his friend, "It must be made of modern stainless steel—or some lost magical alloy." Chloe tugged Mr. Bennett's sleeve. "Uncle Dan, an unpainted iron bucket left in our garden starts rusting in a single rainy season! How has this iron pillar survived outdoors for 1,600 years without crumbling into rust?"

Mr. Bennett smiled. "That is a great detective question. To solve the mystery of the pillar, let's first look closely at why an ordinary iron bucket rusts so fast—and how a surface film can either destroy a metal or protect it."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Corrosion** | The gradual destruction of a metal from its surface inwards by chemical reaction with oxygen, moisture, or other gases in the environment | Rusting of iron; green patina on copper |
| **Rust** | Flaky, reddish-brown hydrated iron(III) oxide, **Fe₂O₃·xH₂O**, that forms on iron and steel in the presence of oxygen and water | Rusted garden gate or nail |
| **Hydrated** | Containing water molecules chemically bound within the crystal structure | The `·xH₂O` in Fe₂O₃·xH₂O |
| **Barrier protection** | Preventing corrosion by placing an impervious surface layer between the metal and air/moisture | Paint, oil, grease, electroplating |
| **Galvanising** | Coating iron or steel with a protective layer of **zinc** (by dipping in molten zinc or electroplating) | Galvanised roofing sheets, buckets, lamp posts |
| **Sacrificial protection** | Attaching a more reactive metal (such as Mg or Zn) to iron/steel so the more reactive metal corrodes instead of the iron | Magnesium or zinc blocks bolted to a ship's hull |
| **Anodising** | Electrolytically thickening the natural protective oxide film (Al₂O₃) on the surface of aluminium | Anodised window frames, cookware, smartphone bodies |
| **Passive layer** | A thin, tough, non-porous, tightly adhering oxide film that seals the metal underneath from further corrosion | Al₂O₃ on aluminium; Cr₂O₃ on stainless steel |

## Part A: Looking Inside Corrosion

In Class 9, you carried out the classic three-test-tube experiment with iron nails and proved that rusting requires **both oxygen (air) and water (moisture)** simultaneously ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md)): **if either oxygen or water is missing, iron does not rust**. Now let's look deeper at the chemical formula and behaviour of rust.

**The true chemical formula of rust.** In Class 9 we wrote a simplified equation, 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃. In reality, water molecules are chemically incorporated into the crystal structure of rust, so rust is **hydrated iron(III) oxide**, written as **Fe₂O₃·xH₂O**. Here **x** represents a variable number of water molecules (which changes depending on humidity and conditions). The complete balanced equation for rusting is:

4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O

Atom count (valid for any value of *x*): Left: Fe = 4, O = 6 + 2*x*, H = 4*x*; Right: Fe = 2 × 2 = 4, O = 2 × (3 + *x*) = 6 + 2*x*, H = 2 × 2*x* = 4*x* ✅. (You can test this with *x* = 1, 2, or 3 and see that every atom balances.)

```
    IRON  +  OXYGEN (from air)  +  WATER   =>   RUST
    Fe       O₂                    H₂O          Fe₂O₃·xH₂O

    All three present     : iron steadily corrodes into flaky brown rust
    Iron + dry oxygen     : no water present -> virtually no rusting
    Iron + air-free water : no dissolved oxygen -> virtually no rusting
    Dry iron alone        : stays bright and unchanged
```

> **Figure 7.1** – Iron needs two partners to rust: oxygen and water. When both are present together, hydrated iron(III) oxide (rust) forms; remove either one, and rusting stops.

### Why Is Rust So Destructive, and What Speeds It Up?

- **Rust is flaky and porous.** Unlike the oxide film on aluminium, rust does not cling tightly to the iron surface. It swells, cracks, and flakes off, continually exposing fresh bare iron underneath to air and water until the entire piece of iron is eaten right through! By contrast, **aluminium** forms a microscopic, tough, non-porous skin of **Al₂O₃** that seals the metal and stops corrosion in its tracks (Chapter 2: [metals-and-non-metals-part2.md](../metals-and-non-metals/metals-and-non-metals-part2.md)).
- **Dissolved salt (electrolytes) accelerates rusting dramatically.** Ships at sea, coastal bridges, and cars driven on salted winter roads rust much faster than iron in fresh water. Dissolved salt ions make the water film on the metal a much better electrical conductor, speeding up electron transfer during corrosion (as you will see in Chapter 4: Electrolysis).
- **Acid rain and acidic pollutants accelerate rusting.** Sulphur dioxide (SO₂) and nitrogen oxides from burning fuels dissolve in rainwater to form acids ([atmospheric-pollution-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/atmospheric-pollution/atmospheric-pollution-part2.md)), which attack protective coatings and speed up metal corrosion.
- **Warmth and high humidity** speed up rusting, whereas cold, dry climates slow it down.

Quick recall of corrosion on other metals (from Class 9): **copper** slowly develops a greenish coat of basic copper carbonate in moist air, and **silver** develops a black tarnish of silver sulphide (Ag₂S) when exposed to trace sulphur compounds in air; both films adhere fairly well and slow further attack, while aluminium's Al₂O₃ passive layer is an even better shield. **Iron is the outlier because its rust flakes off instead of protecting the metal underneath.**

## Part B: Three Safe Rust Investigations (At Home with an Adult)

In Class 9 you tested the conditions needed for rusting. Now here are three safe, hands-on investigations that test **how well different protection methods actually work**. Always do these with an adult, keep the jars away from food, never taste anything, and wash your hands afterwards.

### Investigation 1: Testing Barrier Coatings—and What Happens at a Scratch

Take four identical clean, bright iron nails and place each half-submerged in a small glass jar containing ordinary **tap water**:

1. **Nail A:** Completely bare (control).
2. **Nail B:** Coated thoroughly with petroleum jelly, oil, or grease.
3. **Nail C:** Painted completely with enamel paint (or clear nail varnish) and allowed to dry fully.
4. **Nail D:** Painted just like Nail C, but with **one deep scratch** scraped through the paint down to bare iron (done carefully by an adult).

Observe over 5 to 7 days. **What you will see:** Nail A rusts heavily; Nails B and C stay bright and rust-free; and Nail D develops rust **right along the scratch**, which soon begins to creep underneath the edges of the paint! Takeaway: **a simple barrier coating protects only as long as the barrier remains unbroken**.

### Investigation 2: The Effect of Salt Water

Place two identical clean iron nails into two jars: one containing plain tap water, and the other containing the same volume of tap water with one teaspoon of table salt (NaCl) dissolved in it. Keep both jars side by side. Within just a day or two, **the nail in the salty water develops much heavier rust**.

### Investigation 3: The Effect of Alloying (Iron vs. Stainless Steel)

Place one ordinary iron nail and one **stainless steel teaspoon** into the same jar of salty water for several days. The iron nail rusts rapidly, while the stainless steel spoon comes out gleaming and completely unrusted—showing how alloying iron with chromium and nickel (Topic 6) transforms its corrosion resistance.

> **Safety Note:** None of these tests use acids, bleach, or external batteries. Label the jars clearly, keep them away from kitchen counters and young children, and wash your hands after observing.

## Part C: Methods of Preventing Corrosion at a Glance

Every corrosion-prevention strategy works in one of two ways: either **place a physical barrier** between the metal and air/water, or **use the reactivity series** so a more reactive metal sacrifices itself first. Here are all eight major methods side by side:

| Method | How It Protects | What Happens If Scratched? | Typical Applications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Painting** | Forms a dry barrier against air and moisture | Rust starts at the scratch and creeps under the paint | Bridges, gates, railings, car bodies |
| **Oiling or greasing** | Forms a water-repellent film and lubricates | Film wipes off over time and must be reapplied | Bicycle chains, gears, hinges, garden tools |
| **Galvanising (zinc coat)** | Acts as both a barrier **and** sacrificial protection (Zn > Fe) | **Iron still does NOT rust!** Zinc corrodes first | Roofing sheets, buckets, outdoor fencing, lamp posts |
| **Tin plating** | Acts as a barrier only (Sn is less reactive than Fe) | **Iron rusts even faster** at the scratch! | Inside of food cans |
| **Chromium electroplating** | Hard, mirror-bright, non-porous barrier | If scratched deep to the steel, rust forms at the scratch | Bicycle handlebars, bathroom taps, car trim |
| **Anodising** | Thickens aluminium's natural Al₂O₃ passive layer | Very hard, abrasion-resistant oxide layer | Window frames, cookware, electronics casings |
| **Alloying (stainless steel)** | Chromium forms a microscopic, self-healing Cr₂O₃ passive film | **Self-heals!** Fresh Cr₂O₃ film forms instantly at a scratch | Cutlery, surgical instruments, kitchen sinks |
| **Sacrificial anode blocks** | More reactive metal (Mg or Zn) corrodes instead of the steel | Protects even where paint on the hull/pipe is scratched | Ship hulls, underground steel pipelines, offshore rigs |

> Notice the crucial divide: **pure barrier methods** protect only while the coat is intact, whereas **sacrificial methods** (galvanising and Mg/Zn blocks) and **self-healing alloys** (stainless steel) keep protecting even after a scratch! Let's see why.

## Part D: Galvanising Versus Tin Plating: A Question of the Reactivity Series

Recall from our reactivity series (Topic 1, Figure 1.3, and Chapter 2) that **zinc sits above iron**, whereas **tin sits below iron**:

**Zn  >  Fe  >  Sn**   *(decreasing order of reactivity)*

That single inequality explains the dramatic difference between **galvanised iron** (iron coated with zinc) and **tin-plated iron** (iron coated with tin). As long as both coatings are completely unbroken, both act as effective barriers that keep air and water away from the iron. The real test comes the moment the coating is **scratched or dented**, exposing both the iron and the coating metal together to moisture and oxygen!

```
    GALVANISED IRON (zinc coating)      TIN-PLATED IRON (tin coating)

    zzzzzzzz   zzzzzzzzzzzzzz           tttttttt   tttttttttttttt
    zzzzzzzz\ /zzzzzzzzzzzzzz           tttttttt\ /tttttttttttttt
    ==========================          ==========================   <- iron (Fe)
             ^                                   ^
          scratch                             scratch
       water droplet                       water droplet

    Zn > Fe : zinc is more reactive     Fe > Sn : iron is more reactive
    Zinc oxidises (corrodes) first:     Iron oxidises (corrodes) first:
    Zn -> Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻                    Fe -> Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻
    The iron is PROTECTED!              The iron rusts EVEN FASTER!
```

> **Figure 7.2** – What happens when a coating on iron gets scratched in the presence of moisture. Left (galvanised): because zinc is more reactive than iron (Zn > Fe), zinc loses electrons and corrodes preferentially, protecting the exposed iron. Right (tin-plated): because tin is less reactive than iron (Fe > Sn), the exposed iron corrodes preferentially and rusts even faster than bare iron alone!

Notice the electron chemistry: because zinc is higher in the reactivity series, zinc atoms have a stronger tendency to lose electrons (**Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻**) than iron atoms do, feeding electrons to the iron so the iron cannot oxidise into Fe²⁺. (You will explore the electrochemical cell behind this in Chapter 4: Electrolysis.)

### Solved Reasoning Example: Which Water Tank Rusted Through?

**Question:** Two steel rainwater-harvesting tanks, Tank P and Tank Q, are set up outdoors. Tank P is made of **galvanised steel** (zinc-coated), while Tank Q is made of **tin-plated steel**. During installation, a sharp rock accidentally scratches both tanks right down to the bare steel. Two years later, one tank has rusted right through at the scratch and is leaking, while the other shows no rust hole at all. Which tank leaked, and why? (Given: Zn > Fe > Sn.)

**Solution:**
1. Reactivity order: **Zn > Fe > Sn**. Tank P has a zinc coat; Tank Q has a tin coat.
2. At the scratch on **Tank P**, both zinc and iron touch the rainwater. Since **zinc is more reactive than iron (Zn > Fe)**, the zinc sacrifices itself (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻) and prevents the exposed iron from rusting. **Tank P stays intact.**
3. At the scratch on **Tank Q**, both tin and iron touch the rainwater. Since **iron is more reactive than tin (Fe > Sn)**, the exposed iron acts as the sacrificial metal and corrodes rapidly right along the narrow scratch. **Tank Q rusted through and leaked.**
4. **Conclusion:** **Tank Q leaked; Tank P remained sound.**

**Check:** Without a scratch, both coatings would have acted as simple barriers. The difference appears specifically when scratched ✅. Also check charge balance in Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻: 0 = (+2) + (−2) ✅.

> **Why is tin plating still used for food cans?** Tin is non-toxic and less reactive toward weak organic acids in canned fruit and vegetables than zinc would be (zinc reacts with food acids to form harmful soluble zinc salts). As long as a food can is not dented or scratched on the inside, the tin barrier keeps the steel safe; once opened or dented, however, a tin can will rust quickly at any scratch.
>
> **And why doesn't the zinc on a galvanised roof dissolve away in a week?** Zinc reacts with oxygen, moisture, and CO₂ in the air to form a thin, tough, insoluble **basic zinc carbonate** film on its own surface, which slows its own corrosion to a crawl so a galvanised roof lasts for decades!

## Part E: Sacrificial Protection and Anodising

### Protecting a Ship's Hull with Magnesium or Zinc Blocks

A steel ship's hull spends its entire life immersed in **corrosive salty seawater**, where waves, anchor chains, and docks inevitably scratch the hull paint. Painting alone is never enough. Therefore, marine engineers bolt or weld thick slabs of **magnesium or zinc** directly onto the outside of the steel hull below the waterline. Because magnesium (and zinc) sits well above iron in the reactivity series (**Mg > Zn > Fe**), the magnesium blocks corrode preferentially (**Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻**), supplying electrons to the steel hull and keeping the steel from rusting. Because these blocks slowly dissolve away to save the ship, they are called **sacrificial anodes**.

```
    SALTY SEAWATER (good electrolyte)
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~

    #########                   STEEL SHIP HULL (iron alloy)
    #########  =[ Mg ]=         <- magnesium block bolted directly to hull
    #########
    #########  =[ Mg ]=         <- second magnesium block
    #########
    #########

    Mg -> Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻ : magnesium blocks slowly dissolve over time
    Steel hull       : stays protected from rusting
    When the blocks wear down after a few years, new ones are bolted on in dry dock
```

> **Figure 7.3** – Sacrificial protection of a ship's steel hull using magnesium blocks. Because Mg is more reactive than Fe, the magnesium blocks corrode in seawater instead of the steel hull. Once worn down, the blocks are simply replaced.

The exact same method is used to protect **underground steel oil and water pipelines**: blocks of magnesium or zinc are buried alongside the pipe and connected to it by a stout insulated copper cable. Only a metal **more reactive than iron** (like Mg or Zn) can act as a sacrificial anode; attaching a block of copper or tin would do the exact opposite and make the pipeline rust faster!

> **Crucial detail:** The sacrificial block **must be in electrical contact** with the iron/steel it protects (either bolted directly to the hull or connected by a conducting wire).

### Anodising: Making Aluminium's Natural Armour Extra Thick

As we saw in Chapter 2 and Part A, aluminium naturally forms a microscopic, transparent skin of **aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃)** the instant it meets air. In **anodising**, engineers deliberately make this protective oxide skin **hundreds of times thicker** by making the aluminium object the **anode (+)** in an electrolysis bath containing dilute sulphuric acid. Oxygen gas liberated at the anode reacts immediately with the aluminium surface to build a thick, super-hard, wear-resistant Al₂O₃ layer—which can also absorb bright dyes to create scratch-resistant coloured cookware, window frames, and smartphone bodies. *(Industrial anodising uses acid and electric current and is **for study only**.)*

## Part F: The Iron Pillar of Delhi: The Secret of 1,600 Years

Now we can return to Chloe's question at the Qutub complex! The famous **Iron Pillar of Delhi**—forged around 400 CE (roughly **1,600 years ago**) and standing over **7 metres tall** and weighing more than **6 tonnes**—has stood outdoors through centuries of sun and monsoons with remarkably little rusting. It is not magic, and it is not stainless steel (it contains virtually no chromium). Metallurgical studies revealed three real scientific reasons working together:

1. **High-phosphorus wrought iron:** Ancient Indian metallurgists forged the pillar from **wrought iron** with very low carbon and sulphur content, but a **significantly higher phosphorus content** (~0.15–0.25% P) than modern steel (because they reduced the iron ore using charcoal rather than limestone-fluxed coke, leaving more phosphorus in the metal).
2. **Formation of a protective passive layer:** Over time, that phosphorus helped catalyse the formation of a thin, dense, **tightly adhering crystalline surface film** composed of iron, oxygen, hydrogen, and **phosphate** compounds (crystalline iron hydrogen phosphate hydrate, alongside compact iron oxides). Instead of flaking off like ordinary porous rust, this dark **passive film** sealed the surface and blocked moisture and oxygen from reaching the iron underneath!
3. **Climate and thermal mass:** Delhi's dry climate for most of the year—combined with the huge heat capacity of the 6-tonne pillar, which warms up quickly in the morning sun and evaporates overnight dew—also helped keep the surface dry.

Notice how the Iron Pillar confirms the exact lesson of this topic: **when a surface film is porous and flaky (ordinary rust), corrosion eats right through the metal; when the surface film is dense, adherent, and non-porous (a passive layer), it shields the metal for centuries!**

## Part G: The Economic Cost of Corrosion and the Value of Recycling

Corrosion is not merely an eyesore—it is a massive engineering and economic challenge. Every year, corroded bridges, pipelines, ships, railway tracks, and machinery must be repaired or replaced; global estimates suggest that corrosion costs nations roughly **3% to 4% of their gross domestic product (GDP)** annually. Protecting metals properly—and **recycling** them at the end of their service life—saves both natural resources and energy:

- **High-grade mineral ores are finite:** Once rich deposits of haematite, bauxite, or chalcopyrite are mined out, they do not grow back.
- **Recycling saves huge amounts of energy:** As we saw in Topic 5, recycling scrap aluminium takes only **~5% of the energy** needed to extract primary aluminium from bauxite (a **~95% energy saving**). Similarly, melting scrap steel in an electric-arc furnace to make new steel saves roughly **60% to 70% of the energy** compared with mining iron ore and running a blast furnace!
- **Reduces mining waste and pollution:** Recycling cuts down on red mud, slag heaps, quarry scars, and greenhouse-gas emissions.
- Even **heavily rusted scrap iron** can be collected, melted down in a steelmaking furnace, and reborn as brand-new structural steel.

## Part I: Mixed Problems

### Mixed Problem 1: How Much Rust Forms? (Stoichiometry)

**Question:** Suppose rust forms with the formula **Fe₂O₃·3H₂O** (i.e., *x* = 3). If 8.4 g of pure iron rusts completely into Fe₂O₃·3H₂O, what mass of rust is formed, and what masses of oxygen and water combine with the iron? (Fe = 56, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Solution:** Balanced equation for *x* = 3: **4Fe + 3O₂ + 6H₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·3H₂O**.
- Moles of Fe = 8.4 ÷ 56 = 0.15 mol.
- Moles of rust (Fe₂O₃·3H₂O) = 0.15 × (2 ÷ 4) = 0.075 mol. Molar mass of Fe₂O₃·3H₂O = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 + 3 × 18 = 112 + 48 + 54 = 214 g/mol. Mass of rust = 0.075 × 214 = **16.05 g**.
- Moles of O₂ = 0.15 × (3 ÷ 4) = 0.1125 mol → mass of O₂ = 0.1125 × 32 = **3.60 g**.
- Moles of H₂O = 0.15 × (6 ÷ 4) = 0.225 mol → mass of H₂O = 0.225 × 18 = **4.05 g**.

**Check:** 8.40 + 3.60 + 4.05 = 16.05 g ✅. Atom count: Fe: 4 = 4, O: 6 + 6 = 12 = 2 × (3 + 3) = 12 ✅, H: 12 = 2 × 6 = 12 ✅. Notice that the rust weighs nearly twice as much as the iron that rusted, because oxygen and water from the air became locked into the solid!

### Mixed Problem 2: How Much Iron Does a Sacrificial Magnesium Block Save?

**Question:** A 9.6 kg sacrificial block of magnesium is connected to an underground steel pipeline. Assuming that each Mg atom that oxidises (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻) prevents one Fe atom from oxidising to Fe²⁺ (Fe → Fe²⁺ + 2e⁻), what mass of iron is saved by the time the entire 9.6 kg magnesium block dissolves? (Mg = 24, Fe = 56.)

**Solution:**
- Moles of Mg in 9.6 kg (9600 g) = 9600 ÷ 24 = **400 mol**.
- Since both Mg and Fe lose 2 electrons per atom, 400 mol of Mg saves **400 mol of Fe**.
- Mass of iron saved = 400 × 56 = 22,400 g = **22.4 kg**.

**Check:** 400 × 24 = 9600 g ✅. Because magnesium has a much lower atomic mass (24) than iron (56), 9.6 kg of magnesium can sacrificially protect **22.4 kg** of iron!

### Mixed Problem 3: Mass Percentage of Zinc on a Galvanised Nail

**Question:** A galvanised roof nail has a total mass of 5.0 g, of which 0.2 g is the zinc coating and the rest is iron. Find the mass percentage of zinc and the mass of iron in the nail.

**Solution:** Percentage of zinc = (0.2 ÷ 5.0) × 100 = **4%**. Mass of iron = 5.0 − 0.2 = **4.8 g** (96%).

**Check:** 4% + 96% = 100% ✅.

### Mixed Problem 4: Energy Saved by Recycling Steel

**Question:** Suppose producing 1 kg of new primary steel from iron ore requires 20 MJ (or units) of energy, whereas producing 1 kg of recycled steel from scrap requires only 8 units. ## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Tin plating and galvanising protect scratched iron in the exact same way."**
   Compare a scratched galvanised bucket and a scratched tin-plated can left out in the rain. Because **zinc is more reactive than iron (Zn > Fe)**, zinc corrodes first at a scratch and protects the iron. Because **tin is less reactive than iron (Fe > Sn)**, the exposed iron corrodes first at a scratch—rusting even faster than bare iron! Both look silvery when new, but behave oppositely once scratched.

2. **Mistake 2: "Once you paint an iron gate, it is protected forever."**
   If a bicycle bumps the gate and chips off a tiny flake of paint, oxygen and rainwater reach the bare iron at that chip, rust begins to form, and the swelling rust lifts the surrounding paint from underneath. Paint works only while the barrier is unbroken, so painted outdoor ironwork needs periodic inspection and touch-ups.

3. **Mistake 3: "Since rust is an oxide of iron, it should protect the iron underneath just like aluminium oxide protects aluminium."**
   Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) forms a **dense, tough, non-porous, tightly clinging film** that seals the metal. Iron rust (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O) is **flaky, swollen, and porous**—it holds moisture like a sponge and peels off, continually exposing fresh iron underneath until the metal is eaten right through.

4. **Mistake 4: "Stainless steel can never corrode under any circumstances."**
   It is called *stain-less*, not *stain-impossible*! In harsh marine environments with stagnant salty water or strong acids/chlorides, even stainless steel's chromium oxide film can occasionally suffer tiny pit marks if the wrong grade is used. However, in normal household and outdoor use, its self-healing Cr₂O₃ film resists rusting extraordinarily well.

5. **Mistake 5: "The Iron Pillar of Delhi never rusts because it is made of stainless steel."**
   The pillar was forged ~1,600 years ago—more than fifteen centuries before stainless steel was invented—and contains no chromium. It is made of **high-phosphorus wrought iron**, whose phosphorus content helped form a dense, protective **iron-hydrogen-phosphate passive film** on the surface, aided by Delhi's dry climate for most of the year.

6. **Mistake 6: "A magnesium sacrificial block bolted to a ship's hull lasts forever."**
   The entire job of a sacrificial anode is to **dissolve away in place of the steel hull** (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻)! After a few years at sea, the blocks are worn down to stubs and must be replaced with fresh magnesium or zinc blocks during dry-dock maintenance.

## Quick Recap

- Rust is **hydrated iron(III) oxide, Fe₂O₃·xH₂O**: **4Fe + 3O₂ + 2xH₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·xH₂O**. Both **oxygen and water** must be present for iron to rust; dissolved salt and acidic pollutants speed up rusting.
- Rust is **flaky and porous**, so corrosion continues inwards. Aluminium's **Al₂O₃** film is **dense and non-porous (a passive layer)**, sealing the metal from further attack.
- **Barrier methods:** Painting, oiling/greasing, tin plating, chromium plating. If the barrier is scratched, rust begins at the scratch.
- **Galvanising (zinc coat):** Protects iron **even when scratched** because **Zn > Fe**—zinc oxidises first (sacrificial protection). **Tin plating** makes scratched iron rust **faster** because **Fe > Sn**.
- **Sacrificial anodes** (blocks of **Mg or Zn** bolted to ship hulls or connected to underground pipelines) corrode preferentially to protect steel. **Anodising** electrolytically thickens the protective Al₂O₃ layer on aluminium.
- **Iron Pillar of Delhi (~1,600 years old):** Forged from high-phosphorus wrought iron; protected by a dense, adherent phosphate-rich passive surface film and a mostly dry climate.
- **Recycling metals** conserves finite ores and saves massive amounts of energy: **~60–70% energy saved** for recycled steel, and **~95% energy saved** for recycled aluminium.

## Practice Questions

1. Write the balanced chemical equation for rusting when *x* = 2, i.e., forming rust of formula **Fe₂O₃·2H₂O**. If 33.6 g of pure iron rusts completely to Fe₂O₃·2H₂O, calculate the mass of rust formed, the mass of oxygen consumed, and the mass of water combined. Show that total mass is conserved. (Fe = 56, O = 16, H = 1.)
2. Why does an iron nail rust much faster in salty seawater than in ordinary tap water? And why does rusting continue to eat deeper into an iron bar, whereas corrosion stops at the surface of an aluminium sheet?
3. Four clean iron nails are placed in separate jars of tap water for a week: (a) a bare iron nail, (b) a nail coated with grease, (c) a nail wrapped tightly with a strip of zinc, and (d) a nail wrapped tightly with a piece of copper wire. Using the rule *"A more reactive metal in contact with iron corrodes first and protects the iron; a less reactive metal in contact makes the iron corrode faster"* and the reactivity order **Zn > Fe > Cu**, state which nails rust the least and which nail rusts even faster than the bare nail.
4. A galvanised steel bucket and a tin-plated steel food can are both deeply scratched so the underlying steel is exposed to damp air. Explain which one will rust at the scratch and why. From the standpoint of rust prevention alone, which coating is better for an outdoor bucket that gets bumped and scratched?
5. A scratched metallic coating on a steel sheet caused the exposed steel along the scratch to rust faster than an uncoated steel sheet kept beside it. The coating metal **M** is one of **Zn, Mg, or Sn**. Using the reactivity series **Mg > Zn > Fe > Sn**, identify metal **M** and explain your reasoning.
6. A 32.5 kg sacrificial block of zinc is connected to an underground iron pipeline. Assuming each Zn atom that dissolves saves one Fe atom (both form +2 ions releasing 2 electrons), find the number of moles of zinc in the block and the mass of iron it can save. (Zn = 65, Fe = 56.)
7. A coil of galvanised steel wire has a total mass of 25.0 g, of which 1.5 g is the zinc coating. Find the mass percentage of zinc and the mass of steel (iron) in the coil.
8. A 400 g stainless steel saucepan contains 18% chromium and 8% nickel by mass, the rest being iron. Calculate the mass of chromium, nickel, and iron in the pan, and explain how the chromium protects the pan from rusting.
9. A recycling facility receives 800 kg of used aluminium beverage cans and recovers 760 kg of pure cast aluminium. Calculate the percentage recovery and the mass lost. Explain why recycling aluminium saves ~95% of the energy compared with making primary aluminium from bauxite.
10. Choose the best corrosion-protection method and give a one-line reason for each: (a) a tall steel lattice tower carrying high-voltage power lines across the countryside, (b) the steel handlebar of a road bicycle, (c) a buried cross-country steel oil pipeline, (d) the steel blade of a farm plough stored in a shed between seasons, (e) an aluminium window frame installed in a beachfront apartment.
11. A plumber accidentally joins an unpainted iron water pipe directly to a copper pipe. Right at the junction between the two metals, which pipe will corrode faster and why? Suggest one practical way to prevent this problem.
12. State whether each statement is True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Rust is simply metallic iron wet with water. (b) Tin plating continues to protect iron even where the coating is scratched through. (c) A sacrificial magnesium block must be in electrical contact with the steel hull it protects. (d) The ancient Iron Pillar of Delhi has resisted rust because it was made of stainless steel containing 18% chromium.
13. In your own words, explain the difference between a **barrier method** and a **sacrificial method** of corrosion prevention, giving one clear example of each.
14. Using the five-stage metallurgical flow from Topic 1 (mining → concentration → conversion to oxide → reduction → refining), explain why recycling scrap metal saves so much energy compared with extracting new metal from ore. State clearly which stages are skipped.

### Answers

1. Balanced equation (*x* = 2): **4Fe + 3O₂ + 4H₂O → 2Fe₂O₃·2H₂O**. Atom count: Fe: 4 = 4, O: 6 + 4 = 10 = 2 × (3 + 2) = 10 ✅, H: 8 = 2 × 4 = 8 ✅. Moles of Fe = 33.6 ÷ 56 = 0.6 mol. Moles of rust = 0.6 × (2 ÷ 4) = 0.3 mol; molar mass of Fe₂O₃·2H₂O = 112 + 48 + 36 = 196 g/mol → mass of rust = 0.3 × 196 = **58.8 g**. Moles of O₂ = 0.6 × (3 ÷ 4) = 0.45 mol → mass of O₂ = 0.45 × 32 = **14.4 g**. Moles of H₂O = 0.6 × (4 ÷ 4) = 0.6 mol → mass of H₂O = 0.6 × 18 = **10.8 g**. Check conservation of mass: 33.6 + 14.4 + 10.8 = 58.8 g ✅.

2. Salty seawater contains dissolved ions that make the water film on the iron a strong electrical conductor (electrolyte), accelerating the electrochemical process of rusting (Chapter 4). Rust (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O) is flaky and porous, so it peels off and exposes fresh iron underneath. Aluminium's oxide film (Al₂O₃) is tough, dense, and non-porous (a passive layer), sealing the underlying aluminium from air and water.

3. (a) Bare nail: rusts normally (control). (b) Greased nail: barrier blocks water/air → **virtually no rust**. (c) Zinc-wrapped nail: since Zn > Fe, zinc corrodes sacrificially and protects the iron → **virtually no rust on the iron**. (d) Copper-wrapped nail: since Fe > Cu, iron corrodes preferentially and **rusts even faster than the bare nail**. Least rust: **(b) and (c)**; more rust than bare nail: **(d)**.

4. On the **galvanised bucket**, since Zn > Fe, the zinc coating around the scratch corrodes sacrificially and **protects the exposed steel from rusting**. On the **tin-plated can**, since Fe > Sn, the exposed steel along the scratch corrodes preferentially and **rusts rapidly**. For an outdoor bucket that gets bumped and scratched, **galvanising** is far better.

5. A coating that accelerates rusting at a scratch must be **less reactive than iron**. In the series Mg > Zn > Fe > Sn, only **tin (Sn)** sits below iron. Therefore, **M = Sn (tin)**. (Both Mg and Zn sit above Fe and would have protected it sacrificially.)

6. Moles of Zn = 32,500 g ÷ 65 g/mol = **500 mol**. Since 1 mol of Zn saves 1 mol of Fe, mass of iron saved = 500 mol × 56 g/mol = 28,000 g = **28 kg**. Check: 500 × 65 = 32,500 g ✅.

7. Percentage of zinc = (1.5 ÷ 25.0) × 100 = **6%**. Mass of iron (steel) = 25.0 − 1.5 = **23.5 g** (94%). Check: 6% + 94% = 100% ✅.

8. Mass of chromium = 18% of 400 = **72 g**; nickel = 8% of 400 = **32 g**; iron = 400 − 72 − 32 = **296 g** (74%). Check: 72 + 32 + 296 = 400 g ✅. Chromium reacts with oxygen in the air to form an invisible, dense, non-porous **Cr₂O₃ passive layer** that seals the steel—and immediately reforms (self-heals) if the surface is scratched.

9. Percentage recovered = (760 ÷ 800) × 100 = **95%**; mass lost = 800 − 760 = **40 kg** (5%). Primary aluminium requires mining bauxite, purifying it via the Baeyer process, and running the electricity-intensive Hall-Héroult electrolysis cell (~14 kWh/kg), whereas recycling simply melts existing metallic aluminium at 660 °C, using only ~5% of the energy.

10. (a) **Galvanising (zinc coat):** Long-lasting weather resistance that continues to protect sacrificially even if scratched. (b) **Chromium electroplating:** Hard, mirror-bright finish that resists wear from hands and weather. (c) **Protective coating + buried sacrificial anodes (Mg or Zn blocks):** Because buried pipes cannot be easily dug up for repainting, sacrificial blocks ensure any scratch in the coating is automatically protected. (d) **Oiling or greasing:** Soil abrasion would quickly scrape paint off a plough blade in the field, whereas wiping grease on before shed storage is cheap and easy. (e) **Anodising:** Thickens the Al₂O₃ passive film to withstand salty coastal air without pitting.

11. Because iron is more reactive than copper (**Fe > Cu**), the **iron pipe** will corrode (rust) much faster right at the junction! To prevent this, the plumber should insert a **non-conducting plastic/dielectric union (insulating washer)** between the iron and copper pipes so electrons cannot flow between them (or use galvanised/plastic piping).

12. (a) **False** ❌: Rust is a chemical compound—hydrated iron(III) oxide, **Fe₂O₃·xH₂O**—not merely wet metallic iron. (b) **False** ❌: Because tin is less reactive than iron (Fe > Sn), scratched tin plating makes the exposed iron rust faster. (c) **True** ✅: Electrical contact is essential so electrons from the oxidising magnesium can flow to the steel hull. (d) **False** ❌: The pillar is made of **high-phosphorus wrought iron** (with no chromium), protected by a phosphate-rich passive surface film.

13. A **barrier method** (such as **painting** or oiling) works by putting a physical wall between the iron and air/water; if the barrier is scratched, the exposed iron rusts. A **sacrificial method** (such as bolting **magnesium blocks** to a ship's hull) connects a more reactive metal to the iron so the more reactive metal oxidises instead of the iron—protecting the iron even at bare scratches. (Galvanising combines both!)

14. Producing new metal from ore requires all five stages: **mining, concentration, conversion to oxide (roasting/calcination), reduction (blast furnace or electrolysis), and refining**. Recycling scrap metal **completely skips mining, concentration, conversion to oxide, and energy-hungry reduction/electrolysis**—you simply collect, sort, and remelt the existing metal (with minor refining if needed), saving ~60–70% of the energy for steel and ~95% for aluminium.

## Chapter Summary: Metallurgy

Here is the complete seven-topic journey of **Metallurgy** at a glance—from where metals hide in the earth's crust to how we extract, purify, blend, and protect them:

| Topic | Core Theme | Key Takeaway in One Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic 1** | Minerals, Ores, and Gangue | A mineral is a naturally occurring compound in the earth's crust; an ore is a mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably; gangue is the unwanted rocky impurity, and the 5-step extraction flow depends on the metal's position in the reactivity series. |
| **Topic 2** | Concentration of Ores | Raw ore is cleaned of gangue using five methods—hand-picking, hydraulic washing (density), magnetic separation, froth flotation (for sulphide ores using pine oil), and chemical leaching (bauxite in NaOH)—each based on a specific property difference. |
| **Topic 3** | Roasting and Calcination | Concentrated sulphide ores are heated in excess air (**roasting**, releasing SO₂ which is scrubbed or turned into H₂SO₄), and carbonate/hydrated ores are heated in limited air (**calcination**), converting both into metal oxides that are easier to reduce. |
| **Topic 4** | Reduction of Metal Oxides | Low-reactivity metals are extracted by heating alone or auto-reduction (Cu); middle-group metals by reduction with carbon/CO (iron in the blast furnace with limestone flux forming CaSiO₃ slag) or Al (aluminothermy for Mn, Cr); top-group metals by electrolysis of molten compounds. |
| **Topic 5** | Extraction of Aluminium | Bauxite is purified into white alumina (Al₂O₃) by the **Baeyer process** (using hot NaOH, exploiting Al₂O₃'s amphoteric nature) and electrolysed in molten cryolite (~950 °C) in the **Hall-Héroult cell**; recycling aluminium saves ~95% of this electrical energy. |
| **Topic 6** | Refining and Alloys | Crude metals are purified by electrolytic refining (Cu, with precious metals recovered from anode mud), distillation (Zn, Hg), liquation (Sn, Pb), or zone refining (Si, Ge); blending metals with differently sized atoms creates harder, corrosion-resistant **alloys** (steel, stainless steel, brass, bronze, duralumin, 22-carat gold). |
| **Topic 7** | Preventing Corrosion | Rust is flaky **Fe₂O₃·xH₂O** requiring both O₂ and H₂O; iron is protected by barriers (paint, oil, plating), **galvanising** (Zn > Fe, which protects sacrificially even at scratches unlike tin plating where Fe > Sn), sacrificial Mg/Zn blocks, alloying, and passive films (anodised Al and the 1,600-year-old Iron Pillar of Delhi). |
