# Topic 1: Where Metals Are Found in the Earth: Minerals, Ores, and Gangue

In the previous chapter, we put metals through every test imaginable: lustre, electrical conductivity, sonority, reactions with oxygen, water, and acids, and the long ladder of the reactivity series. Yet one big question remained unanswered: **where do these metals actually come from?** The iron in our bridges, the copper in our wiring, the aluminium foil in our kitchens, and the silver in coins do not simply grow on shelves. This chapter is about **Metallurgy**: the science and technology of extracting metals from their **ores**, purifying them, and shaping them for practical use. Our first step today is to look beneath our feet: what do we actually dig out of the earth, and what do we call it?

## "Boring" Rocks in the Museum Gallery

Mr. Rivera took his nephew Noah to the **mineral gallery** at the natural history museum, since Noah's class had a field trip scheduled there the following week. "Uncle Leo, it's just cases full of rocks!" Noah groaned, making a face. Inside the glass cabinets sat dusty brown, black, and reddish-brown lumps, each with a neat little label underneath: *Haematite*, *Magnetite*, *Bauxite*, *Galena*, *Zinc blende*, *Malachite*.

Mr. Rivera smiled. "These rocks are the **starting form** of your bicycle frame, our kitchen cookware, and the electrical wiring in every building. Look at this **Haematite**: dull reddish-brown, looking like an ordinary rusty stone. Most of the world's iron comes out of this. And see this **Bauxite**: a chunk that looks like dried red clay, yet that's where aluminium foil comes from. And this bright green **Malachite**: it holds copper."

"Wait, Uncle Leo—iron and aluminium are shiny! None of these rocks are shiny at all!"

"That's the whole secret. Deep underground, a metal is rarely found as a **shiny pure metal**; instead, it is locked up inside a **compound**, such as iron chemically bonded with oxygen." Then he led Noah to a corner display where a small glass case had its own spotlight: inside sat a jagged, bright yellow nugget. "That is **native gold**, dug straight out of the earth, already pure without any chemical processing. Every other rock here looks dull, while this one gleams."

"Why is gold so different?" Noah's eyes lit up. "Because gold wasn't in any hurry to react with anything inside the earth," Mr. Rivera laughed. "The answer is hidden on the reactivity ladder you learned last chapter. Come on—let's unlock the secret behind each of these rocks."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mineral** | A naturally occurring substance found in the earth's crust with a definite chemical composition and structure, which may be a compound of a metal or occasionally a free element | Haematite, galena, malachite |
| **Ore** | A mineral from which a metal can be extracted **profitably and conveniently** | Haematite (ore of iron) |
| **Gangue (matrix)** | Unwanted earthy impurities such as sand, clay, and rock mixed with the ore | Silica sand (SiO₂) and clay |
| **Native (free) metal** | A metal found in the earth in its uncombined, elemental state | Gold, silver, platinum |
| **Metallurgy** | The complete process of extracting metals from their ores, purifying them, and preparing them for use | Extraction of iron, aluminium, copper |
| **Oxide ore** | An ore in which the metal is combined with oxygen | Haematite Fe₂O₃, bauxite Al₂O₃·2H₂O |
| **Sulphide ore** | An ore in which the metal is combined with sulphur | Zinc blende ZnS, galena PbS |
| **Carbonate ore** | An ore in which the metal is present as a carbonate | Calamine ZnCO₃ |
| **Mass percentage of metal** | The mass of metal present in 100 g of the pure compound | 70% iron in Fe₂O₃ |

## Part A: Mineral, Ore, and Gangue: Three Distinct Terms

### 1. Mineral

Thousands of different minerals occur in the earth's crust. A **mineral** is a substance that occurs **naturally** in the earth and has a definite chemical formula and composition. Some minerals contain metals (like iron in haematite), while others contain no useful metal at all (like quartz, SiO₂, the main component of ordinary sand).

### 2. Ore

Not every mineral can be used to extract a metal **economically**. An **ore** is a mineral from which a metal can be extracted **cheaply, easily, and in sufficient quantity**.

Consider a classic example: aluminium is present in both **ordinary clay** and **bauxite**. However, extracting aluminium from clay is so difficult and expensive that no industry does it, whereas extracting aluminium from bauxite is commercially viable. Therefore, **bauxite is an ore of aluminium, while clay is not**, even though both are aluminium-containing minerals.

Keep in mind that whether a mineral counts as an ore can **change over time**. If the market price of a metal rises or engineers invent a cheaper extraction process, a rock that was considered useless yesterday can become a valuable ore tomorrow.

### 3. Gangue

Ore dug out of a mine is **never pure**. It comes mixed with soil, sand, and unwanted rock fragments. This worthless rocky material is called **gangue** (or matrix). In the upcoming topics we will see how gangue is separated from the useful mineral; for now, simply remember that **crude ore = useful mineral + gangue**.

## Part B: When Are Metals Found Free, and When as Compounds?

In Chapter 2 (Parts 2 and 4), you saw that highly reactive metals react **rapidly** with oxygen, moisture, and carbon dioxide in the air. Deep inside the earth's crust, the exact same chemistry has been playing out over hundreds of millions of years.

- **Very unreactive metals** (gold, silver, platinum, and occasionally copper) hardly react with anything. As a result, they are found in the **free (native) state**. Gold sits at the very bottom of the reactivity series, just as we saw on the ladder in Chapter 2.
- **More reactive metals** (sodium, magnesium, aluminium, zinc, iron) have long since reacted with oxygen, sulphur, carbonates, or chlorides. Consequently, they are always found as **compounds** (ores), never as free metals.

How a metal occurs in nature also determines the **type** of ore we mine:

| Metal | Name of Ore | Formula | Type of Ore | Typical Appearance (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium | Rock salt (halite) | NaCl | Chloride | White or faintly tinted crystals |
| Magnesium | Magnesite, carnallite | MgCO₃, KCl·MgCl₂·6H₂O | Carbonate, chloride | White |
| Aluminium | Bauxite | Al₂O₃·2H₂O | Hydrated oxide | Reddish-brown, clay-like |
| Zinc | Zinc blende (sphalerite) | ZnS | Sulphide | Yellowish-brown or black |
| Zinc | Calamine (smithsonite) | ZnCO₃ | Carbonate | Off-white to brownish |
| Iron | Haematite | Fe₂O₃ | Oxide | Reddish-brown |
| Iron | Magnetite | Fe₃O₄ | Oxide | Black, attracted to a magnet |
| Lead | Galena | PbS | Sulphide | Heavy, metallic silvery-grey |
| Copper | Cuprite | Cu₂O | Oxide | Deep red |
| Copper | Copper pyrites (chalcopyrite) | CuFeS₂ | Sulphide | Brassy yellow with a metallic sheen |
| Copper | Malachite | CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂ | Basic carbonate | Bright vivid green |
| Mercury | Cinnabar | HgS | Sulphide | Bright vermilion red |

An interesting side note: iron also forms a mineral called **iron pyrites (FeS₂)**, which gleams with a brassy golden colour and looks so much like gold that prospectors nicknamed it **"fool's gold"**. However, it is generally not used as an ore of iron because of its high sulphur content (you will see what happens when it is heated in Topic 3).

**Safety note:** Galena (lead), cinnabar (mercury), and many sulphide ores are **toxic**. In museums and classrooms they are kept inside sealed display cases; never touch them with bare hands, inhale their dust, or put any mineral sample near your mouth.

## Part C: The Complete Journey of Extracting a Metal (The Flow of Metallurgy)

Raw ore hauled out of a mine does not turn into pure metal in a single step. The complete metallurgical journey involves five main stages (not every metal requires all five—some steps can be skipped depending on the ore):

1. **Mining:** Digging the raw ore out of the earth.
2. **Concentration (ore dressing):** Removing the bulk of the gangue. (Topic 2)
3. **Conversion to oxide:** Heating a sulphide or carbonate ore to turn it into an **oxide**—by roasting or calcination. (Topic 3)
4. **Reduction:** Removing oxygen from the metal oxide to obtain the metal. (Topic 4)
5. **Refining:** Purifying the extracted crude metal to remove remaining impurities. (Topic 6)

```

   :  1. MINING THE ORE   :   mixed with soil, sand, rock
   :                      :
   +----------------------+
              :

   +----------------------+
   :  2. CONCENTRATION    :   remove the gangue
   :  (cleaning the ore)  :
   +----------------------+
              :
              v
   +----------------------+
   :  3. FORMING AN OXIDE :   roasting or calcination
   :                      :   (if the ore is not already an oxide)
   +----------------------+
              :
              v
   +----------------------+
   :  4. REDUCTION        :   oxide to crude metal
   :                      :
   +----------------------+
              :
              v
   +----------------------+
   :  5. REFINING         :   remove final impurities
   :                      :
   +----------------------+
              :

```

## Part D: Which Metal, Which Method? (A First Look)

The method used to reduce an ore depends directly on the metal's **reactivity**: the more reactive a metal is, the tighter its hold on oxygen, and the harder it is to break that bond. We can divide the reactivity series into three main groups.

```
   K    potassium     \
   Na   sodium         \   TOP GROUP: highly reactive
   Ca   calcium         >  very strong bond with oxygen
   Mg   magnesium      /   ==> ELECTROLYSIS of molten compound
   Al   aluminium     /
   ------------------------------------------------
   Zn   zinc          \
   Fe   iron           >  MIDDLE GROUP: moderately reactive
   Sn   tin            /  ==> roasting or calcination,
   Pb   lead          /       then REDUCTION with CARBON
   ------------------------------------------------
   [H]  hydrogen (included only for reference)
   ------------------------------------------------
   Cu   copper        \
   Hg   mercury        >  BOTTOM GROUP: low reactivity
   Ag   silver         /  ==> heating alone (or found native)
   Au   gold          /      gold, silver, platinum occur free
```

> **Figure 1.3** – The three regions of the reactivity series and the primary extraction method for each (a preview—the full story is in Topic 4). Hydrogen is shown only as a reference point; it is not a metal.

**A new metal on the ladder:** In Class 9 and Chapter 2, tin (Sn) was not included on our reactivity ladder. In this chapter, **tin appears between iron (Fe) and lead (Pb)**—meaning tin is less reactive than iron and slightly more reactive than lead. This fact will be useful in Topics 4 and 7.

| Region | Metals | Common Form in Ores | Primary Extraction Method |
|---|---|---|---|
| Top (highly reactive) | K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al | Chlorides, oxides, or carbonates; never found free | Electrolysis of the molten compound |
| Middle (moderately reactive) | Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb | Oxides, sulphides, carbonates | Convert to oxide, then reduce with carbon |
| Bottom (low reactivity) | Cu, Hg, Ag, Au | Found free (native), or as sulphides/oxides | Heating alone, or already found in the native state |

Think of this as your **road map** for the chapter. The upcoming topics will unpack each step with balanced equations and real industrial furnaces.

Ores are not spread evenly across the planet; geological processes concentrate them in specific mineral belts (examples below are illustrative):

## Part F: Solved Examples (The Math of Choosing an Ore)

Atomic masses: O = 16, Al = 27, S = 32, Fe = 56, Zn = 65, H = 1, C = 12. Method (from Class 9 percentage composition): **mass percentage of metal = (total mass of metal in formula ÷ formula mass of compound) × 100.**

### Solved Example 1: Iron Minerals—Which One Is the "Richest"?

Consider four iron-bearing minerals: Fe₂O₃, Fe₃O₄, FeCO₃, and FeS₂.

| Mineral | Formula Mass | Mass of Fe | Percentage of Fe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fe₂O₃ | 112 + 48 = 160 | 112 | 112 ÷ 160 × 100 = **70%** |
| Fe₃O₄ | 168 + 64 = 232 | 168 | 168 ÷ 232 × 100 = **72.41%** |
| FeCO₃ | 56 + 12 + 48 = 116 | 56 | 56 ÷ 116 × 100 = **48.28%** |
| FeS₂ | 56 + 64 = 120 | 56 | 56 ÷ 120 × 100 = **46.67%** |

**Conclusion:** The highest iron percentage is in **Fe₃O₄ (magnetite)** at 72.41%, followed closely by Fe₂O₃ (70%), then FeCO₃ (48.28%), and lowest in FeS₂ (46.67%).

**However, percentage alone is not the whole story.** Worldwide, most iron is extracted from **haematite (Fe₂O₃)** because it occurs in vast, easily mined deposits. FeS₂ has a lower iron percentage and releases large amounts of polluting sulphur dioxide when roasted, so it is generally not used as an iron ore. **Choosing an ore depends on three things together: metal percentage, abundance of the deposit, and ease and cost of extraction.**

### Solved Example 2: How Much Iron Can Come from Raw Ore?

A 250 kg batch of raw haematite ore contains 64% Fe₂O₃ (the rest being gangue). What is the maximum theoretical mass of iron that can be extracted from it?

- Mass of Fe₂O₃ = 64% × 250 = 0.64 × 250 = **160 kg**.
- Since pure Fe₂O₃ contains 70% Fe (from Example 1), mass of Fe = 0.70 × 160 = **112 kg**.

Check: 160 kg of Fe₂O₃ = 1000 mol (since 1 mol = 160 g, 160 kg = 1000 mol); each mole contains 2 mol Fe, giving 2000 mol Fe = 2000 × 56 = 112,000 g = **112 kg** ✅.

In an actual smelter, the yield is slightly lower because a small fraction of iron is lost in the slag.

### Solved Example 3: Zinc from Zinc Blende

A 970 kg sample of raw zinc blende ore contains 50% ZnS. What is the maximum mass of zinc that can be obtained?

- Mass of ZnS = 0.50 × 970 = **485 kg**.
- Formula mass of ZnS = 65 + 32 = 97. Percentage of Zn = 65 ÷ 97 × 100 = **67.01%**.
- Maximum mass of Zn = 485 × 65 ÷ 97 = **325 kg**.

Check: 485 ÷ 97 = 5, and 5 × 65 = 325 ✅.

### Solved Example 4: Aluminium from Bauxite

The principal mineral in bauxite is Al₂O₃·2H₂O. Formula mass = 54 + 48 + 36 = **138** (since 2H₂O = 36). Mass of aluminium = 2 × 27 = 54.

- Percentage of Al = 54 ÷ 138 × 100 = **39.13%**.
- Mass of pure bauxite mineral needed to obtain 27 kg of aluminium = 27 ÷ (54 ÷ 138) = 27 × 138 ÷ 54 = **69 kg**.

Check: 69 kg × 39.13% = 27 kg ✅. Because real mined bauxite also contains gangue, a larger mass of raw ore is required in practice.

## Part G: A Data-Interpretation Task

A smelting company has to choose between two zinc ores: **ZnS** (zinc blende) and **ZnCO₃** (calamine). Assume both are available at the same price and in equal abundance. Which one should the plant choose?

The formula mass of ZnCO₃ = 65 + 12 + 48 = 125, so % Zn = 65 ÷ 125 × 100 = **52%**. For ZnS, % Zn = **67.01%** (from Solved Example 3).

- **Metal content:** ZnS contains a higher percentage of zinc (67.01% vs. 52%), so 100 kg of pure ZnS yields more zinc than 100 kg of pure ZnCO₃.
- **First processing step:** ZnS must be heated in air (roasting), producing ZnO and sulphur dioxide (a toxic gas that must be captured); ZnCO₃ is heated in limited air (calcination), producing ZnO and carbon dioxide. Both paths lead to the same intermediate product: **ZnO**, which is then reduced. We will study this in detail in Topic 3.
- **Decision:** Looking purely at metal percentage, **ZnS** wins; however, once you factor in the cost of scrubbing or converting toxic sulphur dioxide gas, the economics can shift. In real industry, engineers weigh **metal percentage, deposit size, and processing/environmental costs** together.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Minerals and ores are the exact same thing."** Incorrect. Remember Noah in the museum gallery looking at a chunk of quartz and assuming it was an "ore." Mr. Rivera asked him: "What metal do we extract from it?" Quartz contains only silicon and oxygen (SiO₂), with no metal to extract. So quartz is a **mineral, not an ore**. Similarly, ordinary clay contains aluminium, but extracting it is too expensive, so clay is also a mineral and not an ore. **Every ore is a mineral, but not every mineral is an ore.**

**Mistake 2: "Miners dig out only pure ore and leave the gangue behind in the ground."** Incorrect: gangue is **intimately mixed right inside the mined rock**. Noah wondered, "Why don't miners just dig around the gangue?" Mr. Rivera explained: "In the rock vein, ore grains and gangue grains are **locked together in the same stone**, like salt mixed with sand. You have to dig the whole rock out first, then crush it and separate them." That is why the concentration step (Topic 2) is essential.

**Mistake 3: "All metals can be found as pure nuggets in the ground."** Incorrect. Only very unreactive metals like **gold, silver, and platinum** (and occasionally copper) occur free in nature. Iron, aluminium, sodium, and zinc are never found uncombined in the earth's crust. When Noah asked where steel beams come from, the answer was: **extracted through multiple chemical steps from ores like haematite.**

**Mistake 4: "The mineral with the highest metal percentage is always the main ore used."** Incorrect (see Solved Example 1). Engineers must consider **deposit abundance** and **ease of processing** alongside percentage. Magnetite has a slightly higher iron content than haematite (72.41% vs. 70%), yet more iron worldwide is produced from haematite because huge, easily accessible haematite deposits are more common.

**Mistake 5: "All ores are oxides."** Incorrect. Ores can be oxides, sulphides, carbonates, or chlorides (see the table in Part B). That is precisely why we need roasting and calcination before reduction.

## Quick Recap

- **Mineral:** A naturally occurring substance in the earth's crust with a definite composition. **Ore:** A mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably. **Gangue:** Earthy impurities like sand and clay mixed with the ore.
- Unreactive metals (gold, silver, platinum, sometimes copper) occur **free (native)**; reactive metals occur combined as oxides, sulphides, carbonates, or chlorides.
- Key ores to know: bauxite Al₂O₃·2H₂O, haematite Fe₂O₃, magnetite Fe₃O₄, zinc blende ZnS, calamine ZnCO₃, galena PbS, cuprite Cu₂O, copper pyrites CuFeS₂, cinnabar HgS, rock salt NaCl.
- Five stages of metallurgy: mining, concentration, conversion to oxide, reduction, and refining.
- Extraction method follows reactivity: top group (K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al) by electrolysis of molten compounds; middle group (Zn, Fe, Sn, Pb) by reduction with carbon; bottom group (Cu, Hg, Ag, Au) by heating alone or found native.
- Mass percentage of metal = (total mass of metal in formula ÷ formula mass) × 100. Fe₂O₃ = 70%, Fe₃O₄ = 72.41%, ZnS = 67.01%, Al₂O₃·2H₂O = 39.13%.
- Ores are concentrated in specific geological regions around the world (such as iron and bauxite belts in Australia, Brazil, and India; copper in Chile and Rajasthan).

### Answers

1. **Mineral:** A naturally occurring substance in the earth's crust with a definite chemical composition. **Ore:** A mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably. **Gangue:** Unwanted earthy impurities such as sand and clay mixed with the ore. Example of a mineral that is not an ore: **quartz (SiO₂)**, because it contains no useful metal (or **clay**, because extracting aluminium from it is too expensive).
2. Cu₂O: **oxide** ore (copper). CuFeS₂: **sulphide** ore (copper). ZnCO₃: **carbonate** ore (zinc). NaCl: **chloride** ore (sodium). Fe₃O₄: **oxide** ore (iron). PbS: **sulphide** ore (lead). MgCO₃: **carbonate** ore (magnesium).
3. Formula mass of Cu₂O = 127 + 16 = 143. % Cu = 127 ÷ 143 × 100 = **88.81%**. Formula mass of CuFeS₂ = 63.5 + 56 + 64 = 183.5. % Cu = 63.5 ÷ 183.5 × 100 = **34.60%**. **Cu₂O is much richer in copper.**
4. Mass of SnO₂ = 0.30 × 500 = **150 kg**. Formula mass of SnO₂ = 119 + 32 = 151. Maximum mass of Sn = 150 × 119 ÷ 151 = **118.21 kg** (approx.).
5. MgCO₃ = 24 + 12 + 48 = 84; % Mg = 24 ÷ 84 × 100 = **28.57%**. MgCl₂ = 24 + 71 = 95; % Mg = 24 ÷ 95 × 100 = **25.26%**. **MgCO₃ has more magnesium** per 100 g.
6. Mass of NaCl = 0.90 × 650 = **585 kg**. Formula mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5. Maximum mass of Na = 585 × 23 ÷ 58.5 = 10 × 23 = **230 kg**. Check: 230 ÷ 585 × 100 = 39.3% ✅ (matches the Class 9 percentage).
7. Formula mass of Ag₂S = 216 + 32 = 248. % Ag = 216 ÷ 248 × 100 = **87.10%**. In 124 g of Ag₂S: 124 ÷ 248 = 0.5 mol, so mass of Ag = 0.5 × 216 = **108 g**.
8. Mass of MnO₂ = 0.87 × 1000 = **870 kg**. Formula mass of MnO₂ = 55 + 32 = 87. Maximum mass of Mn = 870 × 55 ÷ 87 = 10 × 55 = **550 kg**.
9. Calculate percentages first: Cr₂O₃ = 104 + 48 = 152, % Cr = 104 ÷ 152 × 100 = **68.42%** (accepted). MnO₂ = 87, % Mn = 55 ÷ 87 × 100 = **63.22%** (accepted). MgCO₃ = **28.57%** (rejected). NaCl = approx. **39.3%** (rejected). Therefore, **Cr₂O₃ and MnO₂ are accepted**, while MgCO₃ and NaCl are rejected.
10. **Mg and Al:** Top group—electrolysis of the molten compound. **Sn and Pb:** Middle group—convert to oxide and reduce with carbon. **Hg and Ag:** Bottom group—heating alone, or found native (Ag often occurs free as well).
11. (a) **False** ❌: Gold is found free because it is **very unreactive**. (b) **False** ❌: Every ore is a mineral, but not every mineral is an ore. (c) **True** ✅: ZnS is a sulphide ore. (d) **False** ❌: Concentration comes right after mining, whereas refining is the final step. (e) **True** ✅.
12. Correct sequence: **mining, concentration, conversion to oxide, reduction, refining.** Although clay contains aluminium, extracting the metal from clay is far too expensive compared to extracting it from bauxite. Because an ore is defined as a mineral from which a metal can be extracted **profitably**, clay is not an ore of aluminium.
