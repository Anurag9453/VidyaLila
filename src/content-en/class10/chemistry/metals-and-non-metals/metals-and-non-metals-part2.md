# Topic 2: How Metals React with Oxygen

In the previous topic, we recognised metals by their **appearance and physical behaviour**: shine, malleability, ductility, and conductivity. Now let's examine their **chemical personality**: what happens when a metal meets the **oxygen** in the air? Some metals sit quietly in open air for centuries without changing, some gradually develop dark or green coatings, and others are so reactive that they must be kept submerged under kerosene oil! We already saw in Chapter 1 that metallic **oxides** are generally basic; today we will discover **how metallic oxides form, how much oxide is produced, and whether an oxide layer destroys or protects the metal underneath**. Finally, we will meet a remarkable double-agent: an oxide that reacts with **both** acids and bases.

---

## The Tarnished Copper Pot and the Shiny Aluminium Foil

While helping his uncle, Mr. Ross, clean out an old kitchen cupboard,Lucas pulled out an antique copper saucepan. Its surface was dull, dark brownish-black with patches of greenish crust, as if time had weathered it. Yet on the very same shelf sat an open roll of aluminium foil, gleaming like a mirror even though it had been sitting in the same air every day.

"Uncle Ross, how does that work?" Lucas asked. "Both copper and aluminium are metals sitting in the exact same kitchen air. Why did the copper pot turn dark and crusty while the aluminium foil still shines like new?"

Mr. Ross held the copper pot in one hand and the roll of aluminium foil in the other. "That is today's central mystery, Lucas. Air is about 21% oxygen (roughly one part in five), and oxygen readily combines with most metals. However, each metal forms a very different kind of **oxide layer**. On some metals, the coating flakes or corrodes the surface further. On others, the oxide forms a tight, invisible **suit of armour** that shields the metal underneath! Let's trace how different metals react with oxygen one by one."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxide** | A compound formed when an element chemically combines with oxygen | Al₂O₃, CuO, MgO |
| **Tarnish** | A thin, dull surface film formed on a metal by reaction with gases in the air | Black tarnish (Ag₂S) on silver |
| **Patina** | The green surface layer that slowly forms on copper and bronze exposed to moist air | Green coating on the Statue of Liberty or old copper domes |
| **Anodising** | An electrolytic process used to thicken the protective oxide layer on aluminium | Anodised aluminium window frames and cookware |
| **Amphoteric oxide** | A metallic oxide that reacts with **both** acids and bases to form salt and water | Al₂O₃, ZnO |
| **Alkali** | A base that dissolves in water | NaOH, KOH |
| **Passivation (protective oxide film)** | A thin, non-porous surface oxide layer that prevents further corrosion of the metal beneath it | Al₂O₃ layer on aluminium |

## Part A: Metals and Atmospheric Oxygen at a Glance

Metal + Oxygen → **Metal oxide**. In Class 9 we defined this addition of oxygen as **oxidation**. However, different metals react with oxygen at dramatically different speeds: **rapid burning at room temperature**, **burning only when heated**, **slow surface coating**, or **no reaction at all**.

| Metal | What Happens in Air / Oxygen | Balanced Equation | Safety & Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sodium (Na) | A freshly cut shiny surface turns dull within seconds in air; burns with a golden-yellow flame when warmed | 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O | Observation only (teacher demo); stored under kerosene |
| Magnesium (Mg) | Develops a thin dull film in air; when ignited, burns with a blinding white flame to leave white ash | 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO | Observation only (teacher demo); never look directly at the flame |
| Aluminium (Al) | Instantly forms a microscopic, tightly clinging oxide skin; reaction then stops | 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ | The oxide layer protects the metal |
| Iron (Fe) | Solid iron bar does not catch fire on heating, but fine iron wool burns with bright sparks | 3Fe + 2O₂ → Fe₃O₄ | Observation only (teacher demo) |
| Copper (Cu) | Does not catch fire, but when heated in air it becomes coated with a black layer of copper(II) oxide | 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO | Heating is a teacher demo |
| Silver (Ag) | Does not react with oxygen even on strong heating; slowly turns black in air due to trace sulphur gases | 4Ag + 2H₂S + O₂ → 2Ag₂S + 2H₂O | Happens very slowly over months |
| Gold (Au), Platinum (Pt) | No reaction with oxygen at any temperature | No reaction | That is why they never lose their shine |

Atom count check for every equation: Na 4=4, O 2=2; Mg 2=2, O 2=2; Al 4=4, O 6=6; Fe 3=3, O 4=4; Cu 2=2, O 2=2; Ag 4=4, H 4=4, S 2=2, O 2=2 ✅.

Notice how this table previews the **reactivity ladder** we met in Class 9: metals near the top of the ladder react eagerly with oxygen, metals in the middle require heating, and metals at the bottom (silver, gold, platinum) resist oxygen altogether.

## Part B: Why Is Sodium Stored Under Kerosene?

Sodium (along with potassium) is so reactive that a freshly sliced piece reacts with atmospheric oxygen and moisture **within seconds**, and if left exposed or accidentally touched by water, it can catch fire! To prevent accidental contact with air and moisture, sodium and potassium are always stored submerged under **kerosene** (paraffin oil) in the laboratory store.

4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O

Most metallic oxides are insoluble in water, but **sodium oxide (Na₂O)** and **potassium oxide (K₂O)** dissolve readily in water to form strong **alkalis**:

Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH

Similarly, K₂O + H₂O → 2KOH, and CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (slaked lime, whose aqueous solution is limewater). Atom count check: Na 2=2, O 2=2, H 2=2 ✅; K 2=2, O 2=2, H 2=2 ✅; Ca 1=1, O 2=2, H 2=2 ✅.

Remember: **not all** metal oxides dissolve in water! Most metal oxides (such as CuO, Fe₃O₄, and Al₂O₃) are completely insoluble in water. Only the oxides of highly reactive metals near the top of the series (such as Na, K, and slightly Ca) dissolve in water to produce alkalis. (We already saw why metal oxides are classified as basic in [acids-bases-and-salts-part3.md](../acids-bases-and-salts/acids-bases-and-salts-part3.md), Part D.)

> **Quick Check:** If sodium oxide is dissolved in water, what will the solution do to red litmus paper? It will turn red litmus **blue** ✅, because NaOH is an alkali.

**Burning Magnesium Ribbon (Observation only — teacher demonstration):** Holding a strip of cleaned magnesium ribbon with crucible tongs, the teacher ignites it in a Bunsen flame. It burns with a dazzling white light (students must not stare directly at the flame) and leaves behind a crumbly white powder of **magnesium oxide (MgO)**.

## Part C: Aluminium's Built-In Shield

Now let's return to Lucas's roll of shiny aluminium foil! Aluminium is actually a very reactive metal—not far below magnesium on the reactivity ladder. The moment fresh aluminium meets air, oxygen atoms bond to its surface to form an ultra-thin film of **aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃)**, only a few nanometres thick (a few millionths of a millimetre). Unlike rust on iron, however, this Al₂O₃ film is **tough, non-porous, and tightly bonded** to the metal underneath. Neither air nor water can penetrate through it, so the pure aluminium beneath stays completely protected!

```
         AIR (O₂ and moisture)
              :   :   :
              v   v   v
    +--------------------------------+
    : O  Al  O  Al  O  Al  O  Al  O  :   <- Tight, non-porous Al₂O₃ shield
    +--------------------------------+      (blocks air and moisture)
    Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al
    Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al   <- Pure aluminium underneath
    Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al      (staying completely safe!)
    Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al  Al
```

> **Figure 2.1** – The microscopic, tightly adherent layer of Al₂O₃ on the surface of aluminium. Because this layer seals off the metal beneath from air and moisture, corrosion stops right at the surface.

**Why Iron Rusts Away While Aluminium Survives:** Rust on iron (which we studied in Class 9, [chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md)) is **flaky and porous**; it crumbles and peels off, continually exposing fresh iron underneath until the whole piece is eaten through. Aluminium's oxide layer is **compact and non-flaking**—it acts as a permanent protective shield (**passivation**). That is why aluminium foil, beverage cans, aircraft skins, and window frames last for years without rusting.

**Anodising (For study):** In industry, this natural oxide armour on aluminium is deliberately made much thicker using an electrolytic process called **anodising**. A clean aluminium article is made the anode in an electrolytic bath of dilute sulphuric acid; oxygen gas liberated at the anode reacts with the aluminium surface to build a thick, tough **Al₂O₃ coating**. This anodised layer resists scratches and corrosion even better, and it can be dyed in attractive metallic colours for cookware, water bottles, and architectural window frames.

### Safe At-Home Observation: Aluminium Foil vs. an Old Copper Coin (With an Adult)

Take a small square of fresh **aluminium foil** and note its bright shine. Leave it on a shelf exposed to air for a couple of weeks. **Observation:** It shows no flaking or discolouration—at most a barely noticeable satin sheen. Compare it with an old copper coin or copper vessel that has turned dark brown or greenish over time. *(Handle foil edges carefully with an adult so you don't nick your fingers.)*

One practical kitchen rule follows directly from this: **do not store strongly acidic foods (like lemon pickle, tomato sauce, or tamarind) or strongly alkaline cleaners in bare aluminium vessels for long periods**, because both acids and alkalis dissolve the protective Al₂O₃ shield! That brings us to the special amphoteric nature of Al₂O₃ in Part E.

## Part D: Copper and Silver: Changing Colours in Air

In Class 9 ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md)), we briefly met the corrosion of copper, silver, and aluminium. Here we connect those observations to their chemical equations and stoichiometry.

**Copper (Cu):** When bright reddish-brown copper wire or foil is heated strongly in air (**observation only — teacher demonstration**), its surface turns black as a layer of **copper(II) oxide (CuO)** forms:

2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO

Even without heating, copper objects left exposed to moist air for months or years slowly lose their shine. They react with atmospheric **oxygen (O₂), carbon dioxide (CO₂), and water vapour (H₂O)** to develop first a dark brown tarnish and eventually a characteristic green coating called **patina**, which consists mainly of **basic copper carbonate**, CuCO₃·Cu(OH)₂. That is why historic bronze statues (like the Statue of Liberty) and old cathedral domes are green! Like aluminium's oxide, this green patina adheres fairly well and slows down deeper corrosion of the copper underneath.

**Silver (Ag):** Silver does **not** combine directly with oxygen even when heated. Instead, silver jewellery, coins, and cutlery slowly turn black when exposed to trace amounts of **hydrogen sulphide (H₂S)** gas present in the air (released by decaying organic matter, cooked eggs, onions, or industrial pollution):

4Ag + 2H₂S + O₂ → 2Ag₂S + 2H₂O

The black coating is **silver sulphide (Ag₂S)**—a sulphide, not an oxide! Notice that atmospheric oxygen helps drive the reaction by combining with the hydrogen of H₂S to form water, while the sulphur bonds to the silver.

```
   TARNISHED SURFACE (Black/Dark)       CLEANED SURFACE
   Coated by reaction with air gases    Surface film removed

   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~             ------------------------
   xxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxxx  <- Ag₂S    Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag
   Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag               Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag
   Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag               Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag
   Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag  Ag

   Looks dull and black                 Original bright metallic lustre returns
```

> **Figure 2.2** – On a tarnished metal surface, a thin layer of Ag₂S (or CuO / green patina on copper) blocks light reflection, making the metal look dull and dark. Removing that surface film reveals the shiny pure metal underneath.

### Safe At-Home Activity: Cleaning a Dull Copper Coin (With an Adult)

Take an old, dull brownish copper coin (or the base of a copper pot). Place it in a small bowl with a little **lemon juice** or **vinegar** for a few minutes, then rub it gently with a cloth. **Observation:** The dark coating dissolves and the bright reddish-brown shine of copper returns! **Reason:** The citric or acetic acid reacts with and dissolves the basic copper oxide / basic copper carbonate coating on the surface (recall from Chapter 1 Part 3 that metallic oxides and carbonates dissolve in acids). Rinse the coin and wash your hands thoroughly afterwards; never taste anything used in an experiment.

### Observing Silver Tarnish (At Home with an Adult)

Place a small silver spoon or silver trinket inside a sealed jar alongside a slice of raw onion or a peeled hard-boiled egg for a couple of days at room temperature (do not heat the jar, and discard the onion or egg in the trash afterwards—do not eat it!). You will see a dark tarnish film of **Ag₂S** appear on the silver surface, which can be polished off with a soft cloth and mild baking-soda paste.

**Gold and Platinum:** Neither oxygen nor moisture nor H₂S gas affects gold or platinum at all, which is why ancient gold coins and royal jewellery recovered after thousands of years still gleam as brightly as the day they were forged.

## Part E: Amphoteric Oxides: Friends with Both Acids and Bases

In Chapter 1, we learned the general rule: metallic oxides are **basic**, while non-metallic oxides are **acidic**. We also saw that zinc metal reacts with warm NaOH to form sodium zincate (Na₂ZnO₂). Now let's look at the **oxides** of **aluminium (Al₂O₃)** and **zinc (ZnO)**. These two metallic oxides sit right on the chemical borderline: they react with **acids** (behaving like a base) **and** they also react with **strong bases/alkalis** (behaving like an acid) to produce a salt and water in both cases! Such dual-natured oxides are called **amphoteric oxides** (*ampho* = both).

**Reaction with an Acid** (behaving as a basic oxide):

Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O
ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O

**Reaction with a Base / Alkali** (behaving as an acidic oxide):

Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O   (Sodium aluminate)
ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O   (Sodium zincate)

Atom count check: Al 2=2, O 3=3, H 6=6, Cl 6=6 ✅; Zn 1=1, O 1=1, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅; Al 2=2, Na 2=2, O 3+2=5 = 4+1=5, H 2=2 ✅; Zn 1=1, Na 2=2, O 1+2=3 = 2+1=3, H 2=2 ✅.

```
     WITH AN ACID                          WITH A BASE

     Al₂O₃  +  6HCl                        Al₂O₃  +  2NaOH
        \       /                             \        /
         v     v                               v      v
     2AlCl₃  +  3H₂O                       2NaAlO₂  +  H₂O
     (salt)    (water)                     (sodium aluminate + water)

     Oxide acts like a BASE                Oxide acts like an ACID
```

> **Figure 2.3** – An amphoteric oxide (such as Al₂O₃) reacts with an acid to form salt and water (acting like a basic oxide), and also reacts with a strong base to form salt and water (acting like an acidic oxide).

Because Al₂O₃ is amphoteric, aluminium's protective oxide shield dissolves in **both** strong acids and strong alkalis—which is why neither strongly sour pickles nor caustic soda cleaners should ever be stored in aluminium containers!

> **Quick Check:** A basic oxide reacts only with acids, an acidic oxide reacts only with bases, and an **amphoteric oxide** reacts with **both**! ✅

### Summary Rule: Classifying Any Oxide by Its Reactions

| Oxide Tested | Reacts with Acid (HCl)? | Reacts with Base (NaOH)? | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| CuO, MgO, Na₂O | Yes | No | **Basic oxide** (metal oxide) |
| CO₂, SO₂ | No | Yes | **Acidic oxide** (non-metal oxide) |
| Al₂O₃, ZnO | Yes | Yes | **Amphoteric oxide** |

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. How Much Oxide Forms on Aluminium?

**Problem:** Suppose 5.4 g of aluminium reacts completely with oxygen to form Al₂O₃. How many grams of Al₂O₃ are formed, and what mass of oxygen combined with the aluminium? *(Atomic masses: Al = 27, O = 16)*

**Solution:**
- Moles of Al = 5.4 ÷ 27 = **0.2 mol**.
- From 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃: 4 mol of Al produce 2 mol of Al₂O₃, so 0.2 mol of Al produces **0.1 mol of Al₂O₃**.
- Molar mass of Al₂O₃ = 2 × 27 + 3 × 16 = **102 g/mol**. Mass of Al₂O₃ = 0.1 × 102 = **10.2 g**.
- Mass of oxygen combined = 10.2 − 5.4 = **4.8 g**. Check: 3 mol O₂ per 4 mol Al gives 0.15 mol O₂ = 0.15 × 32 = 4.8 g ✅.

**Takeaway:** Whenever a metal combines with oxygen to form an oxide, **the solid mass increases** (here, from 5.4 g to 10.2 g) because the mass of the captured oxygen is added to the metal!

### Example 2. Burning Magnesium Ribbon (Observation Only — Teacher Demo)

**Problem:** If 2.4 g of magnesium ribbon burns completely in air, what mass of white MgO ash is produced? *(Mg = 24, O = 16)*

**Solution:** Moles of Mg = 2.4 ÷ 24 = 0.1 mol. From 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, 0.1 mol of Mg yields **0.1 mol of MgO**. Molar mass of MgO = 24 + 16 = 40 g/mol, so mass of MgO = 0.1 × 40 = **4.0 g**. Thus, 1.6 g of oxygen combined with the ribbon (4.0 − 2.4), and the white ash weighs **more** than the original metal ribbon ✅.

### Example 3. Reacting Aluminium Oxide with Hydrochloric Acid

**Problem:** If the 10.2 g of Al₂O₃ from Example 1 reacts completely with dilute HCl, what mass of HCl is consumed and what mass of AlCl₃ is formed? *(Al = 27, O = 16, H = 1, Cl = 35.5)*

**Solution:** Moles of Al₂O₃ = 10.2 ÷ 102 = 0.1 mol. From Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O: 0.1 mol of Al₂O₃ requires **0.6 mol of HCl** = 0.6 × 36.5 = **21.9 g**. Moles of AlCl₃ formed = 0.2 mol; molar mass = 27 + 3 × 35.5 = 133.5 g/mol, giving mass = 0.2 × 133.5 = **26.7 g**. Water formed = 0.3 mol = **5.4 g**. Check: reactants = 10.2 + 21.9 = 32.1 g; products = 26.7 + 5.4 = 32.1 g ✅.

### Example 4. Identifying Oxides from Laboratory Test Data

**Problem:** Four different oxides—P, Q, R, and S—were tested separately with dilute HCl and aqueous NaOH in the laboratory:

| Oxide | In Dilute HCl | In Aqueous NaOH |
|---|---|---|
| P | Dissolved to give a bluish-green solution | Did not dissolve |
| Q | Dissolved to give a clear solution | Dissolved to give a clear solution |
| R | Did not dissolve | Dissolved |
| S | Did not dissolve | Did not dissolve |

Classify each oxide, and state which one could be aluminium oxide.

**Solution:**
- **P**: Reacts only with acid → **Basic oxide** (specifically CuO, which forms bluish-green CuCl₂ in HCl).
- **Q**: Reacts with both acid and base → **Amphoteric oxide**. This could be aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) or zinc oxide (ZnO).
- **R**: Reacts only with base → **Acidic oxide** (a non-metal oxide such as SO₂ or SiO₂).
- **S**: Reacts with neither in this test → Either a **neutral oxide** (such as CO or N₂O, which we will study in Topic 5) or an unreactive/calcined oxide requiring further testing.

Therefore, **Q** is the oxide that could be aluminium oxide.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Aluminium stays shiny because it does not react with oxygen."**
It does react—and almost instantaneously! What keeps it shiny and prevents further corrosion is the microscopic, transparent, air-tight **Al₂O₃ shield** that forms on its surface.

**Mistake 2: "Every oxide layer eats away its metal the way rust eats iron."**
No! Rust on iron is flaky and destructive, whereas the Al₂O₃ layer on aluminium is compact and **protective**.

**Mistake 3: "All metal oxides dissolve in water to form alkalis."**
Only a few metal oxides (such as Na₂O, K₂O, and slightly CaO) dissolve in water. Most metal oxides (such as CuO, Fe₃O₄, ZnO, and Al₂O₃) are completely **insoluble** in water—if you stir black CuO powder into water, it simply settles to the bottom and leaves red litmus paper unchanged, whereas CaO reacts with water to form alkaline limewater that turns red litmus blue.

**Mistake 4: "The black tarnish on silver is silver oxide."**
No! It is **silver sulphide (Ag₂S)**, formed by reaction with trace H₂S gas in the air.

**Mistake 5: "Amphoteric means an oxide is neutral (neither acidic nor basic)."**
Exact opposite! A neutral oxide reacts with **neither** acids nor bases, whereas an **amphoteric oxide** reacts with **both** acids and bases to form salt and water.

**Mistake 6: "Sodium is stored under water to keep air away."**
Never! Sodium reacts violently with water. It is always stored under **kerosene (paraffin oil)**.

## Quick Recap

- Metal + Oxygen → **Metal oxide**; the rate depends on the metal's reactivity: Na and K react at room temperature, Mg burns with a dazzling white flame when ignited, Al forms an instant protective skin, Fe and Cu react when heated, and Ag, Au, and Pt do not react with oxygen (Ag tarnishes via H₂S).
- Sodium and potassium are stored under **kerosene** to prevent contact with air and moisture; soluble oxides like Na₂O and K₂O dissolve in water to form alkalis (**Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH**).
- **Aluminium** is protected by a thin, non-porous layer of **Al₂O₃** (passivation); **anodising** thickens this layer electrolytically.
- **Copper:** forms black CuO when heated in air, and slowly develops a green **patina** (basic copper carbonate) in moist air. **Silver:** forms black **Ag₂S** tarnish with H₂S and O₂. Gold and platinum never tarnish.
- **Amphoteric oxides** (Al₂O₃, ZnO) react with both acids and bases to form salt and water (forming aluminates/zincates with NaOH).
- When a metal forms an oxide, the solid mass increases by the mass of combined oxygen (e.g., 5.4 g Al → 10.2 g Al₂O₃).

### Answers

1. Sodium reacts rapidly with atmospheric oxygen and moisture (and violently with water), so it is stored submerged under kerosene to keep air and moisture away. On a freshly cut piece: **4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O** (Na 4=4, O 2=2 ✅).

2. Moles of Na = 4.6 ÷ 23 = 0.2 mol. From 4Na + O₂ → 2Na₂O: 0.2 mol of Na yields 0.1 mol of Na₂O. Molar mass of Na₂O = 2 × 23 + 16 = 62 g/mol, so mass = **6.2 g**. From Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH: 0.1 mol of Na₂O yields 0.2 mol of NaOH = 0.2 × 40 = **8.0 g** (NaOH = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40). Check: 6.2 g + 1.8 g water (0.1 mol) = 8.0 g ✅.

3. **3Fe + 2O₂ → Fe₃O₄** (Fe 3=3, O 4=4 ✅). Because red-hot iron wool showers white-hot sparks as it burns, this is performed only as a teacher demonstration.

4. Moles of Fe = 16.8 ÷ 56 = 0.3 mol. From 3Fe → 1Fe₃O₄, we get 0.1 mol of Fe₃O₄. Molar mass of Fe₃O₄ = 3 × 56 + 4 × 16 = 232 g/mol, so mass = **23.2 g**. Mass of combined oxygen = 23.2 − 16.8 = **6.4 g** (0.2 mol O₂ × 32 = 6.4 g ✅).

5. Moles of Cu = 12.7 ÷ 63.5 = 0.2 mol. From 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO: 0.2 mol of CuO is formed. Molar mass of CuO = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5 g/mol, so mass = **15.9 g**. The black coating formed on heating is copper(II) oxide (CuO), whereas the green coating on old statues is patina (basic copper carbonate) formed slowly over years from O₂, CO₂, and moisture in the air.

6. Vulcanised rubber bands slowly release traces of sulphur-containing gas (such as H₂S). The black substance is **silver sulphide, Ag₂S**: **4Ag + 2H₂S + O₂ → 2Ag₂S + 2H₂O** (Ag 4=4, H 4=4, S 2=2, O 2=2 ✅). Although oxygen takes part in the reaction, it combines with the hydrogen of H₂S to leave as water (H₂O), while **sulphur** bonds to the silver. Therefore, the black tarnish is a **sulphide, not an oxide**.

7. Moles of Ag = 10.8 ÷ 108 = 0.1 mol. From 4Ag → 2Ag₂S, 0.1 mol of Ag forms 0.05 mol of Ag₂S. Molar mass of Ag₂S = 2 × 108 + 32 = 248 g/mol, so mass = **12.4 g**.

8. Because aluminium is highly reactive, its surface immediately combines with oxygen to form a thin, tough, non-porous film of **Al₂O₃** that clings tightly to the metal and blocks air and moisture from reaching the aluminium underneath. Iron rust, by contrast, is porous and flakes off, continually exposing fresh iron. 9. **Anodising** is an electrolytic process used to thicken the natural oxide layer (Al₂O₃) on aluminium. Two benefits: it makes the surface much more resistant to corrosion and wear, and the porous outer oxide layer can be dyed in attractive colours. Use: anodised aluminium window frames or cookware.

10. An **amphoteric oxide** is a metallic oxide that reacts with both acids and bases to form a salt and water. Equations for ZnO: ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O (Zn 1=1, O 1=1, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅) and ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O (Zn 1=1, Na 2=2, O 3=3, H 2=2 ✅).

11. (a) Molar mass of ZnO = 65 + 16 = 81 g/mol; moles = 8.1 ÷ 81 = **0.1 mol**. (b) From ZnO + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂O: 0.2 mol of HCl = 0.2 × 36.5 = **7.3 g**. (c) ZnCl₂ = 65 + 71 = 136 g/mol, so 0.1 mol = 13.6 g; water = 0.1 mol = 1.8 g. Reactants: 8.1 + 7.3 = 15.4 g; Products: 13.6 + 1.8 = 15.4 g ✅.

12. From ZnO + 2NaOH → Na₂ZnO₂ + H₂O: 0.1 mol of ZnO requires 0.2 mol of NaOH = 0.2 × 40 = **8.0 g**. Molar mass of Na₂ZnO₂ = 2 × 23 + 65 + 2 × 16 = 143 g/mol, so 0.1 mol = **14.3 g**. Check: reactants 8.1 + 8.0 = 16.1 g; products 14.3 + 1.8 (water) = 16.1 g ✅.

13. Moles of Al₂O₃ = 20.4 ÷ 102 = 0.2 mol. From Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O: 0.4 mol of NaOH = 0.4 × 40 = **16.0 g**; molar mass of NaAlO₂ = 23 + 27 + 32 = 82 g/mol, so 0.4 mol = **32.8 g**; water = 0.2 mol = 3.6 g. Reactants: 20.4 + 16.0 = 36.4 g; Products: 32.8 + 3.6 = 36.4 g ✅.

14. **W**: reacts only with base → **acidic oxide**. **X**: reacts with both acid and base → **amphoteric oxide**. **Y**: reacts only with acid → **basic oxide**. **Z**: dissolves in water to form an alkali (turning red litmus blue) → a **water-soluble basic oxide**, such as Na₂O, K₂O, or CaO (the oxide of sodium, potassium, or calcium). Example: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH.

15. (a) **False** ❌. Gold does not react with oxygen in air at any temperature. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌. Tarnish on silver is silver sulphide (Ag₂S). (d) **False** ❌. Only a few metal oxides (such as Na₂O, K₂O, and CaO) dissolve in water; most (such as CuO and Al₂O₃) are insoluble.
