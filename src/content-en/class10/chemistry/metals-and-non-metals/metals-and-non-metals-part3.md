# Topic 3: How Metals React with Water and Acids

In Topic 2, we saw that different metals react very differently with atmospheric oxygen: sodium tarnishes in seconds, magnesium burns with a blinding flame when ignited, iron reacts only when heated, and gold does not react at all. Today we will test metals against two new chemical partners: **water** (cold water, hot water, and steam) and **acids**. In Class 9 you saw sodium skitter across water, and in Chapter 1 you saw active metals release hydrogen gas from dilute acids. Today we will arrange all these reactions on a single **reactivity ladder**, practice stoichiometry with them, and meet two remarkable chemical characters: **nitric acid**, which refuses to release hydrogen gas with most metals, and **aqua regia ("royal water")**, which can dissolve even solid gold!

---

## Royal Water at the Goldsmith's Workshop

Mr. Sterling took his niece, Zara, to visit an old jewellery quarter in the city. Inside a well-ventilated workshop, master goldsmith Mr. Lorenzo was peering through thick magnifying lenses while cleaning an antique ring. Zara noticed that although the ring gleamed golden, a dark greenish-black crust had built up in its crevices. "Mr. Lorenzo, what is that dark crust on a gold ring?" Zara asked.

Mr. Lorenzo smiled: "This ring isn't 100% pure gold—it is an **alloy of gold and copper**, mixed with a little copper to make the ring strong enough for everyday wear. Over the years, the copper reacted with air and moisture to form that dark tarnish. Pure gold itself never tarnishes."

Then he pointed toward a locked steel safety cabinet in the corner marked with yellow-and-red hazard signs. "Inside that cabinet are my concentrated acids. When refining scrap gold, I prepare a small batch of a special acid mixture under the fume hood while wearing chemical goggles and gloves—and that liquid can dissolve solid gold right before your eyes!"

Zara looked astonished: "Wait—I thought acids couldn't attack gold! We learned that gold and platinum are noble metals that survive acids untouched!"

Mr. Lorenzo winked: "You learned right! No **single** acid can dissolve gold. But when you mix **two specific acids together**, they team up to do what neither acid can do alone. Chemists call that mixture **aqua regia**, which is Latin for **'royal water'**, because it dissolves gold, the king of metals!"

Stepping back from the cabinet with Zara, Mr. Sterling said, "That's a piece of chemistry we only study on paper, from a safe distance. Today let's see how metals react with **cold water**, **steam**, and **acids**—and how aqua regia pulls off its royal trick."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ladder of vigour** | An arrangement of metals in order of how vigorously they react with water and acids | K at the very top, Au at the very bottom |
| **Steam** | Water in the hot gaseous state (above 100 °C) | Steam passed over red-hot iron or zinc |
| **Hydroxide** | A compound of a metal with the hydroxide ion (OH⁻); soluble metal hydroxides are alkalis | NaOH, KOH, Ca(OH)₂ |
| **Oxidising acid** | An acid that oxidises the reacting substance itself instead of releasing hydrogen gas | Nitric acid, HNO₃ |
| **Nitrogen dioxide (NO₂)** | A toxic reddish-brown gas evolved when concentrated nitric acid reacts with metals like copper | Cu + 4HNO₃(conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O |
| **Aqua regia** | A freshly prepared 1 : 3 mixture of concentrated HNO₃ (1 part) and concentrated HCl (3 parts); "royal water" | Dissolves gold and platinum |
| **Noble metals** | Unreactive metals that are not attacked by air, water, or any single acid | Gold (Au), platinum (Pt) |
| **Alloy** | A homogeneous mixture of a metal with one or more other metals (or non-metals) | 18-carat gold (gold + copper/silver), brass |

## Part A: Metals and Water: The Ladder of Vigour

### 1. From the Top of the Ladder to the Bottom

In Class 9 you saw that sodium darts across cold water, calcium sinks and bubbles steadily, and copper does nothing at all. Let's put the whole picture together on a single ladder. On this ladder, **the more reactive the metal**, the **colder the water** it can react with; **the less reactive the metal**, the **more heat** (boiling water or red-hot steam) is required to make it react. And metals near the bottom do not react even with red-hot steam!

```
   METAL        BEHAVIOUR WITH WATER
   --------------------------------------------------------
   K  (potassium)   Reacts VIOLENTLY with cold water; evolved H₂
                    immediately catches fire (lilac flame)
   Na (sodium)      Reacts vigorously with cold water; evolved H₂
                    often catches fire (golden-yellow flame)
   Ca (calcium)     Reacts steadily with cold water; piece floats
                    up as H₂ bubbles cling to its surface
   Mg (magnesium)   Hardly reacts with cold water; reacts with HOT
                    water, and burns brightly in steam
   Al (aluminium)   Reacts only with steam (once oxide film breaks)
   Zn (zinc)        Reacts only with steam
   Fe (iron)        Reacts slowly with steam when red-hot
   --------------------------------------------------------
   Pb (lead)        No reaction with water or steam
   Cu (copper)      No reaction
   Hg (mercury)     No reaction
   Ag (silver)      No reaction
   Au (gold)        No reaction

   ^ Top: reacts with cold water, highly exothermic
   v Bottom: requires steam, or shows no reaction at all
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The Ladder of Vigour: how metals react with water. Moving down the ladder, the reaction becomes progressively gentler—from cold water to hot water, then to steam, and finally to no reaction at all below iron.

### 2. Balanced Equations Along the Ladder

All of these reactions follow a single unifying pattern: **Metal + Water → Metal hydroxide (or Metal oxide) + Hydrogen gas (H₂↑)**. Metals that react with cold or hot liquid water form **metal hydroxides**, whereas less reactive metals that react only with high-temperature **steam** form **metal oxides** (because at the high temperature of steam, their hydroxides decompose into oxides and water).

| Metal | Condition | Balanced Chemical Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Potassium | Cold water | 2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂↑ |
| Sodium | Cold water | 2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑ |
| Calcium | Cold water | Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂↑ |
| Magnesium | Hot water | Mg + 2H₂O → Mg(OH)₂ + H₂↑ |
| Magnesium | Steam | Mg + H₂O(g) → MgO + H₂↑ |
| Aluminium | Steam | 2Al + 3H₂O(g) → Al₂O₃ + 3H₂↑ |
| Zinc | Steam | Zn + H₂O(g) → ZnO + H₂↑ |
| Iron | Steam (red-hot Fe) | 3Fe + 4H₂O(g) ⇌ Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂↑ |

*(Atom-count check for each equation: 2K + 2H₂O: K 2, H 4, O 2 and 2KOH + H₂: K 2, O 2, H 2 + 2 = 4 ✅. Ca + 2H₂O: Ca 1, H 4, O 2 and Ca(OH)₂ + H₂: Ca 1, O 2, H 2 + 2 = 4 ✅. 2Al + 3H₂O: Al 2, H 6, O 3 and Al₂O₃ + 3H₂: Al 2, O 3, H 6 ✅. 3Fe + 4H₂O: Fe 3, H 8, O 4 and Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂: Fe 3, O 4, H 8 ✅. The remaining four equations balance in the same way.)*

Three key observations to remember:

- **Whenever a metal reacts with water or steam, hydrogen gas (H₂) is always evolved.** The active metal displaces hydrogen from water (just as we saw in Class 9).
- **Calcium sinks at first in cold water, but soon floats to the surface!** Why? Because bubbles of hydrogen gas formed on the surface of the calcium metal stick to it and lift the piece upward like tiny life-jackets. The water also turns milky because calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂, is only slightly soluble in water.
- **The reactions of sodium and potassium with cold water are so strongly exothermic** that the heat released ignites the escaping hydrogen gas! With potassium this happens every time; with sodium it happens almost immediately.

> **Activity 3.1 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): Metals and Cold Water.**
> Behind a transparent safety screen, the teacher drops a pea-sized piece of sodium (or calcium) into a wide glass trough half-filled with cold water while students watch from a safe distance. Sodium melts into a silvery globule that skitters and hisses across the water surface; calcium sinks at first and then bobs up and down with streams of H₂ bubbles. As we learned in Topic 2, sodium and potassium are kept under kerosene and cut only by the teacher right before the demo. **Students must never touch sodium, potassium, calcium, or magnesium—neither at home nor in the lab.**

> **Activity 3.2 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): Magnesium and Steam.**
> Behind a safety screen, the teacher passes steam over heated magnesium ribbon, which glows with an intense white light (**never look directly at the burning magnesium flame**). Reactions of steam with red-hot iron, zinc, and aluminium are performed only by trained instructors using specialised open-tube apparatus; never heat any closed container generating gas.

Why does magnesium barely react with cold water? Because its surface is coated with a thin **layer of magnesium oxide** (Topic 2) that blocks cold water. Hot water or steam breaks through that film and allows the reaction to proceed. Similarly, aluminium reacts with steam only after its protective Al₂O₃ skin is penetrated at high temperature.

### 3. Stoichiometry: Calculating Hydrogen Yield (Mass and Moles)

Now consider a great quantitative question: if we react **equal masses** (say, 10 g each) of different metals with steam, which metal produces the greatest mass of hydrogen gas? To find out, we use **mole ratios** and **atomic masses** (Na = 23, Mg = 24, Al = 27, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Zn = 65, H = 1, O = 16).

**Solved Example 1.** When 4.6 g of sodium reacts completely with cold water, how many grams of hydrogen gas and how many grams of NaOH are produced?

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

- Moles of sodium = 4.6 ÷ 23 = **0.2 mol**.
- From the balanced equation, 2 mol of Na yield 1 mol of H₂, so 0.2 mol of Na yields **0.1 mol of H₂** = 0.1 × 2 = **0.2 g of hydrogen**.
- 2 mol of Na yield 2 mol of NaOH, so 0.2 mol of Na yields 0.2 mol of NaOH = 0.2 × 40 = **8.0 g of NaOH** (NaOH = 23 + 16 + 1 = 40).
- *(Check conservation of mass: 4.6 g Na + 0.2 mol × 18 g/mol water (3.6 g) = 8.2 g total reactants. Products: 8.0 g NaOH + 0.2 g H₂ = 8.2 g ✅)*

**Solved Example 2.** If 10 g each of magnesium, aluminium, zinc, and iron react completely with steam, how many grams of hydrogen does each produce?

From the balanced equations in Part A, the mole ratios (Metal : H₂) are: Mg 1 : 1, Al 2 : 3, Zn 1 : 1, Fe 3 : 4.

| Metal (10 g sample) | Moles of Metal | Moles of H₂ Formed | Mass of H₂ Formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magnesium (Mg) | 10 ÷ 24 = 0.4167 | 0.4167 | **0.83 g** |
| Aluminium (Al) | 10 ÷ 27 = 0.3704 | 0.3704 × 1.5 = 0.5556 | **1.11 g** |
| Zinc (Zn) | 10 ÷ 65 = 0.1538 | 0.1538 | **0.31 g** |
| Iron (Fe) | 10 ÷ 56 = 0.1786 | 0.1786 × 4 ÷ 3 = 0.2381 | **0.48 g** |

Key insight: **10 g of aluminium produces the most hydrogen (1.11 g)**—more than 10 g of magnesium (0.83 g), even though magnesium is more reactive! Why? Because aluminium has a low atomic mass (27 u) and each 2 atoms of Al donate 6 electrons to produce 3 molecules of H₂ (1.5 mol H₂ per mol Al). **Zinc produces the least (0.31 g)** because of its high atomic mass (65 u).

### Quick Check (Part A)

- *"Copper reacts with steam to release hydrogen."* ❌ False! Copper lies below hydrogen on the reactivity ladder and does not react even with steam.
- *"Calcium floats in cold water because calcium is less dense than water."* ❌ False! Calcium is denser than water (1.55 g/cm³ vs. 1.0 g/cm³); it floats only because hydrogen gas bubbles cling to the metal piece and buoy it up.

## Part B: Metals and Dilute Acids: The Speed Ladder

We already wrote the balanced equations for Zn, Mg, and Fe reacting with dilute acids in Class 9 ([hydrogen-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/hydrogen/hydrogen-part2.md)), and for Al in Chapter 1, Topic 3 ([acids-bases-and-salts-part3.md](../acids-bases-and-salts/acids-bases-and-salts-part3.md)): **Active Metal + Dilute Acid → Salt + Hydrogen gas (H₂↑)**. Rather than repeating those equations here, let's learn a new scientific skill: **reading the reactivity ladder from quantitative bubble-rate data**.

> **Activity 3.3 (Observation only — teacher demonstration): The Bubble-Rate Race.**
> The teacher pours equal volumes of dilute HCl (at the same concentration and temperature) into five test tubes and drops in equal-sized pieces of cleaned magnesium ribbon, aluminium strip (surface oxide lightly sanded off), zinc granule, iron nail, and copper turnings. From a safe distance, students count the number of hydrogen bubbles rising in each tube over 30 seconds. **Students only observe and count; the teacher handles all acids and metals.**

Here is sample classroom data showing the number of bubbles counted in 30 seconds:

| Metal | Bubbles Counted (in 30 s) |
|---|---|
| Magnesium (Mg) | 60 |
| Aluminium (Al) | 42 |
| Zinc (Zn) | 18 |
| Iron (Fe) | 6 |
| Copper (Cu) | 0 |

```
   Metal       Bubbles (in 30 s), each # = 3 bubbles
   ----------------------------------------------------
   Mg    60    ####################
   Al    42    ##############
   Zn    18    ######
   Fe     6    ##
   Cu     0
```

> **Figure 3.2** – The Speed Ladder: bar chart of bubble counts in 30 seconds when five metals are placed in dilute HCl (sample data). Mg fizzes fastest, followed by Al, Zn, and Fe, while Cu produces zero bubbles.

How to read the chart: the higher the bubble count per 30 seconds, the faster the reaction and the **more reactive the metal**. This gives the exact same order as our Class 9 reactivity series: **Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > (H) > Cu**. (Note: if an uncleaned strip of aluminium is dropped into dilute acid, it fizzes very slowly at first because its protective Al₂O₃ layer blocks the acid; once the acid dissolves that thin oxide layer, rapid bubbling begins!)

Copper, silver, mercury, gold, and platinum produce **zero bubbles** of hydrogen with dilute HCl or dilute H₂SO₄ because they lie **below hydrogen** on the reactivity ladder. *(Safety note: Mercury is a shiny liquid metal whose vapour is toxic; it is studied only in text, and if a mercury thermometer ever breaks, never touch the droplets—inform a teacher or adult immediately.)*

**Solved Example 3 (Reading Data).** A teacher places four unknown metals—P, Q, R, and S—into identical test tubes of dilute HCl. The bubble counts in 30 seconds are: P = 24, Q = 0, R = 9, S = 15. Arrange the metals in decreasing order of reactivity and state which one behaves like copper.

- Decreasing order of reactivity: **P (24) > S (15) > R (9) > Q (0)**.
- **Q** gives 0 bubbles, so Q lies below hydrogen on the reactivity ladder—just like copper or silver!
- We will return to puzzles like this in Topic 4 when we combine acid data with salt-displacement data to map out unknown metals completely.

### Quick Check (Part B)

*"A higher bubble count in dilute acid means a less reactive metal."* ❌ Exact opposite: more bubbles per second means a faster reaction and a **more reactive** metal!

## Part C: Nitric Acid: The Acid That Does Not Give Hydrogen

So far, it might seem that every acid releases hydrogen gas when it meets an active metal. **Nitric acid (HNO₃)** is the famous exception! Why? Because nitric acid is a **powerful oxidising agent**. As soon as any hydrogen begins to form when a metal reacts with HNO₃, the nitric acid immediately **oxidises that hydrogen into water (H₂O)**, while the nitric acid itself gets reduced to one of the oxides of nitrogen (such as NO₂, NO, or N₂O). Therefore, **metals reacting with nitric acid generally do NOT liberate H₂ gas**!

For example, when copper reacts with concentrated nitric acid:

Cu + 4HNO₃ (concentrated) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O

*(Atom-count check: Cu 1=1, H 4=4, N 4 = 2 + 2 = 4, O 12 = 6 + 4 + 2 = 12 ✅)*

In this reaction, dense **reddish-brown, toxic fumes of nitrogen dioxide (NO₂)** billow out and the solution turns **bluish-green** due to copper(II) nitrate, Cu(NO₃)₂. *(Safety: Because concentrated HNO₃ is strongly corrosive and NO₂ gas is toxic to the lungs, this reaction is **studied only in text** or performed only by trained chemists inside a fume hood.)*

*(One small exception worth noting: if **very dilute** nitric acid—around 1%—is reacted with **magnesium (Mg)** or **manganese (Mn)**, the acid is too dilute to oxidise the hydrogen, so H₂ gas is evolved.)*

What about gold (Au) and platinum (Pt)? Even concentrated boiling nitric acid cannot touch them! How, then, did Mr. Lorenzo dissolve solid gold in his refinery cabinet? That brings us to Part D.

## Part D: Aqua Regia: Royal Water and Noble Metals

**The Recipe for Aqua Regia:** Mix **1 part by volume of concentrated nitric acid (HNO₃)** with **3 parts by volume of concentrated hydrochloric acid (HCl)**. It is always prepared fresh right before use, because the fuming orange-yellow mixture decomposes on standing.

**How It Works (The Teamwork Picture):**
- **Concentrated HNO₃ alone** is a strong oxidiser: it can oxidise a tiny fraction of gold atoms on the surface into gold ions (Au³⁺), but the reaction immediately stalls before any noticeable gold dissolves.
- **Concentrated HCl alone** cannot oxidise gold at all, though it is packed with chloride ions (Cl⁻).
- **Together in Aqua Regia:** Nitric acid oxidises gold atoms into Au³⁺ ions, and the abundant chloride ions (Cl⁻) from hydrochloric acid immediately lock onto those Au³⁺ ions to form a stable, soluble complex ion (AuCl₄⁻, chloroauric acid), pulling them into solution so nitric acid can keep oxidising fresh gold atoms! Before long, the entire piece of solid gold dissolves into a clear **golden-yellow solution**.

```
         1 part conc. HNO₃  +  3 parts conc. HCl
                          \     /
                           v   v
                 +-----------------------+
                 :     AQUA REGIA        :   <- "Royal Water"
                 :  (fresh 1 : 3 mix)    :
                 :                       :
                 :        [Au]           :   <- Piece of solid gold
                 +-----------------------+
                            :
                            v
                 +-----------------------+
                 : Golden-yellow solution:   <- Gold has dissolved!
                 +-----------------------+
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Aqua regia dissolving gold (for study only; not performed in school labs): 1 part concentrated HNO₃ and 3 parts concentrated HCl team up to dissolve solid gold into a golden-yellow solution.

Because gold and platinum resist air, moisture, and every single acid on its own, they are called **noble metals**. Master goldsmiths and precious-metal refineries use aqua regia (under strict industrial fume extraction) to **refine and purify scrap gold** and to recover pure gold from old mountings. **For students, aqua regia is strictly for reading and study**—it is intensely corrosive, fumes violently, and must never be stored in a sealed bottle (since evolving gases would build up pressure and shatter the container).

Goldsmiths also use a traditional **touchstone test** (described for study): rubbing a gold ornament on a black stone leaves a thin streak; touching a drop of pure nitric acid to the streak leaves genuine gold untouched, whereas imitation brass or copper-heavy alloys dissolve and turn green!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Mistake 1: "Every acid reacts with metals to release hydrogen gas."**
  False! **Nitric acid (HNO₃)** is a strong oxidising acid that oxidises hydrogen to water (H₂O) and releases nitrogen oxides (such as brown NO₂) instead. Just inspect the products of Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O: there is no H₂ anywhere!
- **Mistake 2: "Calcium floats on cold water because its density is less than 1 g/cm³."**
  False! Calcium's density is about 1.55 g/cm³ (heavier than water, so it sinks initially). It rises to the surface only because bubbles of H₂ gas stick to the metal piece and lift it up.
- **Mistake 3: "Aqua regia is a single super-strong acid that dissolves everything."**
  False! Aqua regia is a **1 : 3 mixture of two acids** (1 part conc. HNO₃ : 3 parts conc. HCl) where each acid plays a different role: HNO₃ oxidises the gold while Cl⁻ ions from HCl complex and dissolve the gold ions. Neither acid alone can dissolve gold—and aqua regia does not dissolve glass!
- **Mistake 4: "For equal masses of metals, the most reactive metal always produces the most hydrogen."**
  False! The mass of hydrogen produced depends on the **atomic mass and valency (mole ratio)** of the metal, not its speed of reaction. As we proved in Solved Example 2, 10 g of Al (less reactive than Mg) produces 1.11 g of H₂, whereas 10 g of Mg produces only 0.83 g of H₂.
- **Mistake 5: "Since sodium reacts with water, we can test a tiny piece in a glass at home."**
  Extremely dangerous and strictly forbidden! Even a pea-sized piece of sodium can ignite hydrogen, explode, and spatter boiling caustic soda (NaOH). It is strictly a teacher demonstration behind a safety screen.

## Quick Recap

- **Ladder with water:** K, Na (cold water, violent) > Ca (cold water, steady) > Mg (hot water or steam) > Al, Zn, Fe (steam only) > Pb, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au (no reaction even with steam).
- **General equation with water:** Metal + Water → Metal hydroxide (in cold/hot water) or Metal oxide (in steam) + **H₂↑**.
- For equal masses of Mg, Al, Zn, and Fe reacting with steam, **aluminium yields the greatest mass of H₂** because of its low atomic mass (27 u) and 2 : 3 mole ratio.
- **Speed ladder with dilute acid:** Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > (H) > Cu (Cu gives 0 bubbles).
- **Nitric acid (HNO₃)** is an oxidising acid: instead of H₂, it produces water and nitrogen oxides (e.g., Cu + 4HNO₃(conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂↑ + 2H₂O).
- **Aqua regia** = **1 part conc. HNO₃ + 3 parts conc. HCl**: dissolves noble metals like gold (Au) and platinum (Pt).
- Sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium with steam, nitric acid, aqua regia, and mercury are **for study or teacher demonstration only**.

### Answers

1. **Calcium**: reacts steadily with cold water because it lies high in the reactivity series. **Aluminium** and **zinc**: react only with steam (once aluminium's oxide film is penetrated), as they sit in the middle of the series. **Lead** and **silver**: do not react with cold water, hot water, or steam. Silver lies below hydrogen; lead lies just above hydrogen, too weakly reactive to decompose even steam.

2. Balanced equation: **2Li + 2H₂O → 2LiOH + H₂**. Left: Li = 2, H = 4, O = 2. Right: Li = 2, O = 2, H = 2 + 2 = 4 ✅. Since lithium reacts more gently than sodium with cold water, it sits **below sodium** on the ladder of vigour (though still in the "reacts with cold water" group alongside Ca).

3. Moles of K = 7.8 ÷ 39 = **0.2 mol**. Since 2 mol of K give 1 mol of H₂, H₂ = **0.1 mol = 0.2 g**. KOH formed: 0.2 mol × 56 g/mol = **11.2 g** (K 39 + O 16 + H 1 = 56).
   Check: 7.8 g K + water (0.2 × 18 = 3.6 g) = 11.4 g total reactants. Products: 11.2 g KOH + 0.2 g H₂ = 11.4 g ✅.

4. Moles of Al = 5.4 ÷ 27 = 0.2 mol. Since 2 mol of Al give 3 mol of H₂, H₂ = 0.2 × 1.5 = **0.3 mol = 0.6 g**. Al₂O₃ = 0.2 ÷ 2 = 0.1 mol × 102 g/mol = **10.2 g** (54 + 48 = 102).
   Check: 5.4 g Al + steam (0.3 × 18 = 5.4 g) = 10.8 g. Products: 10.2 g Al₂O₃ + 0.6 g H₂ = 10.8 g ✅.

5. Moles of Ca = 20 ÷ 40 = 0.5 mol. From Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ (1 : 1 : 1 ratio), H₂ = 0.5 mol = **1.0 g**, and Ca(OH)₂ = 0.5 × 74 g/mol = **37.0 g** (40 + 34 = 74). The piece of calcium rises to the surface because bubbles of hydrogen gas cling to the metal and lift it upward.

6. Calcium: 4 ÷ 40 = 0.1 mol; 1 : 1 mole ratio gives 0.1 mol H₂ = **0.20 g**. Sodium: 4 ÷ 23 = 0.1739 mol; 2 : 1 mole ratio gives 0.0870 mol H₂ = **0.174 g**. **4 g of calcium produces more hydrogen** (0.20 g > 0.174 g) because each divalent calcium atom releases a full H₂ molecule, whereas it takes two monovalent sodium atoms to release one H₂ molecule.

7. Decreasing order of reactivity: **W (55) > Y (20) > Z (7) > X (0)**. **X** could be silver or copper (0 bubbles, below hydrogen). **W** behaves like magnesium (fastest fizzing).
