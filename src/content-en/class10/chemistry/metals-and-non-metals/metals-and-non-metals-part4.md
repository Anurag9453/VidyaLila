# Topic 4: Putting the Reactivity Series to Work

In Topic 3, we tested metals against water and acids, assembling them onto a single reactivity ladder. Today, we put that ladder **directly to work**. The reactivity series is far more than a list to memorize: it allows us to **predict reactions on paper** before mixing chemicals in the lab, **deduce the ranking of unknown metals** from raw experimental data, and **calculate exact changes in mass and moles**. Finally, we explore a spectacular industrial application: how an intense metal-displacement reaction welds heavy steel railway tracks seamlessly in the field.

---

## A Shower of Sparks in the Railway Yard

On a clear Sunday morning, Mr. Sterling took his niece Zara to a safe pedestrian footbridge overlooking the city's railway maintenance yard. Down on a service track, a specialized crew wearing protective white helmets, heavy leather aprons, and dark shaded face shields stood around two heavy steel rails. The ends of the rails were aligned with a gap of several millimetres between them, surrounded tightly by a refractory ceramic mould. A technician ignited a magnesium fuse at the top of a crucible mounted directly over the mould.

Suddenly, a dazzling **fountain of brilliant sparks and incandescent flame** erupted from the crucible! White smoke billowed upwards, and within seconds, blindingly bright molten liquid iron poured smoothly from the tap hole down into the narrow gap between the rails.

Zara gasped in awe: "Uncle Sterling, look at that! Where did they get molten iron out here in an open yard without a massive blast furnace?"

Mr. Sterling gripped the safety railing, keeping Zara back: "Stay right behind the railing—that mixture reaches over 2500 °C, and only certified technicians handle it. There is no blast furnace here at all. The crucible was loaded with a simple dry powder: **aluminium powder mixed with iron(III) oxide**. Because aluminium is significantly more reactive than iron, it aggressively **rips oxygen away from iron oxide**. The reaction releases so much energy in a few seconds that the displaced iron melts instantly into a glowing liquid!"

Minutes later, the joint cooled, the ceramic mould was chipped away, and the two separate steel tracks had fused into **one continuous, seamless rail**.

"Why does aluminium have such tremendous power over iron?" Zara wondered.

Mr. Sterling smiled: "That is the exact question that launches today's lesson. Let's put the reactivity series to work."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Displacement reaction** | A reaction in which a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from an aqueous solution of its salt | $\text{Zn} + 2\text{AgNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{Zn(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{Ag}$ |
| **Reactivity series** | An arrangement of metals in descending order of their chemical reactivity | K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, [H], Cu, Hg, Ag, Au |
| **Net ionic equation** | A chemical equation showing only those ions and atoms that directly participate and change in the reaction | $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$ |
| **Spectator ion** | An ion that remains completely unchanged in solution on both sides of a chemical reaction | $\text{SO}_4^{2-}$ in $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4$ |
| **Thermite reaction** | A highly exothermic displacement reaction between aluminium powder and a metal oxide (usually $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$) | $2\text{Al} + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{Fe}$ |
| **Slag** | A light, glassy by-product (usually an oxide) that floats on top of molten metal | $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ floating on molten iron in thermite welding |
| **Metal strip** | A cleaned, thin piece of solid metal immersed in an electrolyte solution | A zinc strip in copper sulfate solution |
| **Mole ratio** | The ratio of moles of reactants and products derived from the balanced equation | $1\text{ mol Fe} : 1\text{ mol Cu}$ |

## Part A: The Reactivity Series Revisited (and the Role of Hydrogen)

In Class 9 ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)), you encountered the reactivity series, and in Topic 3 you saw how metals react with water and acids. Here is the full series, with **hydrogen explicitly marked** as the definitive benchmark separating metals that release $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute acids from those that cannot:

```
   MOST REACTIVE
          ^
     K    Potassium     Reacts violently with cold water
     Na   Sodium        Reacts vigorously with cold water
     Ca   Calcium       Reacts steadily with cold water
     Mg   Magnesium     Reacts with hot water and steam
     Al   Aluminium     Reacts with steam (slowed by oxide layer)
     Zn   Zinc          Reacts with steam
     Fe   Iron          Reacts with steam when red-hot
     Pb   Lead          Does not react with water
   -----------------------------------------------------
     [H]  Hydrogen      <== THE BENCHMARK: Displaces H₂
   -----------------------------------------------------    from dilute acids
     Cu   Copper        No reaction with dilute acids
     Hg   Mercury       No reaction with dilute acids
     Ag   Silver        No reaction with dilute acids
     Au   Gold          No reaction with dilute acids
          v
   LEAST REACTIVE
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The metal reactivity series (12 metals plus hydrogen). Any metal positioned higher can displace a lower metal from its aqueous salt solution. All metals positioned above hydrogen displace $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute hydrochloric and sulfuric acids; metals positioned below hydrogen do not.

**The Golden Rule of Displacement:** A metal positioned **higher** in the reactivity series will displace a metal positioned **lower** in the series from an aqueous solution of its salt. The reverse reaction **never occurs**.

**Two Crucial Practical Precautions:**
1. **Never use K, Na, or Ca for aqueous displacement tests.** These metals react violently with water itself (Topic 3). When dropped into an aqueous salt solution, sodium reacts instantly with the solvent water to generate $\text{NaOH}$ and explosive $\text{H}_2$ gas rather than cleanly displacing the dissolved metal. For laboratory displacement investigations, we always use moderate metals like **Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, and Cu**.
2. **Aluminium strips bear a passive oxide film ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$)** formed by air exposure (Topic 2). This layer initially impedes contact between solid aluminium and the solution; rubbing the strip with emery paper or allowing it to sit in solution removes this delay.

## Part B: Predicting Displacement Reactions: A Closer Look

### 1. The Class 9 Method in a Nutshell

To predict whether a displacement reaction occurs between a solid metal strip and an aqueous salt solution:
1. Locate both metals on the reactivity series.
2. If the **free (solid) metal is positioned higher** than the metal inside the salt, the reaction occurs.
3. If the free metal is lower, write **"No reaction"**.
4. When a reaction occurs, write the new salt formed by the free metal, set the displaced metal free, and balance the equation.

### 2. Solved Example 1: Predicting Whether a Reaction Occurs

| Metal Strip | Salt Solution | Position in Series | Reaction Occurs? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zn | $\text{AgNO}_3$ | Zn is above Ag | Yes |
| Cu | $\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2$ | Pb is above Cu | No |
| Al | $\text{CuCl}_2$ | Al is above Cu | Yes |
| Ag | $\text{Cu(NO}_3)_2$ | Cu is above Ag | No |
| Zn | $\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2$ | Zn is above Pb | Yes |
| Cu | $\text{AgNO}_3$ | Cu is above Ag | Yes |

Balanced chemical equations for the successful reactions:
- $\text{Zn} + 2\text{AgNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{Zn(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{Ag}$ (Atoms: Zn: 1, Ag: 2, N: 2, O: 6 ✅)
- $2\text{Al} + 3\text{CuCl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3 + 3\text{Cu}$ (Atoms: Al: 2, Cu: 3, Cl: 6 ✅)
- $\text{Zn} + \text{Pb(NO}_3)_2 \rightarrow \text{Zn(NO}_3)_2 + \text{Pb}$ (Atoms: Zn: 1, Pb: 1, N: 2, O: 6 ✅)
- $\text{Cu} + 2\text{AgNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{Ag}$ (Atoms: Cu: 1, Ag: 2, N: 2, O: 6 ✅)

> **Activity 4.1 (Teacher Demonstration Only): Copper Wire in Silver Nitrate.** Under adult supervision with safety goggles and gloves, a teacher suspends a coiled copper wire into a dilute, colourless solution of silver nitrate ($\text{AgNO}_3$). Within minutes, delicate, shimmering, needle-like crystals of pure metallic **silver** grow outward from the wire like a miniature frost garden! Simultaneously, the surrounding solution turns a beautiful **light blue** as hydrated $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions enter solution. Silver nitrate stains skin black on contact and lead salts are toxic; these investigations are demonstrated safely under teacher supervision or analyzed quantitatively on paper.

### 3. Distance on the Ladder: How Vigorous Is the Reaction?

The further apart two metals sit on the reactivity series, the more **vigorously and rapidly** the displacement occurs, releasing more thermal energy. Placing magnesium ribbon into copper sulfate solution generates an immediate, warm reaction with rapid fizzing. In contrast, copper in silver nitrate (which sit relatively close together) deposits silver crystals slowly and gently over several hours.

### 4. Net Ionic Equations: Removing Spectator Ions

In aqueous solution, soluble salts exist as free, hydrated ions. In the reaction $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$, the sulfate ion ($\text{SO}_4^{2-}$) remains completely unchanged in solution: it is a **spectator ion**. Cancelling the spectator ion leaves the **net ionic equation**:

$$\text{Fe} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Fe}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$$

Charge verification: $+2$ on the left, $+2$ on the right ✅.
Similarly, for copper displacing silver:
$$\text{Cu} + 2\text{Ag}^+ \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{Ag}$$
Charge verification: $2 \times (+1) = +2$ on the left, $+2$ on the right ✅.

A single net ionic equation applies universally regardless of the salt used: $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$ represents the reaction whether you use copper(II) sulfate, copper(II) chloride, or copper(II) nitrate! Every displacement is a **redox reaction**: the more reactive solid metal loses electrons (**is oxidised**), while the dissolved metal cation gains electrons (**is reduced**).

## Part C: What Reactivity Really Means: The Ease of Losing Electrons

Why does the reactivity series take this specific order? Metallic character is governed by how readily a neutral metal atom **loses its outermost valence electrons** to form a positive ion:

- $\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$ (Zinc loses 2 electrons: oxidation)
- $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$ (Copper ion gains 2 electrons: reduction)

Because a zinc atom holds its valence electrons more loosely than a copper atom, zinc surrenders electrons readily to $\text{Cu}^{2+}$ ions. Thus, a displacement reaction is fundamentally a **microscopic tug-of-war for electrons**: the more reactive metal gives up electrons, forcing the less reactive metal ion to accept them and deposit as neutral solid metal.

```
   Zn atom   -->  Zn²⁺ ion  +  2 electrons    (Zn loses electrons / oxidised)
                                   :
                                   v
   Cu²⁺ ion  +  2 electrons  -->  Cu atom      (Cu²⁺ gains electrons / reduced)

   Net reaction:   Zn + Cu²⁺  -->  Zn²⁺ + Cu
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Electron transfer in a displacement reaction: the neutral zinc atom transfers two electrons to the copper(II) ion. Zinc enters the solution as a $\text{Zn}^{2+}$ cation, while copper deposits onto the strip as neutral solid metal.

Alkali metals like sodium and potassium have a solitary valence electron far from the nucleus, giving them very low ionisation energy and violent reactivity. Gold holds its valence electrons tenaciously, placing it at the very bottom of the ladder.

## Part D: Constructing an Activity Series from Experimental Data

Now let us act as chemical detectives. You are not given the standard reactivity series; instead, you are presented with **raw experimental observations** and must deduce the definitive reactivity ranking.

### Method

1. For every observed reaction (**"Yes" / displacement occurs**), write a directed inequality: the solid strip metal is **more reactive** than the salt's metal ($\text{Metal}_{\text{strip}} > \text{Metal}_{\text{salt}}$).
2. For every non-reaction (**"No"**), the strip metal is **less reactive** than the salt's metal ($\text{Metal}_{\text{strip}} < \text{Metal}_{\text{salt}}$).
3. Connect the individual inequalities into a single continuous chain: if $A > B$ and $B > C$, then $A > C$ (transitive relationship).
4. Identify the position of hydrogen using reactions with dilute acids ($\text{bubbles of H}_2 \rightarrow \text{above H}$; $\text{no bubbles} \rightarrow \text{below H}$) and cold water (top-tier metals).

### Puzzle 1: Four Mystery Metals A, B, C, D (Grid Analysis)

A chemist immersed strips of four unknown metals into separate aqueous sulfate solutions:

```
   Strip      A-salt     B-salt     C-salt     D-salt
   --------------------------------------------------
     A         -          Yes        No         Yes
     B         No         -          No         No
     C         Yes        Yes        -          Yes
     D         No         Yes        No         -
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Experimental displacement grid: each row represents a solid metal strip; each column represents an aqueous salt solution. A dash ("-") denotes a metal tested in its own salt.

**Solution:**
- Row C shows three "Yes" results: metal C displaces A, B, and D. Therefore, **C is the most reactive metal** ($C > A, C > B, C > D$).
- Row B shows three "No" results: metal B cannot displace any other metal. Therefore, **B is the least reactive metal**.
- Comparing A and D: Strip A displaces D from D-salt ("Yes"), whereas strip D cannot displace A ("No"). Therefore, **A > D**.
- Combining all results: **C > A > D > B**.

**Verification:**
- A displaces B ✅ ($A > B$)
- A does not displace C ✅ ($A < C$)
- D displaces B ✅ ($D > B$)
- D does not displace C ✅ ($D < C$)
All experimental entries are fully consistent!

### Puzzle 2: Real Metals on Paper (Zn, Pb, Cu, Ag)

A laboratory notebook records the following observations:
- A zinc strip in $\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2$ deposits lead. A lead strip in $\text{ZnSO}_4$ shows no change.
- A lead strip in $\text{AgNO}_3$ deposits silver.
- A copper strip in $\text{AgNO}_3$ deposits silver. A copper strip in $\text{Pb(NO}_3)_2$ shows no change.

Deduce the complete reactivity order.

**Solution:**
- Zn displaces Pb and Pb cannot displace Zn $\rightarrow$ **Zn > Pb**.
- Pb displaces Ag $\rightarrow$ **Pb > Ag**.
- Cu displaces Ag $\rightarrow$ **Cu > Ag**.
- Cu cannot displace Pb $\rightarrow$ **Pb > Cu**.
- Combining the inequalities: **Zn > Pb > Cu > Ag**.

*Note:* The facts $\text{Pb} > \text{Ag}$ and $\text{Cu} > \text{Ag}$ alone do not reveal whether Pb or Cu is more reactive. The crucial negative result—that copper cannot displace lead—establishes that $\text{Pb} > \text{Cu}$.

### Puzzle 3: Pinpointing Hydrogen from Water and Acid Tests (A, B, C, D, E)

Five unknown metals gave the following experimental results:
- Metal E reacts vigorously with cold water; the other four show no reaction with cold water.
- With dilute hydrochloric acid, metals A, B, and D release bubbles of hydrogen gas, while metal C shows no reaction.
- Strip A displaces B from its chloride solution.
- Strip B displaces D from its chloride solution.
- Strip D displaces C from its chloride solution.

Deduce the full reactivity order and the exact position of hydrogen.

**Solution:**
1. Metal E alone reacts with cold water: **E is at the very top** ($E > A, B, C, D$).
2. From chloride displacements: $A > B$, $B > D$, and $D > C$ $\rightarrow$ chain **A > B > D > C**.
3. Acid tests: A, B, and D produce $\text{H}_2$ gas, so they lie **above hydrogen**. Metal C produces no gas, so it lies **below hydrogen**.
4. Full order with hydrogen: **E > A > B > D > [H] > C**. Hydrogen sits between D and C.

**Check:** D displaces C, matching the fact that D is above hydrogen and C is below hydrogen ✅.

### Puzzle 4: When Experimental Data Is Incomplete

Three metals X, Y, and Z were tested:
- Metal X displaces Y from Y-salt.
- Metal Z displaces Y from Y-salt.
- Metal Y cannot displace X or Z.

What can you conclusively deduce, and what single additional test is required to finalize the order?

**Solution:**
- The data confirms that **X > Y** and **Z > Y**. Both X and Z are more reactive than Y, making **Y the least reactive metal**.
- However, X and Z were never tested against one another! Thus, the data only establishes **{X, Z} > Y**.
- To resolve their relative rank, perform one test: **place a strip of metal X into an aqueous salt of Z**. If Z deposits, then $X > Z$; if no reaction occurs, then $Z > X$.

*Scientific Lesson:* Never assume a ranking between two elements unless direct or transitive experimental evidence exists!

### Puzzle 5: Spotting an Experimental Contradiction

A student's lab report claims:
- Metal P displaces Q ($P > Q$).
- Metal Q displaces R ($Q > R$).
- Metal R displaces P ($R > P$).

Can this sequence be chemically valid?

**Solution:**
- The first two observations yield $P > Q > R$, which requires that $P > R$.
- The third observation claims that $R > P$. An element cannot simultaneously be more reactive and less reactive than another element in the same medium!
- This forms an **impossible circular loop** ($P > Q > R > P$).
- Therefore, **at least one observation is faulty**. Probable causes: an uncleaned oxide coating on the metal strip, an incorrectly labeled reagent bottle, or contaminated test tubes. The student must thoroughly clean fresh metal strips and retest all three pairs.

## Part E: Mass and Stoichiometry Calculations

Standard atomic masses: $\text{Fe} = 56$, $\text{Cu} = 63.5$, $\text{Zn} = 65$, $\text{Ag} = 108$, $\text{Al} = 27$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{N} = 14$, $\text{S} = 32$.

### Solved Example 2: Iron in Copper Sulfate Solution (0.1 mol)

Reaction: $\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$ ($1 : 1 : 1 : 1$ mole ratio).

If $0.1\text{ mol}$ of iron reacts completely:
- Mass of iron dissolved = $0.1 \times 56 = \mathbf{5.6\text{ g}}$ Fe.
- Mass of copper deposited = $0.1 \times 63.5 = \mathbf{6.35\text{ g}}$ Cu.
- Net change in the strip's mass:
  $$\Delta m = m_{\text{deposited}} - m_{\text{dissolved}} = 6.35\text{ g} - 5.6\text{ g} = +\mathbf{0.75\text{ g}}$$

The strip **gains 0.75 g in mass** because each iron atom that dissolves is replaced by a heavier copper atom ($63.5 > 56$).

### Solved Example 3: Zinc Strip in Copper Sulfate Solution

Reaction: $\text{Zn} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{ZnSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$ ($1 : 1$ mole ratio).

If $6.5\text{ g}$ of zinc dissolves completely:
- Moles of Zn = $6.5 \div 65 = \mathbf{0.1\text{ mol}}$.
- Moles of Cu deposited = $0.1\text{ mol}$.
- Mass of Cu deposited = $0.1 \times 63.5 = \mathbf{6.35\text{ g}}$.
- Net change in the strip's mass:
  $$\Delta m = 6.35\text{ g} - 6.5\text{ g} = -\mathbf{0.15\text{ g}}$$

The strip **loses 0.15 g in mass** because each zinc atom leaving the strip is slightly heavier than the copper atom that replaces it ($65 > 63.5$).

*Key Takeaway:* Whether a metal strip gains or loses mass in a displacement reaction depends strictly on the **ratio of atomic masses**, not on their positions in the reactivity series!

### Solved Example 4: Copper Wire in Silver Nitrate Solution

Reaction: $\text{Cu} + 2\text{AgNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{Cu(NO}_3)_2 + 2\text{Ag}$

Suppose $6.35\text{ g}$ of copper wire reacts completely:
- Moles of Cu = $6.35 \div 63.5 = \mathbf{0.1\text{ mol}}$.
- Moles of Ag formed = $2 \times 0.1 = \mathbf{0.2\text{ mol}}$ (from the $1 : 2$ mole ratio).
- Mass of silver deposited = $0.2 \times 108 = \mathbf{21.6\text{ g}}$ Ag.
- Net change in mass:
  $$\Delta m = 21.6\text{ g} - 6.35\text{ g} = +\mathbf{15.25\text{ g}}$$

The strip gains a massive 15.25 g because one copper atom dissolves for every *two* heavy silver atoms deposited!

**Law of Conservation of Mass Verification:**
- Reactants: Cu ($6.35\text{ g}$) + $\text{AgNO}_3$ ($0.2\text{ mol} \times 170\text{ g/mol} = 34\text{ g}$) = $40.35\text{ g}$.
- Products: $\text{Cu(NO}_3)_2$ ($0.1\text{ mol} \times 187.5\text{ g/mol} = 18.75\text{ g}$) + Ag ($21.6\text{ g}$) = $40.35\text{ g}$ ✅.

## Part F: The Thermite Reaction and Railway Welding

Now we uncover the chemistry behind Mr. Sterling and Zara's railway observation. Aluminium sits substantially **above iron** in the reactivity series. Consequently, aluminium can displace iron from its solid oxide:

$$2\text{Al} + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{Fe}$$

Atom count: Al: 2, Fe: 2, O: 3 on both sides ✅.
Molar masses: $2\text{Al} = 54\text{ g}$, $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 = 2(56) + 3(16) = 160\text{ g}$ (Total reactants = 214 g).
Products: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 = 2(27) + 3(16) = 102\text{ g}$, $2\text{Fe} = 112\text{ g}$ (Total products = 214 g ✅).

**Why does this reaction generate such extreme heat?** Aluminium has an immense chemical affinity for oxygen; the formation of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ releases far more bond energy than is required to break the bonds in $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$. This massive surplus of energy raises the local temperature to approximately **2500 °C**, well above the melting point of iron (1538 °C). The iron is formed directly as a glowing red molten liquid!

```
        Ignition Fuse (Magnesium ribbon / sparker)
                       :
                       v
        +----------------------------+
        |  Fine Aluminium Powder     |
        |  + Iron(III) Oxide Powder  |   <-- Dry thermite mixture inside crucible
        +----------------------------+
                       :
                       v   Exothermic eruption (over 2500 °C)
        +----------------------------+
        |   Al₂O₃ Slag (liquid)      |   <-- Light, floats on top
        |   Molten Iron (liquid)     |   <-- Dense, sinks to bottom
        +----------------------------+
                       :
                       v
        Tapped into refractory mould between rail ends
     ====== Rail 1 ======[ Molten Iron ]====== Rail 2 ======
                           <-- Gap -->
```

> **Figure 4.4** – Schematic of the thermite railway welding process: aluminium powder displaces iron from iron(III) oxide with extreme heat. Dense molten iron pours into the joint cavity between rail tracks, fusing them upon cooling. The lighter aluminium oxide slag floats to the surface and is easily removed.

*Safety Warning:* The thermite reaction is strictly an industrial and specialized educational process. It produces fierce heat exceeding 2500 °C, blinding glare, and molten metal splatter. Students must never attempt this reaction at home or in an unapproved laboratory setting.

### Solved Example 5: Thermite Stoichiometry (Calculations on Paper)

Calculate the masses of aluminium and iron(III) oxide required to produce $280\text{ g}$ of molten iron for a track repair.

- Moles of Fe needed = $280 \div 56 = \mathbf{5\text{ mol Fe}}$.
- Equation: $2\text{Al} + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{Fe}$.
- Mole ratio: $2\text{ mol Fe}$ requires $2\text{ mol Al}$ and $1\text{ mol Fe}_2\text{O}_3$.
- Therefore, $5\text{ mol Fe}$ requires:
  - Moles of Al = $5\text{ mol}$ $\rightarrow$ Mass of Al = $5 \times 27 = \mathbf{135\text{ g}}$.
  - Moles of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 = 5 \div 2 = 2.5\text{ mol}$ $\rightarrow$ Mass of $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 = 2.5 \times 160 = \mathbf{400\text{ g}}$.
  - Mass of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ slag produced = $2.5 \times 102 = \mathbf{255\text{ g}}$.

**Check:** Total mass of reactants = $135 + 400 = 535\text{ g}$; Total mass of products = $255 + 280 = 535\text{ g}$ ✅.

If you attempted to substitute copper powder for aluminium, no reaction would occur because copper lies below iron in the reactivity series and cannot displace it from its oxide.

## Part G: Practical Applications: What Can Be Stored Where?

1. **Storage of Chemical Solutions:** A solution of copper(II) sulfate ($\text{CuSO}_4$) cannot be stored in an iron drum because iron displaces copper ($\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$), dissolving and perforating the container! Conversely, zinc sulfate ($\text{ZnSO}_4$) can safely be stored in a copper vessel because copper lies below zinc and cannot react.
2. **Sacrificial Corrosion Protection (Galvanising):** Iron sheets are coated with a thin layer of zinc. Even if the coating is scratched and iron is exposed to moisture, zinc—being more reactive than iron—corrodes preferentially (sacrificing its own electrons) while protecting the underlying steel. You will study this thoroughly in Chapter 3.
3. **Recovery of Precious Metals:** Scrap metallic copper immersed into spent industrial silver-plating baths displaces valuable silver ions, precipitating pure metallic silver for recycling: $\text{Cu} + 2\text{Ag}^+ \rightarrow \text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{Ag}$.
4. **Smelting and Extraction of Metals:** Highly reactive metals can displace less reactive metals from their ores. The entire principle of pyrometallurgy and chemical extraction builds directly on this hierarchy.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

- **Mistake 1: "Copper displaces zinc because copper is a widely used conducting metal."**
  False! Copper lies far below zinc in the reactivity series. Copper cannot displace zinc under any standard circumstances.
- **Mistake 2: "A metal strip always gains mass during a displacement reaction."**
  False! A strip gains mass only when the displaced metal's atomic mass exceeds that of the dissolving metal. In the reaction of zinc in copper sulfate, the strip loses mass ($65\text{ g Zn}$ dissolved vs. $63.5\text{ g Cu}$ deposited).
- **Mistake 3: "If a metal does not react with water, it cannot undergo displacement reactions."**
  False! Iron and zinc do not react with cold water, yet both vigorously displace copper from copper sulfate solution.
- **Mistake 4: "Potassium or sodium can be used to displace copper from aqueous copper sulfate."**
  Extremely dangerous and incorrect! Potassium and sodium react violently with water itself before any clean displacement of copper ions can occur.
- **Mistake 5: "Every single pair of metals in an unknown set must be tested experimentally."**
  False! Reactivity is transitive: if $A > B$ and $B > C$, then $A > C$ is logically and chemically guaranteed without needing an additional test.

## Quick Recap

- **Reactivity Series:** K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > [H] > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. A higher metal displaces a lower metal from its aqueous salt solution.
- Metals above hydrogen liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute acids; metals below hydrogen do not.
- Net ionic equations omit spectator ions (e.g., $\text{Zn} + \text{Cu}^{2+} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{Cu}$). Reactivity reflects the ease with which neutral metal atoms lose valence electrons (oxidation).
- Deducing rankings from data: "Yes" yields an inequality ($A > B$); transitive chains establish full orders; missing comparisons indicate incomplete data.
- Stoichiometry: A strip's mass change equals mass deposited minus mass dissolved ($\Delta m = m_{\text{dep}} - m_{\text{diss}}$).
- **Thermite reaction:** $2\text{Al} + \text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{Fe}$ generates temperatures over 2500 °C to weld railway rails.
- Practical rules: Never store a less reactive metal's salt in a more reactive metal's container.
