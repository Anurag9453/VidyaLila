# Topic 5: Chemical Properties and Uses of Non-Metals

In previous topics, we systematically mapped out the world of metals: how they react with water, air, and acids, and how they displace one another in solution and in high-temperature thermite welds. Now, we step outside the metallic kingdom into the realm of **non-metals**. Non-metals behave in fundamentally different ways: they do not react with water, nor do they release hydrogen gas from dilute acids. Instead, their chemistry is driven by their eagerness to **gain or share electrons** when reacting with oxygen, hydrogen, and metals. And an everyday household object that you see almost daily embodies this intricate chemistry in action: **a simple safety match**.

---

## A Morning at the Match Factory

During a school break, Mr. Sterling arranged an educational tour for his niece Zara at a safety match manufacturing facility outside the city. A prominent warning sign hung near the visitor portal: *"Authorized Guided Tours Only — Do Not Touch Any Equipment or Chemicals."*

Inside, Zara watched an automated assembly line where tens of thousands of slender aspen wooden splints moved along a track. Mechanical dip arms coated the tip of each stick with a coloured, bulbous head.

The plant chemist held up an empty matchbox for Zara to inspect: "Notice the **reddish-brown abrasive strip** on the side of the box. This is the striker. The match head itself will not ignite against an ordinary smooth wall, table, or shoe. It requires the friction strip on the box to spark into life."

"Why keep the ingredients separated?" Zara asked. "Why not put everything directly into the match head?"

The chemist smiled: "Because that separation is what makes it a **safety** match! The striker strip contains one non-metal (**red phosphorus**), while the match head contains a second non-metal (**sulfur**) alongside a powerful oxygen supplier (**potassium chlorate**). Keeping them apart prevents accidental fires. Only when they strike together does chemistry take over."

Mr. Sterling added: "Today, we will use the safety match as our gateway to understand **non-metals**: how they interact with oxygen, hydrogen, metals, and each other. Remember, safety matches and chemical oxidisers are handled strictly by adults; in the lab, we study these reactions safely on paper and through teacher demonstrations."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Non-metal** | An electronegative element that tends to gain or share electrons in chemical reactions | C, S, P, N, O, Cl |
| **Hydride** | A binary compound formed between hydrogen and another element | HCl, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{CH}_4$ |
| **Covalent compound** | A chemical compound formed when atoms share pairs of electrons | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| **Ionic compound** | A chemical compound formed by complete transfer of electrons between metals and non-metals | NaCl, FeS, KBr |
| **Oxidising agent (Oxidiser)** | A chemical substance that readily provides oxygen or removes electrons to sustain combustion | $\text{KClO}_3$ (potassium chlorate) |
| **Allotrope** | Different structural forms of the exact same element having distinct physical properties | Diamond and graphite; red and white phosphorus |
| **Neutral oxide** | An oxide that exhibits neither acidic nor basic properties and does not form salts with acids or bases | CO, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |

## Part A: Non-Metals with Water, Dilute Acids, and Oxygen

**Reactivity with Water and Dilute Acids:**
In Topic 3, we observed that reactive metals readily liberate hydrogen gas from cold water, steam, or dilute mineral acids. **Non-metals do not react with water or dilute acids.** If you drop sulfur powder, charcoal (carbon), or red phosphorus into dilute hydrochloric acid or water, absolutely nothing happens.

Why this stark contrast? Active metals release hydrogen because their atoms readily **donate valence electrons** to aqueous hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$), reducing them into elemental hydrogen gas ($2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$). Non-metals, by their very nature, are electronegative: they **gain or share electrons**; they do not supply free electrons to reduce $\text{H}^+$ ions! Hence, non-metals never appear on the standard metal displacement ladder for dilute acids.

**Reactivity with Oxygen:**
Non-metals burn in oxygen to form non-metal oxides. Notice how their behaviour mirrors and contrasts with metals:

- Carbon burns in excess air to form carbon dioxide:
  $$\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$$
  *(In limited oxygen, incomplete combustion forms toxic carbon monoxide: $2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}$)*
- Sulfur burns with a gentle blue flame to form pungent sulfur dioxide:
  $$\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$$
- Phosphorus burns vigorously in air to form dense white fumes of phosphorus pentoxide:
  $$\text{P}_4 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$$

*(Atom verification: C: 1=1, O: 2=2; S: 1=1, O: 2=2; P: 4=4, O: 10=10 ✅)*

**Acidic vs. Neutral Oxides:**
Most non-metal oxides are **acidic oxides**: they dissolve in water to produce acids, or react with alkalis to form salt and water (e.g., $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10} + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$). However, a select group forms **neutral oxides**: **carbon monoxide (CO)**, **dinitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$)**, and **water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)** show neither acidic nor basic behaviour.

*Core Rule:* Metal oxides are generally basic (or amphoteric); non-metal oxides are generally acidic (with a few neutral exceptions).

**Environmental Note on $\text{SO}_2$ and Acid Rain:** When sulfur-bearing fossil fuels burn, sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) escapes into the atmosphere. It oxidises further into $\text{SO}_3$, which dissolves in atmospheric raindrops to form sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$), resulting in acid rain that erodes limestone monuments and harms aquatic life.

**The Smell of a Struck Match:** When a safety match is struck, sulfur in the head burns in the liberated oxygen to produce small traces of $\text{SO}_2$ gas, producing the sharp, characteristic smell.

## Part B: Non-Metals with Hydrogen: Covalent Hydrides

Non-metals combine with hydrogen by sharing electron pairs, forming discrete molecular compounds called **covalent hydrides**. These compounds exist predominantly as gases or volatile liquids at room temperature:

| Non-Metal (Valency) | Hydride Formed | Balanced Chemical Equation |
|---|---|---|
| Chlorine (1) | Hydrogen chloride ($\text{HCl}$) | $\text{H}_2 + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{HCl}$ |
| Sulfur (2) | Hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) | $\text{H}_2 + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}$ |
| Oxygen (2) | Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) | $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ |
| Nitrogen (3) | Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$) | $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3$ |
| Carbon (4) | Methane ($\text{CH}_4$) | $\text{C} + 2\text{H}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_4$ |

*(Atom verification: Cl: 2=2, H: 2=2; S: 1=1, H: 2=2; O: 2=2, H: 4=4; N: 2=2, H: 6=6; C: 1=1, H: 4=4 ✅)*

Observe the universal structural pattern: the number of hydrogen atoms bonded to each non-metal atom directly matches the **non-metal's valency** (1 for Cl, 2 for S and O, 3 for N, 4 for C).

*Industrial and Safety Insights:*
- The reversible arrow ($\rightleftharpoons$) in ammonia synthesis represents the Haber process, the industrial foundation of modern synthetic fertilisers.
- **Hydrogen sulfide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$)** is a hazardous, toxic gas with an unmistakable odour of rotten eggs.
- **Hydrogen chloride gas ($\text{HCl}$)** dissolves eagerly in water to form hydrochloric acid.

## Part C: Reactions Between Non-Metals and Metals: Ionic Compounds

When a **metal** (an electropositive electron donor) encounters a **non-metal** (an electronegative electron acceptor), complete electron transfer occurs, producing an **ionic compound**:

- Sodium burns vigorously in chlorine gas to produce sodium chloride:
  $$2\text{Na} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl}$$
- Heating a mixture of iron powder and sulfur powder produces solid black iron(II) sulfide:
  $$\text{Fe} + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{FeS}$$
- Additional examples: $2\text{K} + \text{Br}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{KBr}$; $\text{Ca} + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{CaS}$.

*(Atom verification: Na: 2=2, Cl: 2=2; Fe: 1=1, S: 1=1 ✅)*

Recall the classic Class 9 distinction: A simple dry mixture of iron filings and sulfur powder can easily be separated using a handheld magnet (iron clings to the magnet). However, once heated to trigger the chemical reaction, the resulting **$\text{FeS}$ compound cannot be separated by a magnet** because the iron atoms have surrendered electrons to sulfur, forming a brand-new ionic crystalline lattice!

**Reactions Between Two Non-Metals:**
When two non-metals react with one another, neither is willing to surrender electrons completely. Therefore, they **share electron pairs**, forming **covalent compounds**: $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{HCl}$, and $\text{NH}_3$. For instance, during high-voltage atmospheric lightning strikes, nitrogen and oxygen gases in the air combine to form nitric oxide:
$$\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}$$

```
                           NON-METALS
                               :
      +------------------------+------------------------+
      :                        :                        :
  WATER / DILUTE            OXYGEN                   HYDROGEN
  ACIDS                        :                        :
      :                     Oxides:                  Covalent Hydrides:
  NO REACTION               Generally acidic         HCl, H₂S, NH₃,
  (No H₂ evolved)           (CO₂, SO₂, P₄O₁₀)        CH₄, H₂O
                            Some neutral
                            (CO, N₂O, H₂O)              :
                                                        v
                                                     METALS
                                                        :
                                                     Ionic Compounds:
                                                     NaCl, FeS, KBr, CaS
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Summary map of non-metal reactivity: non-metals show zero reaction with water and dilute acids; form acidic or neutral oxides with oxygen; form covalent hydrides with hydrogen; and react with active metals to yield ionic salts.

## Part D: The Halogen Displacement Ladder

In Class 9 ([periodic-table-part5.md](../../../class9/chemistry/periodic-table/periodic-table-part5.md)), you learned that halogen reactivity decreases down Group 17:

$$\text{F}_2 > \text{Cl}_2 > \text{Br}_2 > \text{I}_2$$

For a non-metal, reactivity measures the **ability to attract and gain an extra electron**. Consequently, **a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halogen from an aqueous solution of its halide salt**. This operates on the exact same logic as metal displacement (Topic 4), except here the competing species are electronegative non-metals:

- Chlorine displaces bromine from potassium bromide solution (solution turns reddish-orange):
  $$\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{KBr} \rightarrow 2\text{KCl} + \text{Br}_2$$
- Chlorine displaces iodine from potassium iodide solution (solution turns dark brown):
  $$\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{KI} \rightarrow 2\text{KCl} + \text{I}_2$$
- Bromine displaces iodine from potassium iodide solution:
  $$\text{Br}_2 + 2\text{KI} \rightarrow 2\text{KBr} + \text{I}_2$$
- Bromine added to potassium chloride solution yields **no reaction** (bromine is less reactive than chlorine):
  $$\text{Br}_2 + 2\text{KCl} \rightarrow \text{No Reaction}$$

*(Atom verification: Cl: 2=2, K: 2=2, Br: 2=2; Cl: 2=2, K: 2=2, I: 2=2; Br: 2=2, K: 2=2, I: 2=2 ✅)*

```
   Reactivity (Electron-attracting power)

   F₂   (Top of Group 17)    ^   A higher halogen displaces
   Cl₂                       :   any lower halogen from its
   Br₂                       :   aqueous halide salt solution
   I₂   (Bottom of Group 17) :

   Cl₂ + 2KBr  -->  2KCl + Br₂        (Successful displacement: Cl is above Br)
   Cl₂ + 2KI   -->  2KCl + I₂         (Successful displacement: Cl is above I)
   Br₂ + 2KI   -->  2KBr + I₂         (Successful displacement: Br is above I)
   I₂  + 2KBr  -->  No Reaction       (Failed: Iodine cannot displace Bromine)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – The halogen displacement ladder: any halogen higher up in Group 17 displaces a lower halogen from its halide salt. The reverse reaction never occurs.

*Safety Precautions:* Elemental chlorine and bromine are toxic and corrosive; bromine vapour severely irritates the eyes and respiratory tract. These reactions are strictly for conceptual study and teacher demonstration under a fume hood. At home, never mix household bleach with acidic or ammoniacal cleaning agents, as hazardous chlorine chloramine gases are released.

## Part E: The Chemistry of a Safety Match

Now we return to Zara's matchbox question. The ingenious design of a modern safety match relies on partitioning reactive chemicals between two separate surfaces:

```
   MATCH HEAD (On the Wooden Splint)      STRIKER STRIP (On the Box Side)

   +-------------------------------+      +-------------------------------+
   | Potassium chlorate (KClO₃)    |      | RED PHOSPHORUS                |
   | (Powerful oxidising agent)    |      | + Powdered glass / abrasive   |
   | + SULFUR                      |      | + Adhesive binder             |
   | + Powdered glass / filler     |      |                               |
   | + Gelatin / glue binder       |      | (Present ONLY on the box)     |
   +-------------------------------+      +-------------------------------+

   Strike the match head firmly against the abrasive strip:
   Frictional heat --> Red phosphorus converts to traces of white phosphorus
   --> Ignites instantly --> KClO₃ supplies concentrated O₂
   --> Sulfur catches fire (releasing SO₂) --> Wooden splint ignites steadily!
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Composition and ignition mechanism of a safety match: the oxidiser ($\text{KClO}_3$) and sulfur reside in the head, while red phosphorus resides exclusively on the striker strip. Separating the fuel from the ignition trigger prevents accidental ignition.

**Step-by-Step Ignition Mechanism:**
1. Striking the head across the abrasive strip creates momentary **frictional heat**.
2. This thermal energy converts a microscopic trace of stable red phosphorus into highly reactive **white phosphorus**, which sparks into flame instantly in ambient air.
3. The spark heats the **potassium chlorate ($\text{KClO}_3$)** in the match head, triggering its rapid decomposition to provide a concentrated burst of pure oxygen gas:
   $$2\text{KClO}_3 \rightarrow 2\text{KCl} + 3\text{O}_2$$
4. This concentrated oxygen instantly ignites the **sulfur** in the match head ($\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$), producing a hot, steady flame that lights the wooden splint.
5. The wooden splint is pre-treated with ammonium phosphate to suppress dangerous "afterglow" smouldering once blown out.

*(Atom verification: K: 2=2, Cl: 2=2, O: 6=6 ✅)*

**Why Is It Called a "Safety" Match?**
Early 19th-century "lucifer" matches contained dangerous white phosphorus directly in the match head. These primitive matches could ignite accidentally in pockets from minor rubbing, and factory workers exposed to white phosphorus vapours suffered from severe, disfiguring bone necrosis known as "phossy jaw". In modern safety matches, toxic white phosphorus is entirely eliminated, and stable **red phosphorus** is segregated strictly onto the external striker strip.

*Safety Reminder:* Striking matches is an adult activity. Always ensure striking is performed away from face and clothing, in well-ventilated areas, and far from flammable liquids, curtains, or aerosol sprays.

## Part F: Allotropes: One Element, Multiple Forms

When a single chemical element exists in two or more distinct physical forms having different molecular arrangements and physical properties, those forms are called **allotropes**:

- **Carbon Allotropes:**
  - **Diamond:** A rigid 3D tetrahedral network of carbon atoms; the hardest naturally occurring substance known; an electrical insulator.
  - **Graphite:** Layered hexagonal sheets held by weak van der Waals forces; soft, slippery, and an excellent electrical conductor (used in pencil leads and electrolytic cell electrodes).
  *(Both diamond and graphite are composed of 100% pure carbon!)*
- **Phosphorus Allotropes:**
  - **White phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$):** Highly reactive, waxy solid that spontaneously ignites in air at ~35 °C; intensely poisonous; stored safely **submerged under water**.
  - **Red phosphorus:** Polymeric, stable, non-volatile solid; does not self-ignite in air; significantly less toxic; used safely on matchbox striker strips.

*Scientific Takeaway:* The identity of the element alone does not dictate material properties—the geometric architecture of atomic bonding plays an equally decisive role.

| **Oxygen** | Hospital medical respiration, oxy-acetylene welding torches | Vigorous supporter of combustion; store tanks far from grease/flames |

## Part H: Solved Examples

**Example 1: Complete combustion of 8 g of sulfur.**
How many grams of sulfur dioxide are formed when $8\text{ g}$ of sulfur burns completely in excess oxygen?
- Equation: $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$
- Molar masses: $\text{S} = 32\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{SO}_2 = 32 + 2(16) = 64\text{ g/mol}$
- Moles of S = $8 \div 32 = 0.25\text{ mol}$
- Moles of $\text{SO}_2$ formed = $0.25\text{ mol}$ (1:1 mole ratio)
- Mass of $\text{SO}_2 = 0.25 \times 64 = \mathbf{16\text{ g}}$
*(Conservation of mass: 8 g S + 8 g O₂ = 16 g SO₂ ✅)*

**Example 2: Classifying element oxides.**
Classify the following oxides as acidic or neutral: $\text{CO}$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{SO}_2$.
- **Acidic oxides:** $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$, and $\text{SO}_2$ (all dissolve in water to produce acids or neutralise alkalis).
- **Neutral oxides:** $\text{CO}$ and $\text{N}_2\text{O}$ (show no reaction with either acids or bases).

**Example 3: Chlorine displacement of bromide ions.**
An excess of chlorine gas is bubbled into a solution containing $23.8\text{ g}$ of potassium bromide ($\text{KBr}$). Calculate the mass of bromine ($\text{Br}_2$) liberated and potassium chloride ($\text{KCl}$) formed.
- Equation: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{KBr} \rightarrow 2\text{KCl} + \text{Br}_2$
- Molar mass of KBr = $39 + 80 = 119\text{ g/mol}$
- Moles of KBr = $23.8 \div 119 = 0.2\text{ mol}$
- Stoichiometric ratios:
  - Moles of $\text{Br}_2 = 0.2 \div 2 = 0.1\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.1 \times 160 = \mathbf{16.0\text{ g}}$
  - Moles of $\text{KCl} = 0.2\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.2 \times 74.5 = \mathbf{14.9\text{ g}}$
- Mass check: Reactants = $23.8\text{ g KBr} + (0.1 \times 71 = 7.1\text{ g Cl}_2) = 30.9\text{ g}$; Products = $14.9 + 16.0 = 30.9\text{ g}$ ✅.

**Example 4: Ranking three mystery halogens.**
Three halogens $X_2$, $Y_2$, and $Z_2$ were tested against potassium salts:

| Mixture Tested | Observation |
|---|---|
| $X_2 + \text{KY}$ | $Y_2$ is liberated |
| $Z_2 + \text{KX}$ | No reaction |
| $Z_2 + \text{KY}$ | No reaction |

- $X_2$ displaces $Y_2 \rightarrow X_2 > Y_2$.
- $Z_2$ fails to displace both $X$ and $Y \rightarrow Z_2$ is less reactive than both $X_2$ and $Y_2$.
- Descending reactivity order: **$X_2 > Y_2 > Z_2$**.

**Example 5: Reaction between iron and sulfur.**
How much sulfur is required to react completely with $14\text{ g}$ of iron powder to form iron(II) sulfide, and what mass of FeS is formed?
- Equation: $\text{Fe} + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{FeS}$
- Moles of Fe = $14 \div 56 = 0.25\text{ mol}$
- Mole ratio is 1:1, so moles of S needed = $0.25\text{ mol}$
- Mass of sulfur = $0.25 \times 32 = \mathbf{8\text{ g}}$
- Mass of FeS formed = $0.25 \times (56 + 32) = 0.25 \times 88 = \mathbf{22\text{ g}}$
*(Conservation check: 14 g Fe + 8 g S = 22 g FeS ✅)*

**Example 6: Combustion of phosphorus.**
Calculate the mass of oxygen required and phosphorus pentoxide ($\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$) formed when $12.4\text{ g}$ of phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$) burns completely. ($P = 31$)
- Equation: $\text{P}_4 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$
- Molar mass of $\text{P}_4 = 4 \times 31 = 124\text{ g/mol}$
- Moles of $\text{P}_4 = 12.4 \div 124 = 0.1\text{ mol}$
- Moles of $\text{O}_2$ required = $5 \times 0.1 = 0.5\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.5 \times 32 = \mathbf{16\text{ g}}$
- Moles of $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ formed = $0.1\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.1 \times 284 = \mathbf{28.4\text{ g}}$
*(Conservation check: 12.4 g P₄ + 16 g O₂ = 28.4 g P₄O₁₀ ✅)*

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Non-metals react with dilute acids to produce hydrogen gas."**
   False! Non-metals cannot provide free electrons to reduce $\text{H}^+$ ions. In a laboratory test tube, adding sulfur powder or charcoal to dilute HCl produces zero bubbles of gas.
2. **Mistake 2: "All non-metal oxides are acidic."**
   False! While most are acidic, key oxides like **$\text{CO}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ are neutral**.
3. **Mistake 3: "A lower halogen can displace a higher halogen from its salt."**
   False! Only a more electronegative (higher) halogen can displace a lower halogen from solution. For example, $\text{Br}_2 + \text{KCl} \rightarrow \text{No reaction}$.
4. **Mistake 4: "Phosphorus is located inside the match head."**
   False! In modern safety matches, red phosphorus is located exclusively on the external striker strip of the box. The match head contains sulfur and potassium chlorate.
5. **Mistake 5: "Diamond and graphite are different chemical elements."**
   False! Both diamond and graphite are allotropes consisting of 100% pure carbon atoms bonded in different structural lattices.
6. **Mistake 6: "White and red phosphorus have identical chemical hazards."**
   False! White phosphorus is hyper-reactive, ignites spontaneously in air, and is highly toxic. Red phosphorus is stable in air at room temperature and far safer.

## Quick Recap

- Non-metals do not liberate hydrogen gas from water or dilute mineral acids.
- Non-metal oxides are generally acidic ($\text{CO}_2$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$), dissolving to form acids; a few are neutral ($\text{CO}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
- Non-metals react with hydrogen to form covalent molecular hydrides ($\text{HCl}$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
- Non-metals react with electropositive metals to form ionic crystal salts ($2\text{Na} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl}$; $\text{Fe} + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{FeS}$).
- Halogen displacement ladder: $\text{F}_2 > \text{Cl}_2 > \text{Br}_2 > \text{I}_2$. A higher halogen displaces a lower halogen from its halide salt solution.
- Modern safety match: Striker contains red phosphorus and abrasive; head contains sulfur fuel and $\text{KClO}_3$ oxidiser.
- Allotropes are distinct structural forms of the same element (diamond/graphite for carbon; white/red for phosphorus).

11. A sample of $6.2\text{ g}$ of white phosphorus ($\text{P}_4$) burns completely in air to form phosphorus pentoxide ($\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$). Calculate the mass of oxygen consumed and the mass of $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ produced. ($P = 31$)
12. Why does a safety match fail to ignite when struck against a smooth polished wooden table, but ignites immediately when struck against the side of the matchbox? Name the active chemical components involved in each surface.
13. Why must white phosphorus be stored permanently submerged under water, and why was it replaced by red phosphorus in the manufacture of safety matches?
14. From the following list of oxides, identify which are neutral and which are acidic: $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{SO}_3$, $\text{CO}$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{CO}_2$.
15. State whether each statement is True or False, and correct the false statements:
    (a) Fluorine can displace chlorine from an aqueous solution of potassium chloride.
    (b) Diamond and graphite represent two completely different chemical elements.
    (c) Household bleach must never be mixed with other acidic or ammoniacal cleaning agents.

### Answers

1. Equation: $\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2$.
   - Moles of C = $9 \div 12 = 0.75\text{ mol}$.
   - Mole ratio is 1:1:1.
   - Mass of $\text{CO}_2 = 0.75 \times 44 = \mathbf{33\text{ g}}$.
   - Mass of $\text{O}_2$ consumed = $0.75 \times 32 = \mathbf{24\text{ g}}$.
   *(Check: 9 g C + 24 g O₂ = 33 g CO₂ ✅)*
2. Active metals like zinc readily donate valence electrons to reduce hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) in dilute hydrochloric acid to hydrogen gas ($\text{Zn} + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + \text{H}_2$). Non-metals like sulfur and carbon are electronegative elements that react by gaining or sharing electrons; they cannot donate electrons to reduce $\text{H}^+$ ions.
3. Equation: $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{KI} \rightarrow 2\text{KCl} + \text{I}_2$ (balanced: Cl: 2=2, K: 2=2, I: 2=2 ✅).
   - Molar mass of KI = $39 + 127 = 166\text{ g/mol}$.
   - Moles of KI = $33.2 \div 166 = 0.2\text{ mol}$.
   - From stoichiometry ($2\text{ KI} : 1\text{ I}_2$), moles of $\text{I}_2 = 0.1\text{ mol}$.
   - Mass of $\text{I}_2 = 0.1 \times 254 = \mathbf{25.4\text{ g}}$.
4. (a) $\text{Br}_2 + \text{NaCl}$: **No reaction** (Bromine is less reactive than chlorine).
   (b) $\text{I}_2 + \text{KBr}$: **No reaction** (Iodine is less reactive than bromine).
   (c) $\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{NaI} \rightarrow 2\text{NaCl} + \text{I}_2$ (Chlorine displaces iodine ✅).
   (d) $\text{Br}_2 + 2\text{NaI} \rightarrow 2\text{NaBr} + \text{I}_2$ (Bromine displaces iodine ✅).
5. $R_2$ displaces both $P$ and $Q$, making $R_2$ the most reactive. $Q_2$ displaces $P$, placing $Q_2$ in the middle. $P_2$ displaces neither, making it the least reactive.
   Order: **$R_2 > Q_2 > P_2$**.
6. (a) **$\text{PH}_3$** (Phosphine), (b) **$\text{SiH}_4$** (Silane), (c) **$\text{HBr}$** (Hydrogen bromide).
   All three contain **covalent bonds** formed by electron sharing between hydrogen and a non-metal. The number of bonded hydrogen atoms equals the non-metal's valency (3, 4, 1).
7. Moles of S = $6.4 \div 32 = 0.2\text{ mol}$.
   Equation: $\text{H}_2 + \text{S} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{S}$ (1:1 mole ratio).
   - Moles of $\text{H}_2\text{S} = 0.2\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.2 \times 34 = \mathbf{6.8\text{ g}}$.
   - Mass of $\text{H}_2$ used = $0.2 \times 2 = \mathbf{0.4\text{ g}}$.
   *(Check: 6.4 g S + 0.4 g H₂ = 6.8 g H₂S ✅)*
8. - **Ionic compounds:** $\text{KBr}$ (potassium metal + bromine non-metal) and $\text{CaS}$ (calcium metal + sulfur non-metal).
   - **Covalent compounds:** $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$, and $\text{NH}_3$ (composed exclusively of non-metals sharing electron pairs).
   - *Governing Rule:* A bond between a metal and a non-metal is ionic (complete electron transfer); a bond between two non-metals is covalent (electron sharing).
9. Moles of $\text{N}_2 = 14 \div 28 = 0.5\text{ mol}$.
   Equation: $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{NH}_3$ (1 : 3 : 2 mole ratio).
   - Moles of $\text{H}_2$ required = $3 \times 0.5 = 1.5\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 1.5 \times 2 = \mathbf{3\text{ g}}$.
   - Moles of $\text{NH}_3$ formed = $2 \times 0.5 = 1.0\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 1.0 \times 17 = \mathbf{17\text{ g}}$.
   *(Check: 14 g N₂ + 3 g H₂ = 17 g NH₃ ✅)*
10. Mass of sulfur = $2\% \text{ of } 1000\text{ kg} = 20\text{ kg S}$.
    Equation: $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$ (Mass ratio $32\text{ g S} : 64\text{ g SO}_2$, or $1 : 2$).
    - Mass of $\text{SO}_2 = 20 \times 2 = \mathbf{40\text{ kg}}$.
    - *Environmental impact:* $\text{SO}_2$ dissolves in atmospheric water vapour to form sulfurous and sulfuric acids, causing acid rain that damages forests, acidifies aquatic ecosystems, and corrodes architectural stone and metals.
11. Moles of $\text{P}_4 = 6.2 \div 124 = 0.05\text{ mol}$.
    Equation: $\text{P}_4 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$.
    - Moles of $\text{O}_2$ consumed = $5 \times 0.05 = 0.25\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.25 \times 32 = \mathbf{8.0\text{ g}}$.
    - Moles of $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ formed = $0.05\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.05 \times 284 = \mathbf{14.2\text{ g}}$.
    *(Check: 6.2 g P₄ + 8.0 g O₂ = 14.2 g P₄O₁₀ ✅)*
12. The match head contains sulfur and potassium chlorate, but lacks the ignition trigger (**red phosphorus**), which resides exclusively on the abrasive striker strip of the matchbox. Rubbing on a table produces only mild friction without the chemical trigger. Striking the box heats red phosphorus into reactive white phosphorus, igniting the head.
13. White phosphorus ignites spontaneously in air at ~35 °C and is highly poisonous; storing it under water isolates it from atmospheric oxygen. Red phosphorus is stable in air at ambient temperatures and non-volatile, eliminating accidental ignition and the severe industrial occupational disease ("phossy jaw").
14. - **Neutral oxides:** $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
    - **Acidic oxides:** $\text{SO}_3$ and $\text{CO}_2$ (react with bases to produce salts and water).
15. (a) **True** ✅: Fluorine lies above chlorine in Group 17 and is more electronegative.
    (b) **False** ❌: Diamond and graphite are allotropes of the exact same chemical element: pure carbon.
    (c) **True** ✅: Mixing household bleach with acid or ammonia releases toxic chlorine and chloramine gases.
