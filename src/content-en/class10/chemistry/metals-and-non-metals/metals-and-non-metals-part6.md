# Topic 6: Metal or Non-Metal? Identification, Ionic Compounds, and Chapter Summary

Across the previous five topics, we investigated metals and non-metals from every angle: their metallic lustre and mechanical hardness, their interactions with air, water, and acids, the predictions of the reactivity series, and the unique chemical fingerprints of non-metals. In this culminating topic, we synthesize all these threads together. When an unidentified sample arrives in the laboratory, how do you decisively prove whether it is a metal, a non-metal, or a metalloid? How do you predict whether its oxide neutralises an acid, and whether its compounds are ionic or covalent? This is the work of a chemical detective—and in this file, you step into that detective role.

## The Scrap Merchant's Scale, Magnet, and Ringing Test

On a bright Sunday morning, Mr. Sterling accompanied his niece Zara to a local metal recycling depot to drop off worn household scrap. In the sorting bay, three distinct collection bins were marked: **"Ferrous Metals"**, **"Non-Ferrous Metals"**, and **"Plastics & Non-Metals"**. The yard manager, Mr. Marcus, inspected each arriving item using three rapid diagnostic checks: first, he swept a powerful neodymium magnet across its surface; second, he tapped it lightly with a small brass mallet to evaluate its acoustic ring; and third, he gave the corner a light scrape with a steel file to inspect the freshly exposed surface.

Zara watched curiously: "Mr. Marcus, why not just sort them by sight? Wouldn't that be much faster?"

Mr. Marcus chuckled: "Appearances can be deceiving! An enamelled steel bucket and an anodised aluminium bucket look almost identical from a few metres away. But a magnet grips steel immediately, while it leaves aluminium untouched. And if you tap them, a brass bell rings out with a clear, resonant chime ('tang!'), a lead sinker gives a dull, heavy thud ('thup!'), and a plastic bottle produces only a hollow, plastic clatter ('tak!'). That simple combination of tests is practical chemistry in action!"

Mr. Sterling smiled at Zara: "Mr. Marcus relies on quick physical cues, but in chemistry we combine physical tests with chemical reactions to build an airtight case. Let's unite all the tests from this chapter to construct a complete identification dossier for any unknown sample."

Zara flipped open her notebook and wrote at the top: **"The Metal Detective's Dossier"**.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Diagnostic test** | A specific physical or chemical test used to identify an unknown material or classify its elemental family | Magnet test, electrical conductivity test, sonorous tap test |
| **Ferrous metal** | A metal or alloy containing iron as its primary constituent, strongly attracted by a magnet | Iron, carbon steel |
| **Non-ferrous metal** | A metal or alloy that does not contain significant iron and is not attracted by an ordinary magnet | Aluminium, copper, brass, zinc |
| **Basic oxide** | A metal oxide that reacts with acids to produce a salt and water | $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$, $\text{MgO}$, $\text{CuO}$ |
| **Acidic oxide** | A non-metal oxide that reacts with bases to produce a salt and water | $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$ |
| **Amphoteric oxide** | An oxide that reacts with both acids and strong bases to yield salt and water | $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$, $\text{ZnO}$ |
| **Ionic compound** | A chemical substance formed by electrostatic attraction between positive metal cations and negative non-metal anions | $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{CaO}$ |
| **Metalloid** | An element with physical and chemical properties intermediate between metals and non-metals | Silicon, germanium |

## Part A: Comprehensive Comparison of Metals and Non-Metals

The table below unifies all physical and chemical characteristics from across the entire chapter:

| Property | Metals | Non-Metals | Key Notable Exceptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Physical State** | Almost all are solids at room temperature | Solids or gases; bromine is a liquid | **Mercury ($\text{Hg}$)** is a liquid metal; **gallium ($\text{Ga}$)** and **caesium ($\text{Cs}$)** melt near body temperature (~29.8 °C and 28.5 °C) |
| **Lustre** | Characteristic metallic lustre (shiny when freshly cut) | Generally dull in appearance | **Graphite** and **iodine** exhibit bright metallic-like lustre |
| **Hardness** | Generally hard with high tensile strength | Solid non-metals are soft or brittle | **Sodium ($\text{Na}$)** and **potassium ($\text{K}$)** can be cut with a butter knife; **diamond** is the hardest natural substance known |
| **Malleability & Ductility** | Highly malleable (hammered into sheets) and ductile (drawn into wires) | Brittle; shatter into fragments when struck | **Silicon** is lustrous but brittle (metalloid); no non-metal can be hammered into foil |
| **Electrical Conductivity** | Excellent electrical conductors (delocalised electrons) | Poor electrical conductors (insulators) | **Graphite** is an excellent electrical conductor; **lead ($\text{Pb}$)** is an unusually poor conductor for a metal |
| **Thermal Conductivity** | Excellent heat conductors (silver and copper are best) | Poor heat conductors | **Diamond** is an exceptional thermal conductor |
| **Sonorous Nature** | Ring with a clear, resonant tone when struck | Make a dull, muffled sound when struck | Soft metals like **sodium** and **lead** do not ring sonorously |
| **Density** | Generally high density | Generally low density | **Lithium ($\text{Li}$)** is lighter than water ($0.53\text{ g/cm}^3$); **osmium ($\text{Os}$)** is the densest ($22.6\text{ g/cm}^3$) |
| **Melting & Boiling Points** | Generally very high | Generally low | **Tungsten ($\text{W}$)** melts at ~3422 °C; **carbon** (graphite/diamond) sublimes above 3600 °C |
| **Oxide Character** | Form basic or amphoteric oxides | Form acidic or neutral oxides | $\text{CO}$, $\text{N}_2\text{O}$, and $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ are strictly neutral oxides |
| **Reaction with Acids** | Metals above [H] liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute mineral acids | Do not liberate $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute acids | **Copper ($\text{Cu}$)** and **silver ($\text{Ag}$)** sit below [H] and do not react with dilute acids |
| **Chemical Bonding** | Form cations by losing valence electrons; form ionic bonds with non-metals | Form anions by gaining electrons, or share electrons in covalent bonds | **Hydrogen** behaves both as an electron donor ($\text{H}^+$) and sharer ($\text{H}_2$) |

> *Crucial Diagnostic Rule:* Every single test provides a valuable clue, but **no single test is infallible on its own**. A reliable chemical detective always evaluates multiple independent tests before reaching a conclusion.

## Part B: Ionic Compounds: A Rapid Review

In Class 9 ([chemical-bonding-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part2.md)), we established that metals lose their outermost valence electrons to form **positive ions (cations)**, while non-metals capture those electrons to form **negative ions (anions)**. The powerful, non-directional electrostatic attraction holding these oppositely charged ions in a rigid crystal lattice is an **ionic bond**.

Let us review four foundational ionic examples:
- **Sodium Chloride ($\text{NaCl}$):** $\text{Na} (2, 8, 1)$ loses $1\text{ e}^-$ to become $\text{Na}^+ (2, 8)$; $\text{Cl} (2, 8, 7)$ gains $1\text{ e}^-$ to become $\text{Cl}^- (2, 8, 8)$. Formula unit mass = $23 + 35.5 = \mathbf{58.5\text{ u}}$.
- **Magnesium Chloride ($\text{MgCl}_2$):** $\text{Mg} (2, 8, 2)$ loses $2\text{ e}^-$ to become $\text{Mg}^{2+} (2, 8)$; two separate chlorine atoms each take $1\text{ e}^-$ to form two $\text{Cl}^-$ ions. Formula unit mass = $24 + 2(35.5) = \mathbf{95\text{ u}}$.
- **Calcium Oxide ($\text{CaO}$):** $\text{Ca} (2, 8, 8, 2)$ transfers $2\text{ e}^-$ to $\text{O} (2, 6)$, forming $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions. Formula unit mass = $40 + 16 = \mathbf{56\text{ u}}$.
- **Aluminium Oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$):** Two aluminium atoms each lose $3\text{ e}^-$ (total 6 electrons transferred) to three oxygen atoms, which each gain $2\text{ e}^-$, forming $2\text{Al}^{3+}$ and $3\text{O}^{2-}$ ions. Formula unit mass = $2(27) + 3(16) = \mathbf{102\text{ u}}$.

*General Bonding Principle:*
- **Metal + Non-Metal $\rightarrow$ Ionic Compound** (complete electron transfer).
- **Non-Metal + Non-Metal $\rightarrow$ Covalent Compound** (shared electron pairs, e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CH}_4$, $\text{HCl}$).

Physical properties of ionic compounds ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)):

| Physical Property | Ionic Compounds | Covalent Compounds (Typical) |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical State** | Hard, crystalline solids at room temperature | Gases, volatile liquids, or soft molecular solids |
| **Melting & Boiling Points** | Very high (NaCl melts at ~801 °C; MgO at ~2852 °C) | Generally low to moderate (e.g., methane melts at −182 °C) |
| **Mechanical Behaviour** | Hard but brittle; cleave or shatter along crystal planes when struck | Soft, flexible, or easily deformed |
| **Electrical Conductivity** | **Insulators in solid state**; excellent conductors when **molten or dissolved in water** | Non-conductors in all states (except polar covalent acids like HCl in water) |
| **Solubility in Water** | Generally soluble in polar water; insoluble in non-polar solvents | Generally insoluble in water; soluble in organic solvents |

> *Why do ionic solids conduct electricity only when liquid or dissolved?* In the solid crystal, ions are locked rigidly in position by intense electrostatic forces. When melted or dissolved in water, the lattice breaks down, freeing the ions to migrate towards oppositely charged electrodes and carry an electric current.

## Part D: Identification Puzzles: Deductions with Full Reasoning

### Puzzle 1. The Five Samples from Figure 6.2

**Question:** Classify samples P, Q, R, S, and T from Figure 6.2 as a metal, non-metal, metalloid, or specific allotrope exception.

**Solution:**
- **Sample P:** Lustrous, conductive, sonorous, and malleable. These are four definitive metallic traits. Because it is not magnetic, it is a **non-ferrous metal** (e.g., aluminium or copper).
- **Sample Q:** Non-lustrous, non-conductive, non-sonorous, and brittle. It is unequivocally a **non-metal** (e.g., solid sulfur or phosphorus).
- **Sample R:** Exhibits all the metallic traits of P, but is strongly attracted by a magnet. It is a **ferrous metal** (iron or carbon steel).
- **Sample S:** Displays metallic lustre, but is a poor semiconductor and shatters when hammered. It is a **metalloid** (e.g., silicon or germanium).
- **Sample T:** Non-lustrous, soft and flaky, yet conducts electricity well. It is **graphite** (an allotrope of carbon, the only common non-metal conductor).

**Check:** P and R differ strictly by magnetic attraction, correctly placing them into distinct metallurgical categories ✅.

### Puzzle 2. Lustrous Yet Brittle

**Question:** A shiny, lustrous grey solid sample shatters into powdery fragments when struck with a mallet and exhibits very weak electrical conductivity. Can this substance be classified as a metal? What is the correct scientific term?

**Solution:**
- True metals are malleable (flattening into foil) and possess high electrical conductivity.
- This sample combines metallic lustre with non-metallic brittleness and weak semiconductivity.
- Therefore, it cannot be classified as a true metal or a non-metal. It is a **metalloid** (such as silicon or germanium).

**Check:** Lustre is metallic, but brittleness and semiconductivity are non-metallic, placing it directly on the staircase boundary ✅.

### Puzzle 3. Two Oxides, Two Solutions

**Question:** The oxides of two unknown elements X and Y are dissolved in water. The aqueous solution of Oxide X turns red litmus paper blue. The aqueous solution of Oxide Y turns blue litmus paper red. Which element is a metal?

**Solution:**
- Metal oxides are basic; when soluble, they produce alkaline hydroxide solutions ($\text{OH}^-$) that turn red litmus paper blue (e.g., $\text{Na}_2\text{O} + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{NaOH}$). Therefore, **X is a metal**.
- Non-metal oxides are acidic; they dissolve in water to produce hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) that turn blue litmus paper red (e.g., $\text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$). Therefore, **Y is a non-metal**.

**Check:** X is a metal forming a basic oxide; Y is a non-metal forming an acidic oxide ✅.

---

### Puzzle 4. An Oxide Reacting with Both Acid and Alkali

**Question:** An unknown oxide powder reacts vigorously with dilute hydrochloric acid to form a colourless solution, and also dissolves completely in aqueous sodium hydroxide solution. What type of oxide is this, and is the parent element a metal or a non-metal?

**Solution:**
- An oxide that reacts with both acids and strong bases is an **amphoteric oxide**.
- Amphoteric oxides are characteristic of specific metals, such as aluminium ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) and zinc ($\text{ZnO}$).
- Therefore, the parent element is a **metal**.
  - Reaction with acid: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 6\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2\text{O}$
  - Reaction with base: $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 + 2\text{NaOH} \rightarrow 2\text{NaAlO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

**Check:** Balanced atoms: Al: 2=2, O: 3+2=5, Na: 2=2, H: 2=2 ✅.

### Puzzle 5. The Mystery of the Molten Salts

**Question:** Three white crystalline solids K, L, and M gave the following experimental data:

| Compound | Melting Point | Solid Conductivity | Molten/Solution Conductivity | Mechanical Behaviour |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| K | 801 °C | None | Excellent | Shatters along planes |
| L | 55 °C | None | None | Waxy, easily compressed |
| M | 714 °C | None | Excellent | Shatters along planes |

Classify each compound as ionic or covalent, with full scientific justification.

**Solution:**
- **Compounds K and M:** High melting points ($801\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $714\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), non-conductive as solids but highly conductive when molten, and brittle crystalline cleavage. These are definitive hallmarks of **ionic compounds** (K corresponds to $\text{NaCl}$; M corresponds to $\text{MgCl}_2$).
- **Compound L:** Very low melting point ($55\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), non-conductive in all states, and soft/compressible. It is a **covalent molecular compound** (such as paraffin wax).

**Check:** High melting point + liquid conductivity = ionic; low melting point + zero conductivity = covalent ✅.

### Puzzle 6. Placing an Unknown Metal on the Reactivity Ladder

**Question:** A metal strip Z displaces copper from a blue copper(II) sulfate solution (depositing a reddish coating), but shows no reaction in zinc sulfate solution. Furthermore, metal Z liberates bubbles of hydrogen gas when immersed in dilute hydrochloric acid. Where does metal Z sit in the reactivity series?

**Solution:**
- Z displaces Cu $\rightarrow$ $Z > \text{Cu}$.
- Z cannot displace Zn $\rightarrow$ $Z < \text{Zn}$.
- Z liberates $\text{H}_2$ from dilute acid $\rightarrow$ $Z > [\text{H}]$.
- Therefore, metal Z sits **between zinc and copper, above hydrogen**: $\mathbf{\text{Zn} > Z > [\text{H}] > \text{Cu}}$. Elements fitting this exact profile include **iron (Fe)** and **lead (Pb)**.

**Check:** Iron displaces copper ($\text{Fe} + \text{CuSO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{Cu}$), does not displace zinc, and liberates $\text{H}_2$ from acid ✅.

### Puzzle 7. Identifying an Unknown Element from Combustion

**Question:** A yellow, brittle, non-conductive solid powder burns in air with a pale blue flame (demonstrated by a teacher under a fume hood) to produce a choking, pungent gas. When this gas is dissolved in water, the resulting solution turns blue litmus paper bright red. Identify the element, write the combustion equation, and state the nature of its oxide.

**Solution:**
- A yellow, brittle, non-conducting solid is a non-metal: **Sulfur ($\text{S}$)**.
- It burns with a blue flame to produce sulfur dioxide:
  $$\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$$
- Sulfur dioxide dissolves in water to form sulfurous acid ($\text{SO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_3$), which turns blue litmus red. The oxide is an **acidic oxide**.

**Check:** S: 1=1, O: 2=2 ✅. Acidity is fully consistent with a non-metal oxide ✅.

### Puzzle 8. The Scrap Yard Sorting Tray

**Question:** Five scrap items were tested with a magnet and tapped with a mallet:

| Scrap Item | Attracted by Magnet? | Sound When Tapped |
|---|---|---|
| Worn tinplate biscuit tin | Yes | Hollow, tinny ring |
| Aluminium soda can lid | No | High-pitched, clear ring |
| Crushed plastic soda bottle | No | Muffled, dull clatter |
| Solid copper pipe offcut | No | Deep, resonant chime |
| Lead fishing sinker | No | Heavy, muffled thud |

Classify each item as ferrous metal, non-ferrous metal, or non-metal, and explain any apparent anomalies.

**Solution:**
- **Tinplate biscuit tin:** Strongly magnetic, resonant ring $\rightarrow$ **Ferrous metal** (tinplate is thin sheet steel coated with a microscopic layer of tin).
- **Aluminium can lid:** Non-magnetic, bright resonant ring $\rightarrow$ **Non-ferrous metal**.
- **Plastic bottle:** Non-magnetic, non-sonorous $\rightarrow$ **Non-metal material (polymer)**.
- **Copper pipe offcut:** Non-magnetic, rich musical ring $\rightarrow$ **Non-ferrous metal**.
- **Lead fishing sinker:** Non-magnetic, produces a dull thud. Although it does not ring sonorously (because lead is extremely soft and dense, damping vibrations), a fresh scrape reveals metallic lustre and high electrical conductivity. It is a **non-ferrous metal**.

**Check:** The magnet attracts only the ferrous steel tin; lead is an understood acoustic exception among metals ✅.

## Part E: Mixed Synthesis Problems Across All Topics

### Mixed 1. Aluminium and Oxygen (Oxidation and Stoichiometry)

**Question:** A $2.7\text{ g}$ sample of pure aluminium powder is burned completely in oxygen. Calculate the mass of aluminium oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$) formed and the mass of oxygen consumed.

**Solution:**
Balanced equation: $4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$
- Molar masses: $\text{Al} = 27\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{O}_2 = 32\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 = 2(27) + 3(16) = 102\text{ g/mol}$.
- Moles of Al = $2.7 \div 27 = \mathbf{0.1\text{ mol}}$.
- Stoichiometric mole ratios:
  - Moles of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 = 0.1 \times \frac{2}{4} = 0.05\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.05 \times 102 = \mathbf{5.1\text{ g}}$.
  - Moles of $\text{O}_2 = 0.1 \times \frac{3}{4} = 0.075\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.075 \times 32 = \mathbf{2.4\text{ g}}$.

**Check:** Conservation of mass: $2.7\text{ g Al} + 2.4\text{ g O}_2 = 5.1\text{ g Al}_2\text{O}_3$ ✅.

### Mixed 2. Magnesium and Chlorine (Ionic Compound Formation)

**Question:** Calculate the mass of magnesium chloride ($\text{MgCl}_2$) formed and the mass of chlorine gas consumed when $4.8\text{ g}$ of magnesium ribbon reacts completely with dry chlorine gas.

**Solution:**
Balanced equation: $\text{Mg} + \text{Cl}_2 \rightarrow \text{MgCl}_2$
- Molar masses: $\text{Mg} = 24\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{Cl}_2 = 71\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{MgCl}_2 = 24 + 2(35.5) = 95\text{ g/mol}$.
- Moles of Mg = $4.8 \div 24 = \mathbf{0.2\text{ mol}}$.
- Mole ratio is 1:1:1.
  - Moles of $\text{Cl}_2 = 0.2\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.2 \times 71 = \mathbf{14.2\text{ g}}$.
  - Moles of $\text{MgCl}_2 = 0.2\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.2 \times 95 = \mathbf{19.0\text{ g}}$.

**Check:** $4.8\text{ g Mg} + 14.2\text{ g Cl}_2 = 19.0\text{ g MgCl}_2$ ✅.

### Mixed 3. Identifying a Trivalent Metal from Acid Evolution

**Question:** A $0.54\text{ g}$ sample of an unknown trivalent metal M (which forms $\text{M}^{3+}$ ions) reacts completely with excess dilute hydrochloric acid to liberate $0.06\text{ g}$ of hydrogen gas. Calculate the atomic mass of metal M and identify it.

**Solution:**
Balanced equation: $2\text{M} + 6\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{MCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2$
- Moles of $\text{H}_2$ gas = $0.06 \div 2 = \mathbf{0.03\text{ mol}}$.
- From stoichiometry ($2\text{ M} : 3\text{ H}_2$):
  $$\text{Moles of M} = 0.03 \times \frac{2}{3} = \mathbf{0.02\text{ mol}}$$
- Atomic mass of M = $\frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Moles}} = \frac{0.54\text{ g}}{0.02\text{ mol}} = \mathbf{27\text{ g/mol}}$.
- The metal with atomic mass $27\text{ u}$ and valency 3 is **Aluminium ($\text{Al}$)**.

**Check:** $2\text{Al} + 6\text{HCl} \rightarrow 2\text{AlCl}_3 + 3\text{H}_2$ (balanced: Al: 2=2, H: 6=6, Cl: 6=6 ✅).

### Mixed 4. Mass Percentage of Metal in an Ionic Compound

**Question:** What is the mass percentage of calcium in calcium oxide ($\text{CaO}$)?

**Solution:**
- Molar mass of $\text{CaO} = 40 + 16 = 56\text{ g/mol}$.
- Mass percentage of Ca = $\frac{40}{56} \times 100\% = \mathbf{71.43\%}$.
- Mass percentage of O = $\frac{16}{56} \times 100\% = 28.57\%$. Total = 100.00% ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "All metals are mechanically hard solids."**
   Sodium and potassium can be sliced effortlessly with a butter knife; lead is soft and malleable; and mercury is a liquid at room temperature. Hardness is a general guideline, not an absolute law.
2. **Mistake 2: "All non-metals are electrical insulators."**
   Graphite (a crystalline carbon allotrope) conducts electricity exceptionally well due to delocalised pi-electrons between its hexagonal sheets.
3. **Mistake 3: "All metals are excellent electrical conductors."**
   While metals conduct well compared to non-metals, lead and mercury are notably poor electrical conductors relative to copper and silver.
4. **Mistake 4: "Hydrogen is a metal because it appears in the metal reactivity series."**
   Hydrogen is a diatomic gaseous non-metal ($\text{H}_2$). It is included in the activity series strictly as a chemical benchmark: metals positioned above hydrogen displace $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute mineral acids; metals below it do not.
5. **Mistake 5: "Magnets attract all metals."**
   Magnets attract only ferromagnetic metals: iron, cobalt, nickel, and certain steels. Everyday metals like copper, aluminium, brass, silver, and gold are completely non-magnetic.
6. **Mistake 6: "All non-metal oxides are acidic."**
   Carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$), dinitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), and water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) are strictly neutral oxides.
7. **Mistake 7: "A single test is sufficient to classify an unknown element."**
   Iodine is shiny like a metal, but brittle and non-conductive; lead is a metal, but does not ring sonorously. Always cross-check multiple physical and chemical tests!

## Quick Recap

- **Metals:** Lustrous, malleable, ductile, sonorous, good heat and electrical conductors; form basic or amphoteric oxides; react by losing electrons to form positive cations.
- **Non-metals:** Generally dull, brittle solids or gases; poor conductors (except graphite); form acidic or neutral oxides; react by gaining or sharing electrons.
- **Key Exceptions:** Mercury (liquid metal), graphite (conductive non-metal), iodine (lustrous non-metal), diamond (hardest substance, thermal conductor), sodium/potassium (soft metals).
- **Ionic Compounds:** Formed between metals and non-metals via electron transfer ($\text{NaCl}$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{CaO}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$); high melting points, brittle lattices, electrical conductors only when molten or dissolved.
- **Diagnostic Method:** Test electrical conductivity $\rightarrow$ test mechanical malleability $\rightarrow$ test oxide litmus reaction $\rightarrow$ test magnetic response.
- Stoichiometric calculations seamlessly link physical properties with quantitative chemical reactions.

6. Using electron-dot structures, show the transfer of electrons between aluminium ($Z = 13$) and chlorine ($Z = 17$) to form aluminium chloride. State the empirical formula and calculate the formula unit mass.
7. State whether each statement is True or False, and correct the false statements:
   (a) All metals are hard solids.
   (b) All non-metals are poor conductors of electricity.
   (c) Hydrogen is chemically classified as a metal because it sits in the reactivity series.
   (d) The oxide of every non-metal element is acidic in nature.
8. A mass of $2.0\text{ g}$ of calcium metal burns completely in oxygen gas. Write the balanced equation, calculate the mass of calcium oxide formed, and calculate the mass of oxygen gas consumed.
9. A mass of $16.2\text{ g}$ of zinc oxide reacts completely with aqueous sodium hydroxide according to:
   $$\text{ZnO} + 2\text{NaOH} \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
   Find the mass of $\text{NaOH}$ required and the mass of sodium zincate ($\text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2$) produced ($\text{Zn} = 65$, $\text{Na} = 23$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{H} = 1$). What does this reaction prove about the chemical nature of zinc oxide?
10. Four metals P, Q, R, and S gave the following displacement results with aqueous sulfate solutions:
    - Q displaces P from P sulfate.
    - P displaces R from R sulfate.
    - R displaces S from S sulfate.
    - S displaces none of the others.
    Arrange metals P, Q, R, and S in descending order of chemical reactivity.
11. Hydrogen is non-metallic, yet it is prominently featured on the metal reactivity series. Explain the scientific reason for its inclusion.
12. Calculate the mass percentage of aluminium in aluminium oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$). How many grams of pure aluminium are contained in $51\text{ g}$ of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$?
13. Provide the name of the chemical substance matching each description:
    (a) An active metal stored permanently under kerosene oil.
    (b) An active non-metal stored permanently submerged under water.
    (c) A metal naturally protected from ongoing atmospheric corrosion by an impermeable self-healing oxide film.
    (d) A freshly prepared 1 : 3 acid mixture that can dissolve solid gold and platinum.
    (e) A crystalline allotrope of carbon that is an excellent conductor of electricity.

### Answers

1. - **Iodine:** Non-metal (lustrous purple solid, but brittle, non-conducting, and sublimes easily).
   - **Silicon:** Metalloid (lustrous grey appearance, but brittle semiconductor).
   - **Tungsten:** Metal (exceptionally high melting point of ~3422 °C, malleable, conducts electricity).
   - **Graphite:** Non-metal allotrope of carbon (hexagonal layered carbon lattice that conducts electricity).
   - **Gallium:** Metal (metallic lustre and conductor, but unusually low melting point of 29.8 °C).
   - **Bromine:** Non-metal (reddish-brown volatile liquid, diatomic $\text{Br}_2$, non-conductor).
2. The substance is a **non-metal** (specifically red phosphorus, $\text{P}_4$). Its combustion produces dense white fumes of phosphorus pentoxide ($\text{P}_4\text{O}_{10}$), which dissolves in water to form phosphoric acid ($\text{H}_3\text{PO}_4$), turning blue litmus red: an **acidic oxide**.
3. - **Compound U is ionic:** High melting point (734 °C), non-conducting as a solid but conducting when molten, and mechanical brittleness (indicative of a crystal lattice of ions like $\text{KBr}$).
   - **Compound V is covalent:** Low melting point (80 °C), non-conducting in all states, and insoluble in water (indicative of a molecular covalent solid like naphthalene).
4. - **Steel spoon:** Attracted by magnet (ferrous steel); produces a resonant metallic ring.
   - **Brass bell:** Not attracted by magnet (copper-zinc alloy); produces a clear, sustained musical ring.
   - **Lead sinker:** Not attracted by magnet; produces a heavy, muffled thud (soft metal damping vibrations).
   - **Zinc sheet:** Not attracted by magnet; produces a clear metallic ring.
   - **Glass bottle:** Not attracted by magnet; produces a high-pitched click/ring, but is brittle non-metallic silicate glass.
   *Only the steel spoon enters the ferrous bin.*
5. Potassium has 1 valence electron: $\text{K} (2, 8, 8, 1) \rightarrow \text{K}^+ + \text{e}^-$.
   Sulfur has 6 valence electrons: $\text{S} (2, 8, 6) + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{S}^{2-}$.
   Two potassium atoms transfer one electron each to a single sulfur atom:
   $$2\text{K}^+ + \text{S}^{2-} \rightarrow \mathbf{K_2S}$$
   Formula unit mass = $2(39) + 32 = 78 + 32 = \mathbf{110\text{ u}}$.
6. Aluminium has 3 valence electrons: $\text{Al} (2, 8, 3) \rightarrow \text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{e}^-$.
   Chlorine has 7 valence electrons: $\text{Cl} (2, 8, 7) + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}^-$.
   One aluminium atom transfers three electrons, one to each of three chlorine atoms:
   $$\text{Al}^{3+} + 3\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \mathbf{AlCl_3}$$
   Formula unit mass = $27 + 3(35.5) = 27 + 106.5 = \mathbf{133.5\text{ u}}$.
7. (a) **False** ❌: Sodium and potassium are soft enough to cut with a knife; mercury is a liquid. Most, but not all, metals are hard.
   (b) **False** ❌: Graphite is an allotrope of carbon that is an excellent conductor of electricity.
   (c) **False** ❌: Hydrogen is a non-metal; it is placed in the reactivity series as an acid-reactivity benchmark.
   (d) **False** ❌: Carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$), dinitrous oxide ($\text{N}_2\text{O}$), and water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) are neutral oxides.
8. Equation: $2\text{Ca} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CaO}$ (balanced: Ca: 2=2, O: 2=2 ✅).
   - Moles of Ca = $2.0 \div 40 = 0.05\text{ mol}$.
   - Moles of $\text{CaO}$ formed = $0.05\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.05 \times 56 = \mathbf{2.8\text{ g}}$.
   - Moles of $\text{O}_2$ consumed = $0.025\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.025 \times 32 = \mathbf{0.8\text{ g}}$.
   *(Check: 2.0 g Ca + 0.8 g O₂ = 2.8 g CaO ✅)*
9. Molar mass of $\text{ZnO} = 65 + 16 = 81\text{ g/mol}$.
   - Moles of $\text{ZnO} = 16.2 \div 81 = \mathbf{0.2\text{ mol}}$.
   - Moles of $\text{NaOH}$ required = $2 \times 0.2 = 0.4\text{ mol} \rightarrow \text{Mass} = 0.4 \times 40 = \mathbf{16.0\text{ g}}$.
   - Molar mass of $\text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2 = 2(23) + 65 + 2(16) = 46 + 65 + 32 = 143\text{ g/mol}$.
   - Mass of $\text{Na}_2\text{ZnO}_2 = 0.2 \times 143 = \mathbf{28.6\text{ g}}$.
   - Water produced = $0.2 \times 18 = 3.6\text{ g}$.
   *(Check: 16.2 + 16.0 = 32.2 g; 28.6 + 3.6 = 32.2 g ✅)*
   *Significance:* This reaction proves that **$\text{ZnO}$ is an amphoteric oxide**, reacting with a strong base ($\text{NaOH}$) as well as acids.
10. Inequalities from observations: $Q > P$, $P > R$, and $R > S$.
    Order: **Q > P > R > S**.
11. Like active metals, hydrogen can lose an electron to form a univalent positive cation ($\text{H}^+$). Placing hydrogen in the series establishes the dividing line for acid reactivity: metals positioned above hydrogen displace $\text{H}_2$ gas from dilute acids, while metals positioned below hydrogen do not.
12. Molar mass of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 = 2(27) + 3(16) = 102\text{ g/mol}$.
    - Mass percentage of Al = $\frac{54}{102} \times 100\% = \mathbf{52.94\%}$.
    - Mass of Al in $51\text{ g}$ of $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3 = 51 \times \frac{54}{102} = \mathbf{27\text{ g}}$.
    *(Check: 51 g Al₂O₃ is exactly 0.5 mol, which contains 1.0 mol of Al = 27 g ✅)*
13. (a) **Sodium** (or potassium).
    (b) **White phosphorus**.
    (c) **Aluminium** (via its passivating $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ layer).
    (d) **Aqua regia** (1 part concentrated $\text{HNO}_3$ : 3 parts concentrated $\text{HCl}$).
    (e) **Graphite**.
