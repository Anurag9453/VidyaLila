# Topic 1: Metals and Non-metals: Physical Properties

In Class 9, we grouped the chemical elements into three broad families—metals, non-metals, and metalloids—and saw that metals are lustrous (shiny), can be hammered into thin sheets, and ring like a bell when struck. In the previous chapter, we saw that zinc releases H₂ gas from dilute acid whereas copper does not. Now we will explore these families **in depth**, starting right here with **how we recognise metals and non-metals by touching, bending, tapping, and testing them with a low-voltage circuit**—that is, their **physical properties**. Their chemical properties (how they react with oxygen, water, and acids) will follow in the upcoming topics. By the end of this topic, we will also understand why engineers choose specific metals for electrical wiring, cooking pans, bells, light-bulb filaments, and suspension bridges.

## Edible Silver Leaf at the Confectionery Shop

Mr. Brooks took his niece, Aria, to a traditional confectionery shop to buy festive sweets. Inside the glass display case sat neat diamond-shaped almond fudge squares, each topped with a shimmering layer of **edible silver leaf** so gossamer-thin that it looked like a mirror painted right onto the sweet.

Aria asked the confectioner if she could see an unused sheet of silver leaf. As the shopkeeper carefully opened the tissue-paper booklet holding a sheet, Aria let out a small breath—and the delicate silver leaf fluttered up, crumpled in mid-air, and clung to her fingers!

"Whoa! A single breath blew it away!" Aria laughed.

Mr. Brooks smiled: "That's why the confectioner keeps each sheet sandwiched between tissue paper. Remember what you learned in Class 9? Metals are **malleable**: when hammered, they spread into thin sheets without shattering. Artisans gently beat silver ribbons for hours until the sheet is thinner than a soap bubble. Try doing that with a piece of charcoal, sulphur, or chalk—it would crumble into powder on the very first tap!"

"Is silver the only metal that can do that?" Aria asked.

"Not at all! Gold leaf can be beaten even thinner. And gold is so **ductile** that a single gram can be drawn into a wire nearly two kilometres long! But why do metals behave like this? Does **every** metal follow these rules—or can a metal be soft enough to cut with a butter knife, or even flow like a liquid at room temperature?" Mr. Brooks grinned. "Let's unpack each physical property one by one—and when we get home, we'll build our own mini testing kit."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Lustre** | The ability of a clean, freshly cut, or polished surface to reflect light with a bright shine | Silver, gold, polished aluminium |
| **Malleable** | Able to be hammered or rolled into thin sheets without breaking | Gold leaf, aluminium foil |
| **Ductile** | Able to be drawn (stretched) into thin wires | Copper wire, gold wire |
| **Conductor** | A material that allows electricity or heat to flow through it easily | Copper, aluminium, silver |
| **Insulator** | A material that does not allow electricity or heat to pass through it easily | Plastic, rubber, dry wood |
| **Sonorous** | Producing a clear, ringing sound when struck | Brass bell, steel plate |
| **Brittle** | Shattering or crumbling into pieces when hammered or bent | Sulphur, coal |
| **Density** | The mass of 1 cm³ of a substance, measured in g per cm³ (g/cm³) | Water = 1 g/cm³, gold ≈ 19.3 g/cm³ |
| **Sublimation** | Changing directly from a solid to a gas on heating (without melting into a liquid) | Iodine, camphor |

## Part A: Physical Properties of Metals, One by One

Here are the general physical properties of metals. (Notice the phrase "in general" for each property—we will meet the fascinating exceptions in Part C!)

1. **Lustre:** A freshly cut or polished metal surface has a characteristic metallic shine. That is why jewellery, coins, trophies, and decorative vessels are made of metals.
2. **Malleability:** When hammered or rolled, metals spread into thin sheets without cracking. Gold leaf can be beaten down to a thickness of roughly **0.1 micrometre** (an approximate value). Since 1 micrometre = 10⁻⁶ m, that is less than one-hundredth the thickness of a human hair!
3. **Ductility:** Metals can be drawn into thin wires. **Roughly** 1 gram of gold can be stretched into a continuous fine wire about **2 km** long.
4. **Good Conductors of Heat and Electricity:** Silver is the best conductor of all, followed by copper, gold, and aluminium. That is why household electrical wiring is made of copper or aluminium, and cooking pans are made of aluminium, copper, or steel. **Exception:** Lead and mercury are comparatively **poor** conductors among metals.
5. **Sonorous:** When struck, metals produce a resonant ringing sound—think of church and temple bells, school bells, guitar strings, and cymbals.
6. **Generally Hard and Dense:** Iron girders in suspension bridges, steel hammers, and heavy padlocks owe their strength to the hardness and high density of metals.
7. **Generally High Melting and Boiling Points, and Solid at Room Temperature:** That is why iron skillets and steel saucepans do not melt on a kitchen stove.

### Why Do Metals Behave Like This? (The "Sea of Electrons" Picture in One Paragraph)

In Class 9 ([chemical-bonding-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/chemical-bonding/chemical-bonding-part4.md)), we saw why ionic and covalent compounds have very different properties. Inside a pure metal, there is a **third kind** of bonding that we can picture simply right now. Metal atoms hold their outermost (valence) electrons loosely, so instead of staying locked to a single atom, these outer electrons **move freely** throughout the entire piece of metal—like a mobile **"sea of electrons"** surrounding an orderly array of positive metal ions. This electron sea glues all the metal ions strongly together (giving most metals hardness and high melting points). When a hammer strikes a metal, layers of metal ions **slide** past one another while the flexible electron sea keeps holding them together—so instead of shattering, the metal flattens into a sheet or stretches into a wire! When a voltage or heat source is applied, these mobile electrons **carry electrical charge and thermal energy rapidly** from one end to the other, making metals excellent conductors. And when light hits a smooth metal surface, the free electrons oscillate and reflect the light back as metallic lustre. In non-metals, electrons are locked in localized bonds and cannot roam freely, which is why non-metals behave so differently. (You will study metallic bonding in full detail in higher classes.)

## Part B: Side-by-Side Comparison of Metals and Non-metals

Non-metals (such as a lump of yellow sulphur or a piece of coal) are generally **dull**, **brittle**, **poor conductors** of heat and electricity, **low in density**, **low in melting point**, and **not sonorous** when struck.

| Property | Metals (in general) | Non-metals (in general) | One-Line Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lustre | Shiny (lustrous) | Dull (non-lustrous) | Free electrons in metals reflect light back |
| When hammered | Malleable; flattens into sheets | Brittle; shatters into powder | Layers of atoms can slide in metals, not in non-metals |
| When stretched | Ductile; draws into wires | Not ductile; snaps | Same reason: sliding atomic layers held by the electron sea |
| Electrical conduction | Good conductor | Poor conductor (mostly insulators) | Mobile electrons are free to carry current in metals |
| Thermal conduction | Good conductor | Poor conductor | Those same mobile electrons transport heat rapidly |
| Sound when struck | Sonorous (rings) | Not sonorous (dull thud) | Rigid, strongly bonded metal lattice transmits vibrations well |
| Density | Generally high | Generally low | Metal atoms pack closely and have higher atomic masses |
| Melting and boiling points | Generally high | Generally low | The electron sea binds metal atoms tightly together |
| State at room temperature | Almost all are solids | Solids, liquids, or gases | Varied molecular structures across the non-metal family |

```
   METALS                                 NON-METALS
   (e.g., copper, silver)                 (e.g., sulphur, coal)

   shiny (lustrous) ..................... dull
   hammered -> thin sheet ............... brittle, crumbles to powder
   drawn -> thin wire ................... cannot be drawn into wire
   electricity: good conductor .......... electricity: poor conductor
   heat: good conductor ................. heat: poor conductor
   when struck: rings like a bell ....... when struck: dull thud
   dense, hard, high melting point ...... low density, low melting point
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Physical properties of metals and non-metals at a glance: each row contrasts a typical metallic property on the left with the opposite non-metallic property on the right. These are general rules; we will explore the key exceptions in Part C.

## Part C: Exceptions: Where the Rules Get Interesting

The properties of metals and non-metals listed above hold true "in general". Nearly every rule has a memorable exception—and knowing these exceptions sharpens our understanding of the rules themselves!

### Exceptions Among Metals

| Metal | What Makes It Unusual | Key Numbers / Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Mercury** | **Liquid** at room temperature (and a relatively poor conductor) | For study only; students must never touch mercury, as its vapour is highly toxic |
| **Gallium** | Melts from the warmth of a human hand | Melting point ≈ **29.8 °C** |
| **Caesium** | Can melt on a warm summer day | Melting point ≈ **28.5 °C** |
| **Sodium and potassium** | Soft enough to be cut with a knife, and **float** on water | Densities ≈ 0.97 g/cm³ and 0.86 g/cm³ |
| **Lithium** | The lightest (least dense) metal of all | Density ≈ **0.53** g/cm³ |
| **Osmium** | The densest metal of all | Density ≈ **22.6** g/cm³ |
| **Tungsten** | Highest melting point of any metal; used in incandescent bulb filaments | Melting point ≈ **3400 °C** |
| **Lead** | A metal, yet a poor conductor of electricity and not sonorous | Never used for electrical wiring |

**Sodium and potassium are for observation only (teacher demonstration).** Because both metals react rapidly with air and violently with water (Topics 2 and 3), students never handle them directly; they are stored immersed in kerosene. Here we simply note their physical exceptions: they are soft like firm wax and less dense than water.

**About Gallium:** Gallium is a silvery, lustrous solid metal at cool room temperature (say, 20 °C), but if held in a gloved palm, it melts into a shiny puddle because normal human body temperature (≈ 37 °C) is above its melting point (29.8 °C). This is shown **for observation only (teacher demonstration)**.

Compare the lightest metal, lithium (0.53 g/cm³), with the densest metal, osmium (22.6 g/cm³): 22.6 ÷ 0.53 = **about 42.6 times**. A small cube of osmium weighs nearly 43 times as much as an identical cube of lithium!

### Exceptions Among Non-metals

| Non-metal | What Makes It Unusual | Why It Is Interesting |
|---|---|---|
| **Graphite** (an allotrope of carbon) | A **good conductor of electricity** | Used to make pencil leads and battery electrodes |
| **Diamond** (another allotrope of carbon) | The **hardest** natural substance known, with a very high melting point (though an electrical **insulator**) | One element (carbon), two opposite extremes—full story in Chapter 10 |
| **Iodine** | A **lustrous (shiny)** dark solid that sublimes into violet vapour on heating | A non-metal with a metallic-looking shine |
| **Bromine** | The only non-metal that is a **liquid** at room temperature | Along with mercury, one of only two liquid elements at room temperature (Class 9). For study only: bromine is toxic and corrosive |

In Class 9 we also met **metalloids**—such as boron, silicon, germanium, and arsenic—which straddle the boundary between metals and non-metals and display intermediate properties.

## Part D: Safe At-Home Investigations

Now it is time for Mr. Brooks's home testing kit! Pay attention to the safety note at the start of each activity.

### Activity 1: Electrical Conductivity Tester

**At home with an adult.** Use **only 2 AA or AAA dry cells (3 V)**. Never connect anything to a wall socket or household mains supply!

Materials: 2 AA cells in a battery holder, a small **LED**, a **100-ohm resistor** wired in series with the LED (or a small 3 V flashlight bulb), insulated connecting wires, and 2 alligator clips. Connecting the resistor in series with the LED is essential so the LED does not burn out (from a 3 V supply, dropping ~2 V across the LED leaves ~1 V across the 100-ohm resistor, limiting the current to a safe ~10 mA).

```
        Cell 1   Cell 2
       +--[+ -]--[+ -]--+
       :                :
      LED               :
       :                :
   Resistor (100 ohm)   :
       :                :
    Clip A           Clip B
        \             /
       [ Object Under Test ]

   LED glows    ->  Object allows current to pass (conductor)
   LED stays off -> Object blocks current (insulator)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – A simple 3 V conductivity tester circuit: 2 dry cells, an LED with a 100-ohm series resistor, and two test clips. Touch Clip A and Clip B to opposite ends of the test object. If the object is a conductor, the circuit closes and the LED lights up.

Test these everyday items one by one: a stainless-steel spoon, a strip of aluminium foil, a bare copper wire, a graphite pencil lead, the wooden barrel of the pencil, a rubber eraser, a rubber band, a plastic comb, and a glass tumbler. Notice: the pencil lead is made of graphite (mixed with clay), so the LED glows even though carbon is a non-metal!

### Activity 2: The Magnet Test

**At home with an adult.** Bring a small fridge magnet close to a steel spoon, an iron nail, a copper wire, a piece of aluminium foil, a brass key, and a plastic ruler.

You will find that **only the iron nail (and most steel spoons, since steel is mostly iron) is attracted to the magnet**. Copper, aluminium, and brass are not attracted at all! Therefore, the statement *"Magnets attract all metals"* is **false**: only a few ferromagnetic metals (iron, nickel, and cobalt) are strongly attracted by a magnet. *(Note: some high-grade stainless-steel spoons are barely attracted or not attracted by a magnet because of the way nickel and chromium are alloyed with iron—yet they are still 100% metallic!)*

### Activity 3: The Ringing Test (Sonority)

**At home with an adult.** Suspend different objects from a piece of string and tap each gently with the back of a small metal spoon.

```
   Steel plate suspended by string
              :
         +----+----+
         :  Plate  :     <--- Gentle tap with a spoon
         +---------+

   Steel / brass: clear, lingering "tinggg..."   (sonorous)
   Wood / plastic: short, dull "thud"            (not sonorous)
   Glass: can ring, yet it is brittle (not a metal)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – The ringing test: tapping a suspended metal object (steel or brass) produces a clear, sustained ringing tone, whereas wood and plastic give a dull thud. (Glass can also clink when tapped, showing why no single test should be used in isolation.)

### Activity 4: The Flexibility (Bending) Test

**At home with an adult.** Take three items: **aluminium foil**, a short piece of **bare copper wire**, and a piece of **graphite pencil lead**.

- Fold and crumple the aluminium foil into a ball and flatten it out again: it bends smoothly without cracking (malleable).
- Bend the copper wire gently back and forth 3 or 4 times: it bends without snapping (ductile and flexible, though repeated sharp bending at the same spot will eventually fatigue and break it).
- Try to bend the pencil lead ever so slightly: it **snaps cleanly in two** (brittle). Do not press hard on the sharp tip.

Takeaway: metals bend without shattering, whereas a solid non-metal (graphite) is brittle—even though graphite conducts electricity!

### Activity 5: Cutting Sodium with a Knife (Observation Only)

**Observation only (teacher demonstration).** Using forceps, the teacher lifts a small piece of sodium metal from its jar of kerosene, blots it on filter paper, and slices it on a tile with a knife: it cuts as smoothly as firm cheese, exposing a bright, silvery-lustrous fresh surface that tarnishes dull grey within seconds on contact with air. Students observe from a safe distance.

## Part E: Solved Examples (With Numbers)

In these examples, use **density = mass ÷ volume**, and take the density of gold as **19.3 g/cm³**.

### Example 1. How Large a Sheet Can Be Beaten from 1 g of Gold?

Suppose gold leaf is beaten to an approximate thickness of 0.1 micrometre = 0.1 × 10⁻⁴ cm = 10⁻⁵ cm.

- Volume of 1 g of gold = 1 ÷ 19.3 = **0.0518 cm³**.
- Since Volume = Area × Thickness, Area = 0.0518 ÷ 10⁻⁵ = **5181 cm²** (approx.).
- Since 1 m² = 10,000 cm², this is roughly **0.52 m²**—more than half a square metre of gold leaf from a single gram of gold!

Check: Area × Thickness = 5181 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.0518 cm³, and 0.0518 × 19.3 = 1.0 g ✅.

### Example 2. Stacking Gold Leaf

Suppose sheets of gold leaf (each 0.1 micrometre thick) are stacked neatly to make a pile just 0.1 mm (= 100 micrometres) thick. How many sheets are in the stack?

- Number of sheets = 100 ÷ 0.1 = **1000 sheets**.

Check: 1000 × 0.1 µm = 100 µm = 0.1 mm ✅. It takes 1000 sheets of gold leaf stacked together just to match the thickness of a single sheet of notebook paper!

### Example 3. Thickness of a 2 km Wire Drawn from 1 g of Gold

Suppose 1 g of gold is drawn into a uniform cylindrical wire of length **2 km** (approximate value).

- Volume = 0.0518 cm³; Length = 2 km = 200,000 cm.
- Cross-sectional area = 0.0518 ÷ 200,000 = 2.59 × 10⁻⁷ cm².
- From Area = π × (diameter ÷ 2)², the diameter of the wire works out to roughly **5.7 µm**.

Check: Recomputing π × (diameter ÷ 2)² × length gives 0.0518 cm³ ✅. That wire is more than ten times thinner than a human hair (≈ 70 µm)!

### Example 4. Comparing Light and Heavy Metals

Sodium has a density of 0.97 g/cm³ and osmium has a density of 22.6 g/cm³. Find the mass of a 10 cm³ block of each:

- Mass of 10 cm³ of sodium = 10 × 0.97 = **9.7 g**.
- Mass of 10 cm³ of osmium = 10 × 22.6 = **226 g**.
- Since the density of water is 1.0 g/cm³, sodium (0.97 g/cm³) is slightly less dense than water and therefore **floats** on water (while reacting vigorously with it—see Topic 3; students never perform this). Osmium (22.6 g/cm³) sinks immediately.

Check: 226 ÷ 9.7 = 23.3 times heavier ✅ (matching the density ratio 22.6 ÷ 0.97 = 23.3).

### Example 5. Why Gallium Melts in Your Hand

Gallium has a melting point of 29.8 °C, while normal human body temperature is about 37.0 °C.

- Body temperature − Melting point = 37.0 − 29.8 = **7.2 °C**.

Because a warm hand is 7.2 °C above gallium's melting point, heat flows from the hand into the gallium and melts it.

Check: 29.8 + 7.2 = 37.0 °C ✅.

### Example 6. Interpreting Conductivity Tester Data

A student recorded the following results using a 3 V LED conductivity tester:

| Object Tested | Did the LED Glow? |
|---|---|
| Stainless-steel spoon | Yes |
| Pencil lead (graphite) | Yes |
| Plastic comb | No |
| Aluminium foil | Yes |
| Rubber eraser | No |
| Dry wooden stick | No |

Question: Which objects are made of metal? Is a glowing LED by itself proof that an object is a metal?

- The steel spoon and aluminium foil are metals, so the LED glowed. Plastic, rubber, and dry wood are insulators, so the LED stayed off.
- However, the LED **also glowed** with the pencil lead, which is made of **graphite (a non-metal)**. Therefore, electrical conductivity **alone** is not sufficient proof that a material is a metal—exceptions like graphite exist!

### Example 7. Matching Materials to Their Engineering Jobs (Reasoning)

| Object / Component | Material Chosen | Property-Based Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Household electrical wiring | Copper or aluminium | Excellent electrical conductor and ductile (can be drawn into flexible wires) |
| Outer coating on electrical wires | PVC plastic or rubber | Electrical insulator, preventing electric shocks and short circuits |
| Base of a frying pan or saucepan | Aluminium, copper, or stainless steel | Good conductor of heat with a high melting point |
| Handle of a frying pan | Heat-resistant plastic or wood | Poor conductor of heat (thermal insulator), keeping the handle cool to hold |
| School or temple bell | Brass or bronze | Strongly sonorous and hard |
| Bridges and railway tracks | Iron / structural steel | High tensile strength, hardness, and durability |
| Kitchen food wrap | Aluminium foil | Highly malleable (rolls into thin sheets), lightweight, and non-toxic |
| Fine jewellery | Gold and silver | Lustrous, highly malleable and ductile, and resistant to corrosion |
| Incandescent light-bulb filament | Tungsten | Extremely high melting point (≈ 3400 °C), so it glows white-hot without melting |
| Pencil core and dry-cell electrode | Graphite | A non-metal that conducts electricity (for electrodes) and leaves soft dark marks as its layers slide (for pencils) |

Notice how a single frying pan uses **both** extremes: a **thermal conductor** (metal) for the base to cook food evenly, and a **thermal insulator** (plastic or wood) for the handle to protect your hand!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "All metals are hard."**

Sodium and potassium are soft enough to cut with a knife, lead is relatively soft, and mercury is a liquid. Always say metals are **generally** hard.

**Mistake 2: "All non-metals are insulators of electricity."**

Graphite is a non-metal (an allotrope of carbon), yet it is a good conductor of electricity.

**Mistake 3: "All metals are equally good conductors of electricity."**

Silver and copper are outstanding conductors, whereas lead and mercury are poor conductors compared with other metals.

**Mistake 4: "Magnets attract all metals."**

Magnets strongly attract only ferromagnetic metals such as iron, nickel, and cobalt (and most steels). Copper, aluminium, silver, and gold are not attracted by an ordinary magnet.

**Mistake 5: "All metals are solids at room temperature."**

Mercury is a liquid at room temperature, and gallium and caesium melt just above room temperature (around 28.5–30 °C).

**Mistake 6: "Only metals have a shiny lustre."**

Iodine and graphite are non-metals that display a bright, metallic-looking lustre.

**Mistake 7: "If the LED glows in a conductivity test, the sample must be a metal."**

A glowing LED only proves that the sample conducts electricity. Since graphite also lights up the LED, you must combine conductivity with other tests (such as malleability) to confirm a metal.

**Mistake 8: Confusing "malleable/ductile" with "soft".**

Malleability and ductility mean a material deforms without cracking under stress—not that it is soft like butter. For instance, iron is hard enough to build bridges, yet a blacksmith can hammer red-hot iron into sheets and draw steel into strong cables without it shattering.

## Quick Recap

- **Metals in general:** lustrous, malleable, ductile, good conductors of heat and electricity, sonorous, dense and hard, high melting and boiling points, solid at room temperature.
- **Non-metals in general:** dull, brittle when solid, poor conductors of heat and electricity, not sonorous, low density, and low melting and boiling points.
- **Sea of electrons:** mobile outer electrons in metals explain their lustre, conductivity, malleability, and ductility.
- **Order of electrical conductivity:** silver > copper > gold > aluminium. Lead and mercury are poor metallic conductors.
- **Metallic exceptions:** mercury (liquid at room temperature), gallium (melts at 29.8 °C) and caesium (28.5 °C), sodium and potassium (soft, cut with a knife, float on water), lithium (lightest metal, 0.53 g/cm³), osmium (densest metal, 22.6 g/cm³), tungsten (highest melting point, ≈ 3400 °C).
- **Non-metallic exceptions:** graphite conducts electricity, diamond is the hardest natural substance, iodine is a lustrous solid that sublimes, bromine is a liquid at room temperature.
- **Gold's extremes:** beaten into leaf ~0.1 µm thick; 1 g drawn into ~2 km of fine wire.
- **Safe investigations:** 3 V LED conductivity tester (with 100-ohm resistor), magnet test, ringing test, and bending test. Cutting sodium is a teacher demonstration only.
- **Choosing materials:** match the engineering job to the physical property (wires: ductile conductor; pan handle: thermal insulator; bell: sonorous; bulb filament: ultra-high melting point).

## Practice Questions

1. A silver bar has a volume of 20 cm³. If the density of silver is 10.5 g per cm³, what is its mass?

2. A jeweller beats 0.5 g of gold (density 19.3 g per cm³) into a leaf about 0.25 micrometre thick. Estimate the area of the leaf in cm².

3. About 1 g of gold can be drawn into a wire of length about 2 km. Estimate the length of wire that can be drawn from 0.25 g, assuming the wire thickness stays the same.

4. Lithium has density 0.53 g per cm³. Find the mass of 100 cm³ of lithium. Also arrange lithium, sodium (0.97), potassium (0.86), and iron (7.87) in increasing order of density, and state which of them are lighter than water.

5. Osmium has density 22.6 g per cm³. A cube of side 2 cm is made of osmium. Find its mass.

6. On a hot day, the room temperature is 31 °C. Will a piece of gallium (melting point 29.8 °C) and a piece of caesium (melting point 28.5 °C) be solid or liquid? By how many degrees is the room above each melting point?

7. A student's conductivity tester gave these results. Copper wire: LED glows. Iron nail: LED glows. Chalk: LED does not glow. Graphite (from an old pencil): LED glows. Dry wood: LED does not glow. Name the conductors, and explain why the graphite result needs a careful explanation.

8. Why are cooking pans made of metals like aluminium, but their handles of plastic or wood?

9. A bell made of lead is tapped and it does not ring well, while one made of bronze rings clearly. Which property is this, and what does it tell you about lead?

10. Why is tungsten used for the filament of an electric bulb and not copper or gold?

11. A magnet attracts an iron nail but not an aluminium foil piece. Does this mean aluminium is a non-metal? Explain.

12. Name one non-metal that is a good conductor of electricity, one that is a liquid at room temperature, and one that is a shiny solid. Give one use of the first.

13. State True or False, and correct the false ones:
    (a) All metals are solid at room temperature.
    (b) Diamond is the hardest natural substance and a good conductor of electricity.
    (c) Sodium can be cut with a knife.
    (d) Iodine is a lustrous non-metal.

### Answers

1. Mass = density × volume = 10.5 × 20 = **210 g**. Check: 210 ÷ 20 = 10.5 ✅.

2. Volume = 0.5 ÷ 19.3 = 0.0259 cm³. Thickness = 0.25 µm = 0.25 × 10⁻⁴ cm = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ cm. Area = volume ÷ thickness = 0.0259 ÷ (2.5 × 10⁻⁵) = **approx. 1036 cm²**. Check: 1036 × 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ = 0.0259 cm³ ✅.

3. For the same wire thickness, length is directly proportional to mass: for 0.25 g of gold, length = 0.25 × 2 km = **0.5 km = 500 m** (approx.).

4. Mass of 100 cm³ of lithium = 100 × 0.53 = **53 g**. Increasing order of density: lithium (0.53) < potassium (0.86) < sodium (0.97) < iron (7.87). Since water has a density of 1.0 g/cm³, lithium, potassium, and sodium (all below 1.0) are lighter than water; iron is much denser.

5. Volume of cube = 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 cm³. Mass = 22.6 × 8 = **180.8 g**. Check: 180.8 ÷ 8 = 22.6 ✅.

6. Since 31 °C is above both melting points, both gallium and caesium will be **liquid**. Gallium: 31 − 29.8 = **1.2 °C** above its melting point. Caesium: 31 − 28.5 = **2.5 °C** above its melting point.

7. Conductors: copper wire, iron nail, and graphite (LED glowed). Chalk and dry wood are insulators. Graphite is a non-metal (carbon) that conducts electricity as an exception, showing that electrical conduction alone is not enough to classify a material as a metal.

8. The pan base must transfer heat rapidly from the stove to the food, so it is made of a good thermal conductor (aluminium). The handle is held by hand, so it is made of a thermal insulator (plastic or wood) to prevent burns.

9. This is the property of **sonority (being sonorous)**. Lead is soft and non-sonorous, so it gives a dull thud instead of a ring; bronze is hard and elastic, producing a clear, sustained ring.
