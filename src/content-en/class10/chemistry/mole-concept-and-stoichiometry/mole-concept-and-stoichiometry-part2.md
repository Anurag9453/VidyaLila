# Topic 2: Vapour Density: Measuring How Light or Heavy a Gas Is

In Topic 1, we revisited Avogadro's Law: **at the same temperature (T) and pressure (P), equal volumes of all gases contain equal numbers of molecules**, regardless of which gas it is. That law showed us why volume ratios equal mole ratios. Now let's ask a new question: if **equal volumes contain equal numbers of molecules**, what causes the **weights** of those equal volumes to differ? The answer is simple: **the weight of each individual molecule**. In this topic, we use that insight to build a quick, neat, and super-useful measure called **vapour density (VD)** — comparing the weight of a gas against an equal volume of **hydrogen** to see how many times heavier it is. From VD, we can find a gas's molecular mass and predict whether it will rise or sink in air.

---

## The Balloon Seller at the Fairground

Mr. Carter took his nephew Leo to the town carnival. A bright stall near the entrance had a crowd gathered around a **balloon vendor**. Leo noticed something curious. Half the balloons, tied with string, were **floating straight up**, tugging hard toward the sky. The other half, tied to a wooden pole on the cart, were **drooping downwards**, bobbing near the ground whenever a breeze blew past.

Behind the stall stood a heavy steel **cylinder**. The vendor fitted the neck of a balloon over the cylinder's nozzle, opened the valve slightly, and the balloon swelled up and floated immediately. The drooping balloons, on the other hand, had been inflated with a **hand pump**. "The floating ones are filled with **helium**, kid," the vendor told Leo. "The ones hanging down just have **ordinary air** inside."

Leo tugged at Mr. Carter's sleeve. "Uncle, both have gas inside. Why does one float up and the other droop down? And when the vendor says helium is 'light', **how light** is it? How can we measure that?"

Mr. Carter smiled. "In Class 9, you learned gas density in grams per litre. Today we'll learn an even simpler comparison: take **1 litre** of any gas, and at the exact same temperature and pressure, take **1 litre of hydrogen**. Weigh both. The ratio of their weights tells you how heavy the gas is — and Avogadro's Law turns that ratio into a remarkably simple number."

> **Safety Note:** Only trained adults handle pressurized gas cylinders; Leo and Mr. Carter watched from a safe distance. Students should never touch gas cylinders or attempt gas experiments at home. Throughout this topic, every gas calculation is **strictly paper-based**. Toxic gases mentioned here (H₂S, SO₂, Cl₂, NO₂, F₂, NH₃, HCl) are **for reading and calculation only**.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Relative density** | How many times heavier a given volume of a gas is compared to an **equal volume** of another reference gas (at the same T and P) | How many times heavier a gas is than hydrogen |
| **Vapour density (VD)** | The relative density of a gas when the reference gas is **hydrogen** | VD of CO₂ = 22 |
| **Same T and P** | Both gases are measured at the exact same temperature and pressure — required for a fair comparison | Both balloons at the same fairground in the same weather |
| **Average molar mass (of air)** | The weighted average mass of 1 mole of a gas mixture, based on the proportions of its component gases | Air: roughly 29 g per mol |
| **Heavier / lighter than air** | Whether a gas's molar mass M is greater than or less than air's average M (about 29) | CO₂ is heavier; CH₄ is lighter |
| **Lift (buoyancy)** | The difference between the weight of the displaced air and the weight of the gas inside the balloon | A helium balloon experiences upward lift |

## Part A: Why a New Measure? Weighing Against Hydrogen

In Class 9, you calculated the density of a gas at STP as **M ÷ 22.4** (in g per L). However, that density changes whenever temperature or pressure changes. Chemists often want a single number that belongs to the gas **itself** and does not change with temperature or pressure. The trick is to **weigh equal volumes of two gases under the exact same conditions**.

Which gas should we use as our baseline standard? We choose the **lightest** gas of all — **hydrogen (H₂)**. Then:

**Vapour density (VD) = (mass of a certain volume of gas) ÷ (mass of an equal volume of hydrogen at the same T and P)**

Because both masses are measured in the same unit (grams), the units cancel out and VD is a **pure, unitless number** — it simply tells you "how many times heavier." (The word "vapour" is historical: VD applies to any gas or vapour. In modern terminology, it is also called the **relative density** of a gas with respect to hydrogen.)

What is the vapour density of hydrogen itself? Comparing hydrogen with an equal volume of hydrogen gives 1 ÷ 1: **VD of H₂ = 1**.

```
           1 litre of Hydrogen                 1 litre of Gas X
           (same T and P)                      (same T and P)

           +-----------+                       +-----------+
           :           :                       :           :
           :   1 L     :                       :   1 L     :
           :   H₂      :                       :   gas X   :
           :           :                       :           :
           +-----------+                       +-----------+
           mass = w₁                           mass = w₂

                  VD (gas X) = w₂ ÷ w₁
```

> **Figure 2.1** – What VD means: equal volumes at the same T and P, one filled with hydrogen and the other with gas X. Divide the mass of gas X by the mass of hydrogen. That ratio is the vapour density of gas X.

### Example 1: Finding VD by Weighing a Flask

**Question:** On paper, an evacuated flask is filled first with hydrogen and then with an unknown gas X at the same T and P. The hydrogen inside weighs **0.5 g**, whereas gas X weighs **8.5 g**. Find the VD of gas X and its molecular mass (using the formula from Part B).

**Solution:**
- VD = 8.5 ÷ 0.5 = **17**. (One litre of X is 17 times as heavy as one litre of hydrogen.)
- Using the Part B formula M = 2 × VD, we get M = 2 × 17 = **34 g per mol**.
- **Check:** M = 34 matches H₂S (M = 2 × 1 + 32 = 34). Gas X could be H₂S (a poisonous gas with a rotten-egg smell: paper-only). 17 × 0.5 = 8.5 g ✅

## Part B: VD = M ÷ 2: A Direct Result of Avogadro's Law

Now watch the magic of Avogadro's Law. Suppose our chosen volume, at a given T and P, contains ***N* molecules**. By Avogadro's Law, the equal volume of hydrogen contains *N* molecules, and the equal volume of gas X also contains ***N* molecules**.

- Mass of the hydrogen volume = *N* × (mass of 1 H₂ molecule).
- Mass of the gas X volume = *N* × (mass of 1 X molecule).

When we take the ratio, ***N* cancels out**:

VD = (*N* × mass of 1 X molecule) ÷ (*N* × mass of 1 H₂ molecule) = (mass of 1 X molecule) ÷ (mass of 1 H₂ molecule)

Since the mass of one molecule (in u) is its molecular mass M (Class 9, [atoms-and-molecules-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/atoms-and-molecules/atoms-and-molecules-part4.md)), and the molecular mass of H₂ is 2 × 1 = 2, we get:

**VD = M(gas) ÷ M(H₂) = M ÷ 2**, which rearranges to **M = 2 × VD**

In other words, the vapour density of any gas is simply **half** its molecular mass! Where did the "2" come from? **Because a hydrogen molecule is diatomic (H₂)**, so its molecular mass is 2, not 1 — the very same diatomic insight Avogadro introduced in Topic 1.

```
    Box 1: Hydrogen                       Box 2: Any gas (here CO₂)
    (equal volume, T, P)                  (equal volume, T, P)

    +------------------------+            +------------------------+
    : H-H      H-H           :            : O=C=O    O=C=O         :
    :                        :            :                        :
    : H-H   H-H              :            : O=C=O  O=C=O           :
    :        H-H             :            :       O=C=O            :
    :                        :            :                        :
    : H-H                    :            : O=C=O                  :
    +------------------------+            +------------------------+
    6 molecules                           6 molecules
    each has M = 2                        each has M = 44
    box mass: 6 x 2 = 12 u                box mass: 6 x 44 = 264 u
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Two equal-volume boxes at the same T and P: both contain 6 molecules (Avogadro's Law). The ratio of the box weights depends only on the weight of a single molecule: 264 ÷ 12 = 22, so the VD of CO₂ is 22 = 44 ÷ 2.

**An Alternative Check from Class 9:** At STP, gas density = M ÷ 22.4 g per L. The density of hydrogen is 2 ÷ 22.4, and the density of our gas is M ÷ 22.4. Taking the ratio gives (M ÷ 22.4) ÷ (2 ÷ 22.4) = **M ÷ 2** ✅. Both routes lead to the exact same formula!

### Example 2: VD Gives Molecular Mass, Not the Identity

**Question:** A gas has a vapour density of 23. What is its molecular mass? Can we be 100% certain which gas it is from VD alone?

**Solution:**
- M = 2 × VD = 2 × 23 = **46 g per mol**.
- NO₂ has M = 14 + 2 × 16 = 46 (a reddish-brown, poisonous gas: paper-only).
- However, ethanol vapour (C₂H₅OH) also has M = 2 × 12 + 6 × 1 + 16 = 46! Both have a VD of 23.
- **Takeaway:** VD tells us the **molecular mass M**, not the **name** of the gas. To identify the gas, we also need clues like colour, odour, or elemental composition (in Topic 3, we will see how to find formulas).
- **Check:** 46 ÷ 2 = 23 ✅

## Part C: Vapour Densities of Common Gases

Finding VD is as simple as dividing M by 2. A few quick examples: CH₄ has M = 12 + 4 = 16, so VD = 16 ÷ 2 = **8**. HCl has M = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5, so VD = 36.5 ÷ 2 = **18.25**. Cl₂ has M = 2 × 35.5 = 71, so VD = 71 ÷ 2 = **35.5**. (Cl₂ is poisonous: paper-only.)

| Gas | Formula | M (g per mol) | VD = M ÷ 2 | Compared to Air (VD 14.5) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H₂ | 2 | 1 | Much lighter |
| Helium | He | 4 | 2 | Much lighter |
| Methane | CH₄ | 16 | 8 | Lighter |
| Ammonia | NH₃ | 17 | 8.5 | Lighter |
| Nitrogen | N₂ | 28 | 14 | Almost equal, slightly lighter |
| Oxygen | O₂ | 32 | 16 | Slightly heavier |
| Hydrogen chloride | HCl | 36.5 | 18.25 | Heavier |
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | 44 | 22 | Heavier |
| Sulphur dioxide | SO₂ | 64 | 32 | Much heavier |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | 71 | 35.5 | Much heavier |

Notice that helium is **monoatomic** (1 atom per molecule), so its M is 4, not 8 (as we learned in Topic 1). Also notice that every VD is measured relative to **hydrogen = 1**, making H₂ the smallest entry on the list.

```
    VD Scale (each # is roughly 1 VD unit)   = mark for air

    H₂            # 1
    He            ## 2
    CH₄           ######## 8
    NH₃           ######### 8.5
    N₂            ############## 14
    air (average) =============== 14.5
    O₂            ################ 16
    HCl           ################## 18.25
    CO₂           ###################### 22
    SO₂           ################################ 32
    Cl₂           #################################### 35.5
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Vapour densities of common gases plotted on a single scale. Gases above the air benchmark (14.5) are lighter than air and rise; gases below it are heavier than air and sink. Bar lengths are rounded to the nearest unit.

## Part D: How Heavy Is Air? Comparing Gases with Air

Air is not a single pure compound — it is a **mixture** of gases: roughly **78% nitrogen, 21% oxygen, and 1% argon** by volume. Because **equal volumes = equal molecules (Avogadro's Law)**, volume percentages are also **molecule (or mole) percentages**. That makes finding the average molar mass of air straightforward: multiply each gas's M by its percentage and add them up.

### Example 3: Average Molar Mass and VD of Air

**Question:** In a sample of 100 air molecules, there are 78 N₂, 21 O₂, and 1 Ar (M = 40). Find the average molar mass M and the vapour density of air.

**Solution:**
- Total mass of 100 molecules (in u) = 78 × 28 + 21 × 32 + 1 × 40 = 2184 + 672 + 40 = **2896 u**.
- Average M = 2896 ÷ 100 = **28.96**, or **approximately 29**.
- VD of air = 28.96 ÷ 2 = 14.48, or **approximately 14.5**.
- **Check:** 78 + 21 + 1 = 100 molecules ✅ and 29 ÷ 2 = 14.5 ✅

(Real air also contains trace amounts of carbon dioxide and water vapour, but the average molar mass still rounds to 29.)

**Rule (Rising or Sinking in Air):** If a gas has a VD **less than 14.5** (or M **less than 29**), it is **lighter than air** and will **rise**; if its VD is **greater than 14.5** (or M **greater than 29**), it is **heavier than air** and will **sink**. (Always compare VD with 14.5 and M with 29 — **never mix the two scales**, as we will see in Common Pitfalls.)

### Example 4: How Many Times Heavier or Lighter Than Air?

**Question:** Butane (C₄H₁₀, used in LPG cylinders) and methane (CH₄, the main gas in piped natural gas): how many times heavier or lighter than air (M = 29) is each gas?

**Solution:**
- Butane: M = 4 × 12 + 10 = 58. Compared to air: 58 ÷ 29 = **2 times as heavy as air**.
- Methane: M = 16. Compared to air: 16 ÷ 29 = 0.55, so it is **about half (0.55 times) as heavy as air** (lighter).
- **Check (using VD):** Butane VD = 58 ÷ 2 = 29, and 29 ÷ 14.5 = 2 ✅. Methane VD = 8, and 8 ÷ 14.5 = 0.55 ✅.

## Part E: Real-World Applications of Vapour Density

### 1. Balloons: Why Some Float and Others Sink

Now we can explain Leo's fairground mystery! The gas from the cylinder is **helium (VD = 2)**, which is much lighter than air (VD = 14.5), so the surrounding air pushes the helium balloon **upwards**. The gas from the hand pump is **ordinary air (VD = 14.5)** — the exact same density as the air outside. It gets zero upward lift, and because the rubber skin and string have weight, the air-filled balloon **droops down** from the pole.

Hydrogen is even lighter (VD = 1), but it is **highly flammable**, so carnival and party balloons are filled with non-flammable **helium** by trained operators. (Giant hot-air balloons work on a different principle: they **heat ordinary air** so it expands and becomes less dense than the cooler air outside, rather than using a different gas with a lower VD.)

### Example 5: Which Lifts More — Helium or Hydrogen?

**Question:** On paper (at STP-like conditions), compare two balloons of equal volume — one filled with helium, the other with hydrogen. What percentage of hydrogen's lifting power does helium provide? And if a rubber balloon plus its string weighs 4 g, what minimum volume of helium is needed to make it float?

**Solution:**
- 1 mole (22.4 L) of air weighs 29 g. 1 mole of helium weighs 4 g, giving an upward lift of 29 − 4 = **25 g per 22.4 L**. Hydrogen weighs 2 g, giving a lift of 29 − 2 = **27 g per 22.4 L**.
- Helium lift ÷ hydrogen lift = 25 ÷ 27 = 0.926, or **about 93%**. Helium gives 93% of hydrogen's lift without any fire hazard!
- Helium lift per litre = 25 ÷ 22.4 = **1.12 g per L** (approx.).
- Minimum volume to lift 4 g = 4 ÷ 1.116 = **3.58 L**, or at least **about 3.6 L** of helium.
- **Check:** 3.58 × 1.116 = 4.0 g ✅

(A sunny fairground is warmer than STP, so actual numbers vary slightly, but this paper calculation shows the exact principle.)

### 2. Carbon Dioxide Fire Extinguishers

CO₂ has a VD of 22 — **about 1.5 times as heavy as air**. When sprayed from a CO₂ fire extinguisher, the heavy gas **settles over the flames like a blanket**, pushing air away and cutting off the oxygen supply so the fire goes out. (Operating a fire extinguisher is for trained adults: if a fire breaks out, alert an adult immediately, evacuate the building, and call emergency services.)

### 3. Gas Leaks: Natural Gas Rises, LPG Sinks (Description Only)

**Piped natural gas** consists mainly of **methane (VD = 8)**: because it is lighter than air, leaked natural gas rises and collects **near the ceiling**. **LPG** (liquefied petroleum gas) contains propane and butane (M = 44 to 58, so VD = 22 to 29): because it is **heavier than air**, leaked LPG sinks and pools **near the floor and in low drains**. That is why natural-gas leak detectors are mounted **high near the ceiling**, whereas LPG leak detectors are mounted **low near the floor**.

```
      NATURAL GAS (CH₄, VD 8)             LPG (propane-butane, VD 22 to 29)

    +----------------------------+     +----------------------------+
    :  [alarm]  ^  ^  ^  ^  ^    :     :                            :
    :           ^  ^  ^  ^       :     :                            :
    :              ^  ^  ^       :     :                            :
    :              ^  ^          :     :                            :
    :            [stove]         :     :            [stove]         :
    :                            :     :           v  v  v  v       :
    :                            :     :        v  v  v  v  v  v    :
    :                            :     :  [alarm]  v  v  v  v  v    :
    +----------------------------+     +----------------------------+
        lighter: rises to ceiling          heavier: sinks to floor
```

> **Figure 2.4** – Gas leak behaviour (descriptive diagram only): methane (lighter than air) rises toward the ceiling, so its alarm is placed high; LPG (heavier than air) sinks toward the floor, so its alarm is placed low. Both stoves sit at counter height where the leak starts.

**Safety Rule:** If you ever smell cooking gas at home, **open windows and doors immediately, do not touch any electrical switches, turn off the gas valve/regulator, and call the gas company from outside**. Adults handle the valve and call; a student's job is to tell an adult right away. We will revisit cooking-gas stoichiometry in Topic 5.

## Part F: From VD Toward the Chemical Formula: First Step

Vapour density gives us the molecular mass M. If we also know **which elements** make up the gas, we can often work out **how many atoms** are in each molecule.

### Example 6: An Oxide of Sulphur, SOₓ

**Question:** A gaseous oxide of sulphur has the formula SOₓ (where *x* is the number of oxygen atoms) and a vapour density of 32. Find *x*. (S = 32, O = 16.)

**Solution:**
- M = 2 × 32 = **64**.
- 32 + 16*x* = 64 → 16*x* = 32 → ***x* = 2**. The gas is **SO₂** (a pungent, toxic gas smelled when a matchstick is struck: paper-only).
- **Check:** For SO₂, M = 32 + 2 × 16 = 64, and 64 ÷ 2 = 32 ✅

This quick method works nicely for simple two-element formulas. In **Topic 3**, we will learn the complete, systematic method: first finding the **empirical formula** (the simplest whole-number atom ratio) from percentage composition, and then using VD or M to find the true **molecular formula**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Thinking VD and molecular mass M are the same number.**
Suppose someone claims the VD of methane is 16. Let's check: at STP, 22.4 L of hydrogen weighs 2 g and 22.4 L of methane weighs 16 g. Their ratio is 16 ÷ 2 = **8**, not 16! One litre of methane is **8 times** as heavy as one litre of hydrogen. VD is **half of M** because we compare against diatomic H₂ (M = 2), not a single H atom (mass = 1).

**Mistake 2: Comparing a gas's VD (14.5 scale) against air's molar mass M (29 scale).**
A student reasons: "CO₂ has a VD of 22, and air has M = 29; since 22 < 29, CO₂ must be lighter than air." Wrong! 22 is a **VD** (on the scale where H₂ = 1), whereas 29 is a **molar mass M**. You cannot mix two different scales! **Compare VD with VD:** 22 > 14.5 (air's VD), so CO₂ is heavier than air. Or **compare M with M:** 44 > 29, so CO₂ is heavier than air. Both scales agree when used consistently.

**Mistake 3: Weighing equal volumes at different temperatures or pressures.**
Suppose you weigh 22.4 L of H₂ at 273 K (1 mol = **2 g**), and then weigh 22.4 L of CO₂ at **546 K** (at the same pressure). Because the CO₂ is twice as hot, the gas expands so that the flask holds only 0.5 mol, weighing 0.5 × 44 = **22 g**. Taking the ratio gives 22 ÷ 2 = **11**, whereas the true VD of CO₂ is **22**! The wrong answer came from weighing the gases at **two different temperatures**. To find VD, **both gases must be at the same T and P** (the exact condition required for Avogadro's Law in Topic 1).

## Quick Recap

- **Vapour density (VD)** = (mass of a given volume of gas) ÷ (mass of an equal volume of hydrogen at the same T and P). It is a unitless number.
- By Avogadro's Law: **VD = M ÷ 2** and **M = 2 × VD** (since M of H₂ = 2).
- VD gives the molecular mass M, not the specific identity of the gas (both NO₂ and ethanol vapour have VD = 23).
- Air has an average M of about **29** and a VD of about **14.5**: gases with VD < 14.5 (M < 29) **rise** in air; gases with VD > 14.5 (M > 29) **sink**.
- Lighter than air: H₂, He, CH₄, NH₃, N₂ (slightly). Heavier than air: O₂ (slightly), HCl, CO₂, SO₂, Cl₂.
- Applications: helium balloons (~93% of hydrogen's lift without flammability), CO₂ fire extinguishers, gas-leak detector placement (methane high, LPG low), and finding molecular formulas.
- Both gases must be compared at **the same temperature and pressure**.

## Practice Questions

All gas problems below are **strictly paper calculations**. Never attempt to prepare, collect, or burn gases at home.

1. Find the vapour density of (a) ethyne, C₂H₂, (b) neon, Ne (M = 20) and (c) ethane, C₂H₆. Which of these are lighter than air and which is heavier? (Air has VD about 14.5.)
2. A gas has a vapour density of 24. Find its molecular mass. If the gas is made only of oxygen atoms (O = 16), how many atoms are there in one molecule? What is its formula?
3. At the same temperature and pressure, a flask holds 0.1 g of hydrogen. The same flask holds 1.9 g of an unknown gas. Find the VD and the molecular mass of the gas. If the gas is made only of fluorine atoms (F = 19), find the formula. (Fluorine is poisonous: paper work only.)
4. A gas mixture contains 9 mol of nitrogen and 1 mol of helium. Find its average molar mass and its VD. Is the mixture lighter or heavier than air?
5. A gas has the formula CₓH₄ and a VD of 20. Find x and write the formula. (C = 12, H = 1.)
6. A large decoration balloon holds 44.8 L of helium (take STP-like conditions on paper). The rubber and thread together weigh 36 g. Find the mass of air displaced, the mass of helium, and the net lift. Will the balloon rise?
7. How many times heavier or lighter than air (M = 29) are (a) CO₂, (b) SO₂, (c) NH₃?
8. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) The VD of oxygen (O₂) is 32. (b) A gas with VD 10 rises in air. (c) Two gases with the same VD have the same molecular mass. (d) The VD of hydrogen (H₂) is 2. (e) You can find a gas's VD by weighing equal volumes at different temperatures.
9. Puzzle: four balloons A, B, C and D are filled one each with the gases helium, methane, carbon dioxide and chlorine, at the same T and P. The following facts are known. Air has VD about 14.5. Balloons A and B rise, balloons C and D sink. The gas in B is 4 times as heavy as the gas in A for equal volumes. The gas in C is lighter than the gas in D for equal volumes. Identify the gas in each balloon, with the VD of each. (Chlorine is poisonous: paper work only.)
10. Natural gas (mostly methane) alarms are usually placed high on the wall, while LPG alarms are placed low. Using VD values, explain why. (Describe only: never test with a real gas.)
11. Explain in three or four lines, using Avogadro's law, why VD = M ÷ 2 and not M.
12. At the same T and P, a flask holds 2 g of hydrogen. Find the mass of nitrous oxide, N₂O, in the same flask. (N = 14, O = 16.)
13. A balloon-seller fills one balloon from a pump (air) and one with helium. Using VD, explain why the pump balloon does not rise even if its rubber is imagined to have no weight.

### Answers

1. (a) C₂H₂: M = 2 × 12 + 2 = 26, VD = 26 ÷ 2 = **13**. (b) Ne: M = 20, VD = **10** (neon is a monoatomic gas). (c) C₂H₆: M = 24 + 6 = 30, VD = **15**. Compared to air (VD 14.5): ethyne (13) and neon (10) are **lighter than air**; ethane (15) is **slightly heavier than air** (30 > 29). Check: 26 ÷ 29 = 0.90, 20 ÷ 29 = 0.69, 30 ÷ 29 = 1.03 ✅
2. M = 2 × 24 = **48**. Number of O atoms = 48 ÷ 16 = **3 atoms**, so the formula is **O₃** (ozone). Check: 3 × 16 = 48, 48 ÷ 2 = 24 ✅
3. VD = 1.9 ÷ 0.1 = **19**. M = 2 × 19 = **38**. Number of F atoms = 38 ÷ 19 = 2 atoms, so the formula is **F₂**. Check: 2 × 19 = 38 ✅
4. Average M = (9 × 28 + 1 × 4) ÷ 10 = (252 + 4) ÷ 10 = **25.6**. VD = 25.6 ÷ 2 = **12.8**. Since 12.8 < 14.5, the mixture is **lighter than air** (by 3.4 g per mole). Check: 9 + 1 = 10 mol ✅
5. M = 2 × 20 = 40. 12*x* + 4 = 40 → 12*x* = 36 → ***x* = 3**. Formula: **C₃H₄**. Check: 3 × 12 + 4 = 40, 40 ÷ 2 = 20 ✅
6. 44.8 L = 44.8 ÷ 22.4 = 2 mol. Mass of displaced air = 2 × 29 = **58 g**. Mass of helium = 2 × 4 = **8 g**. Upward lift = 58 − 8 = **50 g**. Subtracting the 36 g rubber and string gives a net lift of 50 − 36 = **14 g upward**. The balloon **will rise**. Check: 44.8 × 25 ÷ 22.4 = 50 g ✅
7. (a) CO₂: 44 ÷ 29 = **1.52 times as heavy** as air. (b) SO₂: 64 ÷ 29 = **2.21 times as heavy** as air. (c) NH₃: 17 ÷ 29 = **0.59 times as heavy** (lighter than air). Check (using VD): 22 ÷ 14.5 = 1.52 ✅, 32 ÷ 14.5 = 2.21 ✅, 8.5 ÷ 14.5 = 0.59 ✅
8. (a) **False** ❌: the VD of O₂ is 16 (half of M = 32). (b) **True** ✅: 10 < 14.5. (c) **True** ✅: since VD = M ÷ 2, equal VD means equal molecular mass M (though the compounds can be different, like NO₂ and ethanol vapour). (d) **False** ❌: the VD of H₂ is 1 (comparing H₂ with itself gives 1). (e) **False** ❌: both volumes must be weighed at the same T and P, otherwise the number of molecules in the two volumes will differ.
9. The two gases lighter than air (VD < 14.5) are helium (2) and methane (8): these are A and B. The two heavier gases are CO₂ (22) and Cl₂ (35.5): these are C and D. Since gas B is 4 times as heavy as gas A (8 = 4 × 2), **A = helium (VD 2)** and **B = methane (VD 8)**. Since C is lighter than D, **C = CO₂ (VD 22)** and **D = Cl₂ (VD 35.5)**. Check: 8 ÷ 2 = 4 ✅, 22 < 35.5 ✅
10. Methane has VD = 8 (< 14.5): being lighter than air, leaked natural gas rises toward the ceiling, so its detector is placed **high**. LPG (propane/butane) has VD ≈ 22 to 29 (> 14.5): being heavier than air, leaked LPG sinks toward the floor, so its detector is placed **low**. (Over time, gases also diffuse and mix with air, which is why detectors are placed where the gas concentrates first.)
11. By Avogadro's Law, equal volumes of gases at the same T and P contain the same number of molecules (*N*). The mass of the gas is *N* × M(gas) and the mass of hydrogen is *N* × M(H₂), so *N* cancels out: VD = M(gas) ÷ M(H₂). Because hydrogen is a **diatomic** molecule (H₂), M(H₂) = 2, giving VD = M ÷ 2.
12. 2 g of H₂ = 1 mol. At the same T and P, the same flask holds **1 mol** of N₂O. Since M of N₂O = 2 × 14 + 16 = 44, its mass is **44 g**. Check (via VD): VD = 44 ÷ 2 = 22, and 22 × 2 g = 44 g ✅
13. Air from the pump has VD = 14.5, identical to the surrounding air outside: there is zero difference between the weight of the displaced air and the air inside the balloon, so lift = 0. Helium has VD = 2 (much lighter than air), generating upward lift. Once you include the weight of the rubber skin, the air-filled balloon droops downward.
