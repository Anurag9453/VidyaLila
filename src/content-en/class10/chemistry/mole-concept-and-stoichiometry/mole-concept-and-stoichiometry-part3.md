# Topic 3: Empirical and Molecular Formulas

In Topic 2, we saw how vapour density gives us the **molecular mass** of a gas (M = 2 × VD). But an important question remained: if chemists isolate an unknown gas or liquid in the lab, how do they figure out its **chemical formula**? Molecular mass is just a single number. To write the formula, we also need to know **which elements** are present and in **what ratio**. In this topic, we study two types of formulas: one that shows the **simplest whole-number ratio** of atoms (the empirical formula) and one that shows the **actual number** of atoms in one molecule (the molecular formula). We will also master a 5-step method that takes us from percentage composition to the final formula. In Class 9, we went in the forward direction — calculating percentage composition from a known formula ([atoms-and-molecules-part7.md](../../../class9/chemistry/atoms-and-molecules/atoms-and-molecules-part7.md)). Now we make the reverse journey: from percentages back to the formula!

## The Tile Setter's Repeating Pattern

Ms. Alvarez was having new mosaic tiles laid in her living room. Her niece Sofia was visiting for the weekend and watched the tile setter at work. Each large square tile was decorated with a repeating geometric motif: one blue diamond paired with two white dots. The tile setter explained that this basic motif was repeated several times across each large tile.

Sofia counted the shapes on a single tile: "Aunt Elena, this tile has 3 blue diamonds and 6 white dots. But the catalogue says the pattern design is '1 blue, 2 white'. So which one is the real pattern: '3 blue and 6 white', or '1 blue and 2 white'?"

Ms. Alvarez smiled. "Both are right — they just answer two different questions. The smallest **repeating unit** is '1 blue, 2 white': that tells you the simplest **ratio**. But the complete tile in your hand has an **actual count** of '3 blue, 6 white', because the basic unit repeats 3 times on one tile." She added: "Chemical formulas work the exact same way. One formula gives the simplest ratio of atoms, and the other gives the actual count in a single molecule. Let's give both of them their proper chemistry names."

```
    Smallest repeating unit (1 unit)   Full tile (3 repeating units)

         B  w  w                        B  w  w    B  w  w    B  w  w

    B = blue diamond, w = white dot
    ratio B : w = 1 : 2                B : w = 3 : 6 = 1 : 2
```

> **Figure 3.1** – A single repeating motif (1 B, 2 w) versus the full tile (3 B, 6 w). Both share the exact same B : w = 1 : 2 ratio, but their total counts differ. The simplest ratio is like an empirical formula; the actual count on the full tile is like a molecular formula.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical formula** | The simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound | CH₂O for glucose |
| **Molecular formula** | The actual number of atoms of each element present in one molecule of a compound | C₆H₁₂O₆ for glucose |
| **Empirical formula mass** | The sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in the empirical formula | For CH₂O: 12 + 2 + 16 = 30 |
| **n (multiplier)** | The whole number by which the empirical formula subscripts are multiplied to get the molecular formula | n = 6 for glucose |
| **Whole-number ratio** | A ratio in which every term is a whole number, with no fractions or decimals | 2 : 3, not 1 : 1.5 |
| **Formula unit** | The simplest ratio unit of an ionic compound (which forms a crystal lattice rather than discrete molecules) | NaCl, Na₂SO₄ |

## Part A: Two Formulas for the Same Compound

The **empirical formula** tells us the **simplest whole-number ratio** of the atoms in a compound. The **molecular formula** tells us **how many atoms of each element are actually present in a single molecule**.

**Molecular formula = (Empirical formula) × *n***, where ***n* = Molecular mass ÷ Empirical formula mass**.

Let's compare a few familiar compounds:

| Compound | Molecular Formula | Empirical Formula | n |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | CH₃ | 2 |
| Benzene | C₆H₆ | CH | 6 |
| Hydrogen peroxide | H₂O₂ | HO | 2 |
| Glucose | C₆H₁₂O₆ | CH₂O | 6 |
| Acetic acid (the sour compound in vinegar) | C₂H₄O₂ | CH₂O | 2 |
| Water | H₂O | H₂O | 1 |

Notice something striking: glucose and acetic acid share **the exact same empirical formula** (CH₂O), yet they are **completely different substances**! That is why an empirical formula alone cannot identify a compound — we also need its molecular mass. (Benzene and concentrated hydrogen peroxide are mentioned strictly for reading: benzene is toxic, and concentrated H₂O₂ is corrosive. Only mild, diluted pharmacy-grade H₂O₂ is used at home, and only with adult supervision.)

```
    HO  (empirical)                    H₂O₂  (molecular)

      H - O                              H - O - O - H

    1 H to 1 O ratio                   2 H and 2 O: the actual molecule
    (1 repeating unit)                 (2 times the HO unit)
```

> **Figure 3.2** – A molecule of H₂O₂ contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms (4 atoms in total). Because the simplest ratio is 1 : 1, its empirical formula is HO. The actual molecule is twice the HO unit.

**When the empirical and molecular formulas are identical:** Whenever the actual atom counts are already in their lowest whole-number ratio — such as H₂O (2 : 1), CO₂ (1 : 2), or NH₃ (1 : 3) — the multiplier is *n* = 1, so both formulas are the same.

## Part B: From Percentage Composition to Empirical Formula: 5 Steps

Suppose a lab analysis reports: "This compound contains 80% carbon and 20% hydrogen by mass." How do we find its formula? Remember: percentages give the ratio by **mass**, whereas a chemical formula gives the ratio by **number of atoms**. To convert mass into atom counts (moles), we **divide by each element's atomic mass** (our Class 9 mole formula: moles = mass ÷ atomic mass).

```
    Step 1  Treat percentages as grams (in a 100 g sample)
               :
               v
    Step 2  Grams of each element ÷ atomic mass = moles
               :
               v
    Step 3  Divide all mole values by the smallest mole value
               :
               v
    Step 4  If the ratio isn't all whole numbers, multiply by 2, 3, or 4
               :
               v
    Step 5  Write the whole numbers as subscripts: empirical formula
```

> **Figure 3.3** – The 5-step roadmap to the empirical formula. In Step 1, we imagine a 100 g sample of the compound because in 100 g, "per cent" and "grams" are the exact same number.

### Example 1. A Hydrocarbon: C 80%, H 20%

- Step 1: In 100 g, C = 80 g, H = 20 g.
- Step 2: C = 80 ÷ 12 = 6.67 mol. H = 20 ÷ 1 = 20 mol.
- Step 3: Divide by the smaller number (6.67): C = 6.67 ÷ 6.67 = 1. H = 20 ÷ 6.67 = 3.
- Step 4: Both are already whole numbers; no multiplication needed.
- Step 5: **Empirical formula = CH₃**.

**Check:** In CH₃, % C = 12 ÷ 15 × 100 = 80%, and % H = 3 ÷ 15 × 100 = 20% ✅.

Now suppose this compound is a gas with a vapour density of 15 (Topic 2). Then M = 2 × 15 = **30**. The empirical formula mass of CH₃ is 12 + 3 = 15. Thus *n* = 30 ÷ 15 = **2**, and the molecular formula is CH₃ × 2 = **C₂H₆** (ethane).
**Check:** M of C₂H₆ = 24 + 6 = 30 ✅ and 30 ÷ 2 = VD 15 ✅. (Ethane is a flammable gas: paper-only.)

### Example 2. Another Hydrocarbon: C 92.3%, H 7.7%

- Step 2: C = 92.3 ÷ 12 = 7.69 mol. H = 7.7 ÷ 1 = 7.7 mol.
- Step 3: Divide by 7.69: C = 1, H = 7.7 ÷ 7.69 = 1.00 (rounds to 1).
- Step 5: **Empirical formula = CH**.

If its molecular mass is 78: the empirical formula mass of CH is 13, so *n* = 78 ÷ 13 = **6**. The molecular formula is **C₆H₆** (benzene).
**Check:** M of C₆H₆ = 72 + 6 = 78 ✅. % C = 72 ÷ 78 × 100 = 92.3% ✅.

### Example 3. The Whole-Number Multiplier Trick: Fe 70%, O 30%

- Step 2: Fe = 70 ÷ 56 = 1.25 mol. O = 30 ÷ 16 = 1.875 mol.
- Step 3: Divide by 1.25: Fe = 1, O = 1.875 ÷ 1.25 = **1.5**.
- Step 4: 1 : 1.5 is not a whole-number ratio. Multiply both numbers by **2**: 2 : 3.
- Step 5: **Fe₂O₃**.

**Check:** Formula mass of Fe₂O₃ = 2 × 56 + 3 × 16 = 160. % Fe = 112 ÷ 160 × 100 = 70% ✅.

Because this compound is ionic (metal + oxygen), **Fe₂O₃** is a **formula unit**: it represents the simplest ion ratio in the crystal, and there is no separate "molecular formula" (see Part D).

### Example 4. Another Multiplier Trick: Fe 72.4%, O 27.6%

- Step 2: Fe = 72.4 ÷ 56 = 1.293 mol. O = 27.6 ÷ 16 = 1.725 mol.
- Step 3: Fe = 1, O = 1.725 ÷ 1.293 = **1.33**.
- Step 4: Since 1.33 = 4/3, multiply both numbers by **3**: Fe = 3, O = 4.
- Step 5: **Fe₃O₄**.

**Check:** Formula mass of Fe₃O₄ = 168 + 64 = 232. % Fe = 168 ÷ 232 × 100 = 72.4% ✅.

**Which multiplier clears which decimal?** Watch the decimal part of the ratio: if it ends in **.5** (like 0.5 or 1.5), multiply by **2**; if it ends in **.33** or **.67** (like 1.33), multiply by **3**; if it ends in **.25** or **.75** (like 1.25), multiply by **4**. Keep these three fractions in mind!

### Example 5. Three Elements: Na 32.4%, S 22.5%, O 45.1%

- Step 2: Na = 32.4 ÷ 23 = 1.409. S = 22.5 ÷ 32 = 0.703. O = 45.1 ÷ 16 = 2.819.
- Step 3: Divide by the smallest (0.703): Na = 2.00, S = 1, O = 4.01 (rounds to 4).
- Step 5: **Na₂SO₄**.

**Check:** Formula mass of Na₂SO₄ = 46 + 32 + 64 = 142. % Na = 46 ÷ 142 × 100 = 32.4% ✅, % S = 32 ÷ 142 × 100 = 22.5% ✅, % O = 64 ÷ 142 × 100 = 45.1% ✅.

### Example 6. When Masses in Grams Are Given Directly (Skip Step 1)

**Question:** When 1.6 g of sulphur burns, it combines with 2.4 g of oxygen to form an oxide. Find its empirical formula. (This oxide is toxic: paper-only.)

- S = 1.6 ÷ 32 = 0.05 mol. O = 2.4 ÷ 16 = 0.15 mol.
- Divide by 0.05: S = 1, O = 3.
- **Empirical formula = SO₃**.

**Check:** Formula mass of SO₃ = 32 + 48 = 80. % S = 32 ÷ 80 × 100 = 40%; from the given masses, 1.6 ÷ (1.6 + 2.4) × 100 = 40% ✅.

## Part C: Finding the Molecular Formula: Empirical Formula First, Then *n*

Once you have the empirical formula, finding the molecular formula requires one extra piece of data: the **molecular mass**. Either the molecular mass is given directly, or — for a gas — you calculate it from the **vapour density** (M = 2 × VD, Topic 2).

1. Calculate the **empirical formula mass**.
2. Find ***n* = Molecular mass ÷ Empirical formula mass** (this must come out to a whole number).
3. Multiply every subscript in the empirical formula by *n*.

### Example 7. Same CH₂O Empirical Formula, Two Different Compounds

- **Glucose:** M = 180. Empirical formula mass of CH₂O = 12 + 2 + 16 = 30. *n* = 180 ÷ 30 = **6**. Molecular formula = **C₆H₁₂O₆**.
- **Acetic acid:** M = 60. *n* = 60 ÷ 30 = **2**. Molecular formula = **C₂H₄O₂**.

**Check:** C₆H₁₂O₆ = 72 + 12 + 96 = 180 ✅. C₂H₄O₂ = 24 + 4 + 32 = 60 ✅.

Both compounds share the same empirical formula, but their molecular masses differ — giving different multipliers *n* and completely different molecules. Think back to the mosaic tile: the same basic motif can repeat 2 times on a small tile or 6 times on a large tile.

### Example 8. H₂O₂ from Percentage Composition

A compound contains H = 5.88% and O = 94.12% by mass, and its molecular mass is M = 34.

- H = 5.88 ÷ 1 = 5.88 mol. O = 94.12 ÷ 16 = 5.88 mol. Ratio = 1 : 1. Empirical formula = **HO** (mass = 17).
- *n* = 34 ÷ 17 = **2**. Molecular formula = **H₂O₂**.

**Check:** H₂O₂ = 2 + 32 = 34 ✅. % H = 2 ÷ 34 × 100 = 5.88% ✅.

### Example 9. The Complete Journey: Percentages + Vapour Density

**Question:** A hydrocarbon gas contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass, and its vapour density is 28. Find its molecular formula. (Paper-only: flammable gas.)

- C = 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14. H = 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3. H ÷ C = 14.3 ÷ 7.14 = 2.00. Empirical formula = **CH₂** (mass = 14).
- M = 2 × 28 = 56.
- *n* = 56 ÷ 14 = **4**. Molecular formula = **C₄H₈**.

**Check:** C₄H₈ = 48 + 8 = 56 ✅. % C = 48 ÷ 56 × 100 = 85.7% ✅.

```
    Percent / Grams       Empirical Formula        Molecular Formula
    (5 steps)        -->  (simplest ratio)    -->  (actual atom count)
                                                    ^
                          M or VD (M = 2 x VD)      |
                          n = M / empirical mass ---+
```

> **Figure 3.4** – The complete pathway: use the 5 steps to find the empirical formula from percentages or grams, then use the molecular mass (or M = 2 × VD) to find *n* and scale up to the molecular formula.

## Part D: When Empirical and Molecular Formulas Match — and Ionic Compounds

**When both formulas are identical:** In compounds like H₂O, CO₂, and NH₃, the atom counts in a single molecule are already in their simplest whole-number ratio, so *n* = 1. For example, in water, H : O = 2 : 1, which cannot be reduced to any smaller whole numbers.

**Ionic compounds only have empirical formulas (formula units).** In solid sodium chloride (NaCl), there are no separate, individual "NaCl molecules." Instead, Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions are arranged in a giant three-dimensional crystal lattice where every Na⁺ is surrounded by Cl⁻ ions and vice versa in an equal 1 : 1 ratio. Because the formula NaCl simply states this 1 : 1 ratio, we call it a **formula unit**, and we call 58.5 u its **formula unit mass** rather than "molecular mass" (just as we learned in Class 9).

```
    Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-
    Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+
    Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl-
    Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+ Cl- Na+
```

> **Figure 3.5** – A small 2D slice of the NaCl crystal lattice: 12 Na⁺ ions and 12 Cl⁻ ions alternating with their oppositely charged neighbours. Counting them gives a Na⁺ : Cl⁻ ratio of 1 : 1 — which is what the formula unit NaCl represents. No isolated "NaCl molecule" exists.

## Part E: Going in Reverse: From Formula to Percentage and Empirical Formula

Calculating percentage composition was covered in Class 9, so here is a quick refresher:

- **% Fe in Fe₂O₃:** 112 ÷ 160 × 100 = 70% (the reverse of Example 3).
- **From molecular formula to empirical formula:** Divide all subscripts by their greatest common divisor. For C₆H₁₂O₆, divide 6, 12, and 6 by 6 to get C₁H₂O₁ = **CH₂O**. For C₂H₂, divide by 2 to get **CH**. For C₄H₁₀, divide by 2 to get **C₂H₅**.

Keep this key insight in mind: **several different molecular formulas can share the exact same percentage composition**, because percentages only reflect the relative ratio of elements. That is why we always need the molecular mass to pin down the molecular formula.

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Dividing percentages directly without dividing by atomic masses first.**
In Example 1 (80% C, 20% H), if someone simply takes the ratio 80 : 20 = 4 : 1 and writes C₄H, that is completely wrong! 80 : 20 is the ratio of **masses**, not the ratio of atoms. Because one carbon atom weighs 12 u while one hydrogen atom weighs only 1 u, 80 g of carbon contains far fewer atoms than 20 g of hydrogen. Always divide by atomic masses first: 80 ÷ 12 = 6.67 and 20 ÷ 1 = 20, giving a 1 : 3 atom ratio (CH₃).

**Mistake 2: Rounding off a fraction like 1.33 to 1.**
In Example 4 (72.4% Fe, 27.6% O), Step 3 gave an Fe : O ratio of 1 : 1.33. If a student carelessly rounds 1.33 down to 1 and writes FeO, let's check what happens: the percentage of Fe in FeO is 56 ÷ 72 × 100 = 77.8%, which is way off from the given 72.4%! Multiplying 1 : 1.33 by 3 gives Fe₃O₄ (3 : 4), which matches 72.4% Fe. Never round away clean fractions like .33, .5, or .25 — multiply by 3, 2, or 4 to clear them.

**Mistake 3: Calling NaCl a molecule with a molecular formula.**
If NaCl existed as isolated molecules, Figure 3.5 would show separate Na–Cl pairs floating apart. In reality, every Na⁺ ion is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions (and every Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺) in a continuous 3D crystal lattice, with no single pair partnered off. Therefore, for ionic compounds like NaCl, Na₂SO₄, and Fe₂O₃, the correct term is **formula unit** (and **formula unit mass**).

**Mistake 4: Multiplying only the first subscript by *n* when finding the molecular formula.**
For glucose, the empirical formula is CH₂O and *n* = 6. If a student multiplies only the C subscript by 6 and writes C₆H₂O, let's check its mass: 72 + 2 + 16 = 90, which is only half of the given molecular mass of 180! You must multiply **every** subscript in the empirical formula by *n*: CH₂O × 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆. Always verify the total molecular mass at the end.

## Quick Recap

- **Empirical formula** = simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. **Molecular formula** = actual number of atoms in one molecule.
- **Molecular formula = (Empirical formula) × *n***, where *n* = Molecular mass ÷ Empirical formula mass.
- **The 5 Steps:** (1) Take percentages as grams (per 100 g), (2) divide by atomic masses to get moles, (3) divide by the smallest mole value, (4) multiply by 2, 3, or 4 if needed to get whole numbers, (5) write the empirical formula.
- Fraction multipliers: **.5 → × 2**, **.33 / .67 → × 3**, **.25 / .75 → × 4**.
- From VD to molecular mass: **M = 2 × VD** (Topic 2), then find *n*.
- Ionic compounds (NaCl, Na₂SO₄, Fe₂O₃) only have empirical formulas (**formula units**). In compounds like H₂O, CO₂, and NH₃, the empirical and molecular formulas are identical (*n* = 1).
- Different compounds can share the same empirical formula (e.g., glucose and acetic acid are both CH₂O).

Check: Na₂S₂O₃ = 46 + 64 + 48 = 158. % Na = 46 ÷ 158 × 100 = 29.1% ✅, % S = 64 ÷ 158 × 100 = 40.5% ✅, % O = 48 ÷ 158 × 100 = 30.4% ✅.

(KClO₃ is a strong oxidiser: paper-only.)

8. Empirical formula mass of NH₂ = 14 + 2 = 16. M = 2 × 16 = 32. *n* = 32 ÷ 16 = 2. **Molecular formula: N₂H₄**. Check: N₂H₄ = 28 + 4 = 32 ✅.

10. For NO₂: M = 14 + 32 = 46, so % N = 14 ÷ 46 × 100 = 30.4%. For N₂O₄: M = 28 + 64 = 92, so % N = 28 ÷ 92 × 100 = 30.4%. Both have the exact same percentage composition because both share the same 1 : 2 atom ratio (N : O). Therefore, percentage composition alone can only give the empirical formula; we need the molecular mass (or VD) to find the molecular formula.
11. NaCl is an ionic compound: it consists of a giant 3D crystal lattice of alternating Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions (Figure 3.5), not separate two-atom molecules. NaCl is a **formula unit** showing the 1 : 1 ratio of Na⁺ to Cl⁻ ions, and 58.5 u is its **formula unit mass**, not a molecular mass.
12. Ratio B : w = 5 : 15 = 1 : 3. The smallest repeating pattern is **1 blue diamond and 3 white dots** (BW₃), and the multiplier is ***n* = 5**. The smallest 1 : 3 unit is like the **empirical formula**, while the full tile count (B₅W₁₅) is like the **molecular formula**. Check: 1 × 5 = 5 and 3 × 5 = 15 ✅.
13. (a) **False** ❌: the molecular formula is *n* times the empirical formula with *n* ≥ 1, so it can never have smaller subscripts than the empirical formula (at minimum, it is equal when *n* = 1). (b) **True** ✅. (c) **True** ✅: dividing 2 : 2 by 2 gives 1 : 1 (CH). (d) **False** ❌: NaCl does not form molecules; 58.5 u is its **formula unit mass**. (e) **False** ❌: dividing percentages directly gives a mass ratio, not an atom ratio; you must divide each percentage by its atomic mass first to find moles.

"Aunt Elena, now I can crack the formula of any unknown compound as long as I have its percentages and molecular mass!" Ms. Alvarez patted the smooth mosaic tile and smiled: "Wonderful. And next, you'll see that once we have formulas, a balanced chemical equation becomes a complete **recipe**."

**What's Next?** In Topic 4, we learn to read any balanced chemical equation as a **mole recipe**: how many moles of one substance react with how many moles of another, and how to use that mole ratio to calculate the mass of any product or reactant from a given mass — starting with a story about scaling a cake recipe from 1 cake to 40 cakes!
