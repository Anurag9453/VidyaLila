# Topic 4: An Equation Is a Recipe: From Mass to Mass

In the previous topic, we learned how to determine the **chemical formula** of a compound by counting atoms from percentage composition and vapour density. Now comes the next big question: once we know the formulas, when **two or more substances react with each other**, how do we find the mass of one substance from the known mass of another? In Topic 1, we saw that **gas volumes** react in simple whole-number ratios. In this topic, we extend that recipe idea to **masses** — and the bridge that connects them is our trusted friend from Class 9: the **mole**. By the end of this topic, you will be able to look at any balanced chemical equation and say: "From this many grams of reactant, exactly this many grams of product will form."

---

## Scaling a Bakery Order: From 1 Cake to 40

Mr. Miller's niece Clara was running a bake-sale booth for her school fair, and she had an order for **40 sponge cakes**. Mr. Miller pulled out an old family recipe card: **For 1 cake: 2 eggs, 200 g flour, 100 g sugar, 50 g butter.** Clara immediately jotted down the totals: "Uncle, 40 cakes means multiplying everything by **40**: 80 eggs, 8000 g (8 kg) of flour, 4 kg of sugar, and 2 kg of butter." Mr. Miller smiled: "Spot on. A recipe is a **ratio**, and the number of cakes you want is simply the **scale factor**."

Then he showed her an even older recipe card from her great-grandmother: **"2 eggs, 1 cup of flour, 1 cup of sugar."** Clara looked at it and said: "Here the ratio of flour to sugar is 1 : 1, so their **weights** must be equal too!" Mr. Miller placed an **identical measuring cup** of flour and an **identical measuring cup** of sugar on the kitchen scale. The cup of flour weighed about **100 g**, while the cup of sugar weighed about **150 g**. "See that? By **count of cups**, the ratio is 1 : 1, but by **weight**, the ratio is 100 : 150, or 2 : 3 — because a cup of sugar is heavier than a cup of flour."

Clara's eyes lit up: "Uncle, that's just like chemistry! The numbers in front of formulas in an equation must be **'cups'** (counts of particles), not weights!" Mr. Miller clapped his hands: "That is the entire lesson today. A balanced equation is a **recipe**, and its measuring cup is called the **mole**."

**Try this at home (with an adult, 5 minutes):** Using the exact same measuring cup, weigh (or hold in your hands) one level cup each of rice, flour, and sugar. The cup count is 1 : 1 : 1, yet their weights feel noticeably different: rice and sugar feel heavier, while flour feels lighter.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Stoichiometry** | Calculating the exact amounts of reactants used and products formed in a chemical reaction | Using 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O to find the mass of water formed |
| **Coefficient** | The number written in front of a chemical formula in a balanced equation | The 2 in 2H₂O |
| **Mole ratio** | The ratio of the coefficients of two substances in a balanced equation (the ratio of their moles) | CaCO₃ : CaO = 1 : 1 |
| **Given and asked** | The substance whose amount is provided in the question (*given*) and the one you need to find (*asked*) | Given CaCO₃, asked for CaO |
| **Scale factor** | The multiplier used to scale a recipe up or down | From 1 cake to 40 cakes: scale factor is 40 |
| **Mass-mass problem** | A calculation where you start with the mass of one substance and find the mass of another | How many grams of CaO form from 400 g of CaCO₃ |
| **Conservation of mass** | Total mass neither increases nor decreases in a chemical reaction | Total mass of reactants = total mass of products |
| **Exact reacting ratio** | Taking reactants in the exact mole ratio of the recipe so that none is left over | Assumed in all problems in this topic |

## Part A: What an Equation Tells You — and What It Doesn't

Read a balanced chemical equation like a recipe. For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the equation tells us:

- **Molecules:** 2 molecules of H₂ + 1 molecule of O₂ → 2 molecules of H₂O.
- **Moles:** 2 mol of H₂ + 1 mol of O₂ → 2 mol of H₂O (multiplying every molecule count by 6.022 × 10²³ gives moles — the counting unit from [atoms-and-molecules-part5.md](../../../class9/chemistry/atoms-and-molecules/atoms-and-molecules-part5.md)).
- **Gas volumes** (at the same T and P): 2 volumes : 1 volume : 2 volumes (from Avogadro's Law in Topic 1).

What about **mass** (in grams)? The mass ratio is *not* written directly in the coefficients! Instead, we find masses from moles using each substance's **molar mass**:

```
             2H₂      +      O₂       →      2H₂O

  molecules   2              1                2
  moles       2              1                2
  mass (g)    2 x 2 = 4      1 x 32 = 32      2 x 18 = 36
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Reading the recipe 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O in three ways. Both molecules and moles follow the coefficient ratio (2 : 1 : 2). The mass ratio is 4 : 32 : 36 (which simplifies to 1 : 8 : 9) — completely different from 2 : 1 : 2! Even so, total mass is conserved: 4 + 32 = 36.

Just like Clara's measuring cups: the coefficient ratio is 2 : 1 (in "cups" of moles), but one mole of H₂ weighs only **2 g** whereas one mole of O₂ weighs **32 g**. Therefore, you can never read coefficients directly as grams. **Convert to moles first, then to mass.**

(In Chapter 4, when we counted electrons during electrolysis — "2 electrons for every 1 Cu²⁺ ion" — we were using the exact same counting principle. Here, we count reactant and product moles without any electrical charge calculations.)

## Part B: The Mole Ratio Method: Five Steps

In Class 9, for a single substance, we connected mass and moles with *n* = *m* ÷ M. Now we have *two* substances in a reaction, and the **coefficients of the balanced equation form the bridge** between them. Here is the entire pathway in one diagram:

```
     Mass of GIVEN       Moles of GIVEN      Moles of ASKED      Mass of ASKED

     [ m (given) ] --> [ n (given) ] --> [ n (asked) ] --> [ m (asked) ]
               ÷ M            mole ratio              x M
                     (equation coefficients)
```

> **Figure 4.2** – The mass-to-mass pathway: divide the given mass by its molar mass M to get given moles (÷ M), use the equation's coefficient ratio to find asked moles, and multiply by the asked substance's molar mass M (× M) to get the final mass. The bridge in the middle operates strictly in moles.

**The Five Steps:**

1. **Balance the equation** and write down the molar mass M of each relevant formula (Check: atom counts match on both sides).
2. **Write the mole ratio:** identify the *given* substance and the *asked* substance, and write the ratio of their coefficients.
3. **Convert the given mass to moles:** *n* = *m* ÷ M.
4. **Apply the mole ratio:** moles of asked = moles of given × (coefficient of asked ÷ coefficient of given).
5. **Convert asked moles to mass:** *m* = *n* × M. Finally, **Check** using conservation of mass: total mass of reactants = total mass of products.

Throughout this topic, we assume that all other reactants are present in the **exact reacting ratio** (or "in sufficient/excess amount" as stated in the problem). Limiting-reactant problems are saved for higher classes.

## Part C: Solved Examples

(Wherever a reaction involves high heat or hazardous gases, it is marked "paper-only" — we are doing paper calculations only.)

### Example 1: Heating Limestone to Make Quicklime (CaO)

In an industrial lime kiln, pure limestone (CaCO₃) is heated strongly (calcination, as in [metallurgy-part4.md](../metallurgy/metallurgy-part4.md)): **CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**. (Paper-only: industrial kilns operate at extreme temperatures.)

**Question:** 400 g of pure CaCO₃ is heated until it decomposes completely. What masses of CaO and CO₂ are formed?

**Solution:**
1. The equation is balanced: Ca 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 ✅. Molar masses: CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100, CaO = 40 + 16 = 56, CO₂ = 12 + 32 = 44 g/mol.
2. Mole ratio CaCO₃ : CaO : CO₂ = 1 : 1 : 1.
3. Moles of CaCO₃ = 400 ÷ 100 = **4 mol**.
4. Moles of CaO = 4 mol; moles of CO₂ = 4 mol.
5. Mass of CaO = 4 × 56 = **224 g**. Mass of CO₂ = 4 × 44 = **176 g**.

**Check (conservation of mass):** Before: 400 g; After: 224 + 176 = 400 g ✅.

### Example 2: A Two-Step Chain: Making Slaked Lime from Quicklime

Now the 224 g of CaO from Example 1 reacts with water to make slaked lime: **CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂**. **Question:** How much water is needed to react with 224 g of CaO, and what mass of Ca(OH)₂ is produced?

**Solution:**
- Balanced: Ca 1 = 1, O 1 + 1 = 2 = 2, H 2 = 2 ✅. Molar mass of Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × 17 = 74 g/mol.
- Moles of CaO = 224 ÷ 56 = **4 mol**. Since the ratio is 1 : 1 : 1, water needed = 4 mol and Ca(OH)₂ formed = 4 mol.
- Mass of water = 4 × 18 = **72 g**. Mass of Ca(OH)₂ = 4 × 74 = **296 g**.

**Check:** 224 + 72 = 296 g ✅. Tracing the whole chain: 400 g CaCO₃ → 224 g CaO → 296 g Ca(OH)₂ (with 176 g of CO₂ released in the first step and 72 g of H₂O added in the second).

### Example 3: Reducing Iron Ore with CO (Ratio 1 : 3 : 2 : 3)

In a blast furnace ([metallurgy-part4.md](../metallurgy/metallurgy-part4.md)): **Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂**. (Paper-only: CO is a colourless, odourless, poisonous gas.)

**Question:** From 480 g of Fe₂O₃, what mass of iron (Fe) is obtained, what mass of CO is required, and what mass of CO₂ is formed?

**Solution:**
1. Balanced: Fe 2 = 2, O 3 + 3 = 6 = 6, C 3 = 3 ✅. Molar masses: Fe₂O₃ = 112 + 48 = 160, CO = 28, Fe = 56, CO₂ = 44 g/mol.
2. Mole ratio Fe₂O₃ : CO : Fe : CO₂ = 1 : 3 : 2 : 3.
3. Moles of Fe₂O₃ = 480 ÷ 160 = **3 mol**.
4. Moles of CO = 3 × 3 = 9 mol; moles of Fe = 3 × 2 = 6 mol; moles of CO₂ = 9 mol.
5. Mass of Fe = 6 × 56 = **336 g**. Mass of CO = 9 × 28 = **252 g**. Mass of CO₂ = 9 × 44 = **396 g**.

**Check:** Before: 480 + 252 = 732 g; After: 336 + 396 = 732 g ✅.

Notice that the coefficient ratio is 1 : 3 : 2 : 3, whereas the masses are 480 : 252 : 336 : 396. The **mole** ratio is 1 : 3 : 2 : 3, not the mass ratio!

### Example 4: Working Backwards: Given a Product, Find the Reactant (H₂O₂)

When the mass of a *product* is given and a *reactant* is asked, we follow the exact same bridge in reverse. Dilute pharmacy-grade hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) slowly decomposes into water and oxygen: **2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂**. (Used only under adult supervision.)

**Question:** To produce 4.8 g of oxygen gas, what mass of H₂O₂ must decompose, and what mass of water forms alongside it?

**Solution:**
1. Balanced: H 4 = 4, O 4 = 2 + 2 ✅. Molar masses: H₂O₂ = 2 + 32 = 34, H₂O = 18, O₂ = 32 g/mol.
2. Mole ratio H₂O₂ : H₂O : O₂ = 2 : 2 : 1.
3. **Here O₂ is the given substance:** moles of O₂ = 4.8 ÷ 32 = **0.15 mol**.
4. Moles of H₂O₂ = 0.15 × 2 = 0.3 mol. Moles of H₂O = 0.3 mol.
5. Mass of H₂O₂ = 0.3 × 34 = **10.2 g**. Mass of H₂O = 0.3 × 18 = **5.4 g**.

**Check:** 10.2 = 5.4 + 4.8 g ✅.

### Example 5: Metal + Acid, Plus Counting Particles

Aluminium reacts with dilute HCl to release hydrogen gas: **2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂**. (Teacher demonstration only; never ignite or collect H₂ gas.)

**Question:** If 10.8 g of aluminium reacts completely, what mass of HCl is needed, and what masses of AlCl₃ and H₂ are formed? How many H₂ molecules are released?

**Solution:**
1. Balanced: Al 2 = 2, H 6 = 6, Cl 6 = 6 ✅. Molar masses: Al = 27, HCl = 1 + 35.5 = 36.5, AlCl₃ = 27 + 106.5 = 133.5, H₂ = 2 g/mol.
2. Mole ratio Al : HCl : AlCl₃ : H₂ = 2 : 6 : 2 : 3 (which simplifies to 1 : 3 : 1 : 1.5).
3. Moles of Al = 10.8 ÷ 27 = **0.4 mol**.
4. Moles of HCl = 0.4 × 3 = 1.2 mol; moles of AlCl₃ = 0.4 mol; moles of H₂ = 0.4 × 1.5 = 0.6 mol.
5. Mass of HCl = 1.2 × 36.5 = **43.8 g**. Mass of AlCl₃ = 0.4 × 133.5 = **53.4 g**. Mass of H₂ = 0.6 × 2 = **1.2 g**.
6. **Particles:** Number of H₂ molecules = 0.6 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **3.613 × 10²³** (using our Class 9 formula *N* = *n* × 6.022 × 10²³).

**Check:** 10.8 + 43.8 = 54.6 g; 53.4 + 1.2 = 54.6 g ✅. And 0.6 mol H₂ = 0.6 × 2 = 1.2 g ✅.

### Example 6: Kitchen Chemistry: Baking Soda and Vinegar (Safe with an Adult)

You have probably seen the fizzy reaction between baking soda (NaHCO₃) and vinegar (dilute acetic acid): **NaHCO₃ + CH₃COOH → CH₃COONa + H₂O + CO₂**. (Safe to try in an open bowl with an adult, keeping your face back.)

**Question:** How many grams of pure acetic acid are needed to react completely with 8.4 g of baking soda, and what mass of CO₂ gas is released? Since household vinegar is about 5% acetic acid by mass, how many grams of vinegar is that?

**Solution:**
1. Balanced: Na 1 = 1, C 1 + 2 = 3 = 2 + 1 ✅, H 1 + 4 = 5 = 3 + 2 ✅, O 3 + 2 = 5 = 2 + 1 + 2 ✅. Molar masses: NaHCO₃ = 23 + 1 + 12 + 48 = 84, CH₃COOH = 24 + 4 + 32 = 60, CO₂ = 44 g/mol.
2. Mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 across the board.
3. Moles of NaHCO₃ = 8.4 ÷ 84 = **0.1 mol**.
4. Moles of CH₃COOH = 0.1 mol; moles of CO₂ = 0.1 mol.
5. Mass of pure acetic acid = 0.1 × 60 = **6 g**. Mass of CO₂ = 0.1 × 44 = **4.4 g**.
6. Since vinegar is 5% acetic acid: 6 ÷ 0.05 = **120 g of vinegar** (roughly 120 mL).

**Check:** Products: CH₃COONa = 0.1 × 82 = 8.2 g, H₂O = 1.8 g, CO₂ = 4.4 g → total = 14.4 g. Reactants: 8.4 + 6 = 14.4 g ✅.

**What a kitchen scale shows:** If you run this reaction in an open bowl on a digital kitchen scale, the **CO₂ gas escapes into the room**, so the scale reading drops by about **4.4 g**. Mass wasn't destroyed — the gas simply floated away! (Never perform this reaction inside a sealed bottle: the trapped gas pressure can burst the container.)

## Part D: Mass Is Conserved, Moles Are Not Always Conserved

Your best tool for checking any stoichiometry answer is **conservation of mass**: because atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction — only rearranged — **the total mass before and after the reaction must be equal**. However, here is an important twist: **the total number of moles does not have to stay constant!** Look at the reaction N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (from Topic 1, now with masses) starting with 1 mol of N₂:

| Substance | Moles | Mass (g) |
|---|---|---|
| N₂ (1 mol × 28) | 1 | 28 |
| H₂ (3 mol × 2) | 3 | 6 |
| **Total Reactants** | **4** | **34** |
| NH₃ (2 mol × 17) | 2 | 34 |
| **Total Products** | **2** | **34** |

**The total moles dropped from 4 to 2, yet the total mass stayed at 34 g!** Why? Because a mole is a count of molecules (and smaller molecules can combine to form fewer, larger molecules), whereas **the total number of atoms and their combined mass never change**. That is why we always check our final answers by adding up **masses**, not moles.

**Catching an error with the mass check:** Suppose in Example 1 (400 g of CaCO₃ = 4 mol), someone calculated 224 g of CaO properly, but forgot to multiply CO₂ by 4 and wrote **44 g** (the mass of 1 mol). Let's run the mass check: 224 + 44 = 268 g, which falls way short of 400 g! The check immediately flags the error: 400 − 224 = 176 g of CO₂ was needed, and 176 ÷ 44 = 4 mol. Make this mass check a habit on every single problem.

## Part E: Scaling Up: From Grams to Kilograms and Tonnes

Just like scaling Clara's cake recipe, once you know the mass ratio for a reaction, you can **scale it in any mass unit**. From Example 1, we know that 100 g CaCO₃ → 56 g CaO + 44 g CO₂. Because that ratio comes from molar masses and coefficients, it holds true for **any unit of mass**:

**Question:** A lime kiln heats 5 tonnes of pure CaCO₃. How many tonnes of CaO and CO₂ are produced?

**Solution:** Since 100 g of CaCO₃ gives 56 g of CaO, every 1 tonne of CaCO₃ gives 0.56 tonnes of CaO (56 ÷ 100 = 0.56). Similarly, the CO₂ fraction is 44 ÷ 100 = 0.44.
- For 5 tonnes of CaCO₃: CaO = 5 × 0.56 = **2.8 tonnes**. CO₂ = 5 × 0.44 = **2.2 tonnes**.
- **Check:** 2.8 + 2.2 = 5.0 tonnes ✅. (Remember: the 56 : 44 mass split belongs strictly to this specific reaction.)

**Key Takeaway:** The mass ratio between any two substances in a given reaction is fixed, and it comes from **combining molar masses with equation coefficients** — never from reading the coefficients directly as grams.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Treating equation coefficients as a ratio of grams.**

Suppose Clara looks at 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O and says: "The coefficients are 2 : 1, so 2 g of hydrogen needs 1 g of oxygen." Let's test why this fails in three ways:

**Test 1: The measuring-cup analogy.** Great-grandmother's recipe called for 1 cup of flour to 1 cup of sugar. If someone used 100 g of flour and 100 g of sugar, they would only get about 2/3 of a cup of sugar! The 1 : 1 ratio was for **cups (counts)**, not grams.

**Test 2: The law of constant proportions for water.** In Class 9 ([atoms-and-molecules-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/atoms-and-molecules/atoms-and-molecules-part2.md)), we learned that hydrogen and oxygen in water are always combined in a **1 : 8 mass ratio** (as in Figure 4.1: 4 g of H₂ to 32 g of O₂). If 2 g of H₂ combined with 1 g of O₂, the water would have a 2 : 1 mass ratio — which is impossible! The correct calculation: 2 g of H₂ = 1 mol, which requires 0.5 mol of O₂ = **16 g of O₂**, producing 1 mol = **18 g of H₂O** (Check: 2 + 16 = 18 ✅).

**Test 3: Metal and acid.** In Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂, if someone claims "24 g of Mg needs 2 g of HCl (because the coefficients are 1 : 2)," let's compare with the real answer: 24 g of Mg = 1 mol, which requires 2 mol of HCl = 2 × 36.5 = **73 g of HCl**! Using 2 g instead of 73 g is off by more than 36 times — almost all the magnesium would remain unreacted.

**The Right Habit:** Always read equation coefficients as **moles** ("measuring cups"). To convert to grams, multiply each substance's moles by its own molar mass.

## Quick Recap

- A balanced chemical equation is a **recipe**: its coefficients give the ratio of **molecules, moles, and gas volumes** (at the same T and P). The **mass ratio** is not given directly by the coefficients — it requires molar masses.
- **The 5-Step Mass-Mass Method:** (1) Balance the equation and find molar masses, (2) write the mole ratio, (3) convert given mass to moles (*m* ÷ M), (4) apply the mole ratio to find asked moles, (5) convert asked moles to mass (*n* × M) and check mass conservation.
- Pathway: Given mass → Given moles → Asked moles → Asked mass (Figure 4.2), with the **mole ratio** as the central bridge.
- **Conservation of mass:** Total mass of reactants = total mass of products. Total moles do *not* have to stay equal (e.g., in N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, moles drop from 4 to 2 while mass stays at 34 g = 34 g).
- Once a mass ratio is found for a reaction, you can scale it in any mass unit (e.g., 100 g → 56 g scales directly to 5 tonnes → 2.8 tonnes).
- **Counting particles:** Multiply moles by 6.022 × 10²³ to find the number of molecules or atoms.
- **Classic pitfall:** Never read coefficients as grams — 2 g of H₂ needs 16 g of O₂, not 1 g.
- Hazardous reactions (CO, HCl, strong acids, burning metals) are strictly for paper calculations; kitchen vinegar-and-baking-soda activities should be done with an adult.

## Practice Questions

In every question, assume the other reactants are present in the exact reacting ratio (or "in sufficient amount"). Hazardous reactions are for paper calculation only. Use atomic masses: H 1, C 12, N 14, O 16, Na 23, Mg 24, Al 27, S 32, Cl 35.5, K 39, Ca 40, Fe 56, Cu 63.5, Zn 65, I 127, Pb 207, P 31.

1. 2NaOH + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. A school lab neutralises 30 g of NaOH. Find the mass of H₂SO₄ needed, and the masses of Na₂SO₄ and water formed. Check that mass is conserved.
2. Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. Find the mass of NaCl and of CO₂ formed from 15.9 g of Na₂CO₃. How many molecules of CO₂ is that?
3. Ethanol burns as C₂H₅OH + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 3H₂O. For 23 g of ethanol, find the mass of oxygen needed and of CO₂ and H₂O formed. Check conservation of mass. (Ethanol is inflammable: paper work only.)
4. Pb(NO₃)₂ + 2KI → PbI₂ + 2KNO₃. A solution containing 16.6 g of KI is mixed with exactly enough Pb(NO₃)₂ solution. Find the mass of Pb(NO₃)₂ needed and of yellow PbI₂ formed. (Lead compounds are poisonous: paper work only.)
5. Copper(II) carbonate decomposes on strong heating: CuCO₃ → CuO + CO₂. How much CuO and CO₂ come from 37.05 g of CuCO₃? Check by adding the masses.
6. Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂. A teacher uses 18 g of magnesium ribbon with enough dilute acid. Find the mass of MgSO₄ formed and the number of H₂ molecules released.
7. In the thermite reaction Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe, 84 g of iron must be produced. Find the masses of Fe₂O₃ and Al needed, and of Al₂O₃ formed. (Paper work: the reaction throws molten metal and sparks.)
8. A student says: "In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, 10 g of hydrogen needs 5 g of oxygen." Find the actual mass of oxygen needed and the mass of water formed, and explain the student's mistake in one line.
9. Quicklime slowly takes up carbon dioxide from the air: CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃. 14 g of CaO takes up the exact amount of CO₂. Find the mass of CO₂ taken up and of CaCO₃ formed. Compare the total moles before and after the reaction, and the total mass before and after.
10. A pancake recipe for 8 pancakes needs 2 cups flour, 1 cup milk and 1 egg. How much of each is needed for 24 pancakes? Which part of a chemical equation plays the role of "cups", and why can you not add the masses of the ingredients directly as cups?
11. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) In 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, 2 g of Mg needs 1 g of O₂. (b) The total number of moles is always the same before and after a reaction. (c) The total mass is always the same before and after a reaction. (d) If 1 mol of CaCO₃ gives 1 mol of CaO, then 100 g of CaCO₃ gives 100 g of CaO. (e) The mole ratio of reactants and products comes from the coefficients of the balanced equation.
12. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO. Magnesium ribbon of mass 30 g burns completely in oxygen (teacher demo only: never look at the bright flame directly). Find the mass of oxygen used, the mass of MgO formed, and the number of O₂ molecules used. Why is the white ash heavier than the ribbon?

### Answers

1. NaOH = 40 g/mol, so 30 ÷ 40 = **0.75 mol**. Ratio NaOH : H₂SO₄ : Na₂SO₄ : H₂O = 2 : 1 : 1 : 2. H₂SO₄ = 0.375 mol × 98 = **36.75 g**. Na₂SO₄ = 0.375 mol × 142 = **53.25 g**. H₂O = 0.75 mol × 18 = **13.5 g**. Check: 30 + 36.75 = 66.75 g; 53.25 + 13.5 = 66.75 g ✅.
2. Na₂CO₃ = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106, so 15.9 ÷ 106 = **0.15 mol**. Ratio Na₂CO₃ : NaCl : CO₂ = 1 : 2 : 1. NaCl = 0.3 mol × 58.5 = **17.55 g**. CO₂ = 0.15 mol × 44 = **6.6 g**. Molecules of CO₂ = 0.15 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **9.033 × 10²²**. Check: HCl = 0.3 mol × 36.5 = 10.95 g, H₂O = 0.15 × 18 = 2.7 g. Reactants: 15.9 + 10.95 = 26.85 g; Products: 17.55 + 2.7 + 6.6 = 26.85 g ✅.
3. C₂H₅OH = 24 + 6 + 16 = 46, so 23 ÷ 46 = **0.5 mol**. Ratio 1 : 3 : 2 : 3. O₂ = 1.5 mol × 32 = **48 g**. CO₂ = 1 mol × 44 = **44 g**. H₂O = 1.5 mol × 18 = **27 g**. Check: 23 + 48 = 71 g; 44 + 27 = 71 g ✅.
