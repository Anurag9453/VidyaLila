## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Master method** | One universal 5-step sequence for solving any stoichiometry problem | Balance → Ratio → Moles → Calculate → Check |
| **Mole ratio** | The ratio of coefficients in a balanced equation, giving the ratio of moles between two substances | In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1 |
| **Given and asked** | The quantity provided in the problem (*given*) and the quantity you need to find (*asked*) | Given: 3.6 g Mg; Asked: volume of H₂ |
| **Bridge** | The mole — the central hub connecting mass, gas volume, and number of particles | Converting via molar mass, 22.4 L, or 6.022 × 10²³ |
| **Check (verification)** | Confirming that the final answer makes sense: checking units, conservation of mass, or working backwards | Total mass on the left = total mass on the right |
| **Mixed problem** | A multi-part question that combines tools from several topics | Finding a formula from VD + percentages, then using it in an equation |

## Part A: The Five-Step Master Method

```
    STEP 1                 STEP 2                STEP 3               STEP 4               STEP 5
    BALANCE                MOLE RATIO            CONVERT GIVEN        FIND ASKED           CHECK
    write equation,        from coefficients,    TO MOLES             MOLES, then          units, mass
    count atoms            write given : asked   mass / M, or         convert to mass,     conservation,
                                                 volume / 22.4        volume, or particles or reverse check
         :                      :                      :                    :                    :
         v                      v                      v                    v                    v
    2Al + 3H₂SO₄           2 mol Al : 3 mol      given 2.7 g Al       asked moles          mass in = mass
    → ...                  H₂ ...                = 0.1 mol            x factor             out
```

> **Figure 6.1** – The 5-Step Master Method (with an example). All three outer quantities (mass, gas volume, particle count) enter at Step 3 and exit at Step 4 **strictly through moles**; Step 2 always uses the **mole ratio**, never a direct mass ratio.

Here is each step in one line:

1. **Balance:** Write the chemical equation and count atoms of each element on both sides ([language-of-chemistry-part6.md](../../../class9/chemistry/language-of-chemistry/language-of-chemistry-part6.md)). Without a balanced equation, the ratio will be wrong.
2. **Mole ratio:** Pick the two substances involved in the question (*given* and *asked*) and write the ratio of their coefficients. This is a **mole ratio** (Topic 4). If every substance involved is a gas at the same T and P, it is also the **volume ratio** (Topic 1).
3. **Convert the given quantity to moles:** If given mass, divide by molar mass (*m* ÷ M); if given gas volume at STP, divide by 22.4 L; if given particles, divide by 6.022 × 10²³ (Topic 5 Mole Map).
4. **Find asked moles, then convert to the asked unit:** Multiply given moles by the mole ratio, then convert to mass (moles × M), gas volume at STP (moles × 22.4 L), or particles (moles × 6.022 × 10²³).
5. **Check:** Are the units right? Does **conservation of mass** (total reactant mass = total product mass) hold? Is the size of the number reasonable?

See how all six topics fit into this single framework:

```
    Topic 1: Gas volume ratio         ---->  (Step 2 shortcut when all are gases)
    Topic 2: Vapour density           ---->  M = 2 x VD    (gives M for Step 3)
    Topic 3: Empirical & molecular    ---->  builds the right formula (for Step 1)
    Topic 4: Mass-to-mass             ---->  core backbone of Steps 2, 3, and 4
    Topic 5: Volume, particles, map   ---->  new entry/exit doors in Steps 3 and 4
                          :
                          v
    Topic 6: All five working together in one sequence
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Where each of the previous five topics plugs into the Master Method. Topic 6 doesn't add new formulas — it shows how to run all five tools smoothly in sequence.

## Part B: Mixed Solved Examples

**Safety Note:** Every reaction in this section is **strictly for reading and paper calculation** or **teacher-led school lab demonstrations**. Never generate, collect, or ignite gases yourself, and never handle acids, thermite mixtures (Chapter 2), or toxic gases like CO without a teacher.

### Example 1: Metal + Acid: Magnesium and HCl (Chapter 2 Reaction)

**Question:** 3.6 g of magnesium reacts completely with excess dilute HCl. Find the volume at STP and the mass of H₂ gas released, the mass of HCl consumed, and the mass of MgCl₂ formed.

**Solution (Five Steps):**
- **Step 1:** Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Atom check: Mg 1 = 1, H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2 ✅.
- **Step 2:** Mg : HCl : MgCl₂ : H₂ = 1 : 2 : 1 : 1.
- **Step 3:** *n*(Mg) = 3.6 ÷ 24 = **0.15 mol**.
- **Step 4:** H₂ = 0.15 mol → volume at STP = 0.15 × 22.4 = **3.36 L**; mass = 0.15 × 2 = **0.3 g**. HCl = 0.15 × 2 = 0.3 mol; M(HCl) = 36.5 → mass = 0.3 × 36.5 = **10.95 g**. MgCl₂ = 0.15 mol; M(MgCl₂) = 24 + 71 = 95 → mass = 0.15 × 95 = **14.25 g**.
- **Step 5 (Check):** Left: 3.6 + 10.95 = 14.55 g. Right: 14.25 + 0.3 = 14.55 g ✅.

### Example 2: Carbonate + Acid: Na₂CO₃ and HCl (Chapter 1 Reaction)

**Question:** 10.6 g of sodium carbonate reacts completely with dilute HCl. Find the volume of CO₂ released at STP, the mass of NaCl formed, and the mass of HCl used.

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. Atom check: Na 2 = 2, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 = 3, H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2 ✅.
- **Step 2:** Na₂CO₃ : HCl : NaCl : CO₂ = 1 : 2 : 2 : 1.
- **Step 3:** M(Na₂CO₃) = 46 + 12 + 48 = 106 → *n* = 10.6 ÷ 106 = **0.1 mol**.
- **Step 4:** CO₂ = 0.1 mol → volume at STP = 0.1 × 22.4 = **2.24 L**. NaCl = 0.2 mol × 58.5 = **11.7 g**. HCl = 0.2 mol × 36.5 = **7.3 g**.
- **Check:** Left: 10.6 + 7.3 = 17.9 g. Right: NaCl (11.7 g) + H₂O (0.1 × 18 = 1.8 g) + CO₂ (0.1 × 44 = 4.4 g) = 17.9 g ✅.

### Example 3: Metal Extraction: Fe₂O₃ and CO (Chapter 3 Blast Furnace)

**Question:** On paper, 80 g of Fe₂O₃ is completely reduced by carbon monoxide: Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂. Find the mass of iron (Fe) obtained, the mass of CO required, and both the mass and STP volume of CO₂ formed. (Paper-only: CO is poisonous.)

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** Fe 2 = 2, C 3 = 3, O 3 + 3 = 6 = 6 ✅.
- **Step 2:** Fe₂O₃ : CO : Fe : CO₂ = 1 : 3 : 2 : 3.
- **Step 3:** M(Fe₂O₃) = 112 + 48 = 160 → *n* = 80 ÷ 160 = **0.5 mol**.
- **Step 4:** Fe = 2 × 0.5 = 1 mol = **56 g**. CO = 1.5 mol × 28 = **42 g**. CO₂ = 1.5 mol × 44 = **66 g**, and volume at STP = 1.5 × 22.4 = **33.6 L**.
- **Check:** Left: 80 + 42 = 122 g; Right: 56 + 66 = 122 g ✅.

Notice that 1 mol of Fe₂O₃ yields **2 mol** of Fe — matching both the subscript 2 in Fe₂O₃ and the coefficient 2 in front of Fe (we will revisit the difference between subscripts and coefficients in Common Pitfalls).

### Example 4: Electrolysis of Water: From Mass to Gas Volume (Chapter 4)

**Question:** In a teacher's electrolysis demonstration, 7.2 g of water is completely decomposed: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. Find the moles, masses, and STP volumes of H₂ and O₂ produced, and verify their volume ratio. (In [electrolysis-part3.md](../electrolysis/electrolysis-part3.md) we examined the electrode half-reactions; here we count the overall stoichiometry.)

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2 ✅.
- **Step 2:** H₂O : H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 2 : 1.
- **Step 3:** *n*(H₂O) = 7.2 ÷ 18 = **0.4 mol**.
- **Step 4:** H₂ = 0.4 mol → mass = 0.4 × 2 = **0.8 g**, volume at STP = 0.4 × 22.4 = **8.96 L**. O₂ = 0.2 mol → mass = 0.2 × 32 = **6.4 g**, volume at STP = 0.2 × 22.4 = **4.48 L**.
- **Check:** Mass: 0.8 + 6.4 = 7.2 g ✅. Volume ratio: 8.96 : 4.48 = **2 : 1** ✅ (matching Gay-Lussac's ratio from Topic 1).

### Example 5: Gas-to-Gas Combustion of Ethene on Paper (Topics 1 and 5)

**Question:** On paper, 15 L of ethene (C₂H₄) burns completely: C₂H₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO₂ + 2H₂O (steam), with all gases measured at the same T and P. Find the volumes of O₂ needed and of CO₂ and steam formed, plus the volume of air required (assuming air is 21% O₂ by volume).

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** C 2 = 2, H 4 = 4, O 6 = 4 + 2 = 6 ✅.
- **Step 2:** Since all substances are gases at the same T and P, volume ratio = mole ratio = 1 : 3 : 2 : 2. **No need to convert to moles** (Topic 1).
- **Step 4:** O₂ = 3 × 15 = **45 L**. CO₂ = 2 × 15 = **30 L**. Steam = 2 × 15 = **30 L**.
- **Air required:** For 45 L of O₂ at 21% of air: 45 ÷ 0.21 = 214.3 L (≈ **214 L of air**).
- **Check:** Left volume = 15 + 45 = 60 L and right volume = 30 + 30 = 60 L (equal here because gas coefficients sum to 4 on both sides). Atom-volume check for oxygen: left = 45 × 2 = 90; right = 30 × 2 (CO₂) + 30 × 1 (steam) = 90 ✅.

### Example 6: Finding the Formula First, Then Moles: C₃H₆ (Topics 2 and 3)

**Question:** A gaseous hydrocarbon contains 85.7% carbon and 14.3% hydrogen by mass, and its vapour density is 21. Find its molecular formula.

**Solution:**
- In 100 g: C = 85.7 g, H = 14.3 g.
- Moles: C = 85.7 ÷ 12 = 7.14; H = 14.3 ÷ 1 = 14.3.
- Divide by 7.14: C = 1; H = 14.3 ÷ 7.14 = 2.0. Empirical formula = **CH₂** (empirical mass = 12 + 2 = 14).
- Molecular mass M = 2 × VD = 2 × 21 = **42**.
- *n* = 42 ÷ 14 = **3**, so molecular formula = (CH₂) × 3 = **C₃H₆**.
- **Check:** M(C₃H₆) = 36 + 6 = 42 ✅. % C = 36 ÷ 42 × 100 = 85.7% ✅.

### Example 7: Burning That Hydrocarbon: Mass, Volume, and Particles (Topics 4 and 5)

**Question:** 4.2 g of the gas C₃H₆ from Example 6 burns completely: 2C₃H₆ + 9O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O. Find the mass of O₂ used (g), the mass (g) and STP volume (L) of CO₂ formed, the mass of liquid water formed (g), and the number of water molecules produced.

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** C 6 = 6, H 12 = 12, O 18 = 12 + 6 = 18 ✅.
- **Step 2:** C₃H₆ : O₂ : CO₂ : H₂O = 2 : 9 : 6 : 6.
- **Step 3:** *n*(C₃H₆) = 4.2 ÷ 42 = **0.1 mol**.
- **Step 4:** O₂ = 0.1 × (9 ÷ 2) = 0.45 mol → mass = 0.45 × 32 = **14.4 g**. CO₂ = 0.1 × (6 ÷ 2) = 0.3 mol → mass = 0.3 × 44 = **13.2 g**, and STP volume = 0.3 × 22.4 = **6.72 L**. H₂O = 0.3 mol → mass = 0.3 × 18 = **5.4 g** (liquid water at room temperature). Number of water molecules = 0.3 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.807 × 10²³**.
- **Check:** Left: 4.2 + 14.4 = 18.6 g; Right: 13.2 + 5.4 = 18.6 g ✅.

### Example 8: The Thermite Reaction (Chapter 2 Recall, Paper-Only)

**Question:** In the thermite reaction used to weld railway tracks (2Al + Fe₂O₃ → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe), how much Fe₂O₃ is needed to react completely with 13.5 g of aluminium, and what masses of iron and Al₂O₃ are formed? How many iron atoms are produced? (Paper-only: this reaction is intensely exothermic and handled only by professionals.)

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** Al 2 = 2, Fe 2 = 2, O 3 = 3 ✅.
- **Step 2:** Al : Fe₂O₃ : Al₂O₃ : Fe = 2 : 1 : 1 : 2.
- **Step 3:** *n*(Al) = 13.5 ÷ 27 = **0.5 mol**.
- **Step 4:** Fe₂O₃ = 0.25 mol × 160 = **40 g**. Al₂O₃ = 0.25 mol × 102 = **25.5 g**. Fe = 0.5 mol × 56 = **28 g**; number of Fe atoms = 0.5 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **3.011 × 10²³**.
- **Check:** Left: 13.5 + 40 = 53.5 g; Right: 25.5 + 28 = 53.5 g ✅.

### Example 9: Working Backwards from Gas Volume to Reactant Masses

**Question:** On paper, 26.88 L of hydrogen gas (at STP) is to be prepared from aluminium and dilute HCl: 2Al + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂ (paper-only; hydrogen is flammable). What masses of Al and HCl are required, and what mass of AlCl₃ is formed?

**Solution:**
- **Step 1:** Al 2 = 2, H 6 = 6, Cl 6 = 6 ✅.
- **Step 2:** Al : HCl : AlCl₃ : H₂ = 2 : 6 : 2 : 3. Here the product H₂ is *given*, and the reactants are *asked*.
- **Step 3:** *n*(H₂) = 26.88 ÷ 22.4 = **1.2 mol**.
- **Step 4:** Al = 1.2 × (2 ÷ 3) = 0.8 mol → mass = 0.8 × 27 = **21.6 g**. HCl = 1.2 × (6 ÷ 3) = 2.4 mol → mass = 2.4 × 36.5 = **87.6 g**. AlCl₃ = 0.8 mol × 133.5 = **106.8 g**.
- **Check:** Left: 21.6 + 87.6 = 109.2 g; Right: 106.8 + (1.2 × 2 = 2.4 g) = 109.2 g ✅.

Notice how all nine examples follow the exact same rhythm: **convert the given quantity to moles in Step 3, cross the mole-ratio bridge, and convert back to the asked unit in Step 4**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

In addition to the pitfalls from Topic 4 ("reading coefficients as grams") and Topic 5 ("using 22.4 L for liquids/solids"), watch out for these five classic traps in mixed problems:

**Mistake 1: Saying the mass of 2 mol of H₂O is 18 g.**
This was Chloe's buzzer slip! In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, **two moles** of water are formed. 18 g is the molar mass of **one** mole of H₂O, so 2 moles weigh 2 × 18 = **36 g**. A quick conservation-of-mass check catches this immediately: 2 mol H₂ (4 g) + 1 mol O₂ (32 g) = 36 g of reactants, so 18 g of product would leave 18 g unaccounted for! Always multiply **moles × molar mass M**.

**Mistake 2: Mixing up atomic mass (u) and molar mass (g/mol).**
The atomic mass of magnesium is 24 u — that is the mass of **one single Mg atom** (which in grams is only 24 ÷ 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ 3.99 × 10⁻²³ g!). The **molar mass** is 24 g/mol — the mass of **6.022 × 10²³ atoms** (1 mole). If a question asks for the mass of 0.5 mol of Mg, writing "12 u" is wrong; the answer is 0.5 × 24 g/mol = **12 g**. Use **u** when talking about single atoms or molecules, and **g** (or **g/mol**) when working with moles.

**Mistake 3: Thinking an empirical formula must always have smaller numbers than the molecular formula.**
Some students think finding an empirical formula means you *have* to divide the subscripts by something. For glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), dividing by 6 gives CH₂O. But for **H₂O**, the 2 : 1 ratio is already in its lowest whole-number terms, so its empirical formula is still **H₂O**! The same holds for CO₂, NH₃, and CH₄ (*n* = 1). And for ionic compounds (NaCl, CaCl₂), the formula is already an empirical formula unit.

**Mistake 4: Attaching a unit like "g" or "g/L" to vapour density.**
A student writes: "The VD of CO₂ is 22 g." Remember: VD is the **ratio of two masses** (mass of a volume of gas ÷ mass of an equal volume of H₂). Grams divided by grams cancel out, leaving **no unit at all**! Do not confuse VD with gas **density** (in g/L): at STP, the density of CO₂ is 44 ÷ 22.4 = 1.96 g/L, whereas its VD is simply **22** (unitless).

**Mistake 5: Confusing a subscript inside a formula with a coefficient in front of it.**
In Fe₂O₃, the small subscript 2 means **2 Fe atoms inside 1 formula unit of Fe₂O₃**. In the balanced equation Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂, the large coefficient 2 in front of Fe means **2 moles of Fe** are produced per mole of Fe₂O₃, and the coefficient 3 in front of CO means **3 moles of CO** (which is 3 × 28 = 84 g, not 3 g!). Subscripts count atoms within a molecule or formula unit; coefficients count how many moles participate in the reaction.

## Quick Recap

- **The 5-Step Master Method:** (1) Balance the equation, (2) write the mole ratio, (3) convert the given quantity to moles, (4) calculate the asked moles and convert to the target unit, (5) check units and mass conservation.
- **The mole is the central bridge:** Mass ÷ M, Gas volume at STP ÷ 22.4 L, Particles ÷ 6.022 × 10²³.
- **Coefficients represent moles**, not grams; when **all substances are gases at the same T and P**, volume ratio = mole ratio.
- **Always check:** Total mass in = total mass out; verify units and reasonable magnitude.
- For unknown compounds (Topics 2 and 3): use M = 2 × VD and the 5-step percentage method to find empirical and molecular formulas first.
- Watch out for the 5 mixed-problem traps: 2 mol of water is 36 g (not 18 g); u vs. g/mol; empirical formulas with *n* = 1 stay unchanged; VD is unitless; subscripts vs. coefficients.
- Safety first: reactions involving CO, thermite, strong oxidisers, or acids are strictly for paper calculation or teacher demonstrations.

## Practice Questions

All reactions below are **for paper calculation or teacher demonstration only**. Use standard Class 9 atomic masses and molar volume = 22.4 L at STP.

1. 16.2 g of aluminium reacts completely with dilute sulphuric acid: 2Al + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂. Find the volume of hydrogen at STP, the mass of H₂SO₄ needed and the mass of Al₂(SO₄)₃ formed. Check that mass is conserved.
2. 42 g of magnesium carbonate (MgCO₃) reacts completely with dilute HCl: MgCO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂. Find the volume of CO₂ at STP, the mass of MgCl₂ and the mass of HCl needed.
3. In a paper exercise, 39.75 g of copper(II) oxide is reduced by carbon monoxide: CuO + CO → Cu + CO₂. Find the mass of copper, the mass of CO needed and the volume of CO₂ formed at STP. (CO is poisonous: paper only.)
4. In an electrolysis demonstration, 8.4 L of hydrogen is collected at STP from 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. Find the volume of oxygen, the mass of water decomposed and the mass of oxygen. Check with mass conservation.
5. A liquid contains 24.24% carbon, 4.04% hydrogen and 71.72% chlorine by mass, and its vapour has a vapour density of 49.5. Find its empirical formula and its molecular formula. (Cl = 35.5; the liquid is poisonous: paper only.)
6. 4 g of methane burns completely: CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O. Find the mass of oxygen needed, the mass of carbon dioxide formed, and the number of water molecules formed. Check mass conservation.
7. 36 L of carbon monoxide gas is burnt in a paper exercise: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂, all gases at the same temperature and pressure. Find the volume of oxygen needed, the volume of carbon dioxide formed and the volume of air needed (air has about 21% oxygen by volume).
8. A compound has 56.4% phosphorus and 43.6% oxygen by mass, and its vapour density is 110. Find its empirical and molecular formulas. (P = 31, O = 16.)
9. In a teacher demonstration, 28 g of potassium hydroxide is neutralised completely by dilute sulphuric acid: H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O. Find the moles of KOH, the mass of H₂SO₄ needed, the mass of K₂SO₄ formed and the mass of water formed. Check mass conservation. (KOH is caustic.)
10. 12.25 g of potassium chlorate is heated (a paper question only: KClO₃ is a strong oxidiser, never heated by students): 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂. Find the mass of KCl left and the volume of oxygen at STP.
11. A gas collected from a decomposition has a mass of 7.0 g in a volume of 2.24 L at STP. Find its molar mass and its vapour density. Is it lighter or heavier than air (take the vapour density of air as about 14.5)? Would a balloon filled with it float up or sink?
12. State True or False, and correct the false ones: (a) The mass of 3 mol of water is 18 g. (b) The vapour density of oxygen is 16 g. (c) The empirical formula of ethane, C₂H₆, is CH₃. (d) A coefficient 2 in front of HCl means 2 g of HCl. (e) In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (steam) at the same T and P, 10 L of H₂ gives 10 L of steam. (f) The atomic mass of Cu is 63.5 g.

### Answers

1. Step 1: 2Al + 3H₂SO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3H₂ (Al 2 = 2, S 3 = 3, O 12 = 12, H 6 = 6 ✅). *n*(Al) = 16.2 ÷ 27 = **0.6 mol**. Ratio Al : H₂SO₄ : Al₂(SO₄)₃ : H₂ = 2 : 3 : 1 : 3. H₂ = 0.9 mol → STP volume = 0.9 × 22.4 = **20.16 L**. H₂SO₄ = 0.9 mol × 98 = **88.2 g**. Al₂(SO₄)₃ = 0.3 mol × 342 = **102.6 g** (M = 54 + 96 × 3 = 342). Mass of H₂ = 0.9 × 2 = 1.8 g. **Check:** Left: 16.2 + 88.2 = 104.4 g; Right: 102.6 + 1.8 = 104.4 g ✅.
2. MgCO₃ + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂O + CO₂ (Mg 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2, H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2 ✅). M(MgCO₃) = 24 + 12 + 48 = 84 → *n* = 42 ÷ 84 = **0.5 mol**. CO₂ = 0.5 mol → STP volume = **11.2 L**. MgCl₂ = 0.5 × 95 = **47.5 g**. HCl = 1 mol × 36.5 = **36.5 g**. **Check:** Left: 42 + 36.5 = 78.5 g; Right: 47.5 + 9 (0.5 mol H₂O) + 22 (0.5 mol CO₂) = 78.5 g ✅.
3. CuO + CO → Cu + CO₂ (1 : 1 : 1 : 1 ✅). M(CuO) = 63.5 + 16 = 79.5 → *n* = 39.75 ÷ 79.5 = **0.5 mol**. Cu = 0.5 × 63.5 = **31.75 g**. CO = 0.5 × 28 = **14 g**. CO₂ volume at STP = 0.5 × 22.4 = **11.2 L**. **Check:** 39.75 + 14 = 53.75 g; Cu (31.75) + CO₂ (0.5 × 44 = 22) = 53.75 g ✅.
4. *n*(H₂) = 8.4 L ÷ 22.4 = **0.375 mol**. In 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2 ✅), H₂O : H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 2 : 1. Oxygen = 0.1875 mol → volume = **4.2 L** (matches the 2 : 1 volume ratio: 8.4 ÷ 2 = 4.2 L ✅). Mass of water decomposed = 0.375 × 18 = **6.75 g**. Mass of O₂ = 0.1875 × 32 = **6 g**. **Check:** Water (6.75 g) = H₂ (0.375 × 2 = 0.75 g) + O₂ (6 g) ✅.
5. In 100 g: C = 24.24 ÷ 12 = 2.02; H = 4.04 ÷ 1 = 4.04; Cl = 71.72 ÷ 35.5 = 2.02. Divide by 2.02: C = 1, H = 2, Cl = 1. Empirical formula = **CH₂Cl** (mass = 12 + 2 + 35.5 = 49.5). Molecular mass M = 2 × 49.5 = 99. *n* = 99 ÷ 49.5 = **2** → molecular formula = **C₂H₄Cl₂**. **Check:** C₂H₄Cl₂ = 24 + 4 + 71 = 99 ✅; % C = 24 ÷ 99 × 100 = 24.24% ✅. (Toxic liquid: paper-only.)
6. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 2 + 2 ✅). *n*(CH₄) = 4 ÷ 16 = **0.25 mol**. O₂ = 0.5 mol × 32 = **16 g**. CO₂ = 0.25 mol × 44 = **11 g**. H₂O = 0.5 mol (9 g) → water molecules = 0.5 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **3.011 × 10²³**. **Check:** Left: 4 + 16 = 20 g; Right: 11 + 9 = 20 g ✅.
7. 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ (C 2 = 2, O 4 = 4 ✅). All gases at the same T and P → volume ratio 2 : 1 : 2. O₂ = 36 ÷ 2 = **18 L**. CO₂ = **36 L**. Volume of air = 18 ÷ 0.21 = 85.7 L (≈ **86 L**). **Check:** Left volume = 36 + 18 = 54 L, right = 36 L (volume decreases from 3 parts to 2); C atom-volumes: 36 = 36 ✅. (CO is poisonous: paper-only.)
8. In 100 g: P = 56.4 ÷ 31 = 1.819; O = 43.6 ÷ 16 = 2.725. Divide by 1.819: P = 1; O = 2.725 ÷ 1.819 = 1.5. Multiply 1 : 1.5 by 2 to get **2 : 3**. Empirical formula = **P₂O₃** (mass = 62 + 48 = 110). Molecular mass M = 2 × 110 = 220. *n* = 220 ÷ 110 = **2** → molecular formula = **P₄O₆**. **Check:** P₄O₆ = 124 + 96 = 220 ✅; % P = 62 ÷ 110 × 100 = 56.4% ✅. (Oxides of phosphorus are toxic and corrosive: paper-only.)
9. H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O (H 4 = 4, S 1 = 1, O 6 = 6, K 2 = 2 ✅). M(KOH) = 39 + 16 + 1 = 56 → *n*(KOH) = 28 ÷ 56 = **0.5 mol**. Ratio H₂SO₄ : KOH : K₂SO₄ : H₂O = 1 : 2 : 1 : 2. H₂SO₄ = 0.25 mol × 98 = **24.5 g**. K₂SO₄ = 0.25 mol × 174 (78 + 32 + 64) = **43.5 g**. Water = 0.5 mol × 18 = **9 g**. **Check:** Left: 28 + 24.5 = 52.5 g; Right: 43.5 + 9 = 52.5 g ✅.
10. 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ (K 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2, O 6 = 6 ✅). M(KClO₃) = 39 + 35.5 + 48 = 122.5 → *n* = 12.25 ÷ 122.5 = **0.1 mol**. KCl = 0.1 mol × 74.5 = **7.45 g**. O₂ = 0.15 mol → STP volume = 0.15 × 22.4 = **3.36 L** (mass = 0.15 × 32 = 4.8 g). **Check:** 7.45 + 4.8 = 12.25 g ✅. (Teacher demonstration only.)
11. *n* = 2.24 ÷ 22.4 = 0.1 mol. Molar mass M = 7.0 ÷ 0.1 = **70 g/mol**. VD = 70 ÷ 2 = **35** (unitless). Since 35 > 14.5 (air's VD), the gas is **heavier than air**, so a balloon filled with it would **sink**. **Check:** 0.1 × 70 = 7.0 g ✅.
12. (a) **False** ❌: 3 mol of water = 3 × 18 = 54 g (18 g is the mass of 1 mol). (b) **False** ❌: VD has no units; the VD of O₂ is simply 16. (c) **True** ✅: dividing the subscripts of C₂H₆ by 2 gives CH₃. (d) **False** ❌: a coefficient of 2 in front of HCl means 2 **moles** of HCl (which is 2 × 36.5 = 73 g). (e) **True** ✅: H₂ : H₂O(g) = 2 : 2, so 10 L of H₂ produces 10 L of steam at the same T and P. (f) **False** ❌: the atomic mass of Cu is 63.5 **u**; 63.5 g/mol is its molar mass.
