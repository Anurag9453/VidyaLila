# Topic 1: Gay-Lussac and Avogadro: The Law of Counting Gases

At the end of the previous chapter, we asked: how does a chemist count? In Class 9, we built a counting bridge: the **mole**. One mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles, and at STP, 1 mole of any gas occupies a volume of 22.4 L. During electrolysis, we also saw the 2 : 1 volume ratio of H₂ : O₂. Now we will connect this counting bridge to **chemical equations**. The most straightforward first step is **gas volumes**. In reactions involving gases, volumes always combine in ratios of **small whole numbers**, and in this topic we will discover why. Along the way, we will also see why common gases like hydrogen and oxygen exist as **two-atom molecules**.

---

## The Cafe's "Two Parts Water, One Part Milk" Rule

Mr. Brooks took his niece Maya to a busy corner cafe near the train station. The barista had a strict rule for making hot cocoa base: **2 cups of hot water to 1 cup of milk**. For a small batch, he used 2 cups of water and 1 cup of milk; for a medium batch, 6 cups of water and 3 cups of milk; for a large catering order, 60 cups of water and 30 cups of milk. No matter how big the pot was, **the ratio stayed 2 : 1**.

Maya did the math: "Uncle, the small pot has 3 cups in total, and the medium one has 9 cups. Yet the ratio is always 2 : 1. He never needs 'three and a half cups of water' or 'one and a quarter cups of milk'!" Mr. Brooks smiled: "Exactly. Now listen to something fascinating. Gas reactions behave the exact same way. When hydrogen and oxygen react to form steam, **2 litres of hydrogen always need 1 litre of oxygen**, producing 2 litres of steam. If you have 20 litres of hydrogen, you need 10 litres of oxygen — never '1.37 litres'. Just like the cafe's drink recipe, **gases follow a strict recipe written in volumes**."

Maya asked, "Wait, Uncle — the cafe's ratio is chosen by the barista. Who tells the gases to combine in a 2 : 1 ratio?" That is the core question of this topic: **who writes the recipe for gases**, and why does it always come out in whole numbers?

**Try this at home with an adult (no stove needed):** Take two differently sized cups or bowls. Using the first cup as your standard "1 unit," mix cold water and cold milk in a 2 : 1 ratio. Then switch to the second cup size as your "1 unit" and repeat the 2 : 1 ratio. As long as you measure both liquids with **the same cup**, the 2 : 1 ratio holds true. If you measure water with the large cup and milk with the small cup, the ratio is ruined. In this topic, too, **all gas volumes must be measured at the same temperature and pressure**, or the simple ratio falls apart.

(Anything involving a hot stove or burner is strictly for adults. No actual gas reactions are performed in this topic — everything here is **for reading and paper calculations only**.)

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes** | Measured at the same temperature and pressure, the volumes of reacting gases and gaseous products stand in a ratio of small whole numbers | H₂ : Cl₂ : HCl = 1 : 1 : 2 |
| **Avogadro's Law** | Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules | 1 L of H₂ and 1 L of O₂ have the same number of molecules |
| **Molecule** | The smallest group of one or more atoms that moves independently as a single particle in a gas | H₂ (2 atoms), He (1 atom) |
| **Atomicity** | The number of atoms in a single molecule of an element | Atomicity of O₂ is 2 |
| **Monoatomic** | A molecule made of a single atom | He, Ne, Ar |
| **Diatomic** | A molecule made of two atoms | H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂ |
| **Triatomic** | A molecule made of three atoms | O₃ (ozone) |
| **Polyatomic** | A molecule made of more than three atoms | P₄, S₈ |
| **Steam** | The gaseous state of water (at or above roughly 100 °C) | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O(g) |

## Part A: Gay-Lussac's Law: The Volume Recipe for Gases

**First, a quick clarification.** In Class 9 ([gas-laws-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/gas-laws/gas-laws-part4.md)), "Gay-Lussac's law" referred to the **pressure-temperature** relationship (P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂). Here we meet a **second** law by Gay-Lussac, which is about the **combining volumes** of reacting gases. Same scientist, two different laws! Don't let the shared name confuse you: throughout this chapter, "Gay-Lussac's law" always means the **ratio of combining gas volumes**.

In **1808**, the French scientist Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac showed experimentally that when gases react, their volumes always stand in ratios of small whole numbers:

**The Law:** When measured at the same temperature and pressure, the volumes of reacting gases — and of any gaseous products formed — bear a **simple whole-number ratio** to one another.

| Reaction (Equation) | Volume Ratio | In One Line |
|---|---|---|
| H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl | 1 : 1 : 2 | 1 L H₂ and 1 L Cl₂ give 2 L HCl |
| 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (steam) | 2 : 1 : 2 | 2 L H₂ and 1 L O₂ give 2 L steam |
| N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ | 1 : 3 : 2 | 1 L N₂ and 3 L H₂ give 2 L NH₃ |
| CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (steam) | 1 : 2 : 1 : 2 | 1 L methane needs 2 L O₂, giving 1 L CO₂ and 2 L steam |

Notice that the numbers in every volume ratio are **the exact coefficients of the balanced equation**. This is no coincidence — in Part C we will see why.

**Three Important Rules (for paper calculations):**
1. All volumes must be measured at **the same temperature and pressure**. If steam is involved, the temperature must stay high enough for steam to remain a gas.
2. The ratio applies **only to gases**. If a product is a liquid or solid (for instance, liquid water after cooling), its volume is tiny compared to a gas and is not included in the gas volume ratio.
3. The ratio is a ratio of **volumes**, not **masses** (grams). Mass ratios are completely different and will be covered in Topic 4.

```
  H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl : counting with equal-sized boxes

  +---------------+   +---------------+         +---------------+  +---------------+
  :    H₂ H₂ H₂   : + :  Cl₂ Cl₂ Cl₂  :  ====>  :  HCl HCl HCl  :  :  HCl HCl HCl  :
  +---------------+   +---------------+         +---------------+  +---------------+

     1 volume H₂         1 volume Cl₂                  2 volumes HCl
    (3 molecules)       (3 molecules)               (3 + 3 = 6 molecules)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Equal-sized boxes at the same temperature and pressure: 1 volume of H₂ (3 molecules) and 1 volume of Cl₂ (3 molecules) form 2 volumes of HCl. Each box holds 3 molecules (by Avogadro's law), so 3 + 3 = 6 HCl molecules fill two boxes. The drawing is schematic — real gas samples contain on the order of 10²³ molecules. (Chlorine is poisonous: this is strictly a paper illustration.)

## Part B: The "Half-Molecule" Riddle and Avogadro's Solution

Gay-Lussac's experimental results were crystal clear, but they created a **puzzle**. At that time, scientists believed that gases like hydrogen and chlorine were made of **single, individual atoms** (H, Cl). Look at what happens under that assumption:

- 1 volume of hydrogen + 1 volume of chlorine → **2 volumes** of HCl.
- Equal volumes contain equal numbers of particles (as we learned in Class 9). Suppose 1 volume contains *n* particles. Then *n* atoms of H and *n* atoms of Cl produce 2 volumes of HCl, which means **2*n* particles of HCl**.
- To make 2*n* particles of HCl, you need 2*n* H atoms and 2*n* Cl atoms — yet we started with only *n* H atoms and *n* Cl atoms! Every single H and Cl atom would have to **split in two** ("half an atom"). And an atom cannot be cut in half!

In **1811**, the Italian scientist **Amedeo Avogadro** solved this riddle. His insight: the independent particles of hydrogen and chlorine gas are **not single atoms, but pairs of atoms bonded together (molecules)** — H₂ and Cl₂. A molecule splitting into two atoms during a reaction is completely natural: **a molecule can split, whereas an atom cannot**.

```
        1 molecule H₂        1 molecule Cl₂        2 molecules HCl

            H-H        +        Cl-Cl     ====>      H-Cl     H-Cl

   The 2 atoms of H₂ separate, the 2 atoms of Cl₂ separate, and
   two new H-Cl pairs form. Total atoms: 2 H and 2 Cl, both before and after.
```

> **Figure 1.2** – The two atoms in one H₂ molecule and one Cl₂ molecule separate and recombine to form two HCl molecules. Each HCl molecule contains one H and one Cl. 1 molecule of H₂ + 1 molecule of Cl₂ corresponds to a 1 : 1 volume ratio, and 2 molecules of HCl correspond to 2 volumes — giving Gay-Lussac's 1 : 1 : 2 ratio without any "half-atoms."

**Avogadro's Two-Part Breakthrough (1811):** (1) Equal volumes of all gases at the same temperature and pressure contain **equal numbers of molecules**; (2) common elemental gases like hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and chlorine consist of **two-atom (diatomic) molecules**. Together, these two ideas explain Gay-Lussac's law directly. Interestingly, Avogadro's hypothesis was overlooked for **several decades** — it took nearly 50 years before the scientific world fully accepted this simple, elegant idea.

**Why the Law Works (the core takeaway):**
Equal volumes = equal molecules (Avogadro). Therefore, for gases, **volume ratio = molecule ratio = mole ratio = equation coefficient ratio**. That is why Gay-Lussac's ratios are always simple whole numbers: because reactions count whole molecules, and molecule counts are always whole numbers. Just like the barista's "2 cups of water," a gas's "2 volumes" simply means "2 counting units."

## Part C: Atomicity: How Many Atoms in a Molecule?

Now let's see how we know for sure that oxygen is **O₂** rather than **O**. Take the reaction: 2 L hydrogen + 1 L oxygen → 2 L steam. For easy counting, imagine 1 L holds 2 molecules (as shown in Figure 1.3):

- Hydrogen: 4 molecules. Oxygen: 2 molecules. Steam: 4 molecules.
- Each of the 4 steam molecules must contain at least 1 oxygen atom, meaning we need **at least 4 oxygen atoms** in total.
- Yet all those oxygen atoms came from just **2 oxygen molecules**. The only way 2 molecules can supply 4 atoms is if each oxygen molecule contains **2 atoms**!

```
  By volume:     2 volumes H₂   +   1 volume O₂   →   2 volumes H₂O (steam)

  Molecules (each volume = 2 molecules):

   +---------------+     +---------------+       +---------------+
   :   H-H   H-H   :     :               :       : H-O-H   H-O-H :
   +---------------+     :   O=O   O=O   :       +---------------+
   +---------------+     :               :       +---------------+
   :   H-H   H-H   :     +---------------+       : H-O-H   H-O-H :
   +---------------+                             +---------------+

   H atoms: 8 (4 molecules x 2)   O atoms: 4 (2 molecules x 2)   H atoms: 8, O atoms: 4
```

> **Figure 1.3** – 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (steam). Equal-sized boxes (2 molecules per box). Left: 2 boxes of H₂ (4 molecules, 8 H atoms) and 1 box of O₂ (2 molecules, 4 O atoms). Right: 2 boxes of steam (4 molecules), each containing 2 H and 1 O (8 H and 4 O atoms in total). Both atoms and the 2 : 1 : 2 volume ratio are accounted for. Each O₂ molecule gives one O atom to each of two steam molecules, which is why 1 volume of O₂ yields 2 volumes of steam.

**Atomicity Table:**

| Type | Atoms per Molecule | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Monoatomic | 1 | He, Ne, Ar (noble gases) |
| Diatomic | 2 | H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂ |
| Triatomic | 3 | O₃ (ozone) |
| Polyatomic | More than 3 | P₄ (4 atoms), S₈ (8 atoms) |

Different atomicities of the same element create completely different substances: O₂ (the oxygen we breathe) and O₃ (ozone, present in trace amounts with a sharp odour) are both made purely of oxygen atoms. Noble gases like helium travel as single, independent atoms, so helium's formula is simply He, never He₂.

## Part D: Reading an Equation as a Volume Recipe

Back to the cafe recipe! A chemical equation is a recipe, and for gases, its coefficients directly give the **volume ratio**:

**The 3-Step Method:**
1. Write the **balanced equation** (checking atom counts).
2. Read the coefficients of the gases: that is the **volume ratio** (at the same temperature and pressure).
3. **Scale** the given volume using the ratio (just like scaling 2 cups water : 1 cup milk up to 60 : 30).

With this method, **you don't need to calculate moles or grams** when every substance involved is a gas and volumes are given. This shortcut will also come in handy in Topic 5.

## Part E: Solved Examples (Paper-Only, at the Same Temperature and Pressure)

(All gas reactions below are **strictly for reading and paper calculation**. Students should never burn gases, collect gases, or handle gas cylinders.)

### Example 1: How much oxygen does methane need?

**Question:** 20 L of methane (CH₄) burns completely (on paper). What volume of oxygen is required, and what volumes of CO₂ and steam are formed? What is the total gas volume before and after the reaction?

**Solution:**
- CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O(g). Atom check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 2 + 2 = 4.
- Ratio is 1 : 2 : 1 : 2. For 20 L of CH₄ (1 part = 20 L): O₂ = 2 × 20 = **40 L**, CO₂ = **20 L**, steam = 2 × 20 = **40 L**.
- Total volume before: 20 + 40 = 60 L. Total volume after: 20 + 40 = 60 L. The total volume **stays unchanged**.
- **Check:** Sum of coefficients is 1 + 2 = 3 on the left, and 1 + 2 = 3 on the right — no change in volume ✅

### Example 2: Ethyne (acetylene) and oxygen

**Question:** 10 L of ethyne gas (C₂H₂, used in welding torches) is burned completely. What volume of oxygen is needed, and what volumes of CO₂ and steam are produced? (Paper-only: ethyne is highly flammable.)

**Solution:**
- 2C₂H₂ + 5O₂ → 4CO₂ + 2H₂O(g). Atom check: C 4 = 4, H 4 = 4, O 10 = 8 + 2 = 10.
- Ratio is 2 : 5 : 4 : 2. Since 10 L of C₂H₂ represents 2 parts, 1 part = 5 L.
- O₂ = 5 × 5 = **25 L**, CO₂ = 4 × 5 = **20 L**, steam = 2 × 5 = **10 L**.
- Total volume before: 10 + 25 = 35 L; after: 20 + 10 = 30 L. The total volume **decreases** because the sum of coefficients drops from 2 + 5 = 7 to 4 + 2 = 6.
- **Check:** 10 : 25 : 20 : 10 = 2 : 5 : 4 : 2 ✅

### Example 3: Carbon monoxide burning to CO₂

**Question:** 30 L of carbon monoxide (CO) burns in oxygen to form CO₂. What volume of oxygen is used, and what volume of CO₂ forms? By how much does the total volume decrease? (CO is a **poisonous** gas: paper-only.)

**Solution:**
- 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂. Atom check: C 2 = 2, O 2 + 2 = 4 = 4.
- Ratio is 2 : 1 : 2. Since 30 L CO = 2 parts, 1 part = 15 L. O₂ = **15 L**, CO₂ = 2 × 15 = **30 L**.
- Before: 30 + 15 = 45 L. After: 30 L. The total volume **decreases by 15 L** (one-third).
- **Check:** 30 : 15 : 30 = 2 : 1 : 2 ✅

### Example 4: Deducing the equation and atomicity from the volume ratio

**Question:** During a lightning storm, nitrogen and oxygen in the air combine to form nitric oxide (paper-only). In an experiment, 25 mL of nitrogen and 25 mL of oxygen reacted to yield **50 mL** of nitric oxide at the same temperature and pressure. Find the volume ratio. Given that the formula of nitric oxide is NO, what is the atomicity of nitrogen and oxygen?

**Solution:**
- Volume ratio: 25 : 25 : 50 = **1 : 1 : 2**.
- Equal volumes contain equal molecules. Let 1 part = *n* molecules. Then 2*n* molecules of NO are formed, each containing 1 N and 1 O — requiring **2*n* N atoms** and **2*n* O atoms** in total.
- All 2*n* N atoms came from just *n* nitrogen molecules, so each nitrogen molecule must have 2*n* ÷ *n* = **2 atoms**. The same applies to oxygen. Both are **diatomic**: N₂ + O₂ → 2NO.
- **Check (atoms):** Left: N 2, O 2; Right: 2 × (N 1, O 1) = N 2, O 2 ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Confusing Gay-Lussac's combining volumes law with the pressure-temperature law.**
Suppose a friend writes: "By Gay-Lussac's law, 20 L of H₂ needs 10 L of O₂ because P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂." That formula doesn't even contain volume! That is the Class 9 law where the volume of a single gas is held constant. Here, we need the **ratio of volumes**. Quick rule: when **different gases react** and you use equation coefficients, it's the Law of Combining Volumes; when a single gas sample changes pressure and temperature at constant volume, it's the Class 9 law.

**Mistake 2: Assuming total gas volume must stay constant in a reaction.**
In Example 1, the total volume stayed at 60 L, which might tempt you into thinking volume is always conserved. But in Example 2 it dropped from 35 L to 30 L, and in Example 3 from 45 L to 30 L. Gas volume tracks the **number of molecules**: where the sum of gas coefficients decreases, total volume shrinks; where it increases (such as 2NH₃ → N₂ + 3H₂, going from 2 to 4), total volume expands. **Atoms are conserved**, not total volume.

**Mistake 3: Writing oxygen as "O" when counting volumes.**
Suppose a student writes H₂ + O → H₂O and concludes: "1 L of H₂ and 1 L of O give 1 L of steam." In actual experiments, 2 L of H₂ requires only **1 L** of oxygen to form 2 L of steam. The student's ratio is 1 : 1 : 1, whereas experiment gives 2 : 1 : 2. The balanced equation is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: oxygen is **diatomic O₂**, and each O₂ molecule supplies one oxygen atom to each of two steam molecules.

**Mistake 4: Applying the ratio across different temperatures, or counting cooled liquid water as a gas.**
In Example 1, burning 20 L of CH₄ produced 40 L of steam — which holds true only if everything is measured at the same high temperature so that steam stays a gas. If the products are cooled to room temperature, the steam condenses into liquid water whose volume is negligible, leaving only 20 L of CO₂ gas. Thus, the "2 volumes of steam" ratio applies only while the water remains gaseous at the same temperature and pressure.

## Quick Recap

- Gay-Lussac (1808): At the same temperature and pressure, reacting gases and gaseous products combine in **simple whole-number volume ratios** (e.g., H₂ : O₂ : steam = 2 : 1 : 2).
- The Class 9 Gay-Lussac's law (P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂) is a **different** law.
- Avogadro (1811): Equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules, and H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂ are **diatomic**. This resolved the "half-atom" paradox.
- Volume ratio = molecule ratio = mole ratio = coefficient ratio (for gases at the same T and P).
- Atomicity: He, Ne, Ar are monoatomic; H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂ are diatomic; O₃ is triatomic; P₄, S₈ are polyatomic.
- Method: Write the balanced equation, read the gas coefficients as the volume ratio, and scale the given volume.
- Total gas volume can increase, decrease, or remain the same during a reaction. Atoms are always conserved.
- All gas reactions in this topic are strictly for reading and paper calculation.

### Answers

1. Ratio 2 : 1 : 2. 60 L SO₂ = 2 parts, so 1 part = 30 L. O₂ = **30 L**, SO₃ = **60 L**. Before: 60 + 30 = 90 L; after: 60 L — total volume **decreases by 30 L**. Atom check: S 2 = 2, O 4 + 2 = 6 = 6 ✅. Ratio check: 60 : 30 : 60 = 2 : 1 : 2 ✅ (SO₂ and SO₃ are poisonous: paper-only.)
2. Ratio 2 : 3 : 2 : 2. 12 L H₂S = 2 parts, so 1 part = 6 L. O₂ = 3 × 6 = **18 L**, SO₂ = 2 × 6 = **12 L**, steam = 2 × 6 = **12 L**. 3. Ratio 4 : 3 : 2 : 6. 8 L NH₃ = 4 parts, so 1 part = 2 L. O₂ = 3 × 2 = **6 L**, N₂ = 2 × 2 = **4 L**, steam = 6 × 2 = **12 L**. Before: 8 + 6 = 14 L; after: 4 + 12 = 16 L. Atom check: N 4 = 4, H 12 = 12, O 6 = 6 ✅ (Ammonia is a pungent, toxic gas: paper-only.)
4. Ratio 2 : 3 : 2 : 4. 4 L CH₃OH vapour = 2 parts, so 1 part = 2 L. O₂ = 3 × 2 = **6 L**, CO₂ = 2 × 2 = **4 L**, steam = 4 × 2 = **8 L**. Before: 4 + 6 = 10 L; after: 4 + 8 = 12 L — total volume **increases by 2 L**. Atom check: C 2 = 2, H 8 = 8, O 2 + 6 = 8 = 4 + 4 ✅

6. Ratio 2 : 7 : 4 : 6. 6 L C₂H₆ = 2 parts, so 1 part = 3 L. O₂ = 7 × 3 = **21 L**, CO₂ = 4 × 3 = 12 L, steam = 6 × 3 = 18 L. Upon cooling, the steam condenses into liquid water (negligible volume), leaving only **12 L of CO₂** gas. Atom check: C 4 = 4, H 12 = 12, O 14 = 8 + 6 = 14 ✅
7. Ratio 2 : 3. 10 L O₃ = 2 parts, so 1 part = 5 L. O₂ = 3 × 5 = **15 L**. Atom check: left 2 × 3 = 6, right 3 × 2 = 6 ✅. Volume **increases by 5 L** because every 2 ozone molecules reorganize into 3 oxygen molecules — gas volume tracks molecule count. Check: 10 : 15 = 2 : 3 ✅
8. **Avogadro's Law**: Equal volumes contain equal molecules, so 3 L holds 7.5 × 10²² molecules. 1 L holds 7.5 × 10²² ÷ 3 = 2.5 × 10²². 5 L holds 5 × 2.5 × 10²² = **1.25 × 10²³** molecules. Check: 1.25 × 10²³ ÷ 5 = 2.5 × 10²² = 7.5 × 10²² ÷ 3 ✅
9. Reactions with a 2 : 1 : 2 ratio: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (steam) and 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ (or 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃). The ratio is fixed by the balanced coefficients: 2 molecules of the first gas react with 1 molecule of the second so that atoms are conserved. A 1 : 1 : 2 ratio would not conserve atoms — for example, in H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, oxygen atoms balance (2 on each side), but hydrogen has 2 on the left and 4 on the right. The ratio comes from atom conservation in the balanced equation, not personal choice.
10. Ratio 2 : 1 : 2. 18 mL NO = 2 parts, so 1 part = 9 mL. O₂ = **9 mL**, NO₂ = **18 mL**. Before: 18 + 9 = 27 mL; after: 18 mL — total volume **goes down**. Atom check: N 2 = 2, O 2 + 2 = 4 = 4 ✅ (NO₂ is poisonous: paper-only.)
11. Class 9 law: relates P and T of a single gas at constant volume (P₁/T₁ = P₂/T₂), with no chemical reaction. This chapter's law: gives the ratio of volumes of different reacting gases and gaseous products at the same T and P, matching equation coefficients. For H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, use **Gay-Lussac's Law of Combining Volumes** (1 : 1 : 2).
12. (a) **True** ✅: equal volumes at the same T and P have equal numbers of molecules, regardless of particle size. (b) **False** ❌: total gas volume can increase or decrease (e.g., 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ goes from 3 volumes to 2). (c) **False** ❌: oxygen gas is diatomic **O₂** (two atoms per molecule). (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌: the H₂ : O₂ volume ratio is **2 : 1**, not 2 : 2.
