# Topic 2: Ionisation Energy: The Price of Removing an Electron

In Topic 1, we measured the **size** of atoms and ions and saw that just two ideas explain the entire pattern: **number of shells** (distance) and **nuclear pull** (inward pull of protons, reduced by inner-shell shielding). Now let's ask a new question: **if we want to pull an outermost electron completely away from an atom, how much energy will it cost?** That energy cost differs from element to element, and it tells us which elements give up electrons easily (to form cations) and which cling to their electrons tightly. This "price of removing an electron" is called **ionisation energy**. Same two core ideas — applied to a brand-new question!

---

## A Tug-of-War Over a Plush Toy

On Sunday afternoon, Ethan was visiting his uncle Mr. Davies for a family gathering. Ethan's eighteen-month-old cousin Lily was sitting on the rug hugging a small rubber duck. Playfully, Ethan reached out: "Can I borrow your duck for a second?" Lily immediately hugged the toy tight against her chest with both arms. When Ethan gave a gentle tug, she held on even tighter.

Half an hour later, Lily was distracted watching a cartoon, holding the rubber duck loosely at arm's length with just two fingers. Ethan gave the exact same gentle tug, and the toy slipped effortlessly into his hand before Lily even noticed.

"Uncle, look at that!" Ethan laughed. "Earlier it was impossible to budge, and just now it took almost zero effort — same toy, same me!"

Mr. Davies smiled. "The difference was her **grip**. When the toy was held right against her chest with both arms, it would have taken a lot of force to pull it away. When it was held far out at arm's length with a loose grip, it took very little effort. Atoms work the exact same way: how tightly a nucleus holds onto its outermost electron determines how much **energy** is needed to remove it. And just two factors control that grip — the exact two factors you learned in Topic 1: **distance (shells)** and **nuclear pull**."

```
    Held tightly (close to the chest)     Held loosely (out at arm's length)

         _____                                  _____
        ( ^ ^ )                                ( ^ ^ )
         \___/ [toy]                            \___/
          / \  tight grip                        / \  ---------- [toy]
                                                      far out, loose grip

    Takes a lot of effort to remove       Takes very little effort to remove
    (close & tight = high ionisation      (far & loose = low ionisation
    energy)                               energy)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – The plush-toy analogy: the closer and tighter the grip, the more energy it takes to pull the toy away. In an atom, the toy is the outermost electron, and the grip is the inward pull of the nucleus.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ionisation energy (IE)** | The energy required to remove the most loosely held (outermost) electron from an isolated gaseous atom | For Na → Na⁺ + e⁻, IE is about 496 kJ per mol |
| **Gaseous atom** | An isolated atom in the gas phase, far away from neighbouring atoms | A single sodium atom in sodium vapour |
| **First ionisation energy** | The energy needed to remove the first outermost electron from a neutral atom (what is meant when we simply say "IE") | About 496 kJ per mol for Na |
| **Second ionisation energy** | The energy needed to remove a second electron from the **+1 ion** left behind after the first electron is removed | For Na⁺ → Na²⁺ + e⁻, about 4562 kJ per mol |
| **kJ per mol** | The unit of ionisation energy: the total energy in kilojoules required to remove one electron from every atom in 1 mole of gaseous atoms | 496 kJ per mol |
| **Cation** | A positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons | Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺ |
| **Closed inner shell** | A completely filled electron shell closer to the nucleus | The 2, 8 shells of Na⁺ |

## Part A: What Is Ionisation Energy?

Imagine a single, isolated sodium atom floating in the gas phase. We want to remove its outermost electron. That electron will not fly off on its own — we must **put energy in** from outside to overcome the attractive pull of the positively charged nucleus. The minimum energy required to pull that electron completely away is the **ionisation energy**:

**Na(g) + energy → Na⁺(g) + e⁻**

Or in general form for any element M: **M(g) + energy → M⁺(g) + e⁻**

Remember: because energy must be **supplied** (absorbed) to pull a negative electron away from a positive nucleus, ionisation energy is always a **positive** quantity — removing an electron always costs energy, never happens for free.

```
      Before: Neutral Na atom                 After: Na⁺ ion + free electron

       +11 nucleus                             +11 nucleus
      shells: 2, 8, 1                          shells: 2, 8
         o                                                     o --> e⁻ (removed)
      ( o )  ( o o ... )                       ( o )  ( o o ... )
       1 outer electron                         only the 2, 8 shells remain

      11 protons, 11 electrons                11 protons, 10 electrons
      net charge = 0                          net charge = +1
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Na → Na⁺ + e⁻: the single outermost electron is removed, leaving 11 protons and 10 electrons (a +1 cation). The energy supplied to remove that electron is the first ionisation energy.

**Why must the atom be in the gaseous state?** In a solid or liquid, neighbouring atoms are packed closely around each atom and tug on its electrons too. To measure the true grip of **one single atom's nucleus** on its own electron without interference from neighbours, the atom must be isolated in the gas phase.

**Why is the unit kJ per mol?** The energy required to remove one electron from a single atom is tinier than a trillionth of a trillionth of a joule. Instead of writing awkward tiny numbers, chemists measure the energy needed to remove one electron from **every atom in 1 mole** (6.022 × 10²³ atoms — our counting unit from Class 9 and Chapter 5), expressed in kilojoules per mole (**kJ per mol**, where 1 kJ = 1000 J).

**Second ionisation energy (in one line):** After the first electron is removed to leave an M⁺ ion, the energy required to remove a *second* electron (M⁺ → M²⁺ + e⁻) is called the **second ionisation energy** — and it is always larger than the first (in Part C we will see just how much larger!).

## Part B: Periodic Trends: Down a Group and Across a Period

Let's apply our two core ideas. Think of Lily's grip on the rubber duck: when the electron is held close with a strong nuclear pull, removing it costs a lot of energy; when it sits far out behind many shielding shells, removing it is much easier.

### Down a Group: Ionisation Energy Decreases

Look at Group 1: Li, Na, K, ..., Cs. As we move down the group, **more electron shells are added**, placing the outermost electron farther and farther from the nucleus behind more layers of inner-shell **shielding**. Because the nucleus's grip weakens down the group, removing the outer electron becomes easier and easier.

Approximate first ionisation energies (in kJ per mol): **Li 520, Na 496, K 419, Cs 376.**

```
    Group 1: First Ionisation Energy (approx.)     1 # = 20 kJ per mol

    Li   ##########################   520
    Na   #########################    496
    K    #####################        419
    Cs   ###################           376

    Going down: more shells, outer electron farther out, IE decreases
```

> **Figure 2.3** – First ionisation energy decreases down Group 1: Li holds its outer electron tightest, while Cs holds its outer electron most loosely.

We see the exact same trend in Group 2: Mg is about 738 kJ per mol and Ca (one row below Mg) is about 590 kJ per mol — Ca's outer electrons sit one shell farther out, so they are easier to remove.

### Across a Period: Ionisation Energy Increases

Moving from left to right across a period, the number of shells stays constant while **the number of protons increases**. As a result, the inward nuclear pull grows stronger, the atom shrinks (Topic 1), and the outer electrons are held more tightly. Therefore, **ionisation energy increases across a period**, reaching its **highest peak at the noble gas** at the end of the row.

Period 3: **Na ~496, Mg ~738, Ar ~1521 kJ per mol.**
Period 2: **Li ~520, Ne ~2081 kJ per mol.**

```
    Period 3 (from start to end)                   1 # = 50 kJ per mol

    Na   ##########                 496
    Mg   ###############            738
    Ar   ##############################   1521     (noble gas: highest in row)

    Period 2

    Li   ##########                 520
    Ne   ##########################################   2081
```

> **Figure 2.4** – Across a period, from the alkali metal on the far left (Na, Li) to the noble gas on the far right (Ar, Ne), ionisation energy climbs sharply: same number of shells, more protons, tighter grip.

**A quick note for higher classes:** Between a few adjacent elements in the middle of a period (such as from Mg to Al), there is a small dip in IE caused by subshell structure, which you will study in higher classes. The overall trend across any period remains a strong **increase from left to right**.

**One-Line Summary of the Grip:** Down a group = farther out + more shielding = **lower IE**. Across a period = more protons + same shells = **higher IE**.

## Part C: Removing Electrons One After Another: Successive Ionisation Energies

Now for a fascinating question: once the first electron is removed, what does it cost to remove a second electron, or a third? The numbers reveal the exact reason why **sodium only forms Na⁺ and never Na²⁺**, whereas **magnesium forms Mg²⁺**!

### The Case of Sodium (Na)

Sodium's electron configuration is 2, 8, 1. Compare its first and second ionisation energies:

| Which Electron Is Removed? | Ionisation Energy (kJ per mol, approx.) |
|---|---|
| 1st electron (the single outer 3rd-shell electron) | 496 |
| 2nd electron (pulled from the filled 2, 8 inner shell) | 4562 |

Removing the second electron costs **more than 9 times as much energy** as removing the first (4562 ÷ 496 = 9.2)!

**Why such a massive jump?** The first electron was sitting all alone in the 3rd shell, far from the nucleus and shielded by 10 inner electrons (2, 8). Once it leaves, the second electron must be ripped out of the **completely filled 2nd shell, which lies much closer to the nucleus** with far less shielding! Furthermore, all 11 protons are now pulling on just 10 electrons. Because that inner shell is held with an iron grip, breaking into it requires an enormous leap in energy.

### The Case of Magnesium (Mg)

Magnesium's electron configuration is 2, 8, 2. Look at its first three ionisation energies:

| Which Electron Is Removed? | Ionisation Energy (kJ per mol, approx.) |
|---|---|
| 1st electron (from the 3rd shell) | 738 |
| 2nd electron (the second 3rd-shell electron) | 1451 |
| 3rd electron (pulled from the filled 2, 8 inner shell) | 7733 |

Here, the second IE is about twice the first (1451 ÷ 738 = 1.97 — a normal increase because you are pulling an electron away from a positively charged Mg⁺ ion). But the **third IE jumps by more than 5.3 times** (7733 ÷ 1451 = 5.3)! The giant leap happens at the **third electron**, right when we try to break into the filled 2, 8 inner shell.

```
    Successive Ionisation Energies (1 # = 200 kJ per mol, approx.)

    Na   1st    ##                                      496
         2nd    #######################                       4562  <-- HUGE JUMP!

    Mg   1st    ####                                    738
         2nd    #######                                1451
         3rd    #######################################   7733  <-- HUGE JUMP!
```

> **Figure 2.5** – In sodium, the giant energy jump occurs right after the 1st electron (at the 2nd IE). In magnesium, the first two steps are modest and the giant jump occurs after the 2nd electron (at the 3rd IE). The giant jump always marks the boundary where you break into a filled inner shell.

### What Does This Tell Us?

- **Sodium:** The 1st electron comes off easily (496), while the 2nd is prohibitively expensive (4562). So in chemical reactions, Na loses **only 1 electron** to form **Na⁺** (1 valence electron).
- **Magnesium:** The first 2 electrons come off at reasonable cost (738 and 1451), while the 3rd is prohibitively expensive (7733). So Mg loses **2 electrons** to form **Mg²⁺** (2 valence electrons).
- **General Rule for Counting Valence Electrons:** Look for the **giant jump** in successive ionisation energies. **The number of electrons removed *before* the giant jump equals the number of valence electrons!**

In Class 9, we linked valence electrons to group numbers and ionic charges; successive ionisation energies give us the direct **experimental proof** of that rule!

## Part D: Noble Gases Sit at the Highest Peaks

Noble gases have **completely filled outer shells** (He: 2; Ne: 2, 8; Ar: 2, 8, 8) and the highest proton counts in their respective periods. Because their outer electrons are pulled tightly inward, noble gases have the **highest ionisation energies in their periods**: **He ~2372, Ne ~2081, Ar ~1521 kJ per mol.**

Notice also that as you go **down Group 18** (He → Ne → Ar), ionisation energy decreases because each step down adds a new electron shell — the exact same group trend we saw in Part B.

```
    Group 18: IE decreases down the group               1 # = 50 kJ per mol

    He   ###############################################   2372
    Ne   ##########################################        2081
    Ar   ##############################                    1521
```

> **Figure 2.6** – He, Ne, and Ar have exceptionally high ionisation energies (which explains why noble gases are chemically unreactive or "inert"), while still showing the standard decrease down the group as more shells are added.

## Part E: How Cations Form and Reactivity in Group 1

### Forming Cations

An element with a **low ionisation energy** gives up its outer electron(s) easily and readily forms **cations**. That is why the metals of Group 1 and Group 2 (K, Na, Ca, Mg) are the champions of cation formation: their ionisation energies are low. These are the exact cations we met in ionic compounds (Chapter 2) and migrating toward the **cathode** during electrolysis (Chapter 4).

### Reactivity Trend in Group 1

In Class 9 ([periodic-table-part5.md](../../../class9/chemistry/periodic-table/periodic-table-part5.md)), we learned that alkali metal reactivity increases down the group: **Li < Na < K**. Now we have the exact energy explanation: when an alkali metal reacts, it must **lose its outer electron**. **The lower the ionisation energy, the more easily the atom loses its electron, and the more vigorously the metal reacts!** Because K has a lower IE (419 kJ/mol) than Na (496 kJ/mol), potassium loses its electron more readily and reacts even more violently than sodium. (**Safety Note:** The reaction of alkali metals with water is strictly for reading or a teacher demonstration behind a safety screen — students must never touch or handle sodium or potassium.)

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Arrange Li, Na, K in Increasing Order of First Ionisation Energy

- All three are in Group 1. Li has 2 shells, Na has 3 shells, and K has 4 shells.
- Going down the group: more shells → outer electron is farther from the nucleus with more inner-shell shielding → weaker grip.
- Therefore, IE decreases down the group, so in increasing order: **K < Na < Li**.

**Check:** Approximate values: 419 < 496 < 520 kJ per mol ✅.

### Example 2. Which Has the Higher Ionisation Energy: Mg or Ca?

- Mg has 3 shells (2, 8, 2); Ca has 4 shells (2, 8, 8, 2). Both are in Group 2.
- Ca's outer electrons sit one shell farther out behind more shielding shells.
- Therefore, **Mg has the higher ionisation energy** (and Ca has the lower IE).

**Check:** Mg ~738 > Ca ~590 kJ per mol ✅.

### Example 3. Arrange Na, Mg, Ar in Increasing Order Across Period 3

- All three are in Period 3 (3 shells). Protons: Na 11, Mg 12, Ar 18.
- Same number of shells + more protons = stronger nuclear pull = tighter grip on outer electrons.
- Increasing order: **Na < Mg < Ar**.

**Check:** 496 < 738 < 1521 kJ per mol ✅ (with the noble gas Ar at the top).

### Example 4. Why Does Sodium Form Na⁺ and Never Na²⁺?

- For Na: 1st IE is ~496 kJ/mol, whereas 2nd IE jumps to ~4562 kJ/mol.
- After the single 3rd-shell electron is removed, the 2nd electron would have to be pulled from the stable, filled 2nd shell (2, 8) close to the nucleus.
- Chemical reactions cannot supply that huge 4562 kJ/mol cost, so sodium loses **only 1 electron** to form **Na⁺**.

**Check:** Na → Na⁺ + e⁻ leaves 11 − 1 = 10 electrons and 11 protons, giving a +1 charge ✅.

### Example 5. Why Does Magnesium Form Mg²⁺?

- For Mg: 1st IE is ~738, 2nd IE is ~1451, and 3rd IE jumps to ~7733 kJ/mol.
- The giant jump occurs after the first two electrons are removed, so Mg loses **2 electrons** to form **Mg²⁺**: Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻.

**Check:** Removing 2 electrons from (2, 8, 2) leaves the filled (2, 8) core (10 electrons and 12 protons, charge +2) ✅.

### Example 6. Finding Valence Electrons from a Table of Successive IEs

An unknown element X has the following approximate successive ionisation energies (in kJ per mol): **578, 1817, 2745, 11577.**

- 578 → 1817: about 3.1× increase.
- 1817 → 2745: about 1.5× increase.
- 2745 → 11577: about **4.2× increase — a massive jump of nearly 9000 kJ/mol!**
- Because the giant jump occurs on the 4th electron, the first **3 electrons** are in the outer shell: X has **3 valence electrons**, belongs to **Group 13**, and forms the **X³⁺** ion (this element is aluminium, Al³⁺).

**Check:** Al has configuration (2, 8, 3); removing 3 electrons leaves the (2, 8) core (10 electrons, 13 protons, charge +3) ✅.

### Example 7. Energy Calculations Using Moles

**Question 1:** How much energy is required to remove one electron from every atom in 2 moles of gaseous sodium atoms? (First IE of Na ≈ 496 kJ per mol.)

- Energy = 2 mol × 496 kJ/mol = **992 kJ**.
- **Check:** 1 mol takes 496 kJ, so 2 mol takes double = 992 kJ ✅.

**Question 2:** How much total energy is required to convert 1 mole of gaseous magnesium atoms completely into Mg²⁺ ions? (Use 1st IE ≈ 738 and 2nd IE ≈ 1451 kJ per mol.)

- Total energy per mole = 738 + 1451 = **2189 kJ**.
- **Check:** 2189 − 738 = 1451 ✅.

### Example 8. Comparing Group 18 Noble Gases: He, Ne, Ar

Which noble gas has the highest first ionisation energy among He, Ne, and Ar?

- Helium has only 1 shell (2 electrons right next to the nucleus with zero inner-shell shielding).
- Moving down to Ne (2 shells) and Ar (3 shells) increases distance and shielding, so IE decreases down the group.
- Order: **He > Ne > Ar**.

**Check:** ~2372 > ~2081 > ~1521 kJ per mol ✅.

### Example 9. Easiest vs. Hardest to Ionise Across Period 3

Among Na, Mg, and Ar in Period 3, which atom loses an outer electron most easily, and which loses one with the greatest difficulty?

- **Most easily:** **Na** (lowest IE, 496 kJ/mol).
- **Greatest difficulty:** **Ar** (highest IE, 1521 kJ/mol).

**Check:** Na is the largest atom in Period 3 (Topic 1), so its outer electron feels the weakest grip ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Thinking that "high ionisation energy" means an atom loses electrons easily.**

This is completely backwards! Ionisation energy is the **price you must pay** to remove an electron. A high price means the electron is **hard** to remove, not easy! Think back to Lily hugging the rubber duck tightly against her chest: high effort required = hard to take away.

**Let's prove it with three elements:** Na (496), Ne (2081), and K (419). Which atom loses an electron most easily?

- Low IE = easy to remove. K has the lowest price (419), followed by Na (496), while Ne has a sky-high price (2081).
- Ease of losing an electron: **K > Na > Ne**.
- Because neon's IE is huge (2081), neon refuses to lose electrons — which is why neon is an unreactive noble gas!

**Two quick self-check questions whenever you feel confused:**
1. Between K and Ne, which one readily forms a +1 cation? K⁺ (easily); Ne⁺ never forms in normal chemistry.
2. If "high IE" meant "loses electrons easily," then neon would be the most reactive metal in Period 2 — when in reality it is an inert gas!

Memory trick: **Think of IE as a price tag in dollars — the higher the price tag, the harder it is to buy that electron away from the atom.**

## Quick Recap

- **Ionisation energy (IE)** = the energy required to remove the outermost electron from an isolated gaseous atom: M(g) + energy → M⁺(g) + e⁻, measured in **kJ per mol**.
- Because energy must be put in to overcome nuclear attraction, IE is always positive.
- **Down a group:** More shells + more shielding → outer electron is farther out → IE **decreases** (e.g., Li 520, Na 496, K 419, Cs 376 kJ/mol).
- **Across a period:** Same shells + more protons → stronger nuclear pull → IE **increases** (e.g., Na 496, Mg 738, Ar 1521 kJ/mol).
- **Noble gases** have the highest IE in their respective periods (He 2372, Ne 2081, Ar 1521 kJ/mol).
- **Successive ionisation energies:** The 2nd IE is always larger than the 1st. A **giant jump** occurs when an electron must be removed from a filled inner shell (2nd IE for Na at 4562; 3rd IE for Mg at 7733).
- Counting how many electrons are removed *before* the giant jump tells you the number of **valence electrons** and the cation charge (Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺).
- **Low IE = loses electrons easily** = reactive metal that readily forms cations (e.g., K is more reactive than Na because 419 < 496).
- Never confuse high IE with losing electrons easily — high IE means the atom holds its electrons tightly!
