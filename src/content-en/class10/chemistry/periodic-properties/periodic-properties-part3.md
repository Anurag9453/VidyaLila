# Topic 3: Electron Affinity: The Eagerness to Gain an Electron

In Topic 1, we explored atomic size, and in Topic 2 we measured the energy price of **removing** an electron from an atom (ionisation energy). Now let's look at the other side of the coin: while some atoms hate losing electrons, many of those very same atoms are **eager to gain** an extra electron! In this topic, we measure how much energy an atom **releases** when it captures an incoming electron, and we see why the halogens are so eager to grab an electron while the noble gases couldn't care less. Once again, the exact same two core ideas drive everything: **number of shells (distance)** and **nuclear pull (after shielding)**.

---

## Eagerness to Catch the Ball

On Saturday morning, Oliver and his niece Mia were watching a community baseball game at the neighbourhood park. In the third inning, a batter hit a high pop-up toward shortstop. Noah, playing shortstop, sprinted forward, snapped his glove shut around the ball, and pumped his fist with excitement. On the very next play, a gentle fly ball drifted toward the right fielder, Toby, who took a half-hearted step back and let the ball drop untouched onto the grass.

Mia laughed: "Uncle Oliver, Noah is dying to catch every single ball that comes near him, while Toby acts like he doesn't even want the ball in his glove! They're on the same field — why the huge difference?"

Oliver smiled: "Atoms behave the exact same way when an extra electron drifts by. Some atoms — like Noah — **eagerly snatch up** an incoming electron, releasing a burst of energy the moment it locks into place. Other atoms have almost no interest in catching an extra electron. And a few — like Toby — have their gloves completely full already, so the electron just bounces right off." He turned to Mia: "Today, let's figure out which atoms in the Periodic Table are 'Noahs' and which are 'Tobys', and where that eagerness comes from."

```
    Eager fielder (like Cl)              Indifferent fielder (like Ne)

         ball  -->  ( open glove )            ball  -->     ( closed glove )
                          v                                        v
                      CAUGHT IT!                            drops to the ground
                 (burst of energy released)                  (zero interest)
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Two fielders: one eagerly catches the incoming ball, while the other lets it drop. Among atoms, chlorine (Cl) is like the eager fielder (snatching up an electron and releasing energy), while neon (Ne) is like the indifferent fielder (having zero interest in an extra electron).

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electron affinity (EA)** | The amount of energy released when an isolated gaseous atom gains one extra electron to form a negative ion | When Cl gains an electron, it releases about 349 kJ per mol |
| **Gaseous atom** | An isolated atom in the gas phase, not bonded to neighbours (just as in Topic 2) | Cl(g) |
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons | Cl⁻, F⁻, O²⁻ |
| **Energy released** | Energy given off to the surroundings during a process (like the smack of a ball landing in a fielder's glove) | Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) + energy |
| **Affinity (eagerness)** | How strongly an atom attracts and welcomes an incoming electron | Halogens have a very high electron affinity |
| **Full outer shell** | A completely filled valence shell that has no room for additional electrons | Ne (2, 8), Ar (2, 8, 8) |

## Part A: What Is Electron Affinity?

Picture an **isolated gaseous atom** floating alone. Now bring one extra electron near it. If the atom's nucleus attracts that incoming electron into its outer shell, the electron locks into place and **energy is released**. That released energy is the atom's **electron affinity (EA)**:

**X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g) + energy**

The unit is the same as in Topic 2: **kJ per mol** (for 1 mole of gaseous atoms). Notice the crucial contrast with Topic 2: in ionisation energy, energy had to be **put in** to pull an electron off; in electron affinity, energy is **released** when an electron is captured!

```
    Ionisation Energy (Topic 2)             Electron Affinity (Topic 3)

    Na  +  energy  -->  Na+  +  e-          Cl  +  e-  -->  Cl-  +  energy

    REMOVING an electron from an atom       ADDING an electron to an atom
    Energy is ABSORBED (put in)             Energy is RELEASED (given off)
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Ionisation energy and electron affinity are opposite processes: in IE, an electron leaves the atom (requiring energy input); in EA, an extra electron joins the atom (releasing energy).

Here are approximate electron affinity values (in kJ per mol of energy **released**):

| Atom | Electron Affinity (kJ per mol, approx.) |
|---|---|
| Li | 60 |
| O (first electron) | 141 |
| F | 328 |
| S | 200 |
| Cl | 349 |
| Br | 325 |
| I | 295 |
| N | ~0 (studied in higher classes) |
| Ne, Ar | ~0 (no benefit in gaining an electron) |

Two patterns jump out from this table immediately: first, **non-metals** (especially the halogens F, Cl, Br, I, as well as O and S) have large electron affinities; second, **chlorine (Cl) sits at the very top** of the list! Let's see why.

*(Quick note: Saying "Cl has an electron affinity of 349" does **not** mean chlorine has 349 electrons! It means 1 mole of Cl atoms releases 349 kilojoules of energy when each atom captures one electron.)*

---

## Part B: Where Does This Eagerness Come From? The Two Core Ideas

Just like in Topics 1 and 2, our two core ideas explain the entire pattern:
1. **Number of shells (distance):** How close to the nucleus is the outer shell where the incoming electron will sit?
2. **Nuclear pull:** How strong is the net inward pull of the nucleus (protons minus inner-shell shielding)?

When the incoming electron enters a shell **close to the nucleus** that experiences a **strong net nuclear pull**, the new electron is snapped up tightly and a **large amount of energy is released** (high EA). When the outer shell is **far from the nucleus** or feels a **weak net pull**, much less energy is released (low EA).

### Example 1. Where Does the Incoming Electron Go in Cl and F?

- Chlorine has the configuration 2, 8, 7. Its outer shell has 7 electrons — just **1 vacancy short** of a stable octet of 8. The incoming electron drops right into that open spot, completing the octet: 2, 8, 8 (the exact configuration of argon!).
- Furthermore, Cl has 17 protons and only 10 inner-shell electrons (net pull ≈ 7), so the incoming electron is pulled in strongly.

**Check:** Cl⁻ has 17 + 1 = 18 electrons: 2, 8, 8 ✅ (a stable noble-gas octet).

### Example 2. Across Period 2: Li, O, F

All three elements are in Period 2, so the incoming electron enters the **2nd shell** in each case. As we move from left to right, proton count increases: Li (3), O (8), F (9).

- Li (2, 1): Only 3 protons (weak net pull of 1) → low EA (**~60 kJ/mol**).
- O (2, 6): 8 protons (stronger net pull of 6) → higher EA (**~141 kJ/mol**).
- F (2, 7): 9 protons (strongest net pull of 7, and just 1 electron short of a full octet) → very high EA (**~328 kJ/mol**).

**Check:** 60 < 141 < 328 ✅ (matching proton counts 3 < 8 < 9). Across a period from left to right, **electron affinity generally increases**.

*(An honest note: Nitrogen (2, 5) in Period 2 has an EA near zero because of subshell pairing effects, which you will study in higher classes. At Class 10 level, focus on the overall increase across a period Toward Group 17.)*

### Example 3. Down Group 17: Cl, Br, I

Compare Cl (3 shells), Br (4 shells), and I (5 shells). As you go down the group, the incoming electron must enter an outer shell that sits **farther from the nucleus** behind more layers of inner-shell **shielding**. Because the nucleus's pull at that greater distance is weaker, less energy is released:

- Cl (3rd shell): **~349 kJ/mol**
- Br (4th shell): **~325 kJ/mol**
- I (5th shell): **~295 kJ/mol**

**Check:** 349 > 325 > 295 ✅. Down Group 17 (from Cl to I), **electron affinity decreases**.

```
    Group 17: The new electron's home moves farther out down the group

    Cl   [ 2 ] [ 8 ] [ 7+1 ]                   EA ~ 349   (3rd shell, close)
    Br   [ 2 ] [ 8 ] [ 18 ] [ 7+1 ]            EA ~ 325   (4th shell, farther)
    I    [ 2 ] [ 8 ] [ 18 ] [ 18 ] [ 7+1 ]     EA ~ 295   (5th shell, even farther)

    +1 = the incoming electron
```

> **Figure 3.3** – In Cl, Br, and I, the incoming electron lands in the 3rd, 4th, and 5th shells respectively. The farther the outer shell is from the nucleus, the weaker the attraction and the less energy is released.

### Example 4. Why Is Chlorine Slightly Higher Than Fluorine? (An Honest Note)

Looking at the group trend, you might expect fluorine (at the top of Group 17, 2nd shell) to have an even higher EA than chlorine. In actual measurements, fluorine (~328 kJ/mol) is **slightly lower** than chlorine (~349 kJ/mol). Why? Because fluorine's 2nd shell is so tiny (~64 pm) that its 7 outer electrons are packed tightly together and repel the incoming 8th electron slightly. You will study this detail in higher classes; for now, simply remember that **chlorine has the highest electron affinity of any element**, and from Cl → Br → I the trend decreases smoothly down the group.

## Part C: Indifferent Noble Gases and Reluctant Metals

**Noble gases** (He, Ne, Ar) already have **completely filled outer shells**: Ne is (2, 8) and Ar is (2, 8, 8). There is zero room left in the outer shell! If you try to force an extra electron onto a neon atom, it has to sit in the brand-new 3rd shell, far from the nucleus and completely shielded (and repelled) by all 10 inner electrons. As a result, noble gases have **virtually zero electron affinity** (~0). They are just like Toby in the outfield — their glove is already full!

**Metals** (like Li and Na) have only 1 valence electron, large atomic radii, and weak net nuclear pull. Because their nuclei pull weakly on incoming electrons, metals have **very small electron affinities** (e.g., Li is ~60 kJ/mol compared to F at ~328 kJ/mol). Metals are much happier **losing** their outer electron to form cations (Topic 2) than gaining one.

| Group | Valence Electrons | Electron Tendency | Electron Affinity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Group 1 (e.g., Li, Na) | 1 | Wants to **lose** 1 electron | Small |
| Group 17 (F, Cl, Br, I) | 7 | Eager to **gain** 1 electron | Very large |
| Group 18 (Ne, Ar) | 8 (2 for He) | Neither loses nor gains | ~0 |

### Example 5. Which Atom Welcomes an Electron More Eagerly: Ar or Cl?

**Question:** Between Ar and Cl, which atom has the higher electron affinity, and why?

- Cl (2, 8, 7) has 1 open spot in its 3rd shell and a strong net nuclear pull, so gaining 1 electron completes a stable (2, 8, 8) octet.
- Ar (2, 8, 8) already has a full 3rd shell; an extra electron would be forced into the distant, heavily shielded 4th shell.

**Answer: Cl.** **Check:** EA of Cl is ~349 kJ/mol, while EA of Ar is ~0 ✅.

### Example 6. Same Period Comparison: Ne vs. F

**Question:** Both Ne (2, 8) and F (2, 7) are in Period 2. Which one eagerly gains an electron?

- F needs just 1 electron to complete its 2nd shell (2, 8) and has 9 protons pulling strongly.
- Ne already has a full 2nd shell, so its EA is ~0.

**Answer: F.** **Check:** F (~328 kJ/mol) > Ne (~0) ✅.

## Part D: Forming Anions and "Sodium Gives, Chlorine Takes"

When a non-metal atom captures one or more electrons, it becomes a negatively charged **anion**: Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻, F + e⁻ → F⁻, and for oxygen, O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻. (Adding the first electron to O releases ~141 kJ/mol; pushing a second negative electron onto an already negative O⁻ ion actually requires energy input, a detail covered in higher classes.)

Think back to Chapter 4 (*Electrolysis*): during electrolysis, **anions migrate to the anode** (the positive electrode, [electrolysis-part2.md](../electrolysis/electrolysis-part2.md)). When Cl⁻ reaches the anode, it gives back that extra electron to become Cl again — the exact reverse of electron affinity!

**Two Sides of an Ionic Bond: "Sodium Gives, Chlorine Takes."** In sodium chloride (NaCl), sodium has a low ionisation energy (Topic 2: easy to remove its outer electron), while chlorine has a high electron affinity (Topic 3: eager to grab an electron and release ~349 kJ/mol). They are a perfect match: **one eager giver, one eager taker!**

```
    Na (2, 8, 1)        Cl (2, 8, 7)

        Na  ----- e- ----->  Cl
                   v
        Na+ (2, 8)           Cl- (2, 8, 8)
        The Giver            The Taker

        Na+ and Cl- attract each other strongly to form NaCl
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Sodium transfers its single outer electron to chlorine. Na becomes Na⁺ (2, 8) and Cl becomes Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8), giving both ions stable, filled outer shells.

Similarly, in MgO, Mg gives 2 electrons and O takes 2 electrons (forming Mg²⁺ and O²⁻). In CaF₂, Ca gives 2 electrons and two F atoms each take 1 electron (forming Ca²⁺ and 2 F⁻). *(Note: The overall energy stability of an ionic crystal also depends heavily on the strong attraction of ions packing together in a 3D crystal lattice, which you will study in higher classes. Here, focus on the giver-and-taker partnership.)*

*(Table salt in your kitchen is safe NaCl, whereas pure Cl₂ gas is toxic and is never handled by students — paper study only.)*

### Example 7. Identifying the Giver and the Taker in CaF₂

**Question:** In calcium fluoride (CaF₂), explain the roles of Ca and F.

- Ca (2, 8, 8, 2) has a low ionisation energy and readily loses 2 outer electrons: **Ca is the giver**.
- F (2, 7) has a high electron affinity and eagerly gains 1 electron to reach (2, 8): **each F is a taker**. Two F atoms take the 2 electrons from one Ca atom.

**Check:** Charge of Ca²⁺ (+2) + charge of two F⁻ ions (2 × −1 = −2) = 0 ✅.

## Part E: Quick Energy Calculations

Because electron affinity is given in **kJ per mol**, finding the energy released for any number of moles is a simple multiplication:

**Energy released = (number of moles) × (EA per mole)**

### Example 8. 3 Moles of Chlorine Atoms

Each mole of gaseous Cl atoms releases about 349 kJ when gaining electrons. For 3 moles of Cl:
Energy released = 3 × 349 = **1047 kJ** (approx.).

**Check:** 1047 ÷ 3 = 349 ✅.

### Example 9. 0.5 Mole of F and 0.2 Mole of Cl

- For 0.5 mol of F: 0.5 × 328 = **164 kJ** (approx.).
- For 0.2 mol of Cl: 0.2 × 349 = **69.8 kJ** (approx.).

**Check:** 164 ÷ 0.5 = 328 ✅ and 69.8 ÷ 0.2 = 349 ✅.

*(All calculations are strictly paper-based; students never handle fluorine or chlorine gas.)*

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Confusing electron affinity (EA) with ionisation energy (IE).**

Because both involve electrons and both are measured in kJ per mol, students sometimes mix them up. Remember that they are **opposite processes**! Look at chlorine (Cl) for both:

- **Electron affinity (gaining an electron):** Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g) + energy. About **349 kJ per mol is released**.
- **Ionisation energy (losing an electron):** Cl(g) + energy → Cl⁺(g) + e⁻. About **1251 kJ per mol must be put in**.

Same atom, two completely different processes, and two completely different numbers (349 vs. 1251)! In EA, an electron **enters** the atom (releasing energy); in IE, an electron **leaves** the atom (costing energy). Or look at sodium: Na has a low IE (~496 kJ/mol, easy to lose an electron) and a very small EA (~53 kJ/mol, reluctant to gain one).

Think of baseball: **catching a ball** in your glove (ball comes in = EA) and **throwing a ball** from the outfield (ball goes out = IE) are two completely different actions! Just because Noah is great at catching fly balls doesn't mean you use his catching stat to measure his throwing arm. Always check whether the atom is **gaining** an electron (EA) or **losing** an electron (IE).

## Quick Recap

- **Electron affinity (EA)** = the energy **released** when an isolated gaseous atom gains an electron: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g) + energy, measured in **kJ per mol**.
- EA and IE are opposite processes: IE removes an electron (energy absorbed); EA adds an electron (energy released).
- The two core ideas explain EA: **number of shells** (closer shell = stronger attraction) and **nuclear pull**.
- Across a period (left to right), EA generally **increases** (e.g., Li 60, O 141, F 328 kJ/mol). Down Group 17, EA **decreases** from Cl to I (Cl 349, Br 325, I 295 kJ/mol).
- **Chlorine (Cl)** has the highest electron affinity (~349 kJ/mol) among all elements (fluorine is slightly lower at ~328 due to electron crowding in its tiny 2nd shell).
- **Noble gases** (Ne, Ar) have full outer shells and EA ≈ 0. **Metals** have small EAs because they prefer to lose electrons rather than gain them.
- High EA non-metals readily form anions (Cl⁻, F⁻, O²⁻), which migrate to the anode during electrolysis.
- In ionic compounds like NaCl, "Na gives (low IE) and Cl takes (high EA)" to form Na⁺ and Cl⁻.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are for paper calculation only; halogens and other hazardous substances are never handled by students.)

1. Arrange the halogens Cl, Br and I in decreasing order of electron affinity and give the reason using shells and nuclear pull.
2. Br has an electron affinity of about 325 kJ per mol. How much energy is released when 6 mol of Br atoms each gain one electron?
3. Ar and S are both in Period 3. Why does Ar have an electron affinity of about zero, while S has a large one (about 200 kJ per mol)? Answer in two or three sentences.
4. Choose the element with the higher electron affinity in each pair and give a one-line reason: (a) S or Ne (b) Li or Br (c) Br or I.
5. True or false, with a reason: "Electron affinity and ionisation energy are the same thing, because both involve an electron."
6. S has an electron affinity of about 200 kJ per mol. How much energy is released when 2.5 mol of S atoms each take one electron? How does this compare with 2.5 mol of Cl atoms (about 349 kJ per mol)?
7. Write the electronic configuration of Li, Li⁺ and the anion that O forms. For which of these is it natural to say "gives" and for which "takes"?
8. Se (selenium) is just below S in Group 16 and has 6 outer electrons. Predict, without a number, whether its electron affinity will be a large or a small value and whether it will form an anion or a cation. Give a reason. (Se is only a name here: do not handle it.)
9. Table: X has 17 protons and a configuration 2, 8, 7. Y has 3 protons and a configuration 2, 1. Z has 10 protons and a configuration 2, 8. Which one has the largest electron affinity, which one is the smallest or about zero, and why?
10. Which releases more energy: 2 mol of Li atoms each gaining one electron (EA about 60 kJ per mol), or 1 mol of O atoms each gaining their first electron (EA about 141 kJ per mol)? What does this tell you about metals and non-metals?
11. A student says: "I (iodine) is the biggest halogen in the list, so it must have the highest electron affinity." Correct the student with the values and the reason.
12. Mixed: 4 mol of I atoms each gain one electron (EA about 295 kJ per mol). Calculate the energy released. Then say which of F, Cl and Br, if any, has a smaller EA than I, using the table of this topic.
13. In NaCl, which atom is the "giver" and which one is the "taker"? Write the configuration of each ion formed and say why both are stable.

### Answers

1. Order: **Cl > Br > I** (~349 > ~325 > ~295 kJ/mol). Reason: In Cl, the incoming electron enters the 3rd shell (closest to the nucleus among the three), whereas in Br it enters the 4th shell and in I the 5th shell. Greater distance and more inner-shell shielding weaken the nuclear pull on the incoming electron, releasing less energy. **Check:** 349 > 325 > 295 ✅.
2. Energy released = 6 × 325 = **1950 kJ** (approx.). **Check:** 1950 ÷ 6 = 325 ✅.
3. Argon has configuration (2, 8, 8) — its outer shell is completely full, so an incoming electron would have to go into the distant, heavily shielded 4th shell with zero energy benefit (EA ≈ 0). Sulphur (2, 8, 6) has two open spots in its 3rd shell and 16 protons pulling strongly, so capturing an electron releases substantial energy (EA ≈ 200 kJ/mol).
4. (a) **S**: has 2 open spots in its outer shell (EA ~200), whereas Ne has a full shell (EA ~0). (b) **Br**: a halogen just 1 electron short of an octet (EA ~325), whereas Li is a Group 1 metal with weak nuclear pull (EA ~60). (c) **Br**: its incoming electron enters the 4th shell vs. the more distant 5th shell in I (Br ~325 > I ~295). **Check:** 200 > 0 ✅, 325 > 60 ✅, 325 > 295 ✅.
5. **False** ❌. They are opposite processes: in IE, an electron is **removed** from an atom and energy must be **put in** (e.g., Cl → Cl⁺ + e⁻ costs ~1251 kJ/mol); in EA, an electron is **added** to an atom and energy is **released** (e.g., Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻ releases ~349 kJ/mol).
6. For S: 2.5 × 200 = **500 kJ** (approx.). For Cl: 2.5 × 349 = **872.5 kJ** (approx.). Chlorine releases 872.5 − 500 = 372.5 kJ more energy than sulphur because Cl has a stronger net nuclear pull and is just 1 electron short of a full octet. **Check:** 500 ÷ 2.5 = 200 ✅ and 872.5 ÷ 2.5 = 349 ✅.
7. Li: 2, 1. Li⁺: 2 (helium configuration, formed by losing 1 electron). Oxygen's anion is O²⁻: 2, 8 (formed when O: 2, 6 gains 2 electrons). Li **"gives"** electrons (to form Li⁺), while O **"takes"** electrons (to form O²⁻). **Check:** O²⁻ has 8 + 2 = 10 electrons = 2 + 8 ✅.
8. Because Se is in Group 16 (just below S) with 6 valence electrons, it needs only 2 electrons to complete its outer octet. Therefore, it will have a **fairly large electron affinity** (slightly smaller than S's because Se has one more shell) and will readily gain electrons to form an **anion** (Se²⁻), not a cation.
9. X (17 protons, 2, 8, 7) is Cl: 1 electron short of an octet with strong nuclear pull → **largest EA (~349 kJ/mol)**. Y (3 protons, 2, 1) is Li: a metal with weak pull → small EA (~60 kJ/mol). Z (10 protons, 2, 8) is Ne: a noble gas with a completely full outer shell → **smallest EA (~0)**. Order: X > Y > Z. **Check:** 349 > 60 > 0 ✅.
10. For 2 mol of Li: 2 × 60 = **120 kJ**. For 1 mol of O: 1 × 141 = **141 kJ**. Even with half as many moles, oxygen releases more energy (141 > 120 kJ), showing that non-metals (like O) are far more eager to gain electrons than metals (like Li), which prefer to lose electrons (Topic 2). **Check:** 120 ÷ 2 = 60 ✅ and 141 > 120 ✅.
11. **Incorrect.** The measured values are Cl (~349), Br (~325), and I (~295 kJ/mol). Although iodine is the largest atom of the three (5 shells), it has the **lowest** electron affinity among them because the incoming electron lands in the distant 5th shell where the nucleus's pull is weakest. Larger atomic size means weaker pull on an incoming electron, not stronger!
12. Energy released = 4 × 295 = **1180 kJ** (approx.). Looking at the table, Cl (349), F (328), and Br (325) all have **higher** EA values than I (295), so **none of them** has a smaller EA than I. **Check:** 1180 ÷ 4 = 295 ✅.
13. **Na is the giver** and **Cl is the taker**. Na (2, 8, 1) loses 1 electron to become Na⁺ (2, 8), and Cl (2, 8, 7) gains that 1 electron to become Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8). Both ions are stable because both have completely filled noble-gas outer shells (matching Ne and Ar). **Check:** +1 + (−1) = 0 net charge ✅.
