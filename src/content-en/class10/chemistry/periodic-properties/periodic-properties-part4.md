# Topic 4: Electronegativity: The Tug-of-War in a Chemical Bond

In Topic 2, we saw how much energy is needed to pull an electron away from an **isolated** gaseous atom (ionisation energy), and in Topic 3 we measured the energy released when an **isolated** gaseous atom captures an incoming electron (electron affinity). Both of those properties describe an atom floating completely alone in space, without any chemical partners. But in the real world, atoms are almost always **bonded to other atoms**: in H₂O, in HCl, in NaCl. That raises a brand-new question: when two atoms share a pair of electrons in a chemical bond, **which atom pulls those shared electrons closer to itself?** The answer is captured by a simple dimensionless number called **electronegativity**.

---

## One Blanket, Two Children

On a chilly winter evening, Oliver and his niece Mia were visiting family friends. In the living room, two little children were curled up together on the sofa under a single fleece blanket, watching cartoons on television. Mia noticed that the blanket was slowly **sliding towards one side**: one child kept tugging it persistently, leaving the other child's shoulder bare and shivering.

Mia whispered, "Uncle Oliver, the blanket belongs to both of them, so why does one kid end up with almost all of it?"

Oliver smiled gently: "Because their **pulling power** isn't equal. If both children pulled with the exact same strength, the blanket would stay perfectly centered, and each would get a fair half. But when one pulls harder, the blanket shifts toward that side. And if one pulls with overwhelming force, they end up taking the **entire blanket**, leaving the other with nothing at all."

Mia's eyes lit up: "Uncle Oliver, that sounds just like a chemical bond! Two atoms, and a blanket of shared electrons caught between them!"

Oliver beamed: "Spot on! Every atom has its own characteristic **pulling tendency** for shared electrons. In chemistry, we call that tendency **electronegativity**. Today, let's explore which atoms pull the hardest, how this pulling power shifts across the Periodic Table, and what happens when two very different pullers team up."

```
   1) Equal pulling power:           A  ----[ blanket ]----  B    blanket remains centered
   2) B pulls somewhat harder:       A  ------[ blanket ]--  B    blanket shifts towards B
   3) B pulls with extreme force:    A         [ blanket ]-  B    blanket almost entirely with B
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Shared electrons behave like a blanket: the greater the difference in pulling power between A and B, the further the blanket shifts towards one side. The exact same principle applies to a shared pair of electrons in a covalent bond.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electronegativity** | The tendency of an atom in a molecule to attract the shared pair of electrons towards itself | Fluorine has the highest value, approximately 4.0 |
| **Electronegativity scale** | An arbitrary numerical scale used to rank the electron-attracting power of bonded atoms (specifically the Pauling scale, dimensionless) | H ≈ 2.1, O ≈ 3.5 |
| **Shared pair** | A pair of valence electrons shared between two atoms to form a covalent bond | The electron pair between H and Cl in HCl |
| **Negative end** | The atom in a bond that attracts the shared electron pair more strongly, acquiring a partial negative charge | Cl in HCl |
| **Positive end** | The atom in a bond from which the shared electron pair is pulled away, acquiring a partial positive charge | H in HCl |
| **Electronegativity difference** | The absolute numerical difference between the electronegativities of two bonded atoms (always positive) | HCl: 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 |

## Part A: What Is Electronegativity, and How to Read the Scale

**Electronegativity** is defined as the **relative tendency** of an atom, when bonded within a molecule, to attract the **shared pair of electrons** towards itself. Pay close attention to two key phrases: **in a molecule** (it does not apply to an isolated atom floating alone) and **attract towards itself** (it does not mean ripping an electron entirely away, but rather keeping the shared pair closer to its own nucleus).

Electronegativity has **no units**: it is a pure relative number, just like saying "this runner has a pulling power of 3.5 while that one has 2.1". We measure it on the **Pauling scale**, named after the chemist Linus Pauling. Below are standard approximate values (some textbooks list H as 2.2; we use 2.1 in this chapter, which is standard for Class 10):

| Atom | H | Li | C | N | O | F | Na | Mg | Cl | K | Br |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Electronegativity (approx.) | 2.1 | 1.0 | 2.5 | 3.0 | 3.5 | 4.0 | 0.9 | 1.2 | 3.0 | 0.8 | 2.8 |

Notice three striking features in this table:

- **Fluorine (F)** sits right at the very top: approximately **4.0**. It is the most electronegative element in the entire Periodic Table.
- **At the bottom** sit the active metals that love giving away electrons: caesium (Cs) and francium (Fr), each hovering around **0.7 to 0.8** (these sit far down in Group 1).
- **Hydrogen (2.1)** occupies a balanced middle ground: it neither pulls with extreme ferocity nor lets go completely, allowing it to partner with both electron-givers and electron-takers.

### Example 1. Reading the Table

**Question:** Between Na (0.9) and Cl (3.0), which atom pulls the shared pair harder? What is the electronegativity difference between them?

- Chlorine's value is 3.0 while sodium's is 0.9, so **Cl** pulls the shared pair far more strongly.
- Difference = 3.0 − 0.9 = **2.1**.

**Check:** 0.9 + 2.1 = 3.0 ✅.

This is a massive difference. Later in this file, we will see what such a large difference tells us about the nature of the bond.

## Part B: Periodic Trends in Electronegativity

The periodic trend in electronegativity is driven by the exact same two fundamental factors that we have revisited throughout this chapter: **number of shells (distance)** and **nuclear pull (effective nuclear charge)**.

An atom pulls on a shared electron pair effectively when:

1. Its nucleus contains **more protons** (exerting a stronger positive pull), and
2. The shared electrons are located **closer to the nucleus** (fewer intervening electron shells, minimal distance).

```
   Period 2:   Li 1.0   Be 1.5   B 2.0   C 2.5   N 3.0   O 3.5   F 4.0    (INCREASES from left to right)
   Period 3:   Na 0.9   Mg 1.2   ...                              Cl 3.0
   Period 4:   K  0.8   ...                                       Br 2.8

   Down Group 1:   Li 1.0 > Na 0.9 > K 0.8   (DECREASES down the group)
   Down Group 17:  F  4.0 > Cl 3.0 > Br 2.8  (DECREASES down the group)

   Highest: F (top right)            Lowest: Cs, Fr (bottom left)
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Map of electronegativity trends (approximate values). Electronegativity increases across a period from left to right, and decreases down a group from top to bottom. Noble gases (Group 18) are excluded: they do not form conventional chemical bonds, so they have no standard electronegativity values.

### Across a Period: From Li to F

Across Period 2 from lithium to fluorine, the number of electron shells stays constant at **2**, but the nuclear charge increases steadily from 3 protons to 9 protons. The nuclear pull on valence electrons intensifies, the atomic size shrinks (Topic 1), and shared electrons are drawn much closer to the nucleus. As a result, electronegativity climbs steadily from 1.0 to 4.0.

```
   Li  1.0  ##########
   Be  1.5  ###############
   B   2.0  ####################
   C   2.5  #########################
   N   3.0  ##############################
   O   3.5  ###################################
   F   4.0  ########################################
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Electronegativity across Period 2 (each `#` represents 0.1). From Li to F, each step to the right adds nuclear pull, lengthening the bar.

### Example 2. Period 2 Order

**Question:** Arrange Li, O, and C in order of increasing electronegativity, and justify your answer.

- All three elements belong to Period 2 and possess the same number of electron shells (2).
- Nuclear charge: Li has 3 protons, C has 6 protons, O has 8 protons. More protons produce a stronger nuclear pull on shared electrons.
- **Order: Li (1.0) < C (2.5) < O (3.5).**

**Check:** 1.0 < 2.5 < 3.5 ✅, and protons 3 < 6 < 8 ✅.

### Down a Group: From F to Br

Down Group 17 from F → Cl → Br, each successive element adds an extra electron shell (2 shells, 3 shells, 4 shells). The shared electrons sit further and further away from the nucleus, and the additional inner shells shield the incoming shared pair from the nuclear pull (just like being seated further away from a campfire behind crowds of people). Consequently, electronegativity decreases down the group.

```
   F   4.0  ########################################
   Cl  3.0  ##############################
   Br  2.8  ############################
```

> **Figure 4.4** – Electronegativity decreases down Group 17 (each `#` represents 0.1): F at the top has the highest value, while Br further down has a noticeably lower value.

### Example 3. Group Trend Order

**Question:** Among Group 1 elements Li (1.0), Na (0.9), and K (0.8), which atom has the weakest pull on shared electrons, and why?

- K has 4 shells, Na has 3 shells, and Li has 2 shells.
- In K, the shared electrons are furthest from the nucleus and experience the greatest shielding effect from inner shells.
- Therefore, K pulls shared electrons with the least force: **K has the lowest electronegativity**, giving the order **K (0.8) < Na (0.9) < Li (1.0)**.

**Check:** 0.8 < 0.9 < 1.0 and shells 4 > 3 > 2 ✅. (To two decimal places, K is 0.82 and Cs is 0.79, confirming the steady downward trend.)

### Example 4. Combining Trends Across Periods and Groups

**Question:** Among K, Mg, and Br, which element has the highest electronegativity? (Period 4: K, Br. Period 3: Mg.)

- **K and Br** both belong to Period 4: Br has 35 protons while K has only 19 protons. Br sits far to the right, giving it a much stronger pull.
- **Mg** (Period 3, only 3 shells) compared to **K** (4 shells): Mg has fewer shells and higher nuclear charge than K, so Mg pulls harder than K.
- Overall, **Br** has the highest electronegativity.

**Check:** Comparing values: K (0.8) < Mg (1.2) < Br (2.8) ✅.

**A note on noble gases:** Group 18 noble gases are not assigned standard electronegativity values on the Pauling scale because their outer valence shells are completely filled, meaning they do not typically share electrons or form standard chemical bonds.

## Part C: Electronegativity vs. Electron Affinity: Two Different Concepts

Students frequently confuse electronegativity and electron affinity because both describe an atom's attraction for electrons. However, they are fundamentally **different concepts**:

| Feature | Electron Affinity (Topic 3) | Electronegativity (Topic 4) |
|---|---|---|
| Where is the atom? | **Isolated**, in the gaseous state, with no neighbours | **Bonded**, inside a molecule with partner atoms |
| What happens? | The atom **completely gains an extra electron** | It **attracts a shared pair closer** to itself |
| How is it measured? | In units of energy (**kJ per mol**) | As a dimensionless relative number (**no units**) |
| What does it ask? | "How much energy is released when this free atom captures an electron?" | "In a bond, how strongly does this atom pull shared electrons compared to its partner?" |

### Example 5. Same Value, Different Story

**Question:** Nitrogen (3.0) and chlorine (3.0) have nearly identical electronegativities. Does that mean their electron affinities are identical as well?

- No! Chlorine has an exceptionally high electron affinity (Topic 3: approximately 349 kJ per mol, the highest of all elements).
- Nitrogen's electron affinity is near zero (its half-filled stable 2p³ configuration resists accepting an extra electron).
- This proves that having an equal pulling power inside a covalent bond does not mean two free atoms have the same energetic appetite for capturing a solitary electron.

**Check:** Electronegativity is a relative dimensionless comparison in a bond, whereas electron affinity is an energy measurement (kJ per mol) of an isolated gaseous atom: their physical meanings are entirely distinct ✅.

An honest note: Fluorine's electron affinity is slightly lower than chlorine's due to intense inter-electronic repulsion in its tiny 2p shell, yet fluorine has the highest electronegativity of all elements. You will explore that fine detail further in advanced chemistry; for now, keep their fundamental definitions clearly separated.

## Part D: Identifying the Negative End of a Bond

Now let's put electronegativity to work in its most practical application: determining **which way the shared electron pair shifts** in any chemical bond.

Follow this simple 3-step method:

1. Look up the electronegativity of both bonded atoms.
2. The atom with the **higher number** pulls the shared electron pair closer: that atom becomes the **negative end** (acquiring a partial negative charge, written $\delta^-$).
3. The atom with the **lower number** is the **positive end** (acquiring a partial positive charge, written $\delta^+$).

### Example 6. HCl

H = 2.1, Cl = 3.0. Chlorine has the higher number, so **Cl** pulls the shared pair towards itself. Cl becomes the negative end ($\delta^-$), while H becomes the positive end ($\delta^+$). Electronegativity difference = 3.0 − 2.1 = **0.9**.

**Check:** 3.0 > 2.1 ✅ and 3.0 − 2.1 = 0.9 ✅.

### Example 7. H₂O

H = 2.1, O = 3.5. A water molecule contains **two** O–H bonds. In each bond, oxygen has the higher electronegativity. Difference = 3.5 − 2.1 = **1.4**. Thus, **O is the negative end** ($\delta^-$) and both H atoms form the positive ends ($\delta^+$).

**Check:** 3.5 > 2.1 ✅ and 3.5 − 2.1 = 1.4 ✅.

### Example 8. CO₂

C = 2.5, O = 3.5. In each C=O double bond, oxygen has the higher value, so **O is the negative end** ($\delta^-$) and C is the positive end ($\delta^+$). Difference = 3.5 − 2.5 = **1.0**.

**Check:** 3.5 − 2.5 = 1.0 ✅.

### Example 9. H₂ and Cl₂

In an H₂ molecule, both atoms are hydrogen (2.1 and 2.1): difference = **0.0**. Both nuclei pull with equal force, so the shared pair sits perfectly centered. Similarly, in Cl₂ (3.0 and 3.0): difference = **0.0**. There is no positive or negative end.

**Check:** For two identical atoms of the same element, the electronegativity difference is always exactly 0.0 ✅.

```
   H2      H : H          pair sits perfectly centered     difference = 0.0
   HCl     H  :  Cl       pair shifts towards Cl           difference = 0.9    H is d+, Cl is d-
   H2O     H :  O  : H    pair shifts towards O            difference = 1.4    H is d+, O is d-
```

> **Figure 4.5** – Position of the shared electron pair in three molecules: perfectly centered in H₂, and shifted towards the more electronegative atom in HCl (towards Cl) and H₂O (towards O). (This illustrates bond polarity along the bond axis; full 3D molecular geometry will be studied in later chapters.)

## Part E: Electronegativity Difference and Bond Character

In Class 9, you learned about **ionic bonds** (complete transfer of electrons from metal to non-metal) and **covalent bonds** (equal sharing of electrons between non-metals) as two rigid categories. Electronegativity difference unites both types along a **single continuous spectrum**:

- **Difference = 0** (identical atoms): The shared pair sits dead centre. This is a **pure covalent bond**.
- **Small to moderate difference (up to ~1.7):** The shared pair shifts slightly towards the more electronegative partner, but is still shared. This is a **polar covalent bond** (unequal sharing).
- **Very large difference (~2.0 or greater):** One atom pulls with such overwhelming force that the shared pair shifts almost completely into its valence shell. The bond behaves as an **ionic bond**, consisting of discrete positive and negative ions.

There is no rigid wall separating covalent and ionic bonding; it is a **smooth continuum** governed by how unequal the pulling contest is.

```
   Difference = 0         Moderate difference            Very large difference
   H : H                  H  :  Cl                       Na+   Cl-
   Equal sharing          Unequal, skewed sharing        Electron transferred almost completely
   (Pure covalent)        (Polar covalent)               (Ionic)
```

> **Figure 4.6** – The chemical bonding spectrum: as the electronegativity difference widens, bonds transition from equal sharing to unequal sharing, and finally to predominantly ionic character at very large differences. Chemical bonds do not sit in rigid separate boxes.

### Example 10. NaCl: A Very Large Difference

Na = 0.9, Cl = 3.0. Difference = 3.0 − 0.9 = **2.1**, which is very large. Chlorine pulls with such dominating force that the shared electron is effectively transferred entirely to chlorine, creating **Na⁺ and Cl⁻** ions. This provides the quantitative explanation for the Class 9 concept of "sodium gives up an electron and chlorine takes it". It also ties together Topic 2 (sodium has very low ionisation energy) and Topic 3 (chlorine has very high electron affinity): all three properties point in the exact same direction.

**Check:** 3.0 − 0.9 = 2.1 ✅, which is greater than 2.0 ✅.

### Example 11. HF: Extreme Unequal Sharing

H = 2.1, F = 4.0. Difference = 4.0 − 2.1 = **1.9**. This difference is much larger than the 0.9 in HCl, meaning fluorine pulls the shared pair far more aggressively towards itself. Yet HF remains a discrete **molecule**: because the difference sits right near the borderline of ~2.0, HF is a polar covalent bond with very pronounced unequal sharing.

**Check:** 4.0 − 2.1 = 1.9 ✅, and 1.9 > 0.9 (HCl) ✅.

### Example 12. Ranking Bonds by Unequal Sharing

**Question:** Among HCl (difference 0.9), HF (difference 1.9), and the O–H bond in H₂O (difference 1.4), in which bond is the shared pair pulled most unequally towards one side?

- The greater the electronegativity difference, the more unequally the shared pair is held.
- Order of increasing polarity (unequal sharing): **HCl (0.9) < O–H (1.4) < HF (1.9)**.
- In HF, the electron pair is shifted most heavily towards fluorine.

**Check:** 0.9 < 1.4 < 1.9 ✅.

**Looking ahead:** When a shared electron pair shifts towards one atom, the bond develops a dipole (a positive pole and a negative pole), which dramatically influences physical properties like boiling point and solubility. You will study this thoroughly in **Chapter 7: Chemical Bonding II**. For now, remember that the electronegativity difference reveals how the electrons are distributed.

## Try It Yourself: A Towel Tug-of-War

*(Perform this safely under adult supervision. No chemicals needed.)*

Have two friends grasp the opposite ends of a small hand towel:
1. **Equal strength:** Have both friends pull gently with identical force. The middle of the towel stays centered between them (modeling pure covalent sharing, like H₂).
2. **Slightly unequal strength:** Have one friend pull slightly harder while the other yields slightly. The towel shifts towards the stronger friend (modeling unequal sharing, like HCl).
3. **Overwhelming strength:** Have one friend pull with full force while the other lets go: the entire towel ends up in one person's hands (modeling electron transfer and ionic bonding, like NaCl). 

*Safety reminder:* Keep all pulls gentle and avoid sudden jerks to prevent slips or strained wrists.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "The most electronegative element must be the most reactive metal."**
   Remember: the most electronegative element is **fluorine (F)**, which is an aggressive **non-metal** (a halogen). The elements with the lowest electronegativities are **caesium (Cs) and francium (Fr)**, and they are the **most reactive metals** (Group 1). A high electronegativity number indicates an atom eager to attract and pull electrons towards itself (non-metallic character). A reactive metal is one that readily **gives away** its valence electron, meaning it has a very low pulling power (low electronegativity). High number = electron puller (non-metal); low number = electron releaser (reactive metal).

2. **Mistake 2: "Electronegativity is the energy released when an atom captures an electron."**
   That is the definition of **electron affinity** (Topic 3), not electronegativity. Electronegativity is a **dimensionless relative comparison** of pulling power within a chemical bond; it has **no units**. Electron affinity, by contrast, is an energetic quantity measured in **kJ per mol** for an isolated gaseous atom capturing an electron (Example 5). Never attach units like kJ/mol to electronegativity values!

## Quick Recap

- **Electronegativity** is the relative tendency of a bonded atom to attract the shared pair of electrons towards itself. It has no units.
- On the Pauling scale, **fluorine (F ≈ 4.0)** is the highest, while **caesium (Cs) and francium (Fr) (≈ 0.7–0.8)** are the lowest. Common values: H ≈ 2.1, C ≈ 2.5, N ≈ 3.0, O ≈ 3.5, Cl ≈ 3.0, Na ≈ 0.9, K ≈ 0.8.
- **Across a period (left to right), electronegativity increases** as nuclear charge increases while electron shells remain constant.
- **Down a group (top to bottom), electronegativity decreases** as extra shells increase atomic size and shielding.
- Electronegativity (in a bond, no units) and electron affinity (isolated atom, kJ per mol) are fundamentally distinct concepts.
- In any chemical bond, the atom with the **higher electronegativity value forms the negative end** ($\delta^-$).
- Difference = 0: pure covalent bond. Moderate difference: polar covalent bond. Very large difference (~2.0 or more): predominantly ionic bond (e.g., NaCl with difference 2.1).
- Detailed discussions of polar and non-polar molecules will follow in **Chapter 7**.
