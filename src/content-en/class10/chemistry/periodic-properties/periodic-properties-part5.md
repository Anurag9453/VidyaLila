# Topic 5: Metallic and Non-Metallic Character: Bringing It All Together

In the previous topics, we examined an individual atom from several distinct angles: its physical size (atomic radius), the energy cost of stripping away an electron (ionisation energy), the energy released when it captures an extra electron (electron affinity), and how aggressively it tugs on shared electrons in a bond (electronegativity). Now we ask: **what is the overarching real-world consequence of all these properties?** Why does one element behave like a shiny, reactive metal while another behaves like a brittle, non-metallic gas? In this topic, we weave all four properties together. You will master the skill of looking solely at an element's **position** in the Periodic Table to predict almost everything about it: its reactivity, its ions, the nature of its oxide, and whether it acts as an electron lender or an electron borrower.

---

## The Community Lending Library

During the summer holidays, Uncle Oliver took his niece Mia to visit a neighbourhood community tool-and-book lending library where Oliver was an active volunteer. Near the front entrance stood a large bulletin board divided into two columns: **"Lenders"** and **"Borrowers"**.

Mia noticed that certain members were always eager to **lend**: they had spare tools or reference manuals stored loosely on high shelves in their garages, and the moment someone asked, they happily handed them over. Other members were perennial **borrowers**: their tool sets or book collections were short by just **one or two essential items**, and they eagerly hunted for those missing pieces to complete their sets, locking them securely into their toolboxes. In between stood a few flexible members who borrowed on some days and lent on others.

"Uncle Oliver, why are the members so different?" Mia asked. "Why isn't everyone in the library doing the same thing?"

Oliver chuckled: "Think about how securely those items are held. Members who store items far away on a high shelf with a loose grip find it effortless to give them away. But members whose sets are missing just one key piece have a desperate need to acquire it, and they hold onto it with an iron grip. Atoms behave in the exact same way. **Electrons are the library items.** Some atoms are natural lenders, while others are aggressive borrowers."

```
   Library Bulletin Board

   ELECTRON LENDERS (Metals)             ELECTRON BORROWERS (Non-metals)
   Electrons far away, loose grip        Missing 1-2 electrons, fierce nuclear pull

   Na   Mg   Al   <----  Flexible  ---->   P    S    Cl
   (Metals)              Members (Si)      (Non-metals)

   Lend electrons ------------------------> Borrow / take electrons
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Period 3 visualized as a community lending library: elements on the far left readily **lend** (lose) electrons, elements on the far right aggressively **borrow** (gain) electrons, and elements in the middle like silicon (Si) balance between both tendencies.

On their walk back home, Mia wrote a bold summary in her notebook: *"Metals = electron lenders (cations). Non-metals = electron borrowers (anions). Metalloids = right in the middle."* Oliver nodded approvingly: "Exactly. Now let's map that simple rule across the entire Periodic Table."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Metallic character** | The ease with which an atom loses valence electrons to form positive ions (cations); electropositive nature | Na and K have very high metallic character |
| **Non-metallic character** | The ease with which an atom gains electrons to form negative ions (anions); electronegative nature | Cl and F have very high non-metallic character |
| **Reactivity of a metal** | How vigorously and rapidly a metal loses electrons in chemical reactions | K is more reactive than Na |
| **Reactivity of a non-metal** | How vigorously and rapidly a non-metal gains electrons in chemical reactions | F₂ is more reactive than Cl₂ |
| **Displacement reaction** | A reaction in which a more reactive element displaces a less reactive element from its compound or salt solution | Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ |
| **Basic oxide** | An oxide of a metal that reacts with acids to produce salt and water | Na₂O, MgO |
| **Acidic oxide** | An oxide of a non-metal that reacts with bases to produce salt and water | SO₃, P₄O₁₀ |
| **Amphoteric oxide** | An oxide that exhibits both basic and acidic properties, reacting with both acids and bases | Al₂O₃ |

## Part A: Metallic Character: The Tendency to Lose Electrons

**Metallic character** measures how easily an atom **loses its valence electrons**. It is directly linked to **ionisation energy** (Topic 2): an atom with a low ionisation energy requires very little effort to shed an electron, making it strongly metallic (electropositive).

**Across a period from left to right: metallic character decreases.** The underlying reasons remain the two core pillars of this chapter: number of shells (distance) and effective nuclear pull. Across a period, the number of electron shells stays constant, but the number of protons increases. The growing positive charge pulls the electron cloud tighter, holding valence electrons firmly and making them harder to remove.

**Down a group from top to bottom: metallic character increases.** As we descend a group, each step adds a new electron shell. Valence electrons sit progressively further from the positive nucleus and experience heavy shielding from inner electron shells, so they are shed with minimal energy.

Let us inspect the numerical data:

| Element | First Ionisation Energy (approx. kJ/mol) | Atomic Radius (approx. pm) |
|---|---|---|
| Li | 520 | 152 |
| Na | 496 | 186 |
| K | 419 | 227 |
| Mg | 738 | 160 |
| Ca | 590 | 197 |
| Cl | 1251 | 99 |

Down Group 1 from Li to K: ionisation energy drops steadily (520 → 496 → 419 kJ/mol) while atomic radius expands (152 → 186 → 227 pm). Both trends tell the exact same story: valence electrons sit further out and are loosely held, so **metallic character increases**. In contrast, across Period 3 from Na (496) to Mg (738) to Cl (1251 kJ/mol), ionisation energy skyrockets, so metallic character decreases.

**Non-metallic character** is the exact mirror image: it measures how readily an atom **gains electrons**. It is governed by electron affinity (Topic 3) and electronegativity (Topic 4). Because non-metals rely on strong nuclear attraction to grab electrons, the trend runs in the exact **opposite direction** to metallic character:

- Across a period (left to right): non-metallic character **increases** (nuclear pull strengthens).
- Down a group (top to bottom): non-metallic character **decreases** (valence shells become distant and shielded).

```
   Metallic Character                 Non-Metallic Character

   Period 3:  Na  Mg  Al  Si  P  S  Cl
              Most ----------> Least    (Metallic character)
              Least ---------> Most     (Non-metallic character)

   Down Group 1:   Li             Down Group 17:  F
                   Na                             Cl
                   K                              Br
                   v  Metallic increases          v  Non-metallic decreases
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Periodic trends in metallic and non-metallic character. Across a period, metallic character falls while non-metallic character climbs. Down Group 1, metallic character increases; down Group 17, non-metallic character decreases.

A simple mental compass: **The most metallic elements sit at the bottom-left of the Periodic Table (caesium, francium). The most non-metallic elements sit at the top-right (fluorine).** Noble gases are excluded because their closed-shell valence configuration makes them inert under standard conditions.

Recall the "staircase" separating metals from non-metals introduced in Class 9 ([periodic-table-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/periodic-table/periodic-table-part4.md)). Metalloids like boron (B) and silicon (Si) lie along this border where the tendency to lose electrons roughly balances the tendency to gain or share them. Consider Group 13: **B is a metalloid, Al is a metal, and Ga beneath it is an even more distinct metal**. As we move down the group, the elements become increasingly metallic!

### Example 1. Na, Mg, Al: Which Is More Metallic?

- All three elements have 3 electron shells. Protons increase: 11 (Na), 12 (Mg), 13 (Al). Valence electrons: 1, 2, 3.
- Shells are constant, but nuclear charge increases from Na to Al. The atomic radius contracts (186, 160, 143 pm), holding valence electrons tighter.
- Therefore, **Na > Mg > Al** in metallic character.

**Check:** Radii: 186 > 160 > 143 pm, and valence electrons: 1 < 2 < 3. Both confirm Na gives up its electron most easily ✅.

### Example 2. Li, Na, K: Which Is More Metallic?

- Li has 2 shells, Na has 3 shells, K has 4 shells. First ionisation energies: approx. 520, 496, 419 kJ/mol.
- K has the lowest ionisation energy and the largest atomic radius, meaning its outer electron is least tightly bound.
- Therefore, **K > Na > Li** in metallic character.

**Check:** Ionisation energies: 419 < 496 < 520 kJ/mol ✅, and radii: 227 > 186 > 152 pm ✅.

### Example 3. Ca and Mg: Two Divalent Metals

- Both Ca and Mg have 2 valence electrons (Group 2). Ca has 4 electron shells; Mg has 3 shells.
- In Ca, the valence electrons sit further from the nucleus and experience greater shielding. Ionisation energy of Ca (≈ 590 kJ/mol) is lower than Mg (≈ 738 kJ/mol).
- Therefore, **Ca is more metallic than Mg**.

**Check:** In the reactivity series of metals, calcium is placed above magnesium ✅.

### Example 4. Si, P, S, Cl: Which Is More Non-Metallic?

- All four elements have 3 electron shells, with valence electrons 4, 5, 6, 7.
- Protons increase steadily: 14 (Si), 15 (P), 16 (S), 17 (Cl), strengthening the pull on incoming electrons.
- Therefore, **Cl > S > P > Si** in non-metallic character.

**Check:** Atomic radii contract: 118, 110, 103, 99 pm. Smaller atoms with higher nuclear pull attract electrons best ✅.

## Part B: Reactivity: How Fast Elements React

**Reactivity of a metal** is defined by how easily and rapidly it loses electrons to form cations. Therefore, **metal reactivity follows the exact same trend as metallic character**:

- Down a group: metal reactivity **increases** (K > Na > Li).
- Across a period (left to right): metal reactivity **decreases** (Na > Mg > Al).

**Reactivity of a non-metal** is defined by how rapidly and eagerly it **gains** electrons to form anions. Therefore, non-metal reactivity follows non-metallic character:

- Down a group: non-metal reactivity **decreases** (F > Cl > Br > I).
- Across a period (left to right): non-metal reactivity **increases** (Si < P < S < Cl).

*(Noble gases are inert because of their stable valence octets, so they have negligible chemical reactivity under normal conditions.)*

### Reactivity of Metals: Teacher Demonstration Only

*(Alkali metals and reactions with steam are dangerous. These observations are for study and teacher demonstration behind safety screens only; students must never handle active alkali metals.)*

| Metal | Reaction with Cold Water | Underlying Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Na | Rapid, darts across the surface, generates heat and H₂ gas | 1 loosely held electron, low ionisation energy |
| K | Violent, ignites evolved hydrogen with a lilac flame | Valence electron is even further from nucleus than in Na |
| Ca | Steady, produces steady stream of H₂ bubbles | 2 valence electrons, but larger radius than Mg |
| Mg | Barely reacts with cold water; reacts steadily with hot water/steam | 2 valence electrons held closer to nucleus |
| Al | Does not react with cold water; protected by oxide film | 3 valence electrons, strong nuclear pull, plus tough Al₂O₃ layer |

Balanced chemical equation for sodium in water:
**2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂↑**

Atom count verification:
- Reactants: 2 Na, 4 H, 2 O
- Products: 2 Na, 2 O, 4 H (2 in NaOH + 2 in H₂) ✅.

### Reactivity of Non-metals: The Halogen Displacement Ladder

*(Chlorine, bromine, and iodine vapours are hazardous and toxic. This displacement series is for conceptual study and teacher demonstration under a fume hood.)*

A more reactive halogen will **displace** a less reactive halogen from its metal halide salt solution:

- Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂ (chlorine displaces bromine; reddish-brown bromine liberated)
- Cl₂ + 2NaI → 2NaCl + I₂ (chlorine displaces iodine; dark purple/violet iodine liberated)
- Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂ (bromine displaces iodine)
- I₂ + KBr → **No reaction** (iodine is less reactive than bromine, cannot displace it)
- Br₂ + KCl → **No reaction** (bromine cannot displace more reactive chlorine)

```
   Halogen Displacement Ladder

        F₂   ------  Most reactive halogen (highest pull)
         :
        Cl₂  ------  Displaces Br⁻ and I⁻
         :
        Br₂  ------  Displaces I⁻, cannot displace Cl⁻
         :
        I₂   ------  Least reactive halogen (cannot displace Cl⁻ or Br⁻)

   Rule: An upper halogen displaces a lower halogen from its salt solution.
   A lower halogen can NEVER displace an upper halogen.
```

> **Figure 5.3** – The halogen displacement ladder. A halogen higher in Group 17 displaces any halogen below it from its halide salt solution. The reverse reaction never occurs.

This order matches the **trend in non-metallic character**: descending Group 17, the incoming electron shell is further from the nucleus, so the nuclear pull for an extra electron weakens.

### Example 5. Predicting Whether a Reaction Occurs

**Question:** Predict whether a displacement reaction occurs for each pair, and write the balanced equation if it does: (a) Cl₂ + KI (b) Br₂ + NaCl (c) I₂ + KBr.

- (a) Cl₂ sits above I₂ on the halogen ladder: **Reaction occurs!**
  **Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂**
- (b) Br₂ sits below Cl₂: **No reaction.**
- (c) I₂ sits below Br₂: **No reaction.**

**Check:** For (a): Left side: 2 Cl, 2 K, 2 I; Right side: 2 K, 2 Cl, 2 I ✅. In (b) and (c), a weaker halogen cannot displace a stronger one ✅.

## Part C: Nature of Oxides Across Period 3: The Transition from Metallic to Non-Metallic

One of the most elegant real-world manifestations of metallic versus non-metallic character is the **acid-base nature of element oxides**. In earlier chapters, you learned that metal oxides are basic while non-metal oxides are acidic. Across Period 3, as the elements transition from strong metals to metalloids to aggressive non-metals, their oxides undergo a stunning transformation:

| Element | Oxide | Acid-Base Character | Reaction with Water |
|---|---|---|---|
| Na | Na₂O | Strongly basic | Dissolves readily to form NaOH (strong alkali) |
| Mg | MgO | Basic | Sparingly soluble, forms weakly alkaline Mg(OH)₂ |
| Al | Al₂O₃ | Amphoteric | Insoluble; reacts with both strong acids and strong bases |
| Si | SiO₂ | Weakly acidic | Insoluble; reacts only with hot concentrated alkali |
| P | P₄O₁₀ | Acidic | Dissolves vigorously to form H₃PO₄ (phosphoric acid) |
| S | SO₃ | Strongly acidic | Dissolves vigorously to form H₂SO₄ (sulfuric acid) |
| Cl | Cl₂O₇ | Very strongly acidic | Dissolves to form HClO₄ (perchloric acid) |

```
   Metal -----------------------------------------------------> Non-metal

   Na₂O       MgO        Al₂O₃          SiO₂         P₄O₁₀    SO₃      Cl₂O₇
   Strongly   Basic      Amphoteric     Weakly       Acidic   Strongly Very strongly
   basic                                acidic                acidic   acidic

   <---- Basicity decreases -----------------------------------------
                          --------------------> Acidity increases --->
```

> **Figure 5.4** – The nature of oxides across Period 3: Na₂O and MgO are basic, Al₂O₃ is amphoteric, and oxides from SiO₂ to Cl₂O₇ become progressively more acidic. This mirrors the transition from metallic to non-metallic character.

Why does this happen? Elements on the left (Na, Mg) have low ionisation energies and readily lose electrons, forming ionic oxides with O²⁻ ions that react with water to yield hydroxide ions (OH⁻). Elements on the right (P, S, Cl) hold their valence electrons tightly and share electrons with oxygen via covalent bonds; in water, these polar bonds facilitate the release of hydrogen ions (H⁺). Aluminium sits right at the transition point, giving Al₂O₃ amphoteric character.

Key balanced reactions for study:
- **Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH** (Na: 2, H: 2, O: 2 ✅)
- **SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄** (S: 1, H: 2, O: 4 ✅)
- **P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄** (P: 4, H: 12, O: 16 ✅)
- **Cl₂O₇ + H₂O → 2HClO₄** (Cl: 2, H: 2, O: 8 ✅)
- **Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O** (Al₂O₃ acting as a base with acid: Al: 2, O: 3, H: 6, Cl: 6 ✅)
- **Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O** (Al₂O₃ acting as an acid with base: Al: 2, Na: 2, O: 5, H: 2 ✅)

**Group 1 Hydroxides:** As we descend Group 1, metallic character increases, making the metal ion even more electropositive. This loosens the bond to the hydroxide ion, making the hydroxides **increasingly strong bases**: **LiOH < NaOH < KOH**.

### Example 6. Identifying an Unknown Oxide

**Question:** An element belongs to Period 3. Its oxide dissolves readily in water to form a solution that turns red litmus paper deep blue. Identify the element and write the balanced equation.

- A solution turning red litmus blue is strongly alkaline. In Period 3, only the oxide of **sodium (Na)** dissolves readily to form a strong alkali.
- Reaction: **Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH**.

**Check:** Na has 1 valence electron, so its oxide formula is Na₂O (2 × (+1) + (−2) = 0). It dissolves to give sodium hydroxide ✅.

### Example 7. Ranking Oxides in Order of Basicity

**Question:** Arrange the oxides Na₂O, SO₃, Al₂O₃, and MgO in decreasing order of basic strength (from most basic to most acidic).

- Na₂O is strongly basic, MgO is moderately basic, Al₂O₃ is amphoteric, and SO₃ is strongly acidic.
- Order: **Na₂O > MgO > Al₂O₃ > SO₃**.

**Check:** This matches their left-to-right positions across Period 3 in Figure 5.4 ✅.

## Part D: Putting It All Together: Predicting Properties from Periodic Position

Now let us unite all the trends of this chapter into a single comprehensive **Trend Map**:

```
   Across a PERIOD from left to right  ------------------>

   Atomic radius:          DECREASES
   Ionisation energy:      INCREASES
   Electron affinity:      INCREASES
   Electronegativity:      INCREASES
   Metallic character:     DECREASES
   Non-metallic character: INCREASES

   Down a GROUP from top to bottom (v)

   Atomic radius:          INCREASES
   Ionisation energy:      DECREASES
   Electron affinity:      DECREASES
   Electronegativity:      DECREASES
   Metallic character:     INCREASES
   Non-metallic character: DECREASES
```

> **Figure 5.5** – Comprehensive Trend Map: how six key properties shift across a period and down a group. Notice the symmetry: where atomic size shrinks, ionisation energy, electron affinity, and electronegativity all rise together!

A handy mental shortcut: **Atomic size, metallic character, and metal reactivity all travel together. Ionisation energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, and non-metallic character all travel together in the opposite direction.**

### Example 8. Unknown Element X: Electron Configuration 2, 8, 3

For an element X with electron configuration 2, 8, 3, determine its identity, group, period, metallic nature, valency, ion formed, ion size relative to the parent atom, and the nature of its oxide.

- Total electrons = 2 + 8 + 3 = 13 → Atomic number Z = 13: **Aluminium (Al)**.
- 3 electron shells → **Period 3**.
- 3 valence electrons → **Group 13** (3 + 10).
- Positioned left of the metalloid staircase → **Metal**.
- Valency = **3** (loses 3 valence electrons).
- Ion formed: **Al³⁺** (configuration 2, 8, isoelectronic with neon, 10 electrons).
- Size: **Al³⁺ is much smaller than the neutral Al atom** (loss of an entire shell, and 13 protons pull firmly on only 10 electrons).
- Oxide: **Al₂O₃**, which is **amphoteric**.

**Check:** In Al₂O₃: 2 × (+3) + 3 × (−2) = +6 − 6 = 0 ✅. Group 13 has valency 3 ✅.

### Example 9. Unknown Element Y: Deducing an Identity from Clues

**Question:** Element Y belongs to Period 3. It forms a univalent anion Y⁻ containing 18 electrons, and its highest oxide reacts with water to form an intensely acidic solution. Identify element Y and deduce its properties.

- Anion Y⁻ contains 18 electrons, so the neutral atom Y contains 17 electrons. Z = 17: **Chlorine (Cl)**, configuration 2, 8, 7.
- 7 valence electrons → **Group 17**, valency = 1.
- Classification: **Non-metal**, specifically a **halogen**.
- Ion: **Cl⁻** (larger than neutral Cl atom due to electron-electron repulsion among 18 electrons with only 17 protons).
- Oxide: **Cl₂O₇**, strongly acidic, dissolving in water to produce perchloric acid: Cl₂O₇ + H₂O → 2HClO₄.
- Periodic character: Highest non-metallic character and highest non-metal reactivity in Period 3 (excluding noble gases).

**Check:** Equation balanced: 2 Cl, 8 O, 2 H on both sides ✅. Cl⁻ has 18 electrons ✅.

### Example 10. B, Al, and Ga: Same Group, Different Character

**Question:** Boron (B), aluminium (Al), and gallium (Ga) all belong to Group 13. State their order of increasing metallic character and explain why boron behaves differently from aluminium.

- Electron shells: B has 2 shells, Al has 3 shells, Ga has 4 shells.
- Down the group, the valence electrons sit further from the nucleus and experience greater shielding, making them easier to lose.
- Order of increasing metallic character: **B < Al < Ga**.
- Boron is small with a tight grip on its electrons; its tendency to lose electrons is roughly matched by its tendency to share them, placing it on the staircase as a **metalloid**. Aluminium has 3 shells and sheds its valence electrons readily, behaving as a true **metal**.

**Check:** Metallic character increases down every group in the Periodic Table ✅.

### Example 11. Reaction Between Bromine Water and Potassium Iodide

**Question:** What happens when reddish-brown bromine water (Br₂) is mixed with a colourless solution of potassium iodide (KI)? Write the balanced chemical equation.

- Bromine sits above iodine in Group 17, making Br₂ more reactive than I₂.
- Bromine displaces iodide ions from the solution:
  **Br₂ + 2KI → 2KBr + I₂**
- The solution turns dark purple-brown due to the liberation of free iodine.

**Check:** Left side: 2 Br, 2 K, 2 I; Right side: 2 K, 2 Br, 2 I ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Reactivity increases down a group for all elements, whether metals or non-metals."**
   This is a very frequent exam mistake because the word "reactivity" sounds uniform. However, metal reactivity and non-metal reactivity mean opposite things:
   - **Metal reactivity** means eagerness to **lose electrons**. Down a group, atomic radius increases and outer electrons are held loosely, so metal reactivity **increases** (Li < Na < K).
   - **Non-metal reactivity** means eagerness to **gain electrons**. Down a group, the outer shell is further away and shielded, so the nucleus cannot attract incoming electrons as strongly. Thus, non-metal reactivity **decreases** (F > Cl > Br > I).

   *Demonstration using the library:* If the library opens a new branch where items are kept on the 4th floor (extra shells), a member who wants to **give away** an item finds it even easier (loose hold). But a member who needs to **grab** an item must reach all the way to the 4th floor (weak pulling power). Hence, going down a group helps lenders (metals) but hinders borrowers (non-metals)!

   *Demonstration using real reactions:* Potassium reacts more violently with water than sodium does (K > Na). But chlorine displaces bromine from KBr (Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂), while bromine cannot displace chlorine from KCl (Br₂ + KCl → no reaction). This proves Cl₂ is more reactive than Br₂, confirming that non-metal reactivity decreases down the group.

## Quick Recap

- **Metallic character** is the ease of losing valence electrons (low ionisation energy, high electropositivity). **Non-metallic character** is the ease of gaining electrons (high electron affinity and electronegativity).
- **Across a period (left to right):** metallic character decreases; non-metallic character increases.
- **Down a group (top to bottom):** metallic character increases; non-metallic character decreases.
- **Metal reactivity increases down a group** (K > Na > Li) and decreases across a period (Na > Mg > Al).
- **Non-metal reactivity decreases down a group** (F > Cl > Br > I) and increases across a period (P < S < Cl).
- **Halogen displacement ladder:** A higher halogen displaces a lower halogen from its halide salt solution (e.g., Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂). The reverse never occurs.
- **Oxides across Period 3:** Na₂O and MgO are basic, Al₂O₃ is amphoteric, SiO₂ is weakly acidic, P₄O₁₀ and SO₃ are acidic, and Cl₂O₇ is strongly acidic.
- **Group 1 hydroxides** become stronger bases descending the group: LiOH < NaOH < KOH.
- Knowing an element's position on the Periodic Table allows you to predict its electron configuration, group, period, valency, ion type, relative size, and oxide character.

### Answers

1. **K > Ca > Mg.** K has 4 shells and 1 valence electron; Ca has 4 shells and 2 valence electrons; Mg has 3 shells and 2 valence electrons. Comparing K and Ca: both have 4 shells, but Ca has 20 protons versus K's 19 protons, so Ca's nucleus holds its electrons more firmly; K sheds its valence electron most easily. Mg has only 3 shells, so its valence electrons sit closer to the nucleus. Check: First ionisation energies: K (419) < Ca (590) < Mg (738 kJ/mol) ✅.
2. Z = 20: Electron configuration is **2, 8, 8, 2**.
   - 4 shells → **Period 4**.
   - 2 valence electrons → **Group 2**.
   - Valency = **2**.
   - Ion formed: **Ca²⁺** (configuration 2, 8, 8; 18 electrons).
   - Ion size: **Ca²⁺ is smaller than the Ca atom** (loss of outer 4th shell; 20 protons pull strongly on 18 electrons).
   - Oxide: **CaO**, which is **basic**. (The element is calcium.)
3. Reactions will occur in **KBr and KI solutions**, but not in KCl (chlorine cannot displace itself).
   - **Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂** (balanced: 2 K, 2 Cl, 2 Br on both sides ✅).
   - **Cl₂ + 2KI → 2KCl + I₂** (balanced: 2 K, 2 Cl, 2 I on both sides ✅).
   - **KCl:** No reaction occurs because Cl₂ cannot displace chloride ions.
4. - Na: **Na₂O**, basic.
   - Al: **Al₂O₃**, amphoteric.
   - S: **SO₃**, acidic.
   - P: **P₄O₁₀**, acidic.
   - **Al₂O₃** reacts with both acids and bases:
     Al₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2AlCl₃ + 3H₂O
     Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH → 2NaAlO₂ + H₂O.
5. Reactivity order: **P > Q > R**. Across Period 3, metallic reactivity decreases from left to right. Therefore:
   - **P = Na (Sodium)** (violently reacts with cold water).
   - **Q = Magnesium (Mg)** (reacts very slowly with cold water, rapidly with steam).
   - **R = Aluminium (Al)** (protected by an impermeable Al₂O₃ oxide layer).
6. In Group 13, B has 2 shells, Al has 3 shells, and Ga has 4 shells. Moving down the group, atomic radius increases and inner shells shield the valence electrons, lowering ionisation energy: **B < Al < Ga**. Boron has a small radius and holds its valence electrons tightly, behaving intermediate between metals and non-metals as a **metalloid**. Aluminium has a larger radius and lower ionisation energy, readily forming Al³⁺ cations as a true **metal**.
7. Down a group, metallic reactivity **increases** (Li < Na < K) because metals react by losing electrons, which becomes easier as atomic size increases. In contrast, non-metallic reactivity **decreases** down a group (F > Cl > Br > I) because non-metals react by gaining electrons, which becomes harder as the incoming shell is further from the nucleus. The student's claim is false: for example, Cl₂ displaces Br₂ from KBr (Cl₂ + 2KBr → 2KCl + Br₂), showing that Cl₂ is more reactive than Br₂, so non-metal reactivity decreases down the group.
8. **LiOH < NaOH < KOH.** Down Group 1, metallic character increases and ionisation energy decreases (K < Na < Li). A more electropositive metal ion releases the hydroxide ion (OH⁻) into solution more readily, resulting in stronger basicity.
9. **MgO < SiO₂ < P₄O₁₀ < Cl₂O₇.** MgO is basic, SiO₂ is weakly acidic, P₄O₁₀ is acidic, and Cl₂O₇ is strongly acidic. Check: This matches their left-to-right positions across Period 3 ✅.
10. Most non-metallic: **Cl** (3 shells; Br has 4 shells, I has 5 shells). With fewer shells, incoming electrons are closer to the nucleus, giving chlorine the highest attraction for electrons. Most metallic: **Sr** (5 shells; Mg has 3 shells, Ca has 4 shells). With 5 shells, strontium's outer electrons are furthest from the nucleus and experience the greatest shielding, so they are lost most easily.
11. (a) For 5 mol of K: $5 \times 419\text{ kJ} = \mathbf{2095\text{ kJ}}$.
    (b) For 5 mol of Ca: $5 \times 590\text{ kJ} = \mathbf{2950\text{ kJ}}$.
    (c) **Potassium (K) is more reactive**: it requires significantly less energy (2095 kJ vs 2950 kJ) to lose its valence electron. Difference = $2950 - 2095 = 855\text{ kJ}$. Check ✅.
12. (1) Iodine lies below bromine in Group 17, so its non-metallic reactivity is lower. (2) Iodine has 5 electron shells compared to bromine's 4 shells; its greater atomic radius and inner shielding make its nucleus less effective at capturing electrons. Therefore, I₂ cannot displace Br⁻ from KBr.
13. (a) **False** ❌: Metallic character decreases across Period 3 from Na to Cl.
    (b) **True** ✅.
    (c) **False** ❌: Al₂O₃ is an amphoteric oxide.
    (d) **True** ✅.
    (e) **False** ❌: Configuration 2, 8, 1 is Na (3 shells) while 2, 8, 8, 1 is K (4 shells); K is more metallic than Na.
14. Configuration 2, 3: Total electrons = 5 → Z = 5: **Boron (B)**.
    - 2 shells → **Period 2**.
    - 3 valence electrons → **Group 13**.
    - Valency = **3**.
    - Classification: **Metalloid**.
    - Oxide: **B₂O₃**, which is **weakly acidic**.
    - Boron is **less metallic** than aluminium (metallic character increases down Group 13).
