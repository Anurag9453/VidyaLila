## Part A: The Four-Step Method

In Class 9, you used the bonfire analogy ([periodic-table-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/periodic-table/periodic-table-part4.md)); this chapter solidified that picture into two governing physical factors: **number of shells (distance)** and **effective nuclear pull (protons minus shielding)**. Whenever you face an arrangement or explanation question, walk through these four steps:

1. **Count the shells:** Identify which period each element belongs to. More shells mean valence electrons sit further from the nucleus.
2. **Evaluate nuclear pull:** Within the same period, more protons mean a stronger effective pull. Down a group, inner core shells add shielding, keeping the effective pull relatively low despite more protons.
3. **State the trend:** Explicitly state the direction of change (e.g., "increases", "decreases").
4. **State the scientific reason:** Write a clear, concise justification: "Because the number of shells is constant while nuclear charge increases, the pull on valence electrons..."

```
   Question  -->  Step 1: Count shells (same period or different periods?)
                     :
                     v
                  Step 2: Evaluate nuclear pull (protons and shielding)
                     :
                     v
                  Step 3: State the trend (increasing or decreasing)
                     :
                     v
                  Step 4: Write the concise one-line scientific reason
```

> **Figure 6.2** – The 4-step method. Steps 1 and 2 prepare the scientific reasoning; Steps 3 and 4 format the final answer. An arrangement without a clear scientific reason loses essential marks.

### Example 1. Atomic Size in Period 2: B, N, F, Be

**Question:** Arrange B, N, F, and Be in decreasing order of atomic size, providing a full scientific explanation.

- **Step 1:** All four elements belong to Period 2; each possesses 2 electron shells.
- **Step 2:** Nuclear charges: Be has 4 protons, B has 5, N has 7, and F has 9. With constant shells, more protons exert a stronger nuclear pull on the electron cloud.
- **Step 3:** Stronger nuclear pull contracts the electron shells, decreasing atomic radius.
- **Step 4:** **Decreasing order: Be > B > N > F.**
  *Reason:* All four elements have 2 electron shells, but the nuclear charge increases steadily from 4 to 9 protons. The valence electrons experience an intensifying inward pull, drawing the valence shell closer to the nucleus.

*Approximate values:* Be (111 pm), B (88 pm), N (74 pm), F (64 pm).
**Check:** 111 > 88 > 74 > 64 pm ✅.

### Example 2. Group 17 Trends: F, Cl, Br, I

**Question:** Arrange the halogens F, Cl, Br, and I in decreasing order of electronegativity, and name another property that follows this exact same trend.

- **Step 1:** F is in Period 2 (2 shells), Cl in Period 3 (3 shells), Br in Period 4 (4 shells), and I in Period 5 (5 shells).
- **Step 2:** Although nuclear charge increases down the group, each step adds an entirely new electron shell. The inner shells shield the valence shell, placing bonding electrons further from the nucleus.
- **Step 3 & 4:** **Decreasing order: F > Cl > Br > I.**
  *Reason:* As the number of electron shells increases down the group, the shared pair of electrons in a bond sits further from the nucleus and experiences greater shielding, reducing the atom's pulling power.

*Pauling electronegativity values:* F (4.0), Cl (3.0), Br (2.8), I (2.5).
Another property that decreases down Group 17 in the exact same order is **first ionisation energy**: F (1681), Cl (1251), Br (1140), I (1008 kJ/mol).
**Check:** 4.0 > 3.0 > 2.8 > 2.5 ✅ and 1681 > 1251 > 1140 > 1008 kJ/mol ✅.

### Example 3. Isoelectronic Ions: N³⁻, F⁻, Al³⁺

**Question:** The ions N³⁻, F⁻, and Al³⁺ all contain 10 electrons. Arrange them in decreasing order of ionic radius and justify your answer.

- **Step 1:** All three ions are isoelectronic, having the neon configuration (2, 8) with 2 electron shells.
- **Step 2:** Nuclear charges: Nitrogen has 7 protons, Fluorine has 9 protons, and Aluminium has 13 protons.
- **Step 3:** The higher the nuclear charge pulling on the identical 10 electrons, the tighter the electron cloud is pulled inward, resulting in a smaller ion.
- **Step 4:** **Decreasing order: N³⁻ > F⁻ > Al³⁺.**
  *Reason:* Although all three ions have 10 electrons in 2 shells, the nuclear charge increases from 7 protons in N³⁻ to 9 in F⁻ and 13 in Al³⁺. The stronger positive charge pulls the electron cloud closer to the nucleus.

*Approximate ionic radii:* N³⁻ (146 pm), F⁻ (133 pm), Al³⁺ (54 pm).
**Check:** 146 > 133 > 54 pm ✅ and protons: 7 < 9 < 13 ✅.

## Part B: Nine Puzzles, Step by Step

Each puzzle provides specific periodic clues. Read carefully to extract what is given and what is asked.

### Puzzle 1. Mystery Elements from Periodic Clues

**Question:** Element X belongs to Period 3 and has one electron fewer than a complete outer shell. Element Y also belongs to Period 3 and has the largest atomic radius in its period (excluding noble gases). Deduce the nature of the compound formed between X and Y.

- In Period 3, a complete valence shell has 8 electrons; one fewer means 7 valence electrons: **X = Chlorine (Cl)**.
- In Period 3, atomic size decreases from left to right, so the largest atom on the far left is **Y = Sodium (Na)**.
- Pauling electronegativity values: Na ≈ 0.9, Cl ≈ 3.0. Electronegativity difference = $3.0 - 0.9 = \mathbf{2.1}$, which is very large.
- A difference greater than ~2.0 indicates complete electron transfer: the compound is **ionic**, forming **NaCl** ($\text{Na}^+\text{Cl}^-$).

**Check:** Na loses 1 electron ($\text{Na} \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ + \text{e}^-$, configuration 2, 8) and Cl gains 1 electron ($\text{Cl} + \text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cl}^-$, configuration 2, 8, 8). Both attain stable noble gas octets ✅.

### Puzzle 2. Successive Ionisation Energies: Two Mystery Elements

**Question:** X and Y are both Period 2 elements. Their successive ionisation energies (in kJ/mol) are given below. Determine their group, valency, and identities.

| Ionisation Energy | Element X | Element Y |
|---|---|---|
| 1st | 900 | 801 |
| 2nd | 1757 | 2427 |
| 3rd | 14849 | 3660 |
| 4th | 21007 | 25026 |

- **Element X:** The first two energies are moderate (900 and 1757 kJ/mol), but the 3rd ionisation energy jumps enormously to 14849 kJ/mol (an 8.5-fold increase!). This proves that removing the 3rd electron breaks into a filled inner shell ($1\text{s}^2$). Thus, X has 2 valence electrons: **Group 2, valency = 2**. In Period 2, this is **Beryllium (Be)**.
- **Element Y:** The first three energies increase steadily (801, 2427, 3660 kJ/mol), but the 4th ionisation energy spikes to 25026 kJ/mol (a 6.8-fold jump!). Thus, Y has 3 valence electrons: **Group 13, valency = 3**. In Period 2, this is **Boron (B)**.

*Quantitative check:* Energy required to convert 1 mole of gaseous Be into $\text{Be}^{2+}$ ions = $900 + 1757 = \mathbf{2657\text{ kJ}}$. For 2 moles of Be: $2 \times 2657 = \mathbf{5314\text{ kJ}}$. Energy to convert 0.5 mole of gaseous B into $\text{B}^{3+}$ ions: $(801 + 2427 + 3660) \times 0.5 = 6888 \times 0.5 = \mathbf{3444\text{ kJ}}$.

**Check:** Massive jumps occur after 2 electrons for Be and after 3 electrons for B ✅.

### Puzzle 3. Mystery Elements from Family Descriptions

**Question:** Identify each element from its description:
(a) An unreactive gas with 2 electron shells, a completely filled valence shell, and negligible chemical reactivity.
(b) A dark purplish-black solid non-metal with 5 electron shells belonging to Group 17.
(c) A shiny, brittle solid in Period 3 with 4 valence electrons that acts as a semiconductor and forms an insoluble oxide commonly found in sand.

- (a) Period 2 with filled valence octet (2, 8): **Neon (Ne)** ($Z = 10$).
- (b) Group 17 with 5 electron shells: **Iodine (I)** ($Z = 53$).
- (c) Period 3, Group 14 (configuration 2, 8, 4), semiconductor forming $\text{SiO}_2$: **Silicon (Si)** ($Z = 14$).

**Check:** Ne has $2 + 8 = 10$ electrons ✅. Iodine is a period 5 halogen solid ✅. Silicon has $Z = 14$ and forms quartz/sand $\text{SiO}_2$ ✅.

### Puzzle 5. Deducing Elements from Oxide Behaviour

**Question:** Four Period 3 elements form oxides: Oxide A reacts only with acids; Oxide B reacts with both acids and strong alkalis; Oxide C resembles sand and reacts only with hot concentrated alkali; Oxide D dissolves in water to form a violently acidic solution. Identify elements A, B, C, and D from Mg, Al, Si, and Cl, and arrange them in order from left to right across the Periodic Table.

- Oxide A reacts only with acids $\rightarrow$ Basic oxide $\rightarrow$ **Mg** ($\text{MgO}$).
- Oxide B reacts with both acids and alkalis $\rightarrow$ Amphoteric oxide $\rightarrow$ **Al** ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$).
- Oxide C resembles sand, weakly acidic $\rightarrow$ **Si** ($\text{SiO}_2$).
- Oxide D dissolves to form strong acid $\rightarrow$ Strongly acidic oxide $\rightarrow$ **Cl** ($\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$).
- Left-to-right order: **Mg, Al, Si, Cl** ($Z = 12, 13, 14, 17$).

**Check:** Progression: Basic $\rightarrow$ Amphoteric $\rightarrow$ Weakly acidic $\rightarrow$ Strongly acidic mirrors metallic to non-metallic transition ✅.

### Puzzle 6. Bond Character from Electronegativity Differences

Rule of thumb: A difference of **0** indicates pure equal sharing (pure covalent); a difference of **~2.0 or greater** indicates electron transfer (predominantly ionic); intermediate differences indicate **polar covalent** bonding (unequal sharing).

| Bond | Pauling EN Values | Difference | Predicted Bond Character | Negative End |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Br–Br ($\text{Br}_2$) | 2.8, 2.8 | 0.0 | Pure covalent (equal sharing) | None |
| P–Cl ($\text{PCl}_3$) | 2.1, 3.0 | 0.9 | Polar covalent (unequal sharing) | Cl ($\delta^-$) |
| S–H ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$) | 2.5, 2.1 | 0.4 | Weakly polar covalent | S ($\delta^-$) |
| Si–O ($\text{SiO}_2$) | 1.8, 3.5 | 1.7 | Strongly polar covalent | O ($\delta^-$) |
| K–Br ($\text{KBr}$) | 0.8, 2.8 | 2.0 | Predominantly ionic | $\text{Br}^-$ |
| Na–F ($\text{NaF}$) | 0.9, 4.0 | 3.1 | Strongly ionic | $\text{F}^-$ |

Where sharing is unequal, the atom with higher electronegativity pulls the electron cloud closer, acquiring partial negative charge ($\delta^-$). In Si–O, a difference of 1.7 shows substantial polar character, illustrating that chemical bonds form a continuous spectrum rather than two rigid categories.

**Check:** The largest differences occur in metal-nonmetal pairs (NaF = 3.1, KBr = 2.0) ✅.

### Puzzle 7. Descending a Group: Radii vs. Ionisation Energy

**Question:** Down Group 17, the approximate atomic radii of Cl, Br, and I are 99, 114, and 133 pm, while their electronegativities are 3.0, 2.8, and 2.5. Determine: (a) Which atom is physically largest? (b) From which atom is it hardest to remove a valence electron? (c) A student claims: "Bromine has both a larger radius and a higher ionisation energy than chlorine." Is the student correct?

- (a) **Iodine (I)** is physically largest (133 pm), having 5 electron shells.
- (b) "Hardest to remove an electron" means having the highest ionisation energy. Ionisation energy decreases down Group 17: Cl (1251 kJ/mol) > Br (1140 kJ/mol) > I (1008 kJ/mol). It is hardest to remove an electron from **Chlorine (Cl)**.
- (c) The student's claim about radius is correct ($114 > 99\text{ pm}$), but the claim about ionisation energy is **false**: bromine has a *lower* ionisation energy than chlorine ($1140 < 1251\text{ kJ/mol}$) because atomic radius and ionisation energy run in opposite directions down a group!

**Check:** As radius increases ($99 \rightarrow 114 \rightarrow 133\text{ pm}$), ionisation energy decreases ($1251 \rightarrow 1140 \rightarrow 1008\text{ kJ/mol}$) ✅.

### Puzzle 8. Isoelectronic Anions: Same Electrons, Different Sizes

**Question:** The anions $\text{P}^{3-}$, $\text{S}^{2-}$, and $\text{Cl}^-$ all contain 18 electrons. Arrange them in order of decreasing ionic radius and provide the scientific reason.

- **Step 1:** All three ions are isoelectronic with argon (2, 8, 8), possessing 3 electron shells and 18 electrons.
- **Step 2:** Nuclear charges: P has 15 protons, S has 16 protons, and Cl has 17 protons.
- **Step 3 & 4:** **Decreasing order: $\text{P}^{3-} > \text{S}^{2-} > \text{Cl}^-$.**
  *Reason:* All three ions have 18 electrons, but the nuclear charge increases from 15 protons in $\text{P}^{3-}$ to 16 in $\text{S}^{2-}$ and 17 in $\text{Cl}^-$. The greater positive charge in chlorine's nucleus pulls the 18 electrons inward most tightly, making $\text{Cl}^-$ the smallest.

*Approximate ionic radii:* $\text{P}^{3-}$ (212 pm), $\text{S}^{2-}$ (184 pm), $\text{Cl}^-$ (181 pm).
**Check:** $212 > 184 > 181\text{ pm}$ and protons: $15 < 16 < 17$ ✅.

### Puzzle 9. Five Questions from One Spot on the Chalk Grid

**Question:** Mia stands in the chalk grid at the square for Period 3, Group 15.
(i) Which element is she standing on?
(ii) What is its valency?
(iii) Is the element to her immediate left larger or smaller in atomic size?
(iv) Does the element directly below her in Period 4 have higher or lower electronegativity?
(v) Is her element a metal or a non-metal?

- (i) Period 3, Group 15 has electron configuration 2, 8, 5: **Phosphorus (P)** ($Z = 15$).
- (ii) With 5 valence electrons, its non-metallic valency = $8 - 5 = \mathbf{3}$.
- (iii) To her left is Silicon (Si): Si has fewer protons (14 vs 15), meaning a weaker nuclear pull, so Si is **larger** in size (Si 118 pm > P 110 pm).
- (iv) Directly below in Period 4 is Arsenic (As): having more shells, its electronegativity is **lower**.
- (v) Positioned in the upper-right section of the table, phosphorus is a **non-metal**.

**Check:** Group 15 indicates 5 valence electrons and 15 protons ✅.

## Part C: The Chalk Grid Activity (On the Field or in the Courtyard)

This collaborative outdoor activity can be conducted on a school sports ground or courtyard. It uses only chalk, measuring tape, and element cards—no chemicals required.

**Materials needed:** Sidewalk chalk, measuring tape, 18 cards displaying element symbols and proton counts, and teacher supervision.

**Safety tips:** Avoid blowing chalk dust into eyes or faces; wash hands thoroughly afterwards. Do not conduct the activity on wet or slippery surfaces. Walk calmly; do not run.

**Activity Steps:**
1. Draw the 3-row by 8-column grid shown in Figure 6.1 (1-metre squares).
2. Assign each student an element card and have them stand in their respective square.
3. **The Period Walk:** Walk slowly from Na to Cl. At each square, announce: *"Protons increase by 1, shells stay at 3!"* To visually represent atomic contraction, each student holds their arms slightly closer to their sides than their left-hand neighbour, modeling the shrinking atomic radius from Na (186 pm) to Cl (99 pm).
4. **The Group Walk:** Walk down Group 1 from Li to Na to K. At each step, place a wider rope circle on the ground around the student to represent an additional electron shell, demonstrating the expanding atomic radius.
5. **The Tug-of-War Pull Game:** Two students—one representing Na and one representing Cl—hold opposite ends of a short rope. The Cl student holds more "proton cards" (representing effective pull), demonstrating that chlorine pulls the shared rope closer to itself. This models electronegativity: the atom with higher effective pull draws the shared electrons towards itself.

```
   Na   Mg   Al   Si   P    S    Cl
   --> Protons increase steadily, shells remain constant at 3
   --> Atomic radius contracts, electronegativity rises
   --> Character transitions from active metal to active non-metal
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Summary of the Period 3 walk: nuclear charge increases while shells stay constant. Consequently, atomic size contracts, electronegativity increases, and chemical character transitions from electropositive metal to electronegative non-metal.

Oliver asked the students: "Now stand in any square and state how you differ from your four immediate neighbours: left, right, up, down. Four directions, four distinct answers." Mia chose Nitrogen (N):
- To her left (C): fewer protons $\rightarrow$ larger radius, lower electronegativity.
- To her right (O): more protons $\rightarrow$ smaller radius, higher electronegativity.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "The atomic size trend breaks down at noble gases because neon is larger than fluorine."**
   Fluorine's covalent radius is approximately 64 pm, measured between bonded atoms. Neon does not form chemical bonds under standard conditions, so its size is measured as a **van der Waals radius** (the distance of closest non-bonded approach), yielding approximately 154 pm. Comparing a non-bonded van der Waals radius (154 pm) directly to a bonded covalent radius (64 pm) creates a false illusion. If fluorine's van der Waals radius is measured identically, it is approximately 147 pm—very close to neon. *Takeaway:* Always compare like-with-like when analyzing periodic trends, which is why noble gases are evaluated separately.

2. **Mistake 2: "Every property down a group is the exact opposite of its trend across a period."**
   While properties like size, ionisation energy, and electronegativity reverse direction, fundamental structural properties do not:
   - **Valence electrons** increase from 1 to 8 across a period, but remain **constant** down a group (Group 1 elements all have 1 valence electron).
   - **Electron shells** remain **constant** across a period, but **increase** down a group.
   - **Protons** increase in **both** directions!
   *Takeaway:* Do not memorize crude rules of opposites; reason each property from shells and nuclear charge.

3. **Mistake 3: "Total electrons and valence electrons are the same thing."**
   Chlorine has 17 total electrons, but only 7 valence electrons ($2, 8, 7$). Its valency is $8 - 7 = 1$, not 17! Similarly, aluminium has 13 total electrons, but only 3 valence electrons ($2, 8, 3$), forming $\text{Al}^{3+}$. The large jumps in successive ionisation energies (Puzzle 2) occur precisely when you break past the valence electrons into the stable inner core.

4. **Mistake 4: "All elements bordering the staircase are metalloids."**
   The staircase separates metals from non-metals, but elements bordering it are not all metalloids. Boron (B) and Silicon (Si) are true metalloids (brittle, semiconducting). Aluminium (Al) borders the staircase, but it is unequivocally a **metal**: it is malleable, ductile, highly conductive, and forms metallic cations ($\text{Al}^{3+}$). If you strike silicon with a hammer, it shatters into powder; if you strike aluminium, it flattens into foil.

## Quick Recap

- **4-step method:** Count shells, evaluate nuclear pull, state the trend, state the scientific reason.
- **Across a period:** Size decreases; ionisation energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, and non-metallic character increase.
- **Down a group:** Size increases; ionisation energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, and non-metallic character decrease; metallic character increases.
- **Isoelectronic species:** When electron count is identical, the species with more protons is smaller ($\text{N}^{3-} > \text{F}^- > \text{Al}^{3+}$).
- **Ionisation energy jumps:** A sudden massive spike reveals the number of valence electrons (Be: jump after 2nd electron; B: jump after 3rd).
- **Electronegativity differences:** $\Delta\text{EN} = 0 \rightarrow$ pure covalent; intermediate $\rightarrow$ polar covalent; $\Delta\text{EN} \ge 2.0 \rightarrow$ predominantly ionic.
- Distinct definitions: Valence electrons $\ne$ total electrons; metalloids lie on the staircase, but aluminium is a metal; noble gases require distinct non-bonded measurements.

6. For each of the following bonds, calculate the electronegativity difference using H (2.1), I (2.5), Na (0.9), O (3.5), N (3.0), C (2.5), and Cl (3.0). Classify each bond as pure covalent, polar covalent, or ionic, and identify the atom that pulls harder in polar cases:
   (a) N–N in $\text{N}_2$
   (b) H–I
   (c) Na and O in $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$
   (d) C–Cl in $\text{CCl}_4$
7. State whether each statement is True or False, and provide a clear justification:
   (a) Silicon has 14 valence electrons.
   (b) Every element bordering the staircase is classified as a metalloid.
   (c) The electronegativity of sulfur is greater than that of phosphorus.
   (d) The number of valence electrons increases down Group 17.
8. Element W is a yellow solid non-metal in Period 3 with 6 valence electrons. Name it. Compare it with oxygen (its Group 16 neighbour in Period 2) in terms of atomic radius and electronegativity (given: S: 103 pm, EN 2.5; O: 66 pm, EN 3.5).
9. A student stands on the chalk grid at the square for Period 2, Group 16. Name the element, then identify which neighbouring element (left, right, or below) is:
   (a) Larger in size and lower in electronegativity.
   (b) Smaller in size and higher in electronegativity.
10. Calculate the energy required to remove one electron from each atom in 0.5 mole of gaseous bromine atoms, and from 3 moles of gaseous chlorine atoms (First ionisation energies: Br = 1140 kJ/mol, Cl = 1251 kJ/mol). Which element loses its valence electron more readily?
11. Both $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Al}^{3+}$ contain 10 electrons. Which ion has a smaller radius, and why? (Given: Na has 11 protons, Al has 13 protons; approximate radii: 102 pm and 54 pm.)
12. Three oxides of Period 3 elements exhibit the following properties: Oxide A reacts only with acids; Oxide B reacts with both acids and alkalis; Oxide C reacts only with alkalis and produces an acidic solution with water. Match each oxide to its element (chosen from Na, Al, and Cl) and arrange the three elements in order of decreasing metallic character.
13. Four Period 2 elements have 1, 3, 5, and 6 valence electrons. Name them, arrange them in increasing order of electronegativity, and state which one readily forms a simple monoatomic cation.
14. A student states: "Aluminium has 13 electrons, so its valency is 13." Correct the student's statement, showing the proper working to determine aluminium's true valency.

### Answers

1. **Step 1:** All elements belong to Period 3 (3 electron shells).
   **Step 2:** Protons: Al (13), Si (14), P (15), S (16).
   **Step 3 & 4:** Nuclear charge increases while shells stay constant, pulling electrons tighter.
   **Increasing order of atomic size: S < P < Si < Al.** (Approx. radii: $103 < 110 < 118 < 143\text{ pm}$ ✅).
2. **Electronegativity order:** C (2.5) < N (3.0) < O (3.5) $\rightarrow$ **C < N < O**.
   **Atomic size order:** With shells constant at 2, increasing nuclear charge (C: 6, N: 7, O: 8 protons) draws electrons inward $\rightarrow$ **O < N < C** ($66 < 74 < 77\text{ pm}$).
   *Unified reason:* Across Period 2, the number of electron shells remains constant (2) while nuclear charge increases from 6 to 8 protons. This intensified pull contracts atomic radius and increases the pull on shared electrons.
3. Period 3 with 2 valence electrons ($2, 8, 2$): **Magnesium (Mg)** ($Z = 12$). Valency = **2** (loses 2 electrons to form $\text{Mg}^{2+}$). It is a **metal**. Its oxide, $\text{MgO}$, is **basic**.
4. (a) A massive jump occurs after the 4th electron ($4356 \rightarrow 16091\text{ kJ/mol}$, a 3.7-fold increase), indicating **4 valence electrons**.
   (b) 4 valence electrons $\rightarrow$ **Group 14** (Silicon, $Z = 14$).
   (c) Energy for 1 mole = $786 + 1577 + 3232 + 4356 = 9951\text{ kJ}$. For 2 moles: $2 \times 9951 = \mathbf{19902\text{ kJ}}$.
5. - Atomic radius increases ($74 \rightarrow 110\text{ pm}$): P has 3 shells while N has 2; an extra shell places valence electrons further out.
   - Ionisation energy decreases ($1402 \rightarrow 1012\text{ kJ/mol}$): In P, valence electrons are further from the nucleus and shielded by inner shells, requiring less energy to remove.
   - Electronegativity decreases ($3.0 \rightarrow 2.1$): The greater distance and shielding reduce phosphorus's pull on shared electrons.
6. (a) N–N: $3.0 - 3.0 = 0.0$ $\rightarrow$ **Pure covalent (equal sharing)**.
   (b) H–I: $2.5 - 2.1 = 0.4$ $\rightarrow$ **Polar covalent (unequal sharing)**, Iodine (I) pulls harder.
   (c) Na and O: $3.5 - 0.9 = 2.6$ $\rightarrow$ **Ionic bond**, Oxygen attracts electron completely to form $\text{O}^{2-}$.
   (d) C–Cl: $3.0 - 2.5 = 0.5$ $\rightarrow$ **Polar covalent (unequal sharing)**, Chlorine (Cl) pulls harder.
7. (a) **False** ❌: Silicon ($2, 8, 4$) has 14 total electrons, but only 4 valence electrons.
   (b) **False** ❌: Boron and silicon are metalloids, but aluminium borders the staircase and is a true metal.
   (c) **True** ✅: Sulfur (2.5) has more protons than phosphorus (2.1) in Period 3, so its electronegativity is higher.
   (d) **False** ❌: All elements in Group 17 have exactly 7 valence electrons.
8. Period 3 with 6 valence electrons: **Sulfur (S)**.
   - Atomic size: $\text{S} (103\text{ pm}) > \text{O} (66\text{ pm})$. Sulfur has 3 shells while oxygen has 2, making sulfur larger.
   - Electronegativity: $\text{O} (3.5) > \text{S} (2.5)$. Oxygen has fewer shells, so its nucleus attracts bonding electrons more powerfully.
9. Period 2, Group 16 is **Oxygen (O)**.
   (a) Larger in size and lower in electronegativity: **Nitrogen (N)** (to the left: 74 pm, EN 3.0) and **Sulfur (S)** (below: 103 pm, EN 2.5).
   (b) Smaller in size and higher in electronegativity: **Fluorine (F)** (to the right: 64 pm, EN 4.0).
10. For Br: $0.5\text{ mol} \times 1140\text{ kJ/mol} = \mathbf{570\text{ kJ}}$.
    For Cl: $3\text{ mol} \times 1251\text{ kJ/mol} = \mathbf{3753\text{ kJ}}$.
    **Bromine (Br)** loses its valence electron more readily because its ionisation energy is lower ($1140 < 1251\text{ kJ/mol}$) due to its larger atomic radius and greater shielding.
11. **$\text{Al}^{3+}$ is smaller.** Both ions have 10 electrons in 2 shells ($2, 8$), but aluminium has 13 protons while sodium has only 11 protons. The higher positive charge in $\text{Al}^{3+}$ pulls the 10 electrons inward more strongly ($54\text{ pm} < 102\text{ pm}$).
12. Oxide A (reacts only with acids) = $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ $\rightarrow$ **Sodium (Na)**.
    Oxide B (amphoteric) = $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ $\rightarrow$ **Aluminium (Al)**.
    Oxide C (strongly acidic) = $\text{Cl}_2\text{O}_7$ $\rightarrow$ **Chlorine (Cl)**.
    Decreasing metallic character: **Na > Al > Cl**.
13. Period 2 elements: 1 valence $e^- \rightarrow$ **Li**; 3 valence $e^- \rightarrow$ **B**; 5 valence $e^- \rightarrow$ **N**; 6 valence $e^- \rightarrow$ **O**.
    Increasing electronegativity: **Li (1.0) < B (2.0) < N (3.0) < O (3.5)**.
    **Lithium (Li)** readily forms a positive monoatomic cation ($\text{Li}^+$).
14. The student is incorrect because 13 represents the total number of electrons, not the valence electrons. Aluminium has electron configuration $2, 8, 3$. It has 3 valence electrons, which it readily loses to attain a stable octet ($2, 8$), giving aluminium a true valency of **3** (forming $\text{Al}^{3+}$).

| Topic | Core Concept Explored | Essential Summary Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | Atomic and Ionic Radius | Governed by shells and effective pull: decreases across a period, increases down a group; cations shrink, anions expand. |

| **5** | Metallic & Non-metallic Character | Metals lose electrons (basic oxides); non-metals gain electrons (acidic oxides); oxides shift from basic $\rightarrow$ amphoteric $\rightarrow$ acidic across Period 3. |
