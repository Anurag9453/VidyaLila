# Topic 1: How Big Is an Atom? Atomic and Ionic Size

In Class 9, we pictured an atom like people sitting in concentric rings around a campfire: the nucleus is the fire at the centre, the electron shells are the seating rows, and the outermost (valence) electrons sit in the back row ([periodic-table-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/periodic-table/periodic-table-part4.md)). Back then, we saw the basic pattern: atoms shrink as you move from left to right across a period, and grow larger as you move down a group. In this chapter, we take that picture **deeper**: which two physical factors actually control an atom's size, and by how much? We will look at real (approximate) measurements in picometres, and discover what happens to an atom's size when it loses or gains electrons to become an ion.

---

## A Fridge Magnet and Sheets of Notebook Paper

Zara was visiting her uncle, Dr. Brooks, over the weekend. On the refrigerator door was a small round button magnet. Zara slipped a single sheet of notebook paper underneath the magnet, and the magnet held it firmly against the steel door. Then she slipped in a second sheet, and a third. Through three sheets of paper, the magnet's grip still felt solid.

"Uncle, let's see how many sheets it can hold!" Zara said. Dr. Brooks held his palm just below the magnet: "Go ahead, carefully — keep a hand underneath so the magnet doesn't drop. And always do this with an adult nearby." Zara stacked 10 sheets under the magnet, then 20. Suddenly, the magnet's grip grew **weak** and it slid right off into her waiting hand — even though the refrigerator door and the magnet itself hadn't changed at all.

"The magnet didn't lose its magnetism, Uncle. Why did it let go?" Dr. Brooks explained: "The more **layers** of paper you placed between the magnet and the steel door, the less of the magnet's pull reached the door. The magnet's strength stayed the same, but both the distance and the intervening layers blocked its grip."

Zara's eyes lit up: "Does the exact same thing happen inside an atom?" Dr. Brooks smiled: "Spot on. The positively charged nucleus acts like the magnet pulling on the outer electrons, and the filled inner electron shells act like those sheets of paper. Let's see how those two factors determine the size of every atom in the Periodic Table."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atomic radius** | The approximate distance from an atom's nucleus to its outermost electron shell, measured in picometres (pm) | Na is about 186 pm |
| **Picometre (pm)** | An extremely tiny unit of length: 1 pm = 10⁻¹² m | 100 pm = 0.1 nm |
| **Nuclear pull** | The attractive pull of the positively charged protons in the nucleus on the surrounding electrons | More protons = stronger nuclear pull |
| **Shielding (screening)** | The effect of inner-shell electrons blocking and reducing the nuclear pull felt by outer-shell electrons | Like sheets of paper between a magnet and a fridge |
| **Ionic radius** | The radius of an ion (approximate distance from the nucleus to the outermost shell of the ion) | Na⁺ is about 102 pm |
| **Cation** | A positively charged ion formed when an atom loses one or more electrons | Na⁺, Mg²⁺ |
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion formed when an atom gains one or more electrons | Cl⁻, O²⁻ |
| **Isoelectronic** | Atoms or ions that have the exact same total number of electrons | O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ all have 10 electrons |

## Part A: Two Core Ideas That Decide Atomic Size

Every single periodic trend in this chapter can be understood using just **two core ideas**, both illustrated by our fridge-magnet story:

**Idea 1: Number of electron shells (distance).** The more electron shells an atom has, the farther its outermost electrons sit from the nucleus. The farther away an electron is, the weaker the pull it feels (just as a magnet's grip weakens with distance).

**Idea 2: Nuclear pull and shielding.** Protons in the nucleus pull all electrons inward. However, electrons in the filled inner shells **block (shield)** a large portion of that pull, just like the sheets of notebook paper. As a result, an outer electron does not feel the full pull of all the protons — it only feels the **net (remaining) pull**.

Here is a **simple rule-of-thumb estimate** for that net pull: **an outer electron feels a net pull roughly equal to (number of protons) − (number of inner-shell electrons).** (In higher classes you will learn more exact ways to calculate effective nuclear charge, but this simple subtraction explains the trends beautifully.)

| Atom | Protons | Inner-Shell Electrons | Net Pull (Rule-of-Thumb) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Li | 3 | 2 | 3 − 2 = 1 |
| Na | 11 | 10 | 11 − 10 = 1 |
| K | 19 | 18 | 19 − 18 = 1 |
| Cl | 17 | 10 | 17 − 10 = 7 |

Look closely at that table: in Li, Na, and K, the net pull felt by the outermost electron is **the same (1)**! So why do atoms get larger as you go down Group 1? Because each step down adds a whole new electron shell, placing the outer electron **farther** from the nucleus. Now compare Na and Cl across Period 3: both have 10 inner-shell electrons, but the proton count rises from 11 to 17. The net pull jumps from **1 to 7** while the number of shells stays at 3!

```
    Down a Group (Li, Na, K)                  Across a Period (Na, Cl)

    Li:  (+3) ))   e                           Na:  (+11) ))) e
         2 shells, net pull 1, e close              3 shells, net pull 1
    Na:  (+11) )))   e                         Cl:  (+17) ))) e
         3 shells, net pull 1, e farther            3 shells, net pull 7
    K:   (+19) ))))   e                        Same 3 shells, much stronger pull:
         4 shells, net pull 1, e even farther  shells are pulled closer to the nucleus

    (+n) = nucleus with n protons            ) = one electron shell
    e = outermost electron (close or far)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – The two core ideas at a glance. Left: going down a group adds shells while the net pull stays roughly constant (1), so the outer electron sits farther out and the atom gets larger. Right: going across Period 3 from Na to Cl keeps the shell count at 3, but the net pull increases from 1 to 7, pulling the shells inward and making the atom smaller. (Schematic diagram; real atoms are spherical.)

**One honest note about our magnet analogy:** Sheets of paper only add distance and physical layers between the magnet and the fridge. Inside a real atom, inner-shell electrons are negatively charged, so they actively **repel** the negatively charged outer electrons outward, cancelling out part of the nucleus's inward pull. Both ways of picturing it lead to the exact same conclusion!

## Part B: How Atomic Radius Is Measured (A Quick Note)

An atom does not have a hard, sharp outer shell like a billiard ball. Instead, chemists measure atomic radius indirectly — for example, by taking two identical atoms bonded together (in a metal crystal or a covalent molecule) and measuring **half the distance between their nuclei**. Because different measurement methods give slightly different numbers, different reference tables may list slightly different radii. However, **the periodic trend (order of sizes) is identical across every table**, so in this chapter we will use one consistent set of approximate values in picometres (1 pm = 10⁻¹² m).

**A quick note on noble gases:** Because He, Ne, and Ar do not readily form chemical bonds, their radii are measured using non-bonded collisions (van der Waals radii), which makes their numbers artificially large and not directly comparable to bonded radii. For that reason, we examine atomic size trends from Li to F and from Na to Cl (just as we did in Class 9).

## Part C: Down a Group: Atomic Size Increases

Look at the approximate atomic radii (in pm) for Group 1:

| Atom | Shells | Approximate Radius (pm) |
|---|---|---|
| Li | 2 | 152 |
| Na | 3 | 186 |
| K | 4 | 227 |
| Rb | 5 | 248 |

We see the exact same pattern in Group 17: F (2 shells) is about 64 pm, while Cl (3 shells) is about 99 pm. Moving down a group adds a new electron shell at every step, so the atomic radius increases. Notice that the step size isn't uniform (Li to Na increases by 34 pm, Na to K by 41 pm, and K to Rb by 21 pm), but the **direction** never changes: larger as you go down.

### Example 1. Li, Na, K: Which Is Largest, and Why?

- Li: 2 shells (2, 1). Na: 3 shells (2, 8, 1). K: 4 shells (2, 8, 8, 1).
- All three experience roughly the same net nuclear pull of 1 (from the table in Part A).
- Therefore, the number of shells decides the size: more shells = larger atom.
- **K > Na > Li.**

**Check:** The measured values are 227 > 186 > 152 pm — the exact same order ✅

### Example 2. Be and Mg: Same Group, Different Periods

- Be: 2, 2 (2 shells). Mg: 2, 8, 2 (3 shells).
- Both are in Group 2 (2 valence electrons), but Mg has one extra electron shell.
- **Mg is larger.** Approximate radii: Be is 111 pm, Mg is 160 pm.

**Check:** Going down a group increases the number of shells and the atomic radius, and Mg sits below Be ✅

## Part D: Across a Period: Atomic Size Decreases

Compare the approximate atomic radii (in pm) across Period 2 and Period 3:

```
    Period 2:   Li    Be    B     C     N     O     F
    Protons     3     4     5     6     7     8     9
    Shells      2     2     2     2     2     2     2
    Radius      152   111   88    77    74    66    64

    Period 3:   Na    Mg    Al    Si    P     S     Cl
    Protons     11    12    13    14    15    16    17
    Shells      3     3     3     3     3     3     3
    Radius      186   160   143   118   110   103   99

    -----> Same number of shells, more protons, radius decreases ----->
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Approximate atomic radii (in pm) across Period 2 and Period 3. Each step to the right adds one proton and one electron while the number of shells stays constant, pulling the outer shell inward so the radius shrinks at every step.

Notice a remarkable parallel: from Li to F, the radius drops from 152 pm to 64 pm — a total shrinkage of **88 pm**. From Na to Cl, the radius drops from 186 pm to 99 pm — a total shrinkage of **87 pm**! The inward-pulling effect across both periods is nearly identical.

Why does this happen? As you move from left to right across a period, each new electron goes into **the same outer shell**. Because the inner shells do not change, the shielding stays nearly constant. Meanwhile, each step adds one more proton to the nucleus, so the net inward pull increases steadily (from 1 in Na to 7 in Cl), drawing the electron shells closer to the nucleus.

### Example 3. Ordering Li to F by Atomic Size Using Protons

- From Li (3 protons) to F (9 protons), every atom has 2 electron shells.
- More protons = stronger net pull = smaller atomic radius.
- **Li > Be > B > C > N > O > F.**

**Check:** 152 > 111 > 88 > 77 > 74 > 66 > 64 pm ✅ (matching the trend we first saw in Class 9).

### Example 4. Period 3: Step-by-Step Shrinkage

From Na to Mg, the radius drops from 186 pm to 160 pm (a decrease of **26 pm**). From Mg to Al, it drops from 160 pm to 143 pm (a decrease of **17 pm**). How do protons explain this?

- Both Na (11 protons) and Mg (12 protons) have 3 electron shells.
- Mg has one more proton in its nucleus, pulling those 3 shells tighter inward and reducing the radius.
- Moving to Al (13 protons) adds another proton to the same 3 shells, shrinking the radius even further.

**Check:** Na → Mg → Al radii go 186 → 160 → 143 pm, decreasing at each step ✅.

## Part E: A Cation Is Smaller Than Its Parent Atom

From Class 9, we know that a sodium atom loses 1 electron to form Na⁺, and a magnesium atom loses 2 electrons to form Mg²⁺:

Na (2, 8, 1) → Na⁺ (2, 8). Mg (2, 8, 2) → Mg²⁺ (2, 8).

Two effects work together to make a cation much smaller than its neutral parent atom:

1. **The entire outermost shell disappears.** Once Na loses its single 3rd-shell electron, the 3rd shell is empty, leaving the 2nd shell as the new outer boundary.
2. **The remaining electrons feel a stronger pull per electron.** Na⁺ still has 11 protons, but now those 11 protons are pulling on only 10 electrons instead of 11. Fewer electrons sharing the same nuclear pull means they are held tighter.

```
    Na  (11 protons, 11 electrons)         Na+  (11 protons, 10 electrons)

         (+11) ))) e                            (+11) ))
         3 shells, radius ~186 pm               2 shells, radius ~102 pm

    Mg  (12 protons, 12 electrons)         Mg2+ (12 protons, 10 electrons)

         (+12) ))) ee                           (+12) ))
         3 shells, radius ~160 pm               2 shells, radius ~72 pm
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Forming a cation shrinks the atom dramatically. Na → Na⁺: the 3rd shell is lost, and the radius drops from ~186 pm to ~102 pm. Mg → Mg²⁺: the 3rd shell is lost and 12 protons pull on just 10 electrons, shrinking the radius from ~160 pm to ~72 pm.

### Example 5. Comparing Na and Na⁺

- Na: 11 electrons, 3 shells, ~186 pm. Na⁺: 10 electrons, 2 shells, ~102 pm.
- **Na⁺ is much smaller.** Ratio: 102 ÷ 186 = 0.55 — just over half the radius of the neutral Na atom!

**Check:** Losing an outer shell and reducing the electron count both shrink the radius ✅

### Example 6. Comparing Mg and Mg²⁺: An Even Bigger Drop

- Mg: 12 electrons, ~160 pm. Mg²⁺: 10 electrons, 12 protons, ~72 pm.
- Ratio: 72 ÷ 160 = 0.45 — Mg²⁺ is less than half the radius of a neutral Mg atom!
- Why is Mg²⁺ (72 pm) even smaller than Na⁺ (102 pm)? Because both have 10 electrons in 2 shells, but Mg²⁺ has **12 protons** pulling on those 10 electrons, whereas Na⁺ has only 11 protons.

**Check:** Mg²⁺ (72 pm) < Na⁺ (102 pm) ✅, and both cations are much smaller than their neutral atoms ✅

That is why in every ionic compound (Chapter 2) and during electrolysis when cations migrate to the cathode (Chapter 4), every metal cation is significantly smaller than the metal atom it came from.

## Part F: An Anion Is Larger Than Its Parent Atom

Now let's look at the opposite process: a chlorine atom gains 1 electron to form Cl⁻, and an oxygen atom gains 2 electrons to form O²⁻:

Cl (2, 8, 7) → Cl⁻ (2, 8, 8). O (2, 6) → O²⁻ (2, 8).

Here, **the number of shells does not change** (Cl⁻ still has 3 shells; O²⁻ still has 2 shells), yet the ion swells in size for two reasons:

1. The number of protons stays the same (17 in Cl⁻, 8 in O²⁻), while the number of electrons increases (to 18 and 10). **The same nuclear pull is now shared among more electrons**, so each electron is held slightly less tightly.
2. The extra electrons in the outer shell **repel one another**, pushing the outer shell outward so the cloud expands.

```
    Cl  (17 protons, 17 electrons)         Cl-  (17 protons, 18 electrons)

         (+17) ))) 7e                           (+17) ))) 8e
         radius ~99 pm                          radius ~181 pm

    O   (8 protons, 8 electrons)           O2-  (8 protons, 10 electrons)

         (+8) )) 6e                             (+8) )) 8e
         radius ~66 pm                          radius ~140 pm
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Forming an anion makes the particle expand. Cl → Cl⁻: same 3 shells and 17 protons, but electrons increase from 17 to 18, expanding the radius from ~99 pm to ~181 pm. O → O²⁻: same 2 shells and 8 protons, but electrons increase from 8 to 10, more than doubling the radius from ~66 pm to ~140 pm.

### Example 7. Comparing Cl and Cl⁻

- Cl: 17 electrons, ~99 pm. Cl⁻: 18 electrons, ~181 pm.
- **Cl⁻ is much larger** — about 181 ÷ 99 = 1.83 times the radius of a neutral Cl atom.

**Check:** Same 17 protons pulling on more electrons + increased electron-electron repulsion = larger radius ✅. That is why in salt water, the Cl⁻ anion (181 pm) is much larger than the Na⁺ cation (102 pm).

### Example 8. Comparing O and O²⁻: The Effect of Gaining Two Electrons

- O: 8 electrons, ~66 pm. O²⁻: 10 electrons, ~140 pm.
- Ratio: 140 ÷ 66 = 2.1 times as large!
- **O²⁻ is more than twice the size of O.** Gaining two extra electrons against the same 8 protons causes an even greater expansion.

**Check:** Gaining 1 electron (Cl → Cl⁻) expanded the radius by ~1.8×; gaining 2 electrons (O → O²⁻) expanded it by ~2.1× ✅

Here is a snappy memory aid: **"Cation: fewer electrons, smaller. Anion: more electrons, larger."**

## Part G: Isoelectronic Ions: Same Electron Count, Different Sizes

**Isoelectronic** species have the exact same total number of electrons. Look at O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺, and Mg²⁺: every single one of them has **10 electrons** arranged as (2, 8). However, their nuclei contain different numbers of protons:

| Ion | Protons | Electrons | Approximate Radius (pm) |
|---|---|---|---|
| O²⁻ | 8 | 10 | 140 |
| F⁻ | 9 | 10 | 133 |
| Na⁺ | 11 | 10 | 102 |
| Mg²⁺ | 12 | 10 | 72 |

Because all four ions have the exact same electron cloud (10 electrons in 2 shells), **nuclear pull is the sole tiebreaker**: the more protons in the nucleus, the harder those 10 electrons are pulled inward, and the smaller the ion!

```
    Size:    O2-  >  F-  >  Na+  >  Mg2+
    Protons:  8       9      11      12
             (all have 10 electrons in 2 shells)

    More protons  ---->  Stronger inward pull  ---->  Smaller ion
```

> **Figure 1.5** – The 10-electron isoelectronic series: O²⁻ (8 protons) is the largest, and Mg²⁺ (12 protons) is the smallest. When the electron count is identical, proton count determines the size.

### Example 9. Another Isoelectronic Pair: Cl⁻ and K⁺

Both Cl⁻ and K⁺ have **18 electrons** arranged as (2, 8, 8). Cl⁻ has 17 protons, while K⁺ has 19 protons.

- With equal electron counts, K⁺ has more protons pulling inward: **K⁺ is smaller than Cl⁻.**
- Approximate radii: Cl⁻ is 181 pm, K⁺ is 138 pm.

**Check:** 138 < 181 pm ✅. Notice the dramatic flip: as neutral atoms, K (227 pm) is more than twice as large as Cl (99 pm), but once they form ions, K⁺ (138 pm) becomes smaller than Cl⁻ (181 pm)!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Assuming that an atom or ion with more electrons is always larger.**

This is the single most common trap in this topic! Let's test it against real examples:

- Mg has 12 electrons and S has 16 electrons. Yet Mg (~160 pm) is much larger than S (~103 pm) — **the atom with fewer electrons is larger!** Why? Both have 3 shells, and S has 16 protons pulling those 3 shells tighter inward.
- Li (3 electrons, ~152 pm) vs. F (9 electrons, ~64 pm): again, the atom with more electrons is much smaller.
- O²⁻ (10 electrons) and Mg²⁺ (10 electrons) have the **exact same number of electrons**, yet their radii are ~140 pm and ~72 pm (Part G).
- Only when comparing K (19 electrons, 4 shells, 227 pm) with K⁺ (18 electrons, 3 shells, 138 pm) does more electrons mean larger — because that 19th electron adds a whole new 4th shell!

Moral: Atomic and ionic size is governed by **number of shells and net nuclear pull** (Part A), never by raw electron count alone.

**Mistake 2: Thinking that forming a cation only changes the charge, not the size.**

Neutral Na has 3 shells, whereas Na⁺ has only 2 shells, dropping its radius from ~186 pm to ~102 pm. A cation is *always* significantly smaller than its parent atom.

**Mistake 3: Thinking an anion gains protons when it gains electrons.**

Protons live locked inside the nucleus and never change during chemical reactions — only electrons are gained or lost. Cl⁻ still has 17 protons, now shared among 18 electrons, which is why the anion expands.

**Mistake 4: Arguing that going down a group adds protons, so atoms should get smaller down a group.**

Proton count does increase down a group (Li 3, Na 11, K 19), but each step down also adds filled inner shells that shield those extra protons. As we saw in Part A, the net pull in Li, Na, and K stays roughly constant (~1), so the addition of new shells wins completely and the atoms grow larger down the group.

## Quick Recap

- Atomic size is governed by two factors: **number of shells** (more shells = farther out = larger) and **net nuclear pull** (inward pull of protons minus **shielding** by inner-shell electrons).
- Rule-of-thumb net pull ≈ protons − inner-shell electrons. It stays roughly constant down a group and increases from left to right across a period.
- **Down a group:** Shells increase, so atomic radius **increases** (e.g., Li 152, Na 186, K 227, Rb 248 pm).
- **Across a period:** Shells stay the same while protons increase, so atomic radius **decreases** (e.g., Na 186 pm down to Cl 99 pm).
- Noble gas radii are measured by non-bonded methods and are not included in bonded-radius comparisons.
- **A cation is smaller than its parent atom** (outer shell lost, fewer electrons for the same protons). **An anion is larger than its parent atom** (more electrons sharing the same protons + electron-electron repulsion).
- **Isoelectronic ions** (e.g., O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺): same number of electrons, so more protons = smaller ion.
- Never assume "more electrons = larger atom."

### Answers

1. **Rb is larger.** Rb has 5 shells whereas Na has 3 shells, placing Rb's outer electron much farther from the nucleus. Both experience the same approximate net pull (Rb: 37 − 36 = 1; Na: 11 − 10 = 1), so the number of shells decides the size. Radius: 248 > 186 pm ✅.
2. S has 3 shells, whereas O and F have only 2 shells — making S the largest. O and F are both in Period 2 (2 shells), but F has 9 protons compared to O's 8 protons: with the same number of shells, more protons pull tighter and make the atom smaller. Order: **S > O > F** (~103, 66, 64 pm) ✅.
3. Mg: 3 shells, 12 protons (160 pm). Al: 3 shells, 13 protons (143 pm). S: 3 shells, 16 protons (103 pm). Same number of shells, more protons = smaller radius. Increasing order: **S < Al < Mg** ✅ (103 < 143 < 160 pm).
4. Al: 2, 8, 3 (3 shells). Al³⁺: 2, 8 (2 shells). Losing 3 electrons removes the entire 3rd shell, and 13 protons now pull strongly on just 10 electrons. Radius drops from ~143 pm to ~54 pm (54 ÷ 143 = 0.38, or about 38% of the atom's radius) ✅.
5. S: 16 protons, 16 electrons. S²⁻: 16 protons, 18 electrons. With the same 16 protons now shared among 2 extra electrons — plus increased electron-electron repulsion in the outer shell — the radius expands from 103 pm to 184 pm ✅.
6. S²⁻: 16 protons, 18 electrons. K⁺: 19 protons, 18 electrons. Ca²⁺: 20 protons, 18 electrons. All three have 18 electrons (2, 8, 8), so they are isoelectronic. More protons = stronger inward pull = smaller ion. Decreasing order: **S²⁻ > K⁺ > Ca²⁺** (184 > 138 > 100 pm) ✅.
7. Be (4 electrons) is ~111 pm while N (7 electrons) is ~74 pm, so N is **smaller** than Be! Both have 2 shells, but N has 7 protons compared to Be's 4, pulling the 2 shells much closer to the nucleus. Thus "more electrons = larger atom" is false ✅.
8. Neutral atoms: **Be (111 pm) is larger** than F (64 pm). Ions: **F⁻ (133 pm) is much larger** than Be²⁺ (45 pm). The order flips because Be loses 2 electrons (losing its outer 2nd shell so 4 protons pull tightly on 2 electrons), whereas F gains 1 electron (so 9 protons are shared among 10 repelling electrons). Forming a cation shrinks Be; forming an anion expands F ✅.
9. Li to Na: 186 − 152 = **34 pm**. Na to K: 227 − 186 = **41 pm**. K to Rb: 248 − 227 = **21 pm**. The step increase is not uniform, but **the direction of change is always the same** (radius increases at every step down the group) ✅.
10. Rb has 5 shells; Rb⁺ has 4 shells (its outermost shell is lost). The radius falls from 248 pm to 152 pm — a drop of 96 pm, or 96 ÷ 248 × 100 ≈ **38.7%** (about 39%) ✅.
11. Rb has 5 shells while Be has only 2 shells. Their rule-of-thumb net pulls are both small (Rb: 37 − 36 = 1; Be: 4 − 2 = 2), but Rb's outer electron sits 3 shells farther out, making Rb much larger (248 pm vs. 111 pm). If the number of shells stays **constant** (such as across a period from Na to Cl), adding protons always makes the atom **smaller**, never larger ✅.
12. (a) **True** ✅: losing electrons either removes the outer shell or leaves fewer electrons pulled more tightly by the same protons. (b) **False** ❌: proton count never changes when an ion forms; an anion has more *electrons* than its parent atom. (c) **True** ✅: Mg²⁺ has 12 − 2 = 10 electrons, and Ne also has 10 electrons. (d) **True** ✅: Li (~152 pm) is much larger than F (~64 pm). (e) **True** ✅: both ions are isoelectronic (10 electrons), and Na⁺ has 11 protons compared to 12 in Mg²⁺, so Na⁺ pulls its electrons less tightly and is larger (~102 pm vs. ~72 pm).
13. The magnet represents the **nucleus** (providing the inward pull), and the sheets of paper represent the **filled inner electron shells** (adding distance and shielding). Just as adding sheets weakens the magnet's grip, inner shells reduce the pull felt by outer electrons. Two things the analogy does not show: (1) inner electrons actively **repel** outer electrons because both carry negative charges, and (2) electrons in an atom are constantly moving in 3D space rather than sitting still like paper sheets.
