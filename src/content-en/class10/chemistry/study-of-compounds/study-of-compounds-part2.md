# Topic 2: Ammonia: From the Lab to the Farm

In Topic 1, we watched an **acidic gas**, HCl, being prepared in the laboratory: warm the reactants, dry the gas, and collect it in an upright jar by upward displacement of air because it is denser than air. Today's gas is the exact opposite of HCl: a **basic gas**, ammonia (NH₃). Whenever the two gases meet in air, they react to form dense white fumes (NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl from Topic 1). Today we will see how ammonia is generated in the lab, how it is dried and collected (and why the methods used for HCl fail completely here), how industry captures nitrogen from the air to make it, and how it reaches farm fields as fertiliser.

---

## The Garden Supply Store and the Nitrogen Hiding in Plain Sight

It was a busy Saturday morning at the local agricultural and garden supply centre. Ms. Elena had brought her nephew Leo along while picking up supplies. Outside, a farmer was loading sacks onto his pickup truck; each sack had a bold "N" printed on the front along with a percentage number. Once the bags were loaded, the farmer paid the cashier and drove off.

Leo stared after the truck, puzzled. "Aunt Elena, why is that farmer paying money to buy nitrogen? Our science textbook says about 78 percent of the air around us is already nitrogen! There's an ocean of air right above his farm fields. Why pay for something that's floating everywhere for free?"

Ms. Elena smiled. "That is a great question, Leo. Air is indeed packed with nitrogen, but it exists as **N₂ molecules**, where two nitrogen atoms are locked together by an extraordinarily strong triple bond. Crops cannot absorb N₂ gas straight from the air. Plants need 'fixed' nitrogen—nitrogen combined into a **compound** that dissolves in soil water, such as an ammonium salt or a nitrate. Someone has to break apart atmospheric N₂ and turn it into a compound first. And the key chemical that makes almost all of those fertiliser sacks possible is a famous gas."

"Which gas, Aunt Elena?" "One whose sharp smell you may have noticed from a bottle of household glass cleaner: **ammonia**. In Class 9, you met the equation N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃—today we'll see the full story behind it." Leo's eyes lit up. "So nitrogen from the air is turned into ammonia, and ammonia is turned into those bags of fertiliser?" "Spot on. Let's start with how ammonia is made on a small scale in the lab, and then visit the industrial plant."

```
   Atmospheric N₂ (plants cannot absorb it directly)
        :
        :  reacted with hydrogen via the Haber process
        v
   Ammonia (NH₃)
        :
        :  neutralised with acids (or reacted further)
        v
   Fertilisers: ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate, urea
        :
        v
   Farm soil  -->  crops  -->  food on our plates
```

> **Figure 2.1** – The journey of nitrogen: from the air into ammonia, from ammonia into fertilisers, and from farm soil into our food. The rest of this topic unpacks each stage of this journey.

## Part A: How Ammonia Is Prepared in the Lab

> **Safety First (read before going further):**
> - Ammonia gas is **toxic** and severely irritates the eyes, nose, and throat. Students never prepare, sniff, or handle it.
> - Everything in this topic is **for reading only** or **for observation as a teacher demo**, conducted by a teacher in a school fume cupboard wearing safety goggles and gloves.
> - Even when checking for odour, a teacher only gently wafts a trace of air toward the nose—never inhaling directly.
> - Children must never touch household ammonia cleaners. **Never mix ammonia cleaners with bleach or acidic toilet cleaners**, as doing so releases deadly gases.

The standard laboratory method is simple: gently heat an **ammonium salt** with a strong **alkali (base)**. We use ammonium chloride (**NH₄Cl**) and dry **slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂**. In Analytical Chemistry (Chapter 8), we saw that warming any NH₄⁺ salt with OH⁻ releases NH₃ gas. Here we use that exact same reaction to **prepare** the gas:

2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃

Both reactants are **dry solids**, finely ground and mixed together before heating.

### How the Apparatus Is Set Up

```
   Test tube: dry mixture of NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂
   (closed end slightly HIGHER, mouth tilted slightly DOWNWARD)     inverted gas jar
                                                               .-------------.
    .-----------.                                              :      .      :  <-- tube reaches TOP of jar:
   (   mixture   [cork]==.                                     :      :      :      NH₃ collects at the top
    '-----------'         :     .-----------.                  :      :      :
          ^               '====>: lumps of  :===.              :      :      :
       burner                   : CaO       :   :              :      :      :
    (gentle heat)               '-----------'   :              :      :      :
                                drying tower    :              '      :      '
                                                :                     :      <-- open mouth pointing down:
                                                '---------------------'          heavier air escapes here
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Lab preparation of ammonia (teacher demo): on the left, the test tube holds the dry solid mixture and is fitted with a cork and delivery tube. The **mouth of the test tube tilts slightly downward** so that water droplets condensing near the cooler neck cannot trickle back onto the hot glass bottom and crack it. The gas passes up through a drying tower packed with lumps of quicklime (CaO) and then to the top of an inverted gas jar. Because the mouth of the jar is open at the bottom, the apparatus is never sealed.

Notice two crucial details in the setup:
1. **Why is the test tube tilted slightly downward?** The reaction produces water vapour (2H₂O) alongside ammonia. When that steam hits the cooler mouth of the test tube, it condenses into liquid water drops. If the tube tilted upward, those cold drops would run back down onto the hot glass base and crack the tube. Tilting the mouth slightly **downward** prevents this.
2. **Where does the delivery tube end?** Right at the **top** of the inverted gas jar—we will see why in Part B.

### Example 1. Equation and stoichiometry

**Problem:** 107 g of NH₄Cl is heated completely with slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂. Find the amount of NH₃ formed (in moles and in litres at STP) and the mass of Ca(OH)₂ required. (Atomic masses: N = 14, H = 1, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, O = 16.)

**Step 1 (molar masses):** NH₄Cl = 14 + 4 + 35.5 = **53.5** g/mol. Ca(OH)₂ = 40 + 2 × 17 = **74** g/mol.
**Step 2 (moles):** 107 ÷ 53.5 = **2 mol** of NH₄Cl.
**Step 3 (mole ratio 2 : 1 : 2):** 2 mol of NH₄Cl yields **2 mol of NH₃** and requires 1 mol of Ca(OH)₂ = **74 g**.
**Step 4 (volume):** 2 × 22.4 = **44.8 L** of NH₃ at STP. Mass of NH₃ = 2 × 17 = 34 g.

**Check:** Products: CaCl₂ = 1 mol = 111 g, H₂O = 2 mol = 36 g, NH₃ = 34 g. Reactants = 107 + 74 = 181 g; products = 111 + 36 + 34 = 181 g ✅. Atom count: N 2 = 2, H 8 + 2 = 10 = 4 + 6, Cl 2 = 2, Ca 1 = 1, O 2 = 2 ✅.

## Part B: Drying and Collecting Ammonia: What Works and What Fails

Because water vapour is produced alongside ammonia, the gas must be **dried**. In Topic 1, we dried HCl gas by bubbling it through conc. H₂SO₄. Can we dry ammonia the same way? **No!** Recall our core acid-base principle from Chapter 1: acids and bases react with each other. That gives us a golden rule for drying gases: **use an acidic drying agent for an acidic gas, and a basic drying agent for a basic gas.**

Ammonia is a base. Concentrated H₂SO₄ is an acid, so it immediately reacts with ammonia and swallows it up:

2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄

Instead of getting dry ammonia gas, the gas is **converted into a solid salt**! Anhydrous CaCl₂ cannot be used either, because it reacts with ammonia to form an addition compound. The right drying agent is **quicklime (CaO)**: as a basic oxide, it eagerly absorbs moisture (CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂) while leaving basic ammonia gas completely untouched.

### Lighter Than Air, So Invert the Jar

From Chapter 5, vapour density = molar mass ÷ 2. For NH₃, VD = 17 ÷ 2 = **8.5**, whereas for air, VD = 29 ÷ 2 = **14.5**. Because ammonia is **lighter (less dense) than air**, it rises. Therefore, we hold the gas jar **upside down** (mouth pointing down) and run the delivery tube all the way to the **top of the inverted jar**. The light ammonia gas collects at the top and pushes the heavier air downward and out through the open mouth. This method is called **downward displacement of air**.

In Topic 1, HCl was denser than air, so we used an upright jar with the tube at the bottom (upward displacement of air). Ammonia is lighter than air, so we use an inverted jar with the tube at the top. Their opposite setups follow directly from their opposite densities relative to air.

```
   HCl (denser: V.D. 18.25)                NH₃ (lighter: V.D. 8.5)
   upright jar, tube to the BOTTOM         inverted jar, tube to the TOP

       air pushed out the top                  .-------------.
          ^    :                               :  NH₃    .   :  <-- NH₃ collects at the top
      :   ^    :      :                        :         :   :
      :        :      :                        :   air   :   :
      :        :      :                        :    v    :   :
      :  HCl   :      :                        '         :   '
      :  below .      :                            v     :      <-- open mouth at bottom:
      :______________:                            air    :          air pushed downward
         tube reaches bottom of jar                  tube enters from below
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Two gases, two opposite collection methods: dense HCl uses an upright jar with a bottom tube (air escapes upward); light NH₃ uses an inverted jar with a top tube (air is pushed downward).

**Why not collect ammonia over water?** Ammonia is **extraordinarily soluble** in water: 1 volume of water can dissolve about 700 volumes of NH₃ gas! If you tried to collect it over water, the gas would dissolve the instant it touched the water, and water would suck straight back up the delivery tube. So ammonia is never collected over water.

**How do you know the jar is full?** The teacher holds a piece of moist red litmus paper near the open bottom mouth of the inverted jar; as soon as it turns blue, the jar is full.

### Example 2. Choosing the right drying agent

**Problem:** Out of conc. H₂SO₄, quicklime (CaO), and anhydrous CaCl₂, which one is used to dry ammonia gas? Give the reason for each choice.

- **Conc. H₂SO₄:** No. 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄—the acid reacts with basic ammonia and turns it into a salt.
- **Anhydrous CaCl₂:** No. It combines with NH₃ to form an addition product.
- **Quicklime (CaO):** Yes. It is a basic oxide that absorbs moisture without reacting with NH₃.

**Check:** Acidic or reactive driers trap a basic gas; a basic drier leaves a basic gas untouched ✅. Atom count in 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄: N 2 = 2, H 6 + 2 = 8 = 8, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4 ✅.

## Part C: Properties and Reactions of Ammonia

**Physical properties:** Colourless gas with a sharp, pungent odour; lighter than air; extremely soluble in water. When cooled and compressed, it condenses easily into a **liquid** (boiling point about −33 °C), a property widely used in industrial refrigeration systems.

**Ammonia and water:** NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH, which partially ionises in water into NH₄⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Because only a small fraction ionises, aqueous ammonia (NH₄OH) is a **weak base** (Chapter 1: pH around 11). Consequently, moist red litmus turns blue, whereas **dry** NH₃ gas has no effect on dry red litmus paper (without water, no OH⁻ ions are formed—the exact same dry-vs-wet rule we saw in Chapter 1 and Topic 1).

### The Fountain Experiment (Teacher Demo Only)

The teacher fills a dry round-bottom flask with dry ammonia gas (by downward displacement of air) and clamps it upside down. Through one hole of the stopper, a long glass tube ending in a jet reaches near the top of the flask and dips into a beaker of water containing a few drops of colourless phenolphthalein indicator below. Through the second hole sits a dropper holding a little water.

```
       inverted round-bottom flask clamped in place: filled with dry NH₃
           ___________
          /   . . .   \
         /  .  jet  .  \       <-- water sprays upward as a pink fountain
        :     ( ^ )     :
         \    nozzle   /
          \___________/
              : :  :
           [cork]   dropper (a little water)
              :
              :  long glass tube
              :
      :       :       :
      :~~~~~~~:~~~~~~~:   <-- water surface
      :       :       :   <-- lower tip of tube submerged in water
      :     water     :   (contains colourless phenolphthalein)
      :_______________:   beaker
```

> **Figure 2.4** – Setup for the ammonia fountain experiment: an inverted flask of dry NH₃ connected by a long jet tube to a beaker of water + phenolphthalein below, with a water dropper in the stopper. Squeezing the dropper sends a few drops of water into the flask.

**What happens:** the few drops of water from the dropper dissolve a large volume of ammonia gas almost instantly. The **pressure inside the flask drops sharply**, and outside atmospheric pressure forces water from the beaker up the long tube, bursting out of the nozzle as a fountain. As soon as the water enters the flask and dissolves ammonia, the phenolphthalein turns **bright pink** (proving the solution is alkaline; Chapter 1). This single demonstration proves two things at once: ammonia is **extremely soluble in water**, and its aqueous solution is **alkaline**. (We saw the exact same principle in the HCl fountain in Topic 1, except there the solution was acidic.)

### Chemical Reactions of Ammonia

| Reaction | Balanced Equation | Observation / Key Note |
|---|---|---|
| With HCl gas | NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl | Dense white fumes (tiny solid particles of NH₄Cl) |
| Burning in pure oxygen | 4NH₃ + 3O₂ → 2N₂ + 6H₂O | Burns with a yellowish-green flame (does not burn in ordinary air) |
| Catalytic oxidation (hot Pt) | 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O | Forms NO gas—the gateway to nitric acid in Topic 3 |
| With chlorine (excess NH₃) | 8NH₃ + 3Cl₂ → 6NH₄Cl + N₂ | White fumes of NH₄Cl alongside N₂ gas |
| With acids | 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ | Forms ammonium salts used as agricultural fertilisers |

Chlorine is toxic, so its reaction is strictly for reading. Note that if chlorine is in excess instead of ammonia, a dangerously explosive liquid (**NCl₃**, nitrogen trichloride) can form—you will study that reaction in higher classes.

The reaction NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl has a neat practical use: trained technicians can locate a tiny leak in an industrial ammonia pipe by holding a swab moistened with HCl near the joint—a cloud of white fumes pinpoints the leak immediately.

### Example 3. Burning 34 g of ammonia in oxygen

**Problem:** Given 4NH₃ + 3O₂ → 2N₂ + 6H₂O, find the masses of N₂ and water formed and the mass of O₂ consumed when 34 g of NH₃ burns completely.

**Step 1:** 34 ÷ 17 = **2 mol** of NH₃. **Step 2 (ratio 4 : 3 : 2 : 6):** O₂ = 2 × 3 ÷ 4 = **1.5 mol** = 48 g; N₂ = 2 × 2 ÷ 4 = **1 mol** = 28 g; H₂O = 2 × 6 ÷ 4 = **3 mol** = 54 g.

**Check:** Reactants = 34 + 48 = 82 g; products = 28 + 54 = 82 g ✅. Atom count: N 4 = 4, H 12 = 12, O 6 = 6 ✅.

## Part D: The Haber Process: Making Ammonia from Air

The laboratory method is only suitable for making small quantities. To feed the world, industry manufactures millions of tonnes of ammonia directly from nitrogen (N₂ from air) and hydrogen (H₂). From Class 9, recall the equation: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ over an iron catalyst under high pressure—a **reversible** reaction.

**Operating conditions (approximate):** pressure of about **200 atmospheres**, temperature of about **450 °C**, and a finely divided **iron catalyst** (with a small amount of promoter).

### Why Are These Conditions a Compromise?

- The reaction is **reversible**: as NH₃ forms, some of it breaks back down into N₂ and H₂. Thus, the reactor output is an equilibrium mixture containing only a **partial yield** of ammonia on each pass.
- **Lower temperatures** favour a higher percentage of ammonia in the mixture, but make the reaction **far too slow** to be practical. **Higher temperatures** speed up the reaction, but reduce the percentage yield of ammonia. Therefore, engineers choose an intermediate compromise temperature of **about 450 °C**, and use an iron catalyst to keep the rate fast (remember: a catalyst only increases the **speed** of the reaction, not the equilibrium fraction of ammonia).
- **Higher pressures** favour more ammonia because 1 volume of N₂ + 3 volumes of H₂ = **4 gas molecules** combine to form just **2 gas molecules** of NH₃—fewer molecules occupy less space under high pressure. However, extremely high pressures require very thick, expensive steel pipes and vessels, so plants settle on a practical compromise of about **200 atm**.

(You will study the full theory of chemical equilibrium in higher classes; for now, simply remember that the temperature and pressure are practical engineering compromises.)

```
   N₂ (from air) + H₂
         :
         v
   Compressor : ~200 atm
         :
         v
   Converter (iron catalyst, ~450 °C)
         :
         v
   Cooling condenser
         :
         +--> Liquid NH₃ drains off --> storage tanks
         :
         +--> Unreacted N₂ and H₂ gases
                    :
                    +--> pumped back to the compressor (recycled)
```

> **Figure 2.5** – Flowchart of the Haber process: the gas mixture is compressed to ~200 atm and passed over an iron catalyst at ~450 °C. Only part of the N₂ and H₂ converts to NH₃ on each pass; cooling liquefies the ammonia so it can be drained off, while unreacted N₂ and H₂ remain gases and are recycled back into the reactor so nothing goes to waste.

### Example 4. Mass calculation in the Haber process

**Problem:** Assuming complete conversion with no losses, what mass of NH₃ is produced from 140 kg of N₂, and what mass of H₂ is required?

**Step 1:** Molar mass of N₂ = 28 g/mol, so 140 kg = 5 kmol. **Step 2 (ratio 1 : 3 : 2):** NH₃ = 10 kmol = 10 × 17 = **170 kg**; H₂ = 15 kmol = 15 × 2 = **30 kg**.

**Check:** 140 + 30 = 170 kg ✅. Atom count: N 10 = 10, H 30 = 30 ✅.

## Part E: Ammonia to the Farm: Fertilisers

Because ammonia is a gas at room temperature, applying it directly to soil is difficult. Instead, chemical plants react ammonia with acids to produce solid, water-soluble **ammonium salts** that supply nitrogen to crops as NH₄⁺ or NO₃⁻ ions:

2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄ (ammonium sulphate)
NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (ammonium nitrate—we will meet HNO₃ in Topic 3)

A third major nitrogen fertiliser, **urea**, CO(NH₂)₂, is also manufactured from ammonia (learn its formula; its synthesis equation is covered in higher classes). In Class 9, we calculated the percentage of nitrogen in these three fertilisers; here we calculate how much ammonia is needed to make them.

```
   N₂ (air) + H₂
        :
        v
   NH₃ --+--> + H₂SO₄ --> (NH₄)₂SO₄
         :
         +--> + HNO₃  --> NH₄NO₃
         :
         +--> urea, CO(NH₂)₂
         :
         +--> feedstock for making HNO₃ (Topic 3)
```

> **Figure 2.6** – How ammonia branches out into major agricultural fertilisers, with the bottom branch leading directly into Topic 3.

### Example 5. Ammonia needed for ammonium sulphate

**Problem:** How many kilograms of NH₃ are needed to manufacture 264 kg of ammonium sulphate, (NH₄)₂SO₄? (Molar mass of (NH₄)₂SO₄ = 2 × 18 + 96 = 132.)

**Step 1:** 264 ÷ 132 = **2 kmol**. **Step 2 (ratio 2NH₃ : 1 salt):** NH₃ = 4 kmol = 4 × 17 = **68 kg**.

**Check:** H₂SO₄ needed = 2 kmol × 98 = 196 kg; 68 + 196 = 264 kg ✅.

### Example 6. Ammonia needed for ammonium nitrate

**Problem:** How many kilograms of NH₃ are required to make 400 kg of NH₄NO₃? (Molar mass of NH₄NO₃ = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80.)

400 ÷ 80 = **5 kmol**; with a 1 : 1 mole ratio, NH₃ = 5 × 17 = **85 kg**.

**Check:** 85 kg NH₃ + HNO₃ (5 × 63 = 315 kg) = 400 kg ✅.

**Other uses of ammonia:** manufacturing nitric acid (Topic 3), industrial refrigeration in cold-storage warehouses (liquid ammonia absorbs a large amount of heat when it evaporates), and dilute household glass and surface cleaners (dilute NH₄OH—**keep out of reach of children**, and never mix with bleach or acidic cleaners).

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "We can dry ammonia gas with concentrated H₂SO₄ and collect it over water, just like other gases."**
Because conc. H₂SO₄ works so well for drying HCl, students often assume it works for every gas. In fact, both ideas fail completely for ammonia. Let's trace two thought experiments (for reading only):

**Thought Experiment 1: Passing ammonia through conc. H₂SO₄.**

```
   Ammonia enters --> .-------------.   --> nothing comes out into the gas jar!
                      : conc. H₂SO₄ :
                      : (wash bottle)
                      :_____________:
     (NH₄)₂SO₄ forms inside the bottle; the gas is gone
```

> **Figure 2.7** – What goes wrong: basic ammonia reacts with acidic conc. H₂SO₄ in the wash bottle to form ammonium sulphate salt, so no ammonia gas exits the bottle.

Equation: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄. The gas isn't **dried**—it is **consumed**! Anhydrous CaCl₂ causes a similar problem by binding ammonia into a solid addition compound.

**Thought Experiment 2: Trying to collect ammonia over water.** Suppose someone inverts a 1 L gas jar full of water in a trough and bubbles ammonia into it. Because 1 volume of water dissolves roughly 700 volumes of NH₃, even a small amount of water in the trough instantly absorbs all the incoming ammonia gas. The pressure inside the delivery tube drops and **water rushes backward up the tube** (just like the fountain experiment). No gas ever collects in the jar.

| Gas | Correct Drying Agent | Correct Collection Method |
|---|---|---|
| HCl (Topic 1) | conc. H₂SO₄ | upward displacement of air |
| NH₃ (Topic 2) | quicklime (CaO) | downward displacement of air |

**Rule to remember:** always match the **chemical nature** (acidic vs basic) of the drying agent to the gas, and check the gas's **solubility** and **density** before choosing how to collect it.

## Quick Recap

- Lab preparation: 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃ using a dry solid mixture in a test tube tilted slightly downward at the mouth.
- Dried over **quicklime (CaO)**—never conc. H₂SO₄ or anhydrous CaCl₂, both of which react with ammonia.
- Lighter than air (VD 8.5 vs ~14.5 for air): collected by **downward displacement of air** in an inverted jar with the delivery tube reaching the top; never over water because it is extremely soluble.
- NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄OH, a weak base that turns moist red litmus blue.
- The fountain experiment demonstrates both its extreme solubility in water and the alkaline nature of its solution.
- Key reactions: dense white fumes with HCl (NH₄Cl), burning in pure O₂, catalytic oxidation over Pt (4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O), and reaction with Cl₂ to form NH₄Cl and N₂.
- Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ over an iron catalyst at ~200 atm and ~450 °C (compromise conditions), with unreacted gases recycled.
- Uses: fertilisers (ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate, urea), nitric acid manufacture, refrigeration, and household cleaners.
- Ammonia is toxic and irritating: all experiments are strictly for reading or teacher demonstrations.

11. In a Haber plant, 51 kg of NH₃ is made by N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ (ideal case). Find the masses of N₂ and H₂ used. Then explain why plants run at about 450 °C and not at 100 °C, given that "a lower temperature gives a larger share of ammonia in the mixture but the reaction becomes very slow".
12. Choose and justify: which gas jar position (upright or inverted) and which method collects ammonia, and why can it not be collected over water? What would be seen if a student tried?
13. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Ammonia is dried with conc. H₂SO₄. (b) NH₃ is lighter than air. (c) A catalyst increases the amount of ammonia in the Haber mixture. (d) Dry NH₃ turns dry red litmus blue. (e) NH₄Cl, (NH₄)₂SO₄, and NH₄NO₃ are all made from ammonia.

### Answers

1. NH₄Cl = 53.5 g/mol, so 42.8 ÷ 53.5 = **0.8 mol**. Ratio 2 : 1 : 1 : 2 gives Ca(OH)₂ = 0.4 mol × 74 = **29.6 g**; CaCl₂ = 0.4 × 111 = **44.4 g**; NH₃ = 0.8 mol, volume = 0.8 × 22.4 = **17.92 L**. Check: H₂O = 0.8 mol = 14.4 g, NH₃ = 0.8 × 17 = 13.6 g; reactants = 42.8 + 29.6 = 72.4 g; products = 44.4 + 14.4 + 13.6 = 72.4 g ✅.
2. 6.72 ÷ 22.4 = **0.3 mol** of NH₃. Since the mole ratio to NH₄Cl is 1 : 1, NH₄Cl = 0.3 mol = 0.3 × 53.5 = **16.05 g**. Check: 16.05 ÷ 53.5 = 0.3 ✅.
3. Water vapour formed in the reaction condenses into liquid drops near the cooler mouth of the test tube. Tilting the mouth slightly downward prevents those cold drops from running back onto the hot glass bottom and cracking the tube.
4. (a) **No**: 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄—the gas reacts to form a salt. (b) **Yes**: CaO absorbs moisture (CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂) without reacting with ammonia. Check: N 2 = 2, H 8 = 8, S 1, O 4 ✅.
5. Molar mass of urea = 12 + 16 + 2 × (14 + 2) = 12 + 16 + 32 = **60**, containing 28 parts nitrogen. Mass of urea = 70 ÷ 28 × 60 = **150 kg**. Check: N in 150 kg urea = 150 × 28 ÷ 60 = 70 kg ✅.
6. N in 120 kg of urea = 120 × 28 ÷ 60 = **56 kg**. For NH₄NO₃ (M = 80, N = 28): mass needed = 56 ÷ 28 × 80 = **160 kg**. Check: 160 × 28 ÷ 80 = 56 kg ✅.
7. Molar mass of (NH₄)₂SO₄ = 132; 33 ÷ 132 = **0.25 kmol**. NH₃ = 0.5 kmol × 17 = **8.5 kg**; H₂SO₄ = 0.25 × 98 = **24.5 kg**. Check: 8.5 + 24.5 = 33 kg ✅.
8. 23 ÷ 0.46 = **50 kg**. Check: 50 × 0.46 = 23 kg ✅.
9. 3.4 ÷ 17 = **0.2 mol**. From NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl (1 : 1 : 1), HCl = 0.2 × 36.5 = **7.3 g**; NH₄Cl = 0.2 × 53.5 = **10.7 g**. Check: 3.4 + 7.3 = 10.7 g ✅.
10. 20.4 ÷ 17 = 1.2 mol of NH₃. O₂ = 1.2 × 3 ÷ 4 = 0.9 mol, volume = 0.9 × 22.4 = **20.16 L**. Check: O₂ = 0.9 × 32 = 28.8 g; N₂ = 0.6 mol = 16.8 g; H₂O = 1.8 mol = 32.4 g; 20.4 + 28.8 = 49.2 = 16.8 + 32.4 ✅.
11. 51 ÷ 17 = **3 kmol** of NH₃. N₂ = 1.5 kmol × 28 = **42 kg**; H₂ = 4.5 kmol × 2 = **9 kg**. Check: 42 + 9 = 51 kg ✅. At 100 °C the reaction is far too slow for industrial production; heating speeds it up but lowers the equilibrium fraction of ammonia, so ~450 °C with an iron catalyst is chosen as a practical compromise.
12. **Inverted jar** (mouth pointing down) with the delivery tube reaching the **top**, by **downward displacement of air**, because ammonia is lighter than air. It cannot be collected over water because 1 volume of water dissolves about 700 volumes of NH₃—no gas would collect, and water would rush rapidly up into the jar and delivery tube.
13. (a) **False** ❌: conc. H₂SO₄ reacts with ammonia to form a salt; CaO is used instead. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌: a catalyst only increases the speed of the reaction, not the equilibrium yield. (d) **False** ❌: dry NH₃ has no effect on dry litmus; moist red litmus turns blue. (e) **True** ✅: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl, 2NH₃ + H₂SO₄ → (NH₄)₂SO₄, and NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃.
