# Topic 3: Nitric Acid: From the Ostwald Process to Aqua Regia

In Topic 1, we saw HCl gas prepared from NaCl and concentrated H₂SO₄, and in Topic 2 we met ammonia, manufactured on a massive scale by the Haber process. One line in Topic 2's list of uses stood out: ammonia is also used to make **nitric acid**. Today we explore the full story of nitric acid—how it is prepared in the laboratory, how industry converts ammonia into nitric acid via the Ostwald process, why it behaves so differently from other acids when it meets metals, and how a famous two-acid partnership can dissolve even solid gold.

## The Jeweller's Workshop and "Royal Water"

Mr. Morales took his nephew Ryan to a family jeweller's shop to have Ryan's grandmother's old ring inspected. Over the years, the ring had grown dull and tarnished. The jeweller turned it under a bright lamp, rubbed it lightly against a touchstone, and smiled. "It's genuine gold, alloyed with a little copper. We'll get it cleaned and polished."

At the back of the workshop was a small, sealed room with a heavy exhaust fan humming loudly. Peering through a thick glass window in the door, Ryan spotted a glass fume cabinet inside where wisps of **reddish-brown vapour** were swirling above a beaker. The jeweller double-checked that the door was latched shut. "Stay back from there, son. That's where our strong acids are kept. Pure gold resists almost everything—**only one special acid mixture can dissolve it, called 'royal water' (aqua regia)**. Children are never allowed near that room, and even I never touch a bottle without the exhaust hood running and heavy gloves on."

Ryan's eyes widened. "Uncle, gold is supposed to be the most unreactive metal of all—it doesn't rust or react with water! What is this 'royal water'? What is that reddish-brown fog inside the cabinet? And where do jewellers and factories get these acids in the first place?"

On the walk home, Mr. Morales said, "You just asked three great questions, and all three revolve around a single chemical: **nitric acid**. Let's tackle them one by one."

   2. What is that reddish-brown fog?   -->   Part C (NO₂, oxidising acid)

      solid gold?

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Retort** | An all-glass vessel with a spherical bulb and a long downward-sloping neck, used to heat reactants and condense the vapour directly in a receiver | lab preparation of HNO₃ |
| **Distil** | To vaporise a liquid by heating and condense the vapour back into liquid droplets in a cooled container | HNO₃ distilling from retort to receiver |
| **Fuming acid** | A concentrated acid that releases visible vapour or mist when exposed to air | concentrated HNO₃ |
| **Oxidising agent** | A substance that oxidises another substance (for instance, turning Cu into Cu²⁺ or C into CO₂) while being reduced itself | concentrated HNO₃ |
| **Ostwald process** | The industrial process that converts ammonia and atmospheric oxygen into nitric acid | NH₃ → NO → NO₂ → HNO₃ |
| **Gauze** | A fine mesh woven from thin metal wires through which reacting gases pass | Pt-Rh catalytic gauze |
| **Passivation (passive metal)** | When a thin, tight oxide coat forms on a metal's surface and blocks any further reaction with the acid | Fe or Al in concentrated HNO₃ |
| **Aqua regia** | A freshly prepared mixture of 3 volumes of concentrated HCl and 1 volume of concentrated HNO₃ ("royal water") | dissolves gold and platinum |

## Safety First (Read Before Every Section)

- **Concentrated nitric acid** is fiercely corrosive—it burns skin on contact and leaves a **yellow stain**. Its vapours severely irritate the eyes, nose, and throat.
- **NO₂ (reddish-brown gas) and NO** are highly toxic gases that can cause serious, sometimes delayed, lung damage.
- Every preparation and reaction in this topic is **strictly for reading** or **for observation as a teacher demo** inside a working fume cupboard with safety goggles and gloves. No student ever prepares, touches, sniffs, or tests these chemicals.
- Concentrated H₂SO₄ also appears here and is equally corrosive (we cover it in detail in Topic 5).
- If you ever see a bottle at home bearing a hazard symbol, **never touch or open it**: ask an adult, and only read labels with adult supervision.

## Part A: Preparing Nitric Acid in the Lab

Recall the strategy from Topic 1: when we warmed NaCl with concentrated H₂SO₄, HCl escaped because H₂SO₄ is a **non-volatile acid** that displaces a more volatile acid from its salt. We use the exact same strategy here with a **nitrate salt** (KNO₃ or NaNO₃) and concentrated H₂SO₄:

KNO₃ + H₂SO₄ (conc.) → KHSO₄ + HNO₃

With sodium nitrate, the reaction is identical: NaNO₃ + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HNO₃. The mixture is heated gently, keeping the temperature **below about 200 °C**.

**Why keep the heating gentle?** Nitric acid decomposes when exposed to strong heat or sunlight:

4HNO₃ → 4NO₂ + 2H₂O + O₂

Some of the reddish-brown NO₂ gas dissolves back into the liquid acid, giving concentrated nitric acid a **yellowish-brown** tint. To minimise decomposition by light, nitric acid is stored in **dark brown (amber) glass bottles**. That decomposition is also our first clue to the reddish-brown fog Ryan spotted!

**Why must the entire apparatus be made of glass?** Nitric acid vapours **attack and destroy cork and rubber**. Therefore, both the retort and the receiver are made entirely of glass. Moreover, the neck of the retort rests **loosely** inside the mouth of the cooled receiver flask—it is never plugged or sealed—so pressure cannot build up inside the heated apparatus.

```
   Teacher demo (inside a fume cupboard, all-glass apparatus)

                  retort
          ___________
        /             \
       :   KNO₃ +       \_____
       :   conc. H₂SO₄         \______
        \             /               \______  <-- neck slopes downward
         \___________/                       \_____
               ^                                    \
               :                                     v   (loose fit, never sealed)
          gentle heat                          :              :
          (kept below ~200 °C)                 :   HNO₃       :   <-- receiver flask
                                               :   (droplets) :       (cooled in cold water)
                                               :______________:
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Lab preparation of nitric acid (teacher demo). An all-glass retort holding KNO₃ and concentrated H₂SO₄ is heated gently. Nitric acid vapour travels down the sloping neck and condenses into liquid droplets inside the water-cooled receiver flask. The retort neck fits **loosely** into the receiver (no cork, no rubber, and never a closed system), and the entire setup runs inside a fume cupboard.

### Example 1. How much HNO₃ from KNO₃?

**Problem:** 20.2 g of KNO₃ is gently warmed with the exact stoichiometric amount of concentrated H₂SO₄. Calculate the mass of HNO₃ and the mass of KHSO₄ formed. (K = 39, N = 14, O = 16, H = 1, S = 32.)

**Step 1:** Molar masses: KNO₃ = 39 + 14 + 48 = **101** g/mol; HNO₃ = 1 + 14 + 48 = **63** g/mol; KHSO₄ = 39 + 1 + 32 + 64 = **136** g/mol; H₂SO₄ = **98** g/mol.
**Step 2:** Moles of KNO₃ = 20.2 ÷ 101 = **0.2 mol**. The mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
**Step 3:** Mass of HNO₃ = 0.2 × 63 = **12.6 g**. Mass of KHSO₄ = 0.2 × 136 = **27.2 g**.

**Check:** H₂SO₄ required = 0.2 × 98 = 19.6 g. Reactants = 20.2 + 19.6 = 39.8 g; products = 12.6 + 27.2 = 39.8 g ✅. Atom count: K 1, H 2, N 1, S 1, O 3 + 4 = 7 = 4 + 3 ✅.

## Part B: The Industrial Route: The Ostwald Process

In industry, nitric acid is manufactured **from ammonia**—the very same ammonia produced by the Haber process in Topic 2—together with **air** (which supplies oxygen). The process runs in three distinct steps:

**Step 1: Catalytic oxidation of ammonia.** A mixture of ammonia and air is passed over a glowing **Pt-Rh gauze** (platinum-rhodium wire mesh catalyst) at about **800 °C**:

4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O

**Step 2: Oxidation of NO to NO₂.** The colourless nitric oxide (NO) gas is cooled and mixed with more air, reacting with oxygen to form reddish-brown nitrogen dioxide (NO₂):

2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂

**Step 3: Absorption of NO₂ in water.** In the presence of excess oxygen, NO₂ is absorbed in water to form nitric acid:

4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃

Notice that this third step is **the exact same reaction that happens in rainclouds during acid rain** (which we studied in Class 9). The difference is that an industrial plant carries it out inside sealed absorption towers under strict control to manufacture a valuable chemical. As always, the Pt-Rh catalyst gauze only **speeds up** Step 1 without being used up itself.

```
   NH₃ (from Haber process, Topic 2)  +  air (O₂)
                    :
                    v
        hot Pt-Rh gauze, ~800 °C
        4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O
                    :
                    v
        cool and mix with more air (O₂)
        2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂   (reddish-brown gas)
                    :
                    v
        absorb in water in the presence of O₂
        4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃
                    :
                    v
             nitric acid (subsequently concentrated)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Flowchart of the Ostwald process: ammonia and air react over hot Pt-Rh gauze to form NO, which oxidises to reddish-brown NO₂ and is then absorbed in water and oxygen to yield HNO₃.

**Combining all three steps:** NH₃ + 2O₂ → HNO₃ + H₂O (each mole of NH₃ yields one mole of HNO₃—every nitrogen atom is conserved along the chain).

### Example 2. How much nitric acid from 1.7 kg of ammonia?

**Problem:** 1.7 kg of NH₃ is completely converted into nitric acid by the Ostwald process. Find the mass of HNO₃ produced, the mass of O₂ consumed, and the net mass of water formed.

**Step 1:** Molar mass of NH₃ = 17 g/mol. Moles = 1700 g ÷ 17 = **100 mol**.
**Step 2:** From the overall equation NH₃ + 2O₂ → HNO₃ + H₂O, the mole ratio is 1 : 2 : 1 : 1.
**Step 3:** Mass of HNO₃ = 100 × 63 = **6300 g = 6.3 kg**. Mass of O₂ = 200 × 32 = **6400 g = 6.4 kg**. Mass of H₂O = 100 × 18 = **1800 g = 1.8 kg**.

**Check:** Reactants = 1.7 + 6.4 = 8.1 kg; products = 6.3 + 1.8 = 8.1 kg ✅. Atom count: N 1, H 3 = 1 + 2, O 4 = 3 + 1 ✅.

## Part C: Nitric Acid's Distinctive Personality: A Powerful Oxidising Agent

Nitric acid is a **strong acid** (Chapter 1: it ionises completely in water into H₃O⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions). Yet unlike HCl or dilute H₂SO₄, nitric acid is also a **powerful oxidising agent**. That is why we never used nitric acid to prepare hydrogen gas in Class 9, and why in Chapter 2 (Topic 3) we saw Cu + conc. HNO₃ release brown NO₂ fumes instead of H₂. Whenever nitric acid meets a metal, it oxidises the metal into positive ions (such as **Cu²⁺**) while the nitrogen in the acid is reduced to **oxides of nitrogen** and the hydrogen ends up as **water**—so H₂ gas is not released.

### Reaction with Copper

**Concentrated HNO₃ + Cu (teacher demo, for observation only):**

Cu + 4HNO₃ (conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O

Dense **reddish-brown fumes of NO₂** pour out and the solution turns bluish-green due to Cu(NO₃)₂. That reddish-brown NO₂ is the exact fog Ryan saw inside the jeweller's exhaust hood!

**Dilute HNO₃ + Cu (teacher demo, for observation only):**

3Cu + 8HNO₃ (dil.) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O

Here the gas formed inside the tube is **NO** (nitric oxide), which is **colourless**. However, as soon as the colourless NO rises to the mouth of the test tube and meets oxygen in the air, **2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂**: it turns reddish-brown right at the top of the tube!

```
   Conc. HNO₃ + Cu                       Dilute HNO₃ + Cu

      ~ ~ ~  brown NO₂ throughout            ~ ~  turns brown at the mouth
       :          :                           :          :
       : bluish-green soln :                  : bluish-green soln:
       : (Cu pieces below) :                  : (colourless NO   :
       :__________________:                   :  rises inside)   :
                                              :__________________:
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Teacher demo (in an open test tube inside a fume cupboard): with concentrated HNO₃, thick brown NO₂ fumes fill the tube immediately; with dilute HNO₃, colourless NO bubbles up through the liquid and turns brown only when it meets air at the mouth of the tube.

**What about zinc?** Even with an active metal like zinc, neither dilute nor concentrated nitric acid releases hydrogen gas—oxides of nitrogen are formed instead. (You will learn the specific equations for zinc in higher classes.)

### Example 3. Concentrated nitric acid with 6.35 g of copper

**Problem:** 6.35 g of Cu reacts completely with concentrated HNO₃. Find the mass of HNO₃ consumed, the volume of NO₂ released at STP, and the mass of Cu(NO₃)₂ formed. (Cu = 63.5; 1 mol of gas at STP = 22.4 L.)

**Step 1:** Moles of Cu = 6.35 ÷ 63.5 = **0.1 mol**.
**Step 2:** Mole ratio Cu : HNO₃ : Cu(NO₃)₂ : NO₂ : H₂O = 1 : 4 : 1 : 2 : 2.
**Step 3:** Mass of HNO₃ = 0.4 mol × 63 = **25.2 g**. NO₂ = 0.2 mol: volume = 0.2 × 22.4 = **4.48 L** (mass = 0.2 × 46 = 9.2 g). Mass of Cu(NO₃)₂ = 0.1 × 187.5 = **18.75 g**. Mass of H₂O = 0.2 × 18 = 3.6 g.

**Check:** Reactants = 6.35 + 25.2 = 31.55 g; products = 18.75 + 9.2 + 3.6 = 31.55 g ✅.

### Example 4. Dilute nitric acid with 19.05 g of copper

**Problem:** 19.05 g of Cu reacts completely with dilute HNO₃. Calculate the mass of HNO₃ consumed and the volume of NO gas formed at STP.

**Step 1:** Moles of Cu = 19.05 ÷ 63.5 = **0.3 mol**.
**Step 2:** From 3Cu : 8HNO₃ : 2NO, 0.3 mol of Cu requires 0.8 mol of HNO₃ and produces 0.2 mol of NO.
**Step 3:** Mass of HNO₃ = 0.8 × 63 = **50.4 g**. Volume of NO = 0.2 × 22.4 = **4.48 L** (mass = 0.2 × 30 = 6.0 g).

**Check:** Cu(NO₃)₂ = 0.3 × 187.5 = 56.25 g, H₂O = 0.4 × 18 = 7.2 g. Reactants = 19.05 + 50.4 = 69.45 g; products = 56.25 + 6.0 + 7.2 = 69.45 g ✅.

### Reaction with Non-Metals (For Reading Only)

When warmed, concentrated HNO₃ also oxidises non-metals such as carbon and sulphur:

C + 4HNO₃ (conc.) → CO₂ + 4NO₂ + 2H₂O
S + 6HNO₃ (conc.) → H₂SO₄ + 6NO₂ + 2H₂O

Both reactions release reddish-brown NO₂ fumes. Carbon is oxidised to CO₂, while sulphur is oxidised all the way to sulphuric acid (whose sulphate ions can be confirmed with acidified BaCl₂ solution from Chapter 8).

### Example 5. Oxidising 1.2 g of carbon

**Problem:** 1.2 g of carbon reacts completely with hot concentrated HNO₃. Calculate the mass of HNO₃ consumed and the total volume of gases formed at STP.

**Step 1:** Moles of C = 1.2 ÷ 12 = **0.1 mol**.
**Step 2:** Mole ratio C : HNO₃ : CO₂ : NO₂ = 1 : 4 : 1 : 4.
**Step 3:** Mass of HNO₃ = 0.4 × 63 = **25.2 g**. Volumes at STP: CO₂ = 0.1 mol = **2.24 L**, NO₂ = 0.4 mol = **8.96 L**, giving a total gas volume of 0.5 mol = **11.2 L**.

**Check:** H₂O = 0.2 mol = 3.6 g. Reactants = 1.2 + 25.2 = 26.4 g; products = CO₂ (4.4 g) + NO₂ (18.4 g) + H₂O (3.6 g) = 26.4 g ✅.

| Substance | Products with Hot Conc. HNO₃ | Visible Observation |
|---|---|---|
| Cu | Cu(NO₃)₂, NO₂, H₂O | Reddish-brown fumes, bluish-green solution |
| C | CO₂, NO₂, H₂O | Reddish-brown fumes (plus colourless CO₂) |
| S | H₂SO₄, NO₂, H₂O | Reddish-brown fumes, clear solution containing SO₄²⁻ |

## Part D: How Iron and Aluminium Become "Passive"

When **iron or aluminium** is dipped into concentrated HNO₃, something surprising happens: after a momentary initial reaction, **all visible action stops completely**. Why? Concentrated nitric acid immediately oxidises the metal surface to form an **extremely thin, non-porous, protective coat of metal oxide** (just like the protective Al₂O₃ layer we studied in Chapter 2). This oxide skin acts as a barrier that prevents the acid from reaching the metal underneath, rendering the metal **passive**. What is new here is that **concentrated nitric acid itself creates this passive oxide film** on contact.

Because of passivation, industry can safely transport concentrated nitric acid in **aluminium tanker trucks**! (A copper tank, by contrast, would be a disaster—concentrated HNO₃ attacks copper vigorously, as we saw in Part C.)

## Part E: Aqua Regia: "Royal Water"

Neither concentrated nitric acid alone nor concentrated hydrochloric acid alone can dissolve **gold or platinum**. Yet when **3 volumes of concentrated HCl and 1 volume of concentrated HNO₃** are freshly mixed, they form **aqua regia** ("royal water"), which dissolves both noble metals. We first met this recipe and its teamwork mechanism in Chapter 2 (Topic 3); let's briefly review why both acids are needed.

HNO₃ is a powerful **oxidising acid** that can coax gold atoms into becoming gold ions, but by itself that process stalls immediately. Concentrated HCl supplies a high concentration of **chloride ions (Cl⁻)**, which act like "porters" that lock onto the gold ions and carry them into solution as stable chloro-complexes. Working together, the two acids dissolve gold smoothly.

```
   Testing a piece of pure gold:

   conc. HNO₃ alone          -->  no effect
   conc. HCl alone           -->  no effect
   3 : 1 mix (HCl : HNO₃)    -->  yellow solution: the gold dissolves!
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Gold in three acid setups (described for reading only; never performed by students): neither conc. HNO₃ nor conc. HCl alone affects gold, but a fresh 3 : 1 mixture of conc. HCl and conc. HNO₃ dissolves it.

In a jeweller's refining workshop, base metals like **copper** can be dissolved out of scrap alloys by nitric acid alone (Part C), whereas dissolving and refining the gold requires aqua regia. This is strictly a job for **trained professionals**: aqua regia is always mixed **fresh** just before use and **never stored in a sealed bottle**, because it continuously evolves toxic gases that would burst a closed container.

### Example 6. Preparing 40 mL of aqua regia

**Problem:** What volumes of concentrated HCl and concentrated HNO₃ are needed to prepare 40 mL of aqua regia?

A 3 : 1 ratio means 4 equal parts in total. 40 ÷ 4 = 10 mL per part. Thus, conc. HCl = 3 × 10 = **30 mL**, and conc. HNO₃ = **10 mL**.

**Check:** 30 + 10 = 40 mL ✅, and 30 ÷ 10 = 3 : 1 ✅.

## Part F: Additional Notes and Uses

- **Yellow skin stain:** concentrated nitric acid reacts with proteins in skin to leave a bright **yellow stain** (known as the xanthoproteic reaction). Never let it touch skin.
- **Fertilisers:** nitric acid reacts with ammonia to make ammonium nitrate: NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃. Specifically, 63 g of HNO₃ and 17 g of NH₃ combine to give 80 g of NH₄NO₃. So the 6.3 kg of HNO₃ (100 mol) made in Example 2 can be neutralised with another 100 mol (1.7 kg) of ammonia to produce 8.0 kg of NH₄NO₃ fertiliser!
- **Other uses:** manufacturing explosives, synthetic dyes, etching designs onto metals, cleaning metal surfaces, and serving as an oxidiser in rocket propellants.
- **Environmental impact:** NO and NO₂ are toxic air pollutants, and nitrogen oxides (NOx) in the atmosphere react with rain and oxygen to form **acid rain** (Class 9). Consequently, industrial plants must scrub their exhaust gases before releasing them.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Like other acids, nitric acid reacts with metals to release hydrogen gas."**
This sounds plausible because in Class 9 we saw Zn + dilute H₂SO₄ or HCl release H₂ gas, and HNO₃ also starts with H. In Chapter 2 we caught this mistake for concentrated HNO₃; here we see that it holds true even for dilute HNO₃ and active metals like zinc. Let's trace why in three steps:

1. **Recall Class 9.** Zinc with dilute H₂SO₄ or HCl gave H₂ gas (confirmed by the squeaky-pop test), and we specifically noted that nitric acid cannot be used to prepare hydrogen ([hydrogen-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/hydrogen/hydrogen-part2.md)).
2. **Count where the hydrogen atoms go.** In Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O, there are 4 H atoms on the left. On the right: 0 H in Cu(NO₃)₂, 0 H in NO₂, and **4 H in 2H₂O**. Every single hydrogen atom has been oxidised into **water**—zero H₂ gas is formed.
3. **Check dilute nitric acid too.** In 3Cu + 8HNO₃ → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O, all 8 H atoms on the left end up inside **4H₂O** on the right. Still no H₂ gas!

```
   4 H atoms in 4HNO₃             After Cu + conc. HNO₃

   H H H H   --------------->   In 2 H₂O : H H   H H
                                In H₂ gas: none at all
```

> **Figure 3.6** – Tracking the hydrogen atoms: all 4 hydrogen atoms from 4HNO₃ end up locked inside 2 water molecules; not a single one forms H₂ gas.

| Acid + Zn | Gas Released | Gives Pop Test for H₂? |
|---|---|---|
| Dilute H₂SO₄ or HCl | H₂ | Yes |
| HNO₃ (dilute or conc.) | Oxides of nitrogen (NO or NO₂), not H₂ | No |

**Rule to remember:** Metal + nitric acid → salt + oxide of nitrogen + water. Expecting H₂ gas from nitric acid is a classic exam pitfall.

## Quick Recap

- Lab preparation: KNO₃ (or NaNO₃) + conc. H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HNO₃ below ~200 °C inside an **all-glass retort** (because nitric acid vapour attacks cork and rubber). HNO₃ distils over into a cooled receiver.
- Yellow-brown colour: caused by slow decomposition in heat or light (4HNO₃ → 4NO₂ + 2H₂O + O₂), which is why it is stored in dark bottles.
- Ostwald process: 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O (Pt-Rh gauze, ~800 °C); 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂; 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃. Overall: NH₃ + 2O₂ → HNO₃ + H₂O.
- Both a strong acid and a powerful **oxidising agent**; **does not release hydrogen** with metals.
- Cu + 4HNO₃ (conc.) → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O; 3Cu + 8HNO₃ (dil.) → 3Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO + 4H₂O (colourless NO turns brown on meeting air).
- C + 4HNO₃ → CO₂ + 4NO₂ + 2H₂O; S + 6HNO₃ → H₂SO₄ + 6NO₂ + 2H₂O.
- Fe and Al are rendered **passive** by concentrated HNO₃ due to a protective oxide film.
- Aqua regia: freshly mixed 3 volumes conc. HCl + 1 volume conc. HNO₃; dissolves gold and platinum.
- Uses: fertilisers (NH₄NO₃), explosives, dyes, metal etching and cleaning, rocket oxidiser. Stains skin yellow; NO and NO₂ are toxic and contribute to acid rain.
- Every experiment in this topic is strictly for reading or observation as a teacher demo.

### Answers

1. NaNO₃ = 23 + 14 + 48 = **85** g/mol. Moles = 12.75 ÷ 85 = **0.15 mol**. Ratio 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 gives HNO₃ = 0.15 × 63 = **9.45 g**; NaHSO₄ (23 + 1 + 32 + 64 = 120) = 0.15 × 120 = **18 g**; H₂SO₄ = 0.15 × 98 = **14.7 g**. Check: reactants = 12.75 + 14.7 = 27.45 g; products = 9.45 + 18 = 27.45 g ✅.
2. Nitric acid vapours **attack cork and rubber**, so an all-glass apparatus is mandatory. Heating is kept gentle because nitric acid **decomposes at higher temperatures** (4HNO₃ → 4NO₂ + 2H₂O + O₂) and in sunlight, turning yellow-brown from dissolved NO₂—hence it is stored in dark glass bottles.
3. 5.6 L ÷ 22.4 = 0.25 mol of NO. Ratio 2 : 1 : 2 gives O₂ = 0.125 mol = **2.8 L**, and NO₂ = 0.25 mol = **5.6 L**. Check: N 2 = 2, O 2 + 2 = 4 = 4 ✅.
4. NH₃ = 5100 ÷ 17 = **300 mol**. HNO₃ = 300 × 63 = 18,900 g = **18.9 kg**. O₂ = 600 × 32 = 19,200 g = **19.2 kg**. H₂O = 300 × 18 = 5400 g = **5.4 kg**. Check: 5.1 + 19.2 = 24.3 kg = 18.9 + 5.4 ✅.
5. Cu = 15.875 ÷ 63.5 = **0.25 mol**. HNO₃ = 4 × 0.25 = 1.0 mol = **63 g**. NO₂ = 2 × 0.25 = 0.5 mol = 0.5 × 22.4 = **11.2 L**. Check: Cu(NO₃)₂ = 0.25 × 187.5 = 46.875 g, NO₂ = 23 g, H₂O = 9 g; 46.875 + 23 + 9 = 78.875 g = 15.875 + 63 ✅.
6. Cu = 7.62 ÷ 63.5 = **0.12 mol**. HNO₃ = 0.12 × 8 ÷ 3 = 0.32 mol = **20.16 g**. NO = 0.12 × 2 ÷ 3 = 0.08 mol = 0.08 × 22.4 = **1.792 L**. At the mouth of the test tube, **colourless NO turns into reddish-brown NO₂** on contact with oxygen in the air (2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂). Check: Cu(NO₃)₂ = 22.5 g, NO = 2.4 g, H₂O = 0.16 × 18 = 2.88 g; total = 27.78 g = 7.62 + 20.16 ✅.
7. C = 3.6 ÷ 12 = **0.3 mol**. HNO₃ = 4 × 0.3 = 1.2 mol = **75.6 g**. Gases: CO₂ (0.3 mol) + NO₂ (1.2 mol) = 1.5 mol = 1.5 × 22.4 = **33.6 L**. Check: CO₂ (13.2 g) + NO₂ (55.2 g) + H₂O (0.6 mol = 10.8 g) = 79.2 g = 3.6 + 75.6 ✅.
8. S = 1.6 ÷ 32 = **0.05 mol**. H₂SO₄ = 0.05 × 98 = **4.9 g**. NO₂ = 6 × 0.05 = 0.3 mol = 0.3 × 22.4 = **6.72 L**. Test: acidify with dilute HCl and add BaCl₂ solution to get a white precipitate of BaSO₄ insoluble in acid. Check: HNO₃ = 0.3 × 63 = 18.9 g; 1.6 + 18.9 = 20.5 g = 4.9 + 13.8 + 1.8 ✅.
9. **She is mistaken.** Because nitric acid is a strong oxidising acid, instead of releasing H₂ gas it oxidises zinc to Zn²⁺ ions while its own nitrogen is reduced to **oxides of nitrogen** and all the hydrogen forms water.
10. Concentrated HNO₃ forms a **thin, protective oxide layer** on aluminium (passivation) that halts further reaction, making aluminium tankers safe. **Copper** does not passivate: it reacts vigorously via Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O, which would corrode the tank and release toxic NO₂ fumes.
11. A 3 : 1 ratio means 4 parts total. 180 ÷ 4 = 45 mL per part. Conc. HCl = **135 mL**, conc. HNO₃ = **45 mL**. Check: 135 + 45 = 180 mL ✅, and 135 ÷ 45 = 3 ✅. **Copper** dissolves in concentrated HNO₃ alone, whereas **gold** requires aqua regia.
12. (a) **False** ❌: HNO₃ attacks rubber, so all-glass apparatus is required. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌: NO is colourless; NO₂ is reddish-brown. (d) **False** ❌: it is 3 volumes of conc. HCl to 1 volume of conc. HNO₃. (e) **False** ❌: it gives NO gas and water, not hydrogen.
13. **X = S**, **Y = C**. (Copper would give a bluish-green solution, whereas both solutions here are colourless; sulphate precipitate BaSO₄ comes only from S, and CO₂ turning lime water milky comes only from C.) Equations: X: S + 6HNO₃ → H₂SO₄ + 6NO₂ + 2H₂O; Y: C + 4HNO₃ → CO₂ + 4NO₂ + 2H₂O. Check: S 1, H 6, N 6, O 18 = 4 + 12 + 2 ✅; C 1, H 4, N 4, O 12 = 2 + 8 + 2 ✅.
