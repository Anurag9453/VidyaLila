# Topic 4: How Is Sulphuric Acid Made? The Contact Process

In Topic 1 (to make HCl) and again in Topic 3 (to make HNO₃), we relied on the exact same laboratory reagent: **concentrated sulphuric acid**. One acid was being used to liberate two other acids! That naturally raises the question: where does sulphuric acid itself come from? It doesn't grow on farms, and it isn't mined directly from the earth. Today we step inside the industrial plant that manufactures it: the **Contact process**. Along the way, two familiar threads—the roasting of sulphide ores from Chapter 3 and acid rain from Class 9—will tie together into one cohesive story.

## The Auto Battery Shop and the Factory Smokestack

Mr. Brooks took his nephew Ethan along to an auto parts shop to pick up a replacement car battery. In the corner sat a heavy black battery casing stamped with a bright yellow-and-red warning label: **"DANGER: CORROSIVE ACID. For adult handling only."** Next to the text were hazard pictograms for corrosive liquid and poison.

"Uncle, there's acid inside a car battery?" Ethan asked.

"Yes—inside a lead-acid car battery is **dilute sulphuric acid**, which is why it carries such a strict warning label," Mr. Brooks replied. "That's why we let the technician carry and install it. We only read the label from a safe distance; we never touch or open the casing."

On the drive home, their route passed an industrial district. High in the air, a faint plume rose from a tall chimney, and a sharp, choking smell—like a freshly struck match—drifted past. Ethan covered his nose. "Uncle, what is that sharp smell? Is that the kind of smokestack we learned about when studying acid rain?"

Mr. Brooks rolled up the car windows. "Well spotted, Ethan. That smell is **SO₂**—sulphur dioxide, formed when sulphur burns. Now think about this: if SO₂ escapes into the open sky, it reacts in rainclouds to become sulphuric acid and falls as acid rain (Class 9). Yet if **an industrial plant captures that exact same SO₂ and converts it under controlled conditions** into sulphuric acid, it becomes one of the most useful chemicals on Earth—used to make fertilisers, car batteries, detergents, and paints! One gas: an environmental pollutant when released, a vital resource when captured. Let's head home and see how the Contact process does this cleanly and safely."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Contact process** | The industrial method for manufacturing sulphuric acid, in which reacting gases come into **contact** with a solid catalyst | S → SO₂ → SO₃ → H₂SO₄ |
| **Converter** | The large reactor vessel holding beds of catalyst where SO₂ is oxidised into SO₃ | the heart of the Contact plant |
| **Catalyst** | A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed itself | V₂O₅ (vanadium pentoxide) |
| **Reversible reaction** | A reaction that proceeds in both forward and backward directions (⇌) | 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ |
| **Oleum** | Fuming sulphuric acid, formed by dissolving SO₃ in concentrated H₂SO₄ | H₂S₂O₇ (pyrosulphuric acid) |
| **Absorption tower** | A tall packed column in which a rising gas is absorbed by a liquid trickling downward | absorbing SO₃ in conc. H₂SO₄ |
| **Acid mist** | A fine fog of tiny liquid acid droplets suspended in air that refuses to settle easily | formed if SO₃ meets water directly |
| **Sulphide ore** | A natural mineral in which a metal is chemically combined with sulphur | FeS₂ (iron pyrites) |

## Safety First (Read Carefully)

This entire topic is **strictly for reading**: it describes an industrial chemical plant, not a school or home experiment.

- **SO₂ and SO₃ are toxic, choking gases** that severely irritate the eyes, nose, throat, and lungs. In moist air, SO₃ forms a corrosive acid mist.
- **Concentrated sulphuric acid and oleum** cause severe chemical burns to skin and char fabric, paper, and wood on contact. In industry, they are handled inside sealed piping by trained personnel wearing full protective gear.
- Whenever a teacher demonstrates burning a tiny speck of sulphur in a school laboratory, it is **strictly a teacher demonstration** inside a working fume cupboard with safety goggles, while students observe from a safe distance.
- Children must **never touch or open** car batteries or household chemical containers; read labels only with adult supervision.

## Part A: Raw Materials and the Three-Step Roadmap

To manufacture sulphuric acid, a Contact plant needs four straightforward inputs:

1. **A source of sulphur:** either elemental **sulphur (S)**—a bright yellow solid, much of which is recovered today by cleaning sulphur impurities out of petroleum and natural gas—or **SO₂ gas** obtained by roasting **sulphide ores** such as iron pyrites (**FeS₂**) in air (Chapter 3). Both sources supply the same starting gas: **SO₂**.
2. **Air:** the most economical source of oxygen (O₂).
3. **A catalyst, V₂O₅** (vanadium pentoxide).
4. **Water and concentrated H₂SO₄:** used in the final absorption and dilution stage (explained in Part D).

The entire manufacturing process unfolds in **three main steps**:

```
   Step 1:  Burn sulphur in air                   S + O₂ --> SO₂
   Step 2:  Convert SO₂ to SO₃ over a catalyst    2SO₂ + O₂ <==> 2SO₃
   Step 3:  Absorb SO₃ in conc. H₂SO₄             SO₃ + H₂SO₄ --> H₂S₂O₇ (oleum)
            then dilute oleum with water          H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O --> 2H₂SO₄

   Sulphur --> SO₂ --> SO₃ --> oleum --> sulphuric acid
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Master roadmap of the Contact process: sulphur is burned to SO₂, oxidised over a catalyst to SO₃, absorbed in concentrated H₂SO₄ to make oleum, and finally diluted with water to give sulphuric acid. Notice that SO₃ is never bubbled straight into water—we will see why in Part D and Common Pitfalls.

### Example 1. Atom balance across all four equations

**Problem:** Verify that the number of atoms of each element is conserved on both sides of all four equations in the Contact process.

**Step 1:** S + O₂ → SO₂.
**Step 2:** 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃.
**Step 3a:** SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇.
**Step 3b:** H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.

**Check:** Step 1: S 1 = 1, O 2 = 2 ✅. Step 2: S 2 = 2, O (4 + 2) = 6 = 6 ✅. Step 3a: S (1 + 1) = 2 = 2, O (3 + 4) = 7 = 7, H 2 = 2 ✅. Step 3b: H (2 + 2) = 4 = 2 × 2, S 2 = 2, O (7 + 1) = 8 = 2 × 4 ✅. Notice that in Step 3a one molecule of H₂SO₄ is invested, and in Step 3b two molecules of H₂SO₄ are recovered—giving a **net gain** of one new H₂SO₄ molecule per SO₃ molecule!

## Part B: Step 1: Burning Sulphur and Purifying the Gas

In the plant, molten sulphur is sprayed into a **furnace (burner)** and burned in a stream of **pre-dried air** (moisture is removed first so water droplets cannot corrode the pipes or harm the catalyst):

S + O₂ → SO₂

In Class 9, you met this exact equation when sulphur impurities in coal or oil burned to release SO₂ out of a smokestack. There, it was an unwanted pollutant; here, it is the **essential feedstock**.

**Teacher demo (for observation only):** In a school fume cupboard, a teacher burns a tiny pinch of yellow sulphur powder in a gas jar. It burns with a **pale blue flame**, filling the jar with pungent SO₂ gas (the teacher never inhales it, and students watch from behind the fume hood sash). A strip of moist blue litmus paper held inside the jar turns **red**, showing that SO₂ is an acidic oxide. This is strictly a teacher demo because SO₂ is toxic.

In the factory, the hot SO₂ gas leaving the burner must be thoroughly **purified and dried**: dust particles and moisture are removed because impurities can poison (deactivate) the catalyst and corrode the steel plant. To dry the gas stream, plants use **concentrated H₂SO₄** itself (whose drying power we will explore in Topic 5).

If the plant starts with **iron pyrites (FeS₂)** instead of elemental sulphur, the ore is roasted in air (just like the roasting equations in Chapter 3):

4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂

Notice the stoichiometry: **each formula unit of FeS₂ contains two S atoms and yields two molecules of SO₂** (8 SO₂ ÷ 4 FeS₂ = 2), leaving solid **Fe₂O₃** behind.

**Check:** Fe 4 = 4, S 8 = 8, O 22 = 6 + 16 = 22 ✅.

```
            (conc. H₂SO₄)                                     |
                  :                                           v
                  +-------------------------------> [ BURNER ] --> SO₂ + excess air
                                                                         :

                                                  [ Purification: remove dust & moisture ]
                                                                         :

```

> **Figure 4.2** – Front end of the Contact process: dried air and sulphur burn to produce SO₂; dust and moisture are scrubbed out, and the clean, dry gas mixture is brought to the right temperature before entering the converter.

## Part C: Step 2: The Converter, the Catalyst, and the 450 °C Compromise

Now comes the central step. The clean, dry mixture of SO₂ and oxygen (air) is passed over beds of solid **V₂O₅ (vanadium pentoxide) catalyst** inside a reactor called the **converter**. Because the reaction happens where the gases make physical **contact** with the catalyst surface, the entire industrial method is named the Contact process:

2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃

Keep two key points in mind:

1. **The reaction is reversible (⇌).** Just as we saw with the Haber process in Topic 2, the reaction can go both ways, so not all of the SO₂ converts in a single pass.
2. **What does the catalyst do?** It **only speeds up the rate** at which equilibrium is reached, without being used up itself.

### The Temperature Compromise

The forward reaction (2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃) is **exothermic**—it releases heat. Consequently:

- At **very low temperatures**, the equilibrium favours a **very high percentage of SO₃**, but the reaction proceeds **far too slowly** for a factory to operate economically.
- At **very high temperatures**, the reaction is **fast**, but the equilibrium shifts backward, leaving a **poor yield of SO₃**.
- Therefore, engineers choose an optimum compromise temperature of **about 450 °C**: fast enough (with the V₂O₅ catalyst) to produce acid rapidly, while still achieving a very high conversion to SO₃.

**What about pressure?** The Contact process runs at just **1 to 2 atmospheres** (barely above normal atmospheric pressure). Even though 3 volumes of gas (2SO₂ + O₂) shrink to 2 volumes (2SO₃), the conversion to SO₃ at 450 °C over V₂O₅ is **already over 98 percent** at 1–2 atm! Unlike the Haber process (~200 atm), there is no need to pay for expensive high-pressure compressors and thick-walled vessels.

```
        SO₂ + O₂ (hot gas mixture enters)
                 :
                 v
        +------------------+
        :  V₂O₅ bed 1      :   <--- gas passes through successive catalyst beds
        :------------------:
        :  V₂O₅ bed 2      :
        :------------------:
        :  V₂O₅ bed 3      :
        +------------------+
                 :
                 v
        SO₃ + a trace of unreacted SO₂ and O₂ (exits)

        converter operating at ~450 °C
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Simplified view of the converter: hot SO₂ and O₂ pass downward through successive beds of V₂O₅ catalyst at ~450 °C, emerging mainly as SO₃. Heat released by the exothermic reaction is captured in heat exchangers to warm the incoming gas stream.

Here is the temperature trade-off at a glance:

```
   Temperature          Reaction Rate    Equilibrium Yield of SO₃
   -------------------  ---------------  ------------------------
   Too low              far too slow     high
   About 450 °C         fast (with V₂O₅) very good (compromise)
   Too high             very fast        low
```

> **Figure 4.4** – The temperature compromise (qualitative): low temperature gives a high yield but is too slow; high temperature is fast but ruins the yield. Operating at ~450 °C over V₂O₅ balances both.

### Example 2. Gas volume stoichiometry in the converter

**Problem:** In a converter, 120 L of SO₂ reacts with oxygen at the same temperature and pressure. Assuming complete conversion to SO₃, what volume of O₂ is consumed and what volume of SO₃ is formed? (Use Gay-Lussac's volume ratio from Chapter 5.)

**Step 1 (equation):** 2SO₂ + O₂ → 2SO₃. The gas volume ratio is 2 : 1 : 2.
**Step 2:** Volume of O₂ needed = 120 ÷ 2 = **60 L**.
**Step 3:** Volume of SO₃ formed = **120 L** (2 : 2 ratio).

**Check:** Before reaction: 120 + 60 = 180 L of gas; after reaction: 120 L of gas. The total volume **decreases** because 3 volumes of reactants combine into 2 volumes of product (3 → 2) ✅.

## Part D: Step 3: Absorbing SO₃ in H₂SO₄ (Oleum)

Once SO₃ leaves the converter, we need to turn it into sulphuric acid. On paper, the most obvious equation is SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄. Yet **no industrial plant ever bubbles SO₃ directly into water**! Instead, the plant absorbs SO₃ gas in **concentrated sulphuric acid (~98 percent)** inside a packed absorption tower, forming **oleum** (fuming sulphuric acid, H₂S₂O₇):

SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇

Next, the oleum is carefully mixed with a **controlled amount of water** to convert it into two molecules of concentrated sulphuric acid:

H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄

By adjusting the exact amount of water added to oleum, the plant can produce sulphuric acid of any desired concentration smoothly and safely.

```
        concentrated H₂SO₄ (sprayed from the top)
                      :
                      v
             +--------------------+
             :  ceramic packing   :
             :  (maximises contact:
             :   between gas and  :
             :   liquid: SO₃ rises:
             :   as acid trickles :
             :   downward)        :
             +--------------------+
               ^                :
               :                v
        SO₃ gas enters     oleum drains out at bottom
        (from converter)   (then diluted with controlled water to give H₂SO₄)
```

> **Figure 4.5** – The SO₃ absorption tower: concentrated H₂SO₄ trickles down over ceramic packing while SO₃ gas rises from the bottom. They react smoothly to form liquid oleum (H₂S₂O₇), which drains from the bottom and is then diluted with a measured amount of water to yield H₂SO₄.

### Example 3. Oleum and its dilution with water

**Problem:** 40 kg of SO₃ is completely absorbed in concentrated H₂SO₄. (a) What mass of H₂SO₄ is required to absorb it, and what mass of oleum (H₂S₂O₇) is formed? (b) When this oleum is subsequently diluted with the exact stoichiometric amount of water, what mass of water is used and what total mass of H₂SO₄ is obtained? (S = 32, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Step 1 (molar masses):** SO₃ = 32 + 48 = **80**; H₂SO₄ = 2 + 32 + 64 = **98**; H₂S₂O₇ = 2 + 64 + 112 = **178**; H₂O = **18**.
**Step 2 (moles):** 40 kg ÷ 80 = **0.5 kmol** of SO₃. In SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇, the mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 1.
**Step 3 (a):** H₂SO₄ needed = 0.5 × 98 = **49 kg**; oleum formed = 0.5 × 178 = **89 kg**.
**Step 4 (b):** In H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄, water needed = 0.5 × 18 = **9 kg**; total H₂SO₄ obtained = 0.5 × 2 × 98 = **98 kg**.

**Check:** Conservation of mass: 40 + 49 = 89 kg of oleum ✅; 89 + 9 = 98 kg of H₂SO₄ ✅. Notice that subtracting the 49 kg of H₂SO₄ we invested in Step 3a leaves a **net gain of 98 − 49 = 49 kg of brand-new H₂SO₄**, formed from 40 kg of SO₃ + 9 kg of water (40 + 9 = 49 kg) ✅!

## Part E: From Sulphur to Sulphuric Acid: Mass Stoichiometry

Linking all three steps gives a remarkably clean atom-conservation chain (the same chain we used for acid rain calculations in Class 9): **1 S → 1 SO₂ → 1 SO₃ → 1 H₂SO₄** (every single sulphur atom in the raw material ends up inside one molecule of H₂SO₄). In terms of mass, **1 mole of S (32 g) produces 1 mole of H₂SO₄ (98 g)**.

### Example 4. How much acid from 16 kg of pure sulphur?

**Problem:** An industrial plant burns 16 kg of pure sulphur and converts it completely into sulphuric acid with no losses. Find the mass of SO₂ formed and oxygen used in Step 1, and the final net mass of H₂SO₄ produced. (S = 32, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Step 1:** Moles of S = 16 ÷ 32 = **0.5 kmol**.
**Step 2 (Step 1 reaction):** From S + O₂ → SO₂, SO₂ = 0.5 kmol × 64 = **32 kg**, and O₂ = 0.5 × 32 = **16 kg**.
**Step 3 (full chain):** By the 1 : 1 mole ratio (1 S → 1 H₂SO₄), H₂SO₄ = 0.5 kmol × 98 = **49 kg**.

**Check:** Step 1 mass balance: 16 + 16 = 32 kg ✅. Overall mass ratio: 49 ÷ 16 = 3.0625, which equals 98 ÷ 32 = 3.0625 ✅.

### Example 5. Making sulphuric acid from iron pyrites (FeS₂)

**Problem:** 240 kg of iron pyrites (FeS₂) is roasted completely (4FeS₂ + 11O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + 8SO₂) and all the resulting SO₂ is converted into H₂SO₄. Calculate the mass of H₂SO₄ produced. (Fe = 56, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of FeS₂ = 56 + 64 = **120** g/mol. Moles = 240 kg ÷ 120 = **2 kmol**.
**Step 2:** Since 4 FeS₂ gives 8 SO₂ (i.e., 1 FeS₂ gives 2 SO₂), SO₂ = 2 × 2 = **4 kmol**.
**Step 3:** Since 1 SO₂ gives 1 H₂SO₄, H₂SO₄ = 4 kmol × 98 = **392 kg**.

**Check:** Count sulphur: 2 kmol of FeS₂ contains 4 kmol of S atoms, and 4 kmol of H₂SO₄ also contains 4 kmol of S atoms ✅. Mass of S = 4 × 32 = 128 kg, and S inside 392 kg of H₂SO₄ = 392 × 32 ÷ 98 = 128 kg ✅.

## Part F: Connecting Smokestacks, Acid Rain, and Roasting

Let's return to Ethan's question as they drove past the smokestack. Because 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ is reversible, a tiny fraction of SO₂ remains unreacted after passing through the catalyst beds. Modern plants pass the gas through multiple catalyst stages and **scrub** the tail gas before it reaches the chimney, because releasing SO₂ into the sky causes air pollution and acid rain.

- **Link to Metallurgy (Chapter 3):** When smelters roast sulphide ores like zinc blende (ZnS) or iron pyrites (FeS₂), huge volumes of SO₂ gas are given off. Instead of venting that toxic SO₂ into the atmosphere, smelters pipe it straight into an on-site **Contact process plant** to manufacture sulphuric acid!
- **Link to Acid Rain (Class 9):** In the open atmosphere, escaped SO₂ slowly oxidises to SO₃ and dissolves in rainwater to fall as destructive acid rain. Inside a Contact plant, the exact same chemical sequence is run **inside sealed pipes and towers**, turning a potential pollutant into an essential industrial product.

| Feature | Escaped SO₂ in the Atmosphere | Captured SO₂ in the Contact Process |
|---|---|---|
| Driven by | Sunlight, atmospheric dust, clouds | V₂O₅ catalyst at ~450 °C inside a converter |
| Outcome | Destructive acid rain | Valuable sulphuric acid |
| Control | Uncontrolled environmental pollution | Contained, closed industrial system |

**Major uses of sulphuric acid:** manufacturing agricultural fertilisers (such as superphosphate and ammonium sulphate—the single largest use), lead-acid car batteries (dilute H₂SO₄, ~30 percent), synthetic detergents, dyes, paints, pigments, petroleum refining, pickling steel before galvanising or plating, and making other acids (HCl and HNO₃). We will explore the dramatic chemical properties of concentrated H₂SO₄ in Topic 5.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "In the Contact process, SO₃ gas from the converter is bubbled directly into water: SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄."**
While the equation SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ is chemically balanced (and happens in rainclouds), **industrial plants never do this directly**. Let's trace why in three steps:

1. **Direct reaction with water is violently exothermic:** when SO₃ gas meets liquid water directly, it releases an enormous burst of heat. The water vaporises locally and the acid condenses into a **dense fog of microscopic liquid droplets (an acid mist)**.
2. **Why acid mist is a nightmare:** these tiny suspended droplets refuse to settle or condense into bulk liquid; instead, the corrosive mist floats right out of the tower with the exhaust gas stream!
3. **The industrial solution (via oleum):** by absorbing SO₃ in **concentrated H₂SO₄** first (SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇), the gas dissolves smoothly without forming any mist. Then, mixing liquid oleum with water in a controlled way (H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄) yields liquid sulphuric acid cleanly.

```
   Wrong Route (Direct Water)               Correct Industrial Route (Via Oleum)

   SO₃ + water                              SO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄  --> liquid oleum (H₂S₂O₇)
        :                                                              |
        v                                                              v
   violent heat creates a fine              oleum + water (mixed under control)
   corrosive acid mist that                 --> liquid H₂SO₄ collected smoothly
   refuses to settle!
```

> **Figure 4.6** – Two routes from SO₃ to H₂SO₄: bubbling SO₃ directly into water creates an uncollectable, hazardous acid mist; absorbing SO₃ in concentrated H₂SO₄ first forms liquid oleum, which can then be diluted safely with water.

**Rule to remember:** Although the net overall stoichiometry is SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄, the **actual industrial pathway** is always SO₃ → oleum (H₂S₂O₇) → H₂SO₄.

## Quick Recap

- **Raw materials:** sulphur (or SO₂ from roasting sulphide ores like FeS₂), air (O₂), V₂O₅ catalyst, water, and concentrated H₂SO₄.
- **Three main steps:** (1) S + O₂ → SO₂; (2) 2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ (over V₂O₅ catalyst at ~450 °C and 1–2 atm); (3) SO₃ + H₂SO₄ → H₂S₂O₇ (oleum), followed by H₂S₂O₇ + H₂O → 2H₂SO₄.
- **Why ~450 °C is a compromise:** lower temperatures give a higher equilibrium yield of SO₃ but are far too slow; higher temperatures are fast but lower the yield.
- **Why 1–2 atm is enough:** conversion to SO₃ is already very high at near-atmospheric pressure, so expensive high-pressure equipment is unnecessary.
- **Role of the catalyst (V₂O₅):** speeds up the reaction rate without changing the equilibrium yield or being consumed.
- **Never bubble SO₃ directly into water:** the violent exothermic reaction forms a corrosive **acid mist** that does not settle; absorb in conc. H₂SO₄ to make oleum first.
- **Mass stoichiometry:** 1 mol S → 1 mol SO₂ → 1 mol SO₃ → 1 mol H₂SO₄ (32 g of S yields 98 g of H₂SO₄; 1 mol of FeS₂ yields 2 mol of H₂SO₄).
- **Connections:** links sulphide ore roasting (Chapter 3) with acid rain prevention (Class 9).
- **Safety:** SO₂ and SO₃ are toxic gases; oleum and conc. H₂SO₄ are fiercely corrosive; all content here is strictly for reading or teacher demonstration.

For Fe₂O₃: 4 FeS₂ gives 2 Fe₂O₃, so 3 kmol FeS₂ gives 1.5 kmol Fe₂O₃; molar mass of Fe₂O₃ = 112 + 48 = 160; mass = 1.5 × 160 = **240 kg**. **Check:** Fe atoms: 3 kmol in FeS₂ = 3 kmol in 1.5 kmol Fe₂O₃ ✅. S atoms: 6 kmol in FeS₂ = 6 kmol in H₂SO₄ ✅.
4. Volume ratio 2 : 1 : 2 gives O₂ = 336 ÷ 2 = **168 L**, and SO₃ = **336 L**. **Check:** 336 + 168 = 504 L before reaction, 336 L after (volume shrinks in a 3 → 2 ratio) ✅.
5. SO₃ = 200 ÷ 80 = **2.5 kmol**. H₂SO₄ taken up = 2.5 × 98 = **245 kg**. Oleum formed = 2.5 × 178 = **445 kg**. **Check:** 200 + 245 = 445 kg ✅.
6. H₂S₂O₇ = 17.8 ÷ 178 = **0.1 kmol**. Water needed = 0.1 × 18 = **1.8 kg**. H₂SO₄ formed = 0.1 × 2 × 98 = **19.6 kg**. **Check:** 17.8 + 1.8 = 19.6 kg ✅.

8. SO₂ = 192 ÷ 64 = **3 kmol**. O₂ = 3 ÷ 2 = 1.5 kmol × 32 = **48 kg**. SO₃ = 3 × 80 = **240 kg**. **Check:** 192 + 48 = 240 kg ✅.
9. Reacting SO₃ directly with water is **violently exothermic**, causing the water to boil and creating a dense **acid mist** of tiny droplets that refuses to settle and escapes with the exhaust gas. Instead, plants absorb SO₃ in **concentrated H₂SO₄** to make liquid oleum (H₂S₂O₇), and then dilute the oleum with water under controlled conditions to form 2H₂SO₄.
10. Because the forward reaction releases heat, **low temperatures** give a higher equilibrium yield of SO₃ but make the reaction **far too slow** for industry. **High temperatures** make the reaction fast but reduce the yield of SO₃. Choosing **~450 °C** over a V₂O₅ catalyst is a practical **compromise** that achieves both a fast rate and a high yield.
11. Raw materials: **sulphur (or sulphide ore), air, water, and concentrated H₂SO₄**; catalyst: **V₂O₅**. Correct order: (1) burning sulphur, (2) purifying the gas, (3) passing over V₂O₅, (4) absorption in conc. H₂SO₄ (forming oleum), (5) adding water to oleum.
12. (a) **True** ✅. (b) **False** ❌: 1 to 2 atmospheres is sufficient; ~200 atmospheres is used in the Haber process. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **True** ✅ (1 : 1 mole ratio across each step). (e) **False** ❌: SO₂ is toxic and causes acid rain.
13. Roasting sulphide ores (Chapter 3) produces large quantities of **SO₂ gas**. Capturing this SO₂ and feeding it into a Contact process plant converts it via V₂O₅ and oleum into valuable **H₂SO₄**, preventing the SO₂ from escaping up the smokestack to cause **acid rain**.
