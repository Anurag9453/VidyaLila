# Topic 5: Concentrated Sulphuric Acid: Dehydration, Oxidation, and Safety

In Topic 4, we saw how industrial plants manufacture sulphuric acid from sulphur, and that the final product of the Contact process is **concentrated H₂SO₄**—roughly 98 percent acid and 2 percent water. Now comes the big question: **how does concentrated H₂SO₄ behave?** In Class 9 and in Chapter 1, we worked with **dilute** H₂SO₄, which released hydrogen with zinc and CO₂ with carbonates. Concentrated H₂SO₄ has a **completely different chemical personality**. Today we will examine its four distinct roles: a dehydrating agent that strips water out of compounds, an oxidising agent when hot, a non-volatile acid, and a drying agent for gases—along with why diluting it demands strict safety rules.

---

## The Black Tower at the Science Fair

Dr. Lin took her niece Sofia to the high school science fair. In one corner of the chemistry lab, behind a barrier rope that kept students and visitors well back, the chemistry teacher was preparing a demonstration inside a glass-fronted **fume cupboard**. Inside the cabinet sat a beaker half-filled with ordinary white table sugar. Wearing heavy acid-resistant gloves, a lab coat, and splash goggles, the teacher poured concentrated sulphuric acid into the sugar and lowered the glass sash.

For a few seconds, nothing seemed to happen. Then the white sugar crystals turned yellow, darkened to brown, and suddenly turned **jet black**. Seconds later, a **steaming, porous black column** rose up out of the beaker like a growing charcoal snake, accompanied by wisps of steam and a sharp, choking odour drawn up into the exhaust vent. Sofia's eyes went wide. "Aunt Lin, it **burned** into charcoal! Where did the fire come from? The teacher didn't even light a match!"

Dr. Lin smiled. "That is a great observation, Sofia. There was no fire at all! The sugar didn't burn in oxygen—instead, **its water was ripped right out of its molecules**. Concentrated sulphuric acid has such a fierce craving for water that it strips hydrogen and oxygen out of sugar, leaving pure black carbon behind. And the steam you saw rising is proof of how much heat is released when that acid grabs water. Let's look at how that works."

"Wait, Aunt Lin—is that the same sulphuric acid used in car batteries?" "The very same formula, H₂SO₄—except battery acid is **dilute**, whereas this acid is **concentrated**. Same chemical formula, completely different behaviour!"

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Concentrated acid** | An acid containing a very high percentage of acid molecules and very little water | ~98 percent H₂SO₄ |
| **Dilute acid** | An acid dissolved in a large proportion of water | car battery acid, lab bench dilute H₂SO₄ |
| **Dehydrating agent** | A substance that chemically strips water (or H and O in a 2 : 1 ratio) out of another compound | conc. H₂SO₄ charring sugar |
| **Oxidising agent** | A substance that oxidises another substance (supplying oxygen or removing hydrogen/electrons) while being reduced itself | hot conc. H₂SO₄ reacting with copper |
| **Non-volatile acid** | An acid with a very high boiling point that does not easily vaporise on heating | conc. H₂SO₄ |
| **Drying agent (desiccant)** | A substance that absorbs physical moisture (water vapour) from a gas or mixture without reacting with it | conc. H₂SO₄ in a wash bottle |
| **Exothermic** | A process that releases heat to its surroundings | mixing conc. H₂SO₄ with water |
| **Fume cupboard** | A ventilated glass enclosure that safely exhausts toxic vapours away from the room | used for all conc. H₂SO₄ demos |

## Safety First (Read This First)

> **Safety Warning: Concentrated Sulphuric Acid**
> - Concentrated H₂SO₄ is **intensely corrosive**: it causes severe chemical burns to skin and eyes, and chars clothing, paper, and wood on contact. Handling it is **strictly a job for a trained teacher or professional**.
> - Every demonstration in this topic is **strictly for observation as a teacher demo** inside a working fume cupboard with goggles, gloves, and a lab apron, while students stand well back. No student ever performs these experiments at home or unsupervised.
> - Several reactions release **SO₂ gas**, which is toxic and choking. They must never be carried out outside a fume cupboard.
> - **The Golden Dilution Rule:** always add **Acid to Water** slowly with constant stirring—never pour water into concentrated acid (we explain the full physics and chemistry in Part E).
> - Never touch or open car batteries or heavy-duty drain cleaners. If acid ever touches skin or eyes, **tell a teacher or adult immediately** and seek medical care.

## Part A: Concentrated vs Dilute: One Formula, Two Personalities

What is actually inside the bottle? **Concentrated H₂SO₄** is about 98 percent acid by mass: a **dense, oily, syrupy liquid** about 1.8 times as dense as water. **Dilute H₂SO₄**, by contrast, consists of acid molecules dissolved in plenty of water. In Chapter 1, we learned that in water H₂SO₄ ionises completely into **H₃O⁺** and SO₄²⁻ ions, and **those H₃O⁺ ions are responsible for all typical acid reactions** (with active metals, carbonates, and bases).

In **concentrated** H₂SO₄, there is **almost no free water**, so very few H₃O⁺ ions exist. Instead, its behaviour is dominated by intact H₂SO₄ molecules, which have two remarkable traits: an **intense affinity for water** and, when heated, a strong tendency to **act as an oxidising agent**. Therefore:

- **Dilute H₂SO₄:** behaves as a typical strong acid (via H₃O⁺ ions), e.g., Zn + H₂SO₄ (dil.) → ZnSO₄ + H₂.
- **Concentrated H₂SO₄:** acts as a dehydrating agent, an oxidising agent, a non-volatile acid, and a drying agent—**a completely different story**.

Figure 5.1 summarises the four roles of concentrated H₂SO₄ at a glance.

## Part B: Dehydrating Agent: Stripping Out Water

To **dehydrate** a compound means to remove chemically combined water from its molecules. Because concentrated H₂SO₄ binds to water with a huge release of heat, it pulls hydrogen and oxygen atoms out of many organic compounds in the exact 2 : 1 ratio of water (H₂O).

### Charring of Sugar (The "Carbon Snake" Demo)

Table sugar (sucrose) has the molecular formula **C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁**. Notice the ratio of hydrogen atoms to oxygen atoms: **22 : 11 = 2 : 1**, identical to water (H₂O)! That is why sugars, starch, and cellulose are called **carbohydrates** ("hydrates of carbon"). When concentrated H₂SO₄ is poured onto sugar, it strips out all 11 water molecules per sucrose molecule and leaves behind a black mass of **pure carbon**:

C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O (in the presence of conc. H₂SO₄)

Notice that **conc. H₂SO₄ is written above the arrow (in the presence of)** because the acid is not consumed in this dehydration equation—it grabs and holds the water that is removed. The intense heat released when that water mixes with the acid boils part of the water into steam, puffing the black carbon up into a **spongy, porous black column**.

   :     Before                      Seconds later      :

   :                                   /#####\          :
   :                                  :#######:  <-- black, porous
   :       ~ sugar ~                  :#######:      column of
   :    (white crystals)              :#######:      pure carbon

   :    :  beaker  :                 : beaker  :

> **Figure 5.2** – Dehydration of sugar (teacher demo only): inside a fume cupboard, concentrated H₂SO₄ is poured onto white sugar crystals. The sugar turns brown, then black, and swells into a steaming porous column of carbon. The glass sash stays lowered throughout.

Why was there also a sharp, choking smell in Sofia's science fair demo? Because the mixture gets so hot that some of the hot concentrated acid oxidises a little of the newly formed carbon (Part C), releasing **SO₂ gas**. That is why this demonstration must **always** be performed inside a fume cupboard.

### Example 1. Stoichiometry of dehydrating 3.42 g of sugar

**Problem:** In a fume-cupboard demonstration, a teacher dehydrates 3.42 g of sucrose (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) completely with concentrated H₂SO₄. Calculate the mass of carbon left behind and the mass of water removed. (Atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16.)

**Step 1 (equation):** C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O.
**Step 2 (molar mass):** C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ = 12 × 12 + 22 × 1 + 11 × 16 = 144 + 22 + 176 = **342** g/mol.
**Step 3 (moles):** 3.42 ÷ 342 = **0.01 mol**.
**Step 4 (from mole ratio):** Carbon = 12 × 0.01 = 0.12 mol, mass = 0.12 × 12 = **1.44 g**. Water = 11 × 0.01 = 0.11 mol, mass = 0.11 × 18 = **1.98 g**.

**Check:** Atom count: C 12 = 12, H 22 = 11 × 2 = 22, O 11 = 11 ✅. Conservation of mass: 1.44 + 1.98 = 3.42 g ✅.

### Dehydrating Blue Vitriol (Copper(II) Sulphate Pentahydrate)

In Class 9, you saw that crystals of blue vitriol (**CuSO₄·5H₂O**) contain water of crystallisation, and that heating drives off this water to leave white anhydrous CuSO₄ ([water-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/water/water-part4.md)). Concentrated H₂SO₄ is such a strong dehydrating agent that it strips out this water of crystallisation **even without heating** (teacher demo):

CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O (with conc. H₂SO₄)

The bright blue crystals crumble into a **white** powder of anhydrous CuSO₄ because the water molecules that gave the crystal its blue colour have been pulled into the acid.

```
   Before                             After adding conc. H₂SO₄
   (teacher demo, dry test tube)

     :   :                              :   :
     :***:  blue crystals               :...:  white powder
     :***:  CuSO₄·5H₂O                  :...:  CuSO₄ (anhydrous)
     +---+                              +---+
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Dehydration of blue vitriol (teacher demo only): concentrated H₂SO₄ strips the water of crystallisation out of blue CuSO₄·5H₂O crystals, turning them into white anhydrous CuSO₄.

### Example 2. How much water of crystallisation is removed?

**Problem:** 12.475 g of blue vitriol (CuSO₄·5H₂O) is completely dehydrated by concentrated H₂SO₄. Calculate the mass of water removed and the mass of white anhydrous CuSO₄ remaining. (Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of CuSO₄·5H₂O = 63.5 + 32 + 64 + 5 × 18 = 159.5 + 90 = **249.5** g/mol.
**Step 2:** Moles = 12.475 ÷ 249.5 = **0.05 mol**.
**Step 3:** From CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O, water = 5 × 0.05 = 0.25 mol, mass = 0.25 × 18 = **4.5 g**. Anhydrous CuSO₄ = 0.05 × 159.5 = **7.975 g**.

**Check:** 7.975 + 4.5 = 12.475 g ✅. Atom count: Cu 1, S 1, O 4 + 5 = 9, H 10 = 5 × 2 ✅.

### Action on Paper, Wood, Cotton Fabric, and Skin

Paper, wood, and cotton fabric are made of **cellulose**, another carbohydrate containing hydrogen and oxygen in a 2 : 1 ratio. A drop of concentrated H₂SO₄ immediately dehydrates cellulose, leaving a **charred black hole** in paper or cloth. **Human skin** also contains water and organic compounds—which is why concentrated H₂SO₄ causes instant, severe chemical burns on contact.

## Part C: Oxidising Agent: Hot Concentrated Acid Oxidises Metals and Carbon

With **dilute** H₂SO₄, copper shows **no reaction at all**: as we learned in Class 9 and Chapter 2, copper sits below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace H₂ from dilute acids. However, **hot concentrated** H₂SO₄ reacts vigorously with copper—and instead of releasing hydrogen, it releases **SO₂ gas**! Here, hot concentrated H₂SO₄ acts as an **oxidising agent**: it oxidises copper metal to Cu²⁺ (forming CuSO₄) while part of the acid is reduced to SO₂ and water:

Cu + 2H₂SO₄ (conc., hot) → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O

```
   Teacher demo (inside a fume cupboard) : Cu + hot conc. H₂SO₄

                   delivery tube (passing through stopper)
             .------------------------------.
             :                              :
          [cork]                            v
          :    :                        \       /   <-- inverted funnel: rim just
          : Cu :                         \     /        touches NaOH solution surface
          : +  :                       +--\~~~/--------+
          :conc.                       :  ~~~~~~~~~~~~ :  <-- aqueous NaOH solution
          :H₂SO₄:                      :  (absorbs toxic SO₂)
          :____:                       +---------------+
         boiling tube    ^   gentle heat (teacher)
         (connected to open funnel, never sealed)
```

> **Figure 5.4** – Teacher demo of copper with hot concentrated H₂SO₄ (for observation only): a delivery tube leads from the boiling tube to an inverted funnel whose rim just touches aqueous NaOH solution, safely absorbing the toxic acidic SO₂ gas (Chapter 1). Before stopping the burner, the teacher lifts the funnel off the liquid surface so suck-back cannot occur.

In this demonstration, the liquid gradually turns **blue** as CuSO₄ forms, while colourless, pungent **SO₂ gas** bubbles out. Because SO₂ is **toxic**, this demo is performed strictly inside a fume cupboard.

### Example 3. Volume of SO₂ from 9.525 g of copper

**Problem:** 9.525 g of copper reacts completely with hot concentrated H₂SO₄. Find the mass of CuSO₄ formed, the volume of SO₂ released at STP, and the mass of H₂SO₄ consumed. (Cu = 63.5, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1; molar volume = 22.4 L at STP.)

**Step 1 (equation):** Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Mole ratio Cu : H₂SO₄ : CuSO₄ : SO₂ = 1 : 2 : 1 : 1.
**Step 2 (moles):** Cu = 9.525 ÷ 63.5 = **0.15 mol**.
**Step 3 (from mole ratio):**
- CuSO₄ = 0.15 mol × 159.5 = **23.925 g**.
- SO₂ = 0.15 mol × 22.4 = **3.36 L** at STP.
- H₂SO₄ = 0.3 mol × 98 = **29.4 g**.

**Check:** Atom count: Cu 1, S 2 = 1 + 1, O 8 = 4 + 2 + 2, H 4 = 4 ✅. Mass balance: left = 9.525 + 29.4 = 38.925 g; right = CuSO₄ (23.925 g) + SO₂ (0.15 × 64 = 9.6 g) + H₂O (0.3 × 18 = 5.4 g) = 38.925 g ✅.

### Oxidising Non-Metals: Carbon

Hot concentrated H₂SO₄ also oxidises the non-metal **carbon** to carbon dioxide while being reduced to sulphur dioxide:

C + 2H₂SO₄ (conc., hot) → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O

This is the exact secondary reaction that produced the sharp smell of SO₂ during the sugar dehydration demo in Part B!

### Example 4. Oxidising 1.8 g of carbon (For Reading Only)

**Problem:** 1.8 g of carbon reacts completely with hot concentrated H₂SO₄. Calculate the total volume of gases (CO₂ + SO₂) formed at STP and the mass of H₂SO₄ consumed. (C = 12.)

**Step 1:** Moles of C = 1.8 ÷ 12 = **0.15 mol**. Mole ratio C : CO₂ : SO₂ : H₂SO₄ = 1 : 1 : 2 : 2.
**Step 2:** CO₂ = 0.15 mol and SO₂ = 0.3 mol, giving a total gas amount of 0.45 mol × 22.4 = **10.08 L** at STP. Mass of H₂SO₄ = 0.3 mol × 98 = **29.4 g**.

**Check:** Atom count: C 1, S 2, O 8 = 2 + 4 + 2, H 4 ✅. Mass balance: 1.8 + 29.4 = 31.2 g; right = CO₂ (6.6 g) + SO₂ (19.2 g) + H₂O (5.4 g) = 31.2 g ✅.

## Part D: Non-Volatile Acid and Drying Agent

### Non-Volatile Acid: Displacing More Volatile Acids from Their Salts

Concentrated H₂SO₄ has an exceptionally high boiling point (above 330 °C)—it is a **non-volatile acid**. Acids like HCl and HNO₃ are much more volatile. Therefore, warming a chloride salt (like NaCl) or a nitrate salt (like KNO₃) with concentrated H₂SO₄ drives off the **volatile acid (HCl or HNO₃) as a gas or vapour**, while the non-volatile H₂SO₄ stays behind as a hydrogen sulphate salt: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl. That is the exact principle behind the laboratory preparations of **HCl (Topic 1)** and **HNO₃ (Topic 3)**.

### Drying Agent (Desiccant): Removing Moisture from Gases

Notice the distinction between **dehydrating** and **drying**:
- A **dehydrating agent** chemically breaks down a compound by stripping H and O out of its molecular formula (as in C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O).
- A **drying agent** simply absorbs physical moisture (water vapour, H₂O) mixed with a gas stream without changing the gas chemically.

In Class 9 ([hydrogen-part2.md](../../../class9/chemistry/hydrogen/hydrogen-part2.md)) and in Topic 1, we saw how a wash bottle containing concentrated H₂SO₄ dries moist gases:

```
         moist gas in              dry gas out
             :                      ^
             v                      :
           +-+--+------------------+-+--+
           [  two-hole stopper          ]
           +--:--------------------:----+
           :  :                    :    :
           :  :                         :
           :  :                         :
           :~~:~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~:   <-- level of conc. H₂SO₄
           : o:o  bubbles               :
           +------------------------------+
          long inlet tube dips into acid; short outlet tube stays above acid
```

> **Figure 5.5** – Drying a gas in a wash bottle (for reading only): moist gas enters down the long tube, bubbles up through concentrated H₂SO₄ (which absorbs the water vapour), and exits dry through the short tube.

Concentrated H₂SO₄ is an excellent drying agent for acidic or neutral gases such as **HCl, Cl₂, SO₂, and H₂**, because they do not react with it. However, it can **never be used to dry ammonia (NH₃)**, because basic NH₃ reacts immediately with the acid to form ammonium sulphate (Topic 2; quicklime CaO is used instead). Also, because concentrated H₂SO₄ is **hygroscopic** (absorbs moisture from the air), a bottle left unstoppered on a shelf will gradually absorb atmospheric moisture, **gain mass, and become more dilute**—so its stopper must always be kept tightly closed.

## Part E: The Dilution Rule and Safety: Acid into Water, Never the Reverse

Mixing concentrated H₂SO₄ with water is **fiercely exothermic** (we first met this heat release and safety rule in Chapter 1: [acids-bases-and-salts-part2.md](../acids-bases-and-salts/acids-bases-and-salts-part2.md); here we see the full physical reason). The rule is absolute: **always add concentrated Acid slowly to a large volume of Water with constant stirring; NEVER pour water into concentrated acid.**

**Why does the order matter so much?** Concentrated H₂SO₄ is much **denser** than water (~1.84 g/cm³ vs 1.0 g/cm³).
- **If you pour water onto concentrated acid (WRONG):** the lighter water **floats as a thin layer on top** of the heavy acid. All the intense heat of mixing is released right at that narrow boundary layer, instantly boiling the water into steam and **violently spattering boiling acid** outward into the face and eyes!
- **If you pour concentrated acid slowly into water while stirring (CORRECT):** the heavy acid **sinks into the large body of water** and disperses as you stir. Because water has a very high heat capacity, the heat is absorbed safely throughout the entire volume of water without local boiling or splashing.

```
   WRONG (Extremely Dangerous!)             CORRECT
   Pouring water into conc. acid            Pouring conc. acid slowly into water
   
      water drops                              acid added slowly down the side
           :                                       :
           v                                       v   (stirring continuously)
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  <-- lighter          +-------------+
   :  ! boils & spatters!\   water floats      : ~~~~~~~~~~~ :  <-- large volume
   :   boiling acid !    :   on top            :   water     :      of water
   :  HEAVY CONC. ACID   :                     : (acid sinks :
   +--------------------+                      +-------------+   & heat disperses)
   all heat trapped at thin top boundary       heat spreads safely throughout water
```

> **Figure 5.6** – The dilution rule (for reading only): on the left, lighter water floats on dense concentrated acid, trapping heat in a thin top layer that flashes into steam and spatters acid. On the right, dense acid poured slowly into a large volume of water sinks and mixes smoothly as it is stirred, dispersing the heat safely.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Dilute and concentrated H₂SO₄ behave the same way—the concentrated acid just reacts faster."**
It is tempting to think concentration only changes speed, since both share the formula H₂SO₄. In fact, their **chemical reactions are completely different**. Compare three cases (for reading or teacher demo):

1. **With copper metal:** Copper placed in dilute H₂SO₄ shows **no reaction at all** (Class 9 and Chapter 2). Yet in hot concentrated H₂SO₄, copper dissolves to form a **blue CuSO₄ solution** and releases **SO₂ gas**.
2. **With table sugar:** Sugar stirred into dilute H₂SO₄ or water simply dissolves into a clear solution. Sugar touched by concentrated H₂SO₄ is **dehydrated into a steaming black mass of carbon**.
3. **Which gas comes out with metals?** When dilute H₂SO₄ reacts with active metals like Zn, it releases **H₂ gas** (via H₃O⁺ ions). When hot concentrated H₂SO₄ oxidises metals like Cu, it releases **SO₂ gas, never H₂**.

| Test Case | Dilute H₂SO₄ | Concentrated H₂SO₄ |
|---|---|---|
| With copper (Cu) | No reaction | Dissolves on heating to give CuSO₄ + SO₂ |
| With sugar (C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁) | Dissolves without charring | Dehydrates to black carbon + steam |
| On paper | Wets the paper | Chars a black hole |
| Dominant behaviour | Strong acid (via H₃O⁺ ions) | Dehydrating and oxidising agent |

**Rule to remember:** Dilute H₂SO₄ acts through H₃O⁺ ions; concentrated H₂SO₄ acts as a dehydrating and oxidising agent. Never treat them as interchangeable!

**Mistake 2: "When diluting concentrated acid, pouring water into acid is the same as pouring acid into water because the final mixture is identical."**
Even though the end mixture has the same composition, **the pathway determines whether it explodes in your face**. Because water is less dense than concentrated H₂SO₄, water poured onto acid floats on top; all the heat of mixing concentrates in that thin surface layer, flashing the water into steam and **spattering boiling acid**. Always remember **"A into W"** (**A**cid into **W**ater, slowly, with stirring; Figure 5.6).

## Quick Recap

- **Concentrated H₂SO₄** (~98 percent, dense, oily liquid) and **dilute H₂SO₄** have **completely different chemical behaviours**: dilute acid acts via H₃O⁺ ions; concentrated acid acts as a dehydrating agent, oxidising agent, non-volatile acid, and drying agent.
- **Dehydrating agent:** C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ → 12C + 11H₂O (sugar chars into a porous black carbon column); CuSO₄·5H₂O → CuSO₄ + 5H₂O (blue crystals turn into white powder); chars paper, wood, cloth, and burns skin.
- **Oxidising agent (when hot):** Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O; C + 2H₂SO₄ → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O. Releases **SO₂**, not H₂.
- **Non-volatile acid:** displaces volatile HCl and HNO₃ from their salts (Topics 1 and 3).
- **Drying agent:** dries acidic/neutral gases like HCl, Cl₂, and SO₂, but **never basic NH₃**. Left open, a bottle absorbs moisture from air, gaining mass and becoming dilute.
- **Dilution rule:** always add **Acid slowly to Water** with stirring—never water to acid, because lighter water floats on dense acid and boils violently, spattering acid.
- **Safety:** all demos are strictly performed by a teacher in a fume cupboard; SO₂ is toxic.

## Practice Questions

(All questions are for written calculation. Atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Mg = 24, S = 32, Cu = 63.5. Molar volume = 22.4 L at STP.)

1. Glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆, is dehydrated completely by concentrated sulphuric acid in a teacher demo: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 6C + 6H₂O. Find the mass of carbon and of water formed from 9.0 g of glucose.
2. Cane sugar, C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁, is dehydrated in a teacher demo. For 17.1 g of sugar, find the mass of carbon left and the mass of water removed.
3. Treat paper as made of a repeating unit C₆H₁₀O₅ that is dehydrated completely: C₆H₁₀O₅ → 6C + 5H₂O. Find the masses of carbon and water formed from 32.4 g of paper.
4. A teacher dehydrates 7.485 g of blue vitriol, CuSO₄·5H₂O, with concentrated sulphuric acid. Find the mass of water removed, the mass of anhydrous copper(II) sulphate left, and its colour.
5. In a teacher demo, 12.7 g of copper is completely reacted with hot concentrated sulphuric acid: Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O. Find the mass of CuSO₄ formed, the volume of SO₂ at STP, and the mass of H₂SO₄ used up. Why must this demo be done in a fume cupboard?
6. Carbon (0.6 g) reacts completely with hot concentrated sulphuric acid: C + 2H₂SO₄ → CO₂ + 2SO₂ + 2H₂O. Find the total volume of gases (CO₂ and SO₂) at STP and the mass of acid used.
7. Anhydrous magnesium sulphate, MgSO₄, can also take up water to become MgSO₄·7H₂O: MgSO₄ + 7H₂O → MgSO₄·7H₂O. How much water can 6.0 g of MgSO₄ absorb, and what is the mass of the hydrated salt?
8. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Dilute and concentrated sulphuric acid react with copper in the same way. (b) Sugar turns black with concentrated sulphuric acid because it burns in air. (c) Concentrated H₂SO₄ is a suitable drying agent for ammonia. (d) The hydrogen to oxygen ratio in sugar is the same as in water. (e) The acid should be poured slowly into water, not water into acid.
9. Name the property of concentrated H₂SO₄ shown in each: (a) blue crystals of CuSO₄·5H₂O turn white; (b) copper dissolves on heating and a pungent gas leaves; (c) a drop on paper leaves a black hole; (d) a wash bottle of acid makes a moist gas dry.
10. Why does dilute sulphuric acid not react with copper, but hot concentrated sulphuric acid does? Which gas is formed and which gas would have been formed if copper had behaved like zinc?
11. A student claims "pouring water into concentrated acid is the same as pouring acid into water because the final mixture is the same". Explain in 3 to 4 lines why the order matters even though the final mixture is the same.
12. Ask an adult and read (without opening) the label of a battery or cleaner. List two hazard words or symbols you find, and say why children must never open or mix such products.
13. An open bottle of concentrated sulphuric acid left on a shelf for weeks is found to be heavier and thinner (less syrupy). Explain which property causes this and what should have been done.

### Answers

1. Glucose = 6 × 12 + 12 + 6 × 16 = 72 + 12 + 96 = **180** g/mol. Moles = 9.0 ÷ 180 = **0.05 mol**. Ratio 1 : 6 : 6 gives carbon = 0.3 mol × 12 = **3.6 g**, and water = 0.3 mol × 18 = **5.4 g**. Check: 3.6 + 5.4 = 9.0 g ✅; atoms C 6, H 12, O 6 on both sides ✅.
2. Sucrose = **342** g/mol. Moles = 17.1 ÷ 342 = **0.05 mol**. Carbon = 12 × 0.05 = 0.6 mol × 12 = **7.2 g**. Water = 11 × 0.05 = 0.55 mol × 18 = **9.9 g**. Check: 7.2 + 9.9 = 17.1 g ✅.
3. C₆H₁₀O₅ = 72 + 10 + 80 = **162** g/mol. Moles = 32.4 ÷ 162 = **0.2 mol**. Carbon = 6 × 0.2 = 1.2 mol × 12 = **14.4 g**. Water = 5 × 0.2 = 1.0 mol × 18 = **18.0 g**. Check: 14.4 + 18.0 = 32.4 g ✅; atoms C 6, H 10, O 5 on both sides ✅.
4. CuSO₄·5H₂O = **249.5** g/mol. Moles = 7.485 ÷ 249.5 = **0.03 mol**. Water removed = 5 × 0.03 = 0.15 mol × 18 = **2.7 g**. Anhydrous CuSO₄ = 0.03 × 159.5 = **4.785 g**, colour **white**. Check: 2.7 + 4.785 = 7.485 g ✅.
5. Cu = 12.7 ÷ 63.5 = **0.2 mol**. Ratio 1 : 2 : 1 : 1 gives CuSO₄ = 0.2 × 159.5 = **31.9 g**; SO₂ = 0.2 × 22.4 = **4.48 L**; H₂SO₄ = 0.4 mol × 98 = **39.2 g**. It must be done in a fume cupboard because **SO₂ is a toxic, choking gas that irritates the eyes and throat**. Check: 12.7 + 39.2 = 51.9 g = 31.9 + (0.2 × 64 = 12.8) + (0.4 × 18 = 7.2) ✅.
6. C = 0.6 ÷ 12 = **0.05 mol**. CO₂ = 0.05 mol and SO₂ = 0.1 mol, giving a total of **0.15 mol × 22.4 = 3.36 L** at STP. Acid used = 0.1 mol × 98 = **9.8 g**. Check: 0.6 + 9.8 = 10.4 g = CO₂ (2.2 g) + SO₂ (6.4 g) + H₂O (1.8 g) ✅.
7. MgSO₄ = 24 + 32 + 64 = **120** g/mol. Moles = 6.0 ÷ 120 = **0.05 mol**. Water absorbed = 7 × 0.05 = 0.35 mol × 18 = **6.3 g**. Hydrated salt = 6.0 + 6.3 = **12.3 g**. Check: MgSO₄·7H₂O = 120 + 126 = 246; 0.05 × 246 = 12.3 g ✅.
8. (a) **False** ❌: dilute acid does not react with copper, whereas hot concentrated acid oxidises it. (b) **False** ❌: there is no flame; concentrated acid **strips water out of the sugar**, leaving carbon. (c) **False** ❌: NH₃ is a base and reacts with the acid; CaO is used instead. (d) **True** ✅: 2 : 1 (22 : 11 in C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁). (e) **True** ✅.
9. (a) **Dehydrating agent** (removes water of crystallisation); (b) **oxidising agent** (hot acid oxidises Cu and releases SO₂); (c) **dehydrating agent** (strips H and O as water from cellulose); (d) **drying agent**.
10. Copper sits **below hydrogen** in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace H₂ from H₃O⁺ ions in dilute acid. Hot concentrated H₂SO₄ acts as an **oxidising agent**, oxidising Cu to CuSO₄ while being reduced to **SO₂** gas. Had copper behaved like zinc with dilute acid, **H₂** gas would have been formed.
11. Even though the final mixture has the same composition, **where the heat is released** during mixing is completely different. Concentrated acid is much denser than water. Pouring water onto acid leaves a **thin floating layer of water on top** where intense heat concentrates, flashing the water into steam and **spattering boiling acid**. Pouring dense acid slowly into a large volume of water while stirring lets the acid sink and disperses the heat safely throughout the water.
12. Examples: "Danger / Corrosive", skull-and-crossbones symbol, "Keep out of reach of children". Children must never open or mix them because they are **corrosive and toxic** (causing severe burns to eyes, skin, and lungs), and mixing cleaners or bleach can release deadly gases.
13. **Hygroscopic / drying property of concentrated H₂SO₄**: it absorbs water vapour from the surrounding air, increasing the total mass and diluting the acid so it becomes less viscous. The bottle should have been kept **tightly stoppered**.
