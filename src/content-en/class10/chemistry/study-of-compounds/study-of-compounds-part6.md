# Topic 6: Putting It All Together: Practice and Chapter Summary

Across Topics 1 to 5, we met four major industrial compounds: **HCl, ammonia, nitric acid, and sulphuric acid**. Each had its own preparation, its own chemical personality, and its own safety rules. Now comes the most rewarding step: placing all four **side by side** and applying what we have learned to solve new problems. Today we will build a master comparison table, learn a simple two-question decision tree for collecting and drying any gas, solve "Which compound is it?" detective puzzles, and catch four subtle misconceptions that haven't appeared yet.

## "Name That Compound": The School Science Quiz

Mr. Park sat in the auditorium watching his niece Maya compete in her school's annual science quiz bowl, **"Name That Compound"**. On stage, the quizmaster read out clue cards one by one, and each team had to identify the mystery substance from its chemical clues alone.

First card: "A colourless gas with a sharp, pungent odour that turns moist red litmus paper blue." Maya's team hit the buzzer immediately: "Ammonia!" Second card: "A colourless gas that fumes in moist air and gives a curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ solution." Another quick point: "Hydrogen chloride!" On the third card, however, the team hesitated: "A colourless gas with the choking smell of a struck match, denser than air and very soluble in water." Maya whispered to her teammates, "I think it's SO₂—how do we lock it in for sure?"

On the walk home after the quiz, Mr. Park said, "Maya, a chemistry quiz isn't just about memorising isolated facts. Behind every clue is a **why**. How is the gas prepared, how is it collected, how is it dried, and what does it react with? Ask those four questions together and you can identify any gas in the syllabus." Maya grinned. "And what if someone picks the wrong drying agent and ruins the gas?" Her uncle laughed. "That is one of the best traps in today's lesson—let's dive right in."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Drying agent (desiccant)** | A substance that absorbs moisture (water vapour) from a gas without reacting with it | conc. H₂SO₄, quicklime (CaO) |
| **Upward displacement of air** | Collecting a denser-than-air gas in an upright jar from the bottom up, pushing air out the top | HCl, Cl₂, SO₂ |
| **Downward displacement of air** | Collecting a lighter-than-air gas in an inverted jar from the top down, pushing air out the bottom | NH₃ |
| **Vapour density (VD)** | How many times as dense a gas is compared to an equal volume of hydrogen; VD = M ÷ 2 (Chapter 5) | VD of HCl = 18.25 |
| **Gas wash bottle** | A bottle fitted with a two-hole stopper through which a gas bubbles to be dried or washed | wash bottle holding conc. H₂SO₄ |
| **Suck-back** | Water rushing backward up a delivery tube when a soluble gas dissolves rapidly or a hot flask cools | dissolving HCl or NH₃ in water |
| **Catalyst** | A substance that speeds up a chemical reaction without being consumed | V₂O₅, Fe, Pt-Rh |
| **Clue card** | A set of diagnostic observations—colour, odour, fumes, litmus, precipitate—that uniquely identify a substance | "Name That Compound" quiz |

## Safety First (For All Substances in This Topic)

- Every gas, acid, and reaction in this topic is **strictly for reading** or **for observation as a teacher demo** inside a school fume cupboard with safety goggles and gloves. No student ever prepares, touches, sniffs, or tests these chemicals.
- **Chlorine (Cl₂), SO₂, H₂S, NO₂, HCl gas, and ammonia gas are all toxic**: they irritate or damage the eyes, nose, throat, and lungs. Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) is especially dangerous even at low concentrations.
- **Concentrated H₂SO₄ and concentrated HNO₃** cause severe chemical burns to skin: never touch them.
- At home: acidic toilet cleaners, ammonia cleaners, and bleach are **for adult use only** and must **never be mixed together**, as mixing releases deadly gases. Read labels only with adult supervision; never open the containers.

## Part A: All Four Compounds in One Master Table

Our first table compares **laboratory preparation, collection, and drying** across Topics 1 to 4 (all strictly teacher demos or for reading):

| Compound | How Prepared in the Lab | How Collected | How Dried |
|---|---|---|---|

| **NH₃ gas** | 2NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂ → CaCl₂ + 2H₂O + 2NH₃; test tube mouth tilted slightly downward | Downward displacement of air (lighter than air: VD 8.5); never over water (extremely soluble) | Over quicklime (CaO); never conc. H₂SO₄ or anhydrous CaCl₂ |
| **HNO₃** | KNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ → KHSO₄ + HNO₃, below ~200 °C, in an all-glass retort | Liquid: distils over from the retort into a water-cooled receiver | Liquid (not dried like a gas) |
| **H₂SO₄** | Not prepared in the lab; manufactured industrially by the Contact process | Dense oily liquid | It is itself a powerful drying agent |

Our second table compares **identification clues, hazards, and industrial manufacture** across Topics 1 to 5:

| Compound | Diagnostic Clues | Key Hazards | Industrial Process |
|---|---|---|---|
| **HCl** | Colourless, misty fumes in moist air, turns moist blue litmus red, dense white fumes with NH₃, curdy white ppt with acidified AgNO₃ (Chapter 8) | Gas chokes throat and eyes; conc. acid burns skin | Direct synthesis: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl |
| **NH₃** | Sharp pungent odour, turns moist red litmus blue, dense white fumes with HCl | Irritates eyes, nose, throat; never mix cleaner with bleach | Haber process: N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ |
| **HNO₃** | Yellow-brown fuming liquid (dissolved NO₂), gives brown NO₂ with Cu, stains skin yellow | Corrosive; NO and NO₂ are toxic and cause acid rain | Ostwald process (from NH₃) |
| **conc. H₂SO₄** | Dense syrupy liquid; chars white sugar black; turns blue CuSO₄·5H₂O white | Severe burns; never pour water into acid | Contact process (from S or FeS₂) |

## Part B: A Simple Decision Tree for Collecting and Drying Gases

To figure out how to collect any gas in the laboratory, just ask **two questions**: (1) Does it dissolve in or react with water? (2) Is it denser than air? Recall from Chapter 5 that the vapour density (VD) of air is about **14.5**. Any gas with **VD > 14.5** is denser than air; any gas with **VD < 14.5** is lighter than air.

```
   Gas generated in a teacher demonstration
        :
        v
   Does it dissolve in or react with water?
        :                                      :
     No :                                  Yes :
        v                                      v
   Collect over water                 Is it denser than air? (VD > 14.5)
   (e.g., H₂ in Class 9)                       :                    :
                                         Yes   :                    : No
                                               v                    v
                                  Upward displacement     Downward displacement
                                  of air: upright jar,    of air: inverted jar,
                                  tube to bottom of jar   tube to top of jar
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Decision tree for collecting a gas: first check water solubility/reactivity, then compare its vapour density against air (VD ≈ 14.5). Any gas that is very soluble in water is never collected over water.

```
   (a) Denser-than-air gas: upright jar         (b) Lighter-than-air gas: inverted jar

       gas enters (from generator)                  ______________
             :                                     :      .       :  <- closed top of jar
             :  <- delivery tube                   :      :       :     (light gas collects here)
        air  :        air                          :      :       :
         ^   :         ^                           :      :       :
         :   :         :                           :      :       :
        :    :       :                             :      :       :
        :    :       :  <- open mouth at top       :      :       :
        :    :       :                             :______:_______:
        :    :       :                              ^     ^     ^
        : gas fills  :                        air pushed        delivery tube
        :............:                        down & out        (reaches the top)
```

> **Figure 6.2** – (a) Denser-than-air gas (HCl, Cl₂, SO₂): the delivery tube reaches the bottom of the upright jar, filling it from the bottom up while pushing lighter air out the top. (b) Lighter-than-air gas (NH₃): the jar is held upside down with the delivery tube reaching the top, so the light gas collects at the top and pushes heavier air out the open bottom mouth.

### Drying Gases: Match the Chemical Personality

A drying agent must absorb moisture without reacting with the gas itself. From Chapter 1, we know that **acids react with bases**. Therefore, most acidic gases (such as HCl, Cl₂, and SO₂) are dried by bubbling them through acidic **conc. H₂SO₄**, whereas basic **NH₃** is dried over basic **quicklime (CaO)**. Inside a wash bottle, the gas always enters through the long submerged tube and exits through the short tube above the liquid:

```
         moist gas in                   dry gas out
               :                              ^
               :                              :
         .-----:----- two-hole stopper -------:-----.
         :     :                              :     :  <- short tube: ends just
         :     :                                    :     below the stopper
         :     :                                    :
         :~~~~~:~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~:  <- level of conc. H₂SO₄
         :     :   o  o                             :
         :     :  (bubbles)                         :
         :     :                                    :
         :__________________________________________:
          gas wash bottle (long inlet tube submerged in acid)
```

> **Figure 6.3** – A gas wash bottle: moist gas enters down the long tube and bubbles through concentrated H₂SO₄, leaving its moisture behind and exiting dry through the short outlet tube above the liquid surface.

### Example 1. Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S): How to collect and dry it?

**Problem:** Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) is a toxic gas with a rotten-egg smell that is moderately soluble in water. In a fume-cupboard demonstration, find its vapour density, state how it should be collected, and explain whether conc. H₂SO₄ can be used to dry it. (H = 1, S = 32.)

**Step 1 (molar mass):** H₂S = 2 × 1 + 32 = **34** g/mol. **Step 2 (VD):** 34 ÷ 2 = **17**.
**Step 3 (collection):** Since 17 > 14.5, H₂S is denser than air, so it is collected by **upward displacement of air**.
**Step 4 (drying):** Even though H₂S is weakly acidic, it **cannot** be dried with conc. H₂SO₄—because conc. H₂SO₄ is an oxidising agent that oxidises H₂S to yellow sulphur! Another non-reactive drying agent must be used instead.

**Check:** 17 > 14.5 ✅ (denser than air, upright jar). We will examine why conc. H₂SO₄ oxidises H₂S in Common Pitfalls below.

### Example 2. Sulphur dioxide (SO₂): Collection and drying

**Problem:** SO₂ is a colourless, toxic gas that is very soluble in water. Determine how it is collected and dried in the laboratory. (S = 32, O = 16.)

Molar mass M = 32 + 32 = **64** g/mol, so VD = **32** > 14.5 (more than twice as dense as air). Because it is very soluble in water, it cannot be collected over water; instead, it is collected by **upward displacement of air**. Because SO₂ is an acidic gas that is not oxidised further by conc. H₂SO₄, it is dried by bubbling through **conc. H₂SO₄** (never quicklime CaO, which is basic and would absorb acidic SO₂).

**Check:** VD 32 > 14.5 ✅, and acidic SO₂ pairs safely with acidic conc. H₂SO₄ ✅.

## Part C: "Which Compound Is It?" Puzzles

### Example 3. Identifying compounds from clue cards

**Problem:** Match each of the four clue cards below to one of our chapter's compounds:

- **Card A:** A yellowish-brown fuming liquid that reacts with copper to release dense reddish-brown fumes.
- **Card B:** A dense, syrupy liquid that chars white table sugar into a steaming black mass.
- **Card C:** A colourless gas, lighter than air (VD 8.5), that produces dense white fumes when held near HCl.
- **Card D:** A colourless gas (VD 18.25) that fumes in moist air and gives a curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃.

**Solution:**
- **Card A = HNO₃** (yellow tint from dissolved NO₂; Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O).
- **Card B = conc. H₂SO₄** (powerful dehydrating agent).
- **Card C = NH₃** (reacts with HCl via NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl to form white fumes).
- **Card D = HCl**.

**Check:** Each card uniquely identifies one of the four flagship compounds ✅. Atom balance for Card A: Cu 1, H 4 = 4, N 4 = 2 + 2, O 12 = 6 + 4 + 2 ✅.

### Example 4. Three unlabelled gas jars

**Problem:** Three gas jars (1, 2, and 3) contain HCl, NH₃, and SO₂ in some order. Jar 1 turns moist blue litmus red and gives a curdy white precipitate with acidified AgNO₃ solution. Jar 2 turns moist red litmus blue. Jar 3 turns moist blue litmus red, gives no precipitate with AgNO₃, and has the choking smell of burning sulphur. Identify the gas in each jar.

**Solution:** Only **NH₃** is basic and turns moist red litmus blue, so **Jar 2 = NH₃**. Between the two acidic gases, only HCl contains Cl⁻ (forming curdy white AgCl with AgNO₃), so **Jar 1 = HCl**. That leaves **Jar 3 = SO₂**.

**Check:** Three jars, three distinct gases, each matched unambiguously ✅.

## Part D: Industrial Map and Stoichiometry

```
   N₂ from air + H₂ : N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃                 (Fe catalyst, ~200 atm, ~450 °C)
        ammonia ---(Pt-Rh, ~800 °C)--> NO ---(O₂)--> NO₂ ---(O₂, H₂O)--> HNO₃
                       4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O   2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂   4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃

   Sulphur S ---(O₂)--> SO₂ ---(O₂, V₂O₅, ~450 °C)--> SO₃ ---(conc. H₂SO₄)--> oleum ---(H₂O)--> H₂SO₄

   Brine electrolysis (chlor-alkali) --> H₂ and Cl₂ ---> H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Master industrial map of all four compounds: the Haber and Ostwald processes linking N₂ to NH₃ and HNO₃; the Contact process turning S into H₂SO₄ via oleum; and direct synthesis of HCl from chlor-alkali H₂ and Cl₂ (Chapter 4).

### Example 5. From NH₃ to HNO₃ and ammonium nitrate fertiliser

**Problem:** In an Ostwald plant, 0.85 g of NH₃ is converted completely into HNO₃. If an equal amount (0.85 g) of fresh NH₃ is then reacted with that HNO₃ to make ammonium nitrate fertiliser (NH₄NO₃), what mass of HNO₃ was formed in the first stage, and what mass of NH₄NO₃ is produced at the end? (H = 1, N = 14, O = 16.)

**Step 1:** Combining 4NH₃ + 5O₂ → 4NO + 6H₂O, 2NO + O₂ → 2NO₂, and 4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃ shows that 4 moles of NH₃ yield 4 moles of HNO₃—a **1 : 1** mole ratio.
**Step 2:** Molar mass of NH₃ = 17 g/mol, so moles = 0.85 ÷ 17 = **0.05 mol**. Molar mass of HNO₃ = 1 + 14 + 48 = 63 g/mol, so mass of HNO₃ = 0.05 × 63 = **3.15 g**.
**Step 3:** In NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃ (1 : 1 : 1), 0.05 mol of NH₃ + 0.05 mol of HNO₃ gives 0.05 mol of NH₄NO₃ (molar mass = 14 + 4 + 14 + 48 = 80 g/mol), so mass = 0.05 × 80 = **4.0 g**.

**Check:** Conservation of mass in Step 3: 0.85 g NH₃ + 3.15 g HNO₃ = 4.0 g NH₄NO₃ ✅. Atom count: N 2, H 4, O 3 on both sides ✅.

### Example 6. Direct synthesis of HCl: mass and volume

**Problem:** 7.1 g of Cl₂ reacts completely with excess H₂ according to H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Calculate the volume at STP and the mass of HCl gas formed. (Cl = 35.5, H = 1; molar volume = 22.4 L at STP.)

Molar mass of Cl₂ = 71 g/mol, so moles = 7.1 ÷ 71 = **0.1 mol**. From the 1 : 2 mole ratio (Cl₂ : 2HCl), HCl formed = **0.2 mol**. Volume at STP = 0.2 × 22.4 = **4.48 L**. Mass = 0.2 × 36.5 = **7.3 g**.

**Check:** Mass of Cl₂ (7.1 g) + mass of H₂ (0.1 mol × 2 = 0.2 g) = 7.3 g of HCl ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Every acidic gas can be dried with concentrated H₂SO₄."**
While acidic gases do not undergo an acid-base reaction with conc. H₂SO₄, remember from Topic 5 that concentrated H₂SO₄ is also an **oxidising agent**! Let's compare what happens when three acidic gases pass through a conc. H₂SO₄ wash bottle (for reading only):

| Acidic Gas | What Happens Inside a Conc. H₂SO₄ Wash Bottle |
|---|---|
| HCl | Only moisture is absorbed; dry HCl gas exits cleanly |
| SO₂ | Only moisture is absorbed; dry SO₂ gas exits cleanly |
| H₂S | Conc. H₂SO₄ oxidises H₂S to yellow solid sulphur; the gas is destroyed! |

**Rule to remember:** Before choosing conc. H₂SO₄ as a drying agent, check both acid-base reactivity AND redox reactivity. Easily oxidised gases like **H₂S, HBr, and HI** cannot be dried with concentrated H₂SO₄ because it oxidises them.

**Mistake 2: "A given metal reacts the same way with every acid."**
Compare the behaviour of zinc and copper across different acids (for reading only):

| Metal + Acid | What Happens |
|---|---|
| Zn + dilute HCl | Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ (releases hydrogen gas) |
| Cu + dilute HCl | No reaction at all (Cu is below H in the reactivity series; Class 9) |
| Cu + conc. HNO₃ | Cu + 4HNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2NO₂ + 2H₂O (releases brown NO₂, never H₂) |
| Cu + hot conc. H₂SO₄ | Cu + 2H₂SO₄ → CuSO₄ + SO₂ + 2H₂O (releases pungent SO₂, never H₂) |

Copper is the exact same metal in the last three rows, yet changing the acid changes the outcome from no reaction, to NO₂, to SO₂! **The gas produced depends on both the metal and the specific acid (and its concentration).**

**Mistake 3: "A gas that dissolves moderately in water can still be collected over water."**
Consider SO₂, which dissolves at roughly 40 volumes of gas per 1 volume of water. A 5 L pneumatic trough of water can swallow up **5 × 40 = 200 L** of SO₂! A 1 L gas jar would never fill—all the gas would dissolve into the trough water, turning it acidic and risking suck-back. Even Cl₂ dissolves appreciably in water. **Rule to remember:** Soluble gases are always collected by displacement of air; collection over water is reserved for nearly insoluble gases like H₂ or O₂.

**Mistake 4: "Adding a catalyst increases the total mass of product formed."**
In the Contact process (2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃), 128 g of SO₂ (2 mol) can theoretically form at most 160 g of SO₃ (2 mol). If two identical reactors run at the same temperature and pressure—Reactor A with 5 g of V₂O₅ catalyst and Reactor B without it—Reactor A reaches equilibrium **much faster**, but the **equilibrium fraction of SO₃ is identical** in both, and the 5 g of V₂O₅ weighs **5 g** at the end. A catalyst only increases the **rate** of reaction, never the equilibrium yield.

## Quick Recap

- Four flagship compounds: **HCl** (NaCl + conc. H₂SO₄; industrial H₂ + Cl₂), **NH₃** (NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂; industrial Haber process), **HNO₃** (KNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄; industrial Ostwald process), and **H₂SO₄** (industrial Contact process).
- Gas collection decision tree: first check water solubility; if soluble, compare vapour density to air (VD ≈ 14.5). Denser (VD > 14.5) → **upward displacement of air**; lighter (VD < 14.5) → **downward displacement of air**.
- Drying gases: acidic gases like HCl, Cl₂, and SO₂ use **conc. H₂SO₄** (but never H₂S, HBr, or HI, which get oxidised); basic NH₃ uses **quicklime (CaO)**.
- Diagnostic clues: colour, odour, fumes with NH₃/HCl, moist litmus, and AgNO₃/BaCl₂ precipitate tests (Chapter 8).
- Metal + acid outcome depends on both the metal and whether the acid is an oxidising acid.
- A catalyst speeds up the reaction without changing the equilibrium yield or being consumed.

### Answers

1. Molar mass of Cl₂ = 71, so VD = **35.5** > 14.5 (denser than air). It is collected by **upward displacement of air** and dried with **conc. H₂SO₄** (since Cl₂ is acidic and not oxidised by it). It is not collected over water because Cl₂ dissolves appreciably in water and reacts slightly with it.
2. HBr: molar mass = 80 + 1 = 81, so VD = **40.5**. HI: molar mass = 127 + 1 = 128, so VD = **64**. **Conc. H₂SO₄ is wrong** for drying them because it oxidises both gases. Since both have VD > 14.5, both are collected by **upward displacement of air**. Check: both VDs > 14.5 ✅.
3. P = **NO₂**, Q = **H₂S**, R = **Cl₂**, S = **SO₂**. **H₂S** cannot be dried with conc. H₂SO₄ because the acid oxidises it to sulphur.
4. 4.48 ÷ 22.4 = 0.2 mol of NO. Ratio 2 : 1 : 2 gives O₂ = 0.1 mol = **2.24 L**, and NO₂ = 0.2 mol: mass = 0.2 × 46 = **9.2 g**, volume = 0.2 × 22.4 = **4.48 L**. Check: 4.48 + 2.24 = 6.72 L of reactants become 4.48 L of product (3 → 2 volume ratio), and N, O balance ✅.
5. 13.8 ÷ 46 = **0.3 mol**. Molecules = 0.3 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.8066 × 10²³**. Volume at STP = 0.3 × 22.4 = **6.72 L**.
6. Molar mass of urea = 12 + 16 + 2 × (14 + 2) = **60** g/mol. Moles = 12 ÷ 60 = **0.2 mol**. Molecules = 0.2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **1.2044 × 10²³**. Since 1 molecule of CO(NH₂)₂ contains 8 atoms (1 C, 1 O, 2 N, 4 H), total atoms = 0.2 × 8 = **1.6 mol of atoms** = 1.6 × 6.022 × 10²³ ≈ **9.64 × 10²³ atoms**.
7. It tests for the **NH₄⁺ (ammonium) ion**: warming with NaOH releases **NH₃ gas**, which turns moist red litmus paper **blue** (Chapter 8). Safety rule: performed strictly as a teacher demo inside a fume cupboard with goggles and gloves; students never handle hot NaOH or inhale NH₃.
8. Vapour densities in increasing order: H₂S (17) < NO₂ (23) < SO₂ (32) < Cl₂ (35.5) < HBr (40.5) < HI (64). **None of them** is collected by downward displacement of air, because all six have VD > 14.5 (all are denser than air and use upward displacement).
9. (a) **False** ❌: H₂S, HBr, and HI are oxidised by conc. H₂SO₄. (b) **False** ❌: a catalyst only increases the rate of reaction. (c) **False** ❌: NO₂ reacts with water (4NO₂ + O₂ + 2H₂O → 4HNO₃). (d) **False** ❌: Cl₂ has VD 35.5 and is much denser than air. (e) **False** ❌: HI is oxidised by conc. H₂SO₄.
10. The **rate (speed)** of the reaction changed (from 2 hours down to 10 minutes), whereas the **yield of product (6.9 g)** and the mass of the catalyst did not change. Moles of NO₂ = 6.9 ÷ 46 = **0.15 mol**.
11. Both SO₂ (VD = 32) and H₂S (VD = 17) are denser than air (VD ≈ 14.5), so leaked gas **sinks and accumulates near the floor** rather than rising to the ceiling. Low-level exhaust ventilation and fume cupboards are therefore essential.
12. (i) Making diagnostic clue cards (listing colour, odour, litmus change, and precipitate) to quiz a classmate. (ii) Drawing the gas-collection and drying decision tree from memory. **No chemicals are used** because these gases and concentrated acids are toxic and corrosive, and can only be handled by trained adults in a laboratory fume cupboard.

| Topic | Core Focus | One-Line Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | HCl gas and hydrochloric acid | Prepared from NaCl + conc. H₂SO₄ and collected by upward displacement; dry HCl is covalent, becoming an acid only in water |
| 2 | Ammonia: from lab to farm | Prepared from NH₄Cl + Ca(OH)₂, dried over CaO, collected by downward displacement; made industrially via the Haber process for fertilisers |
