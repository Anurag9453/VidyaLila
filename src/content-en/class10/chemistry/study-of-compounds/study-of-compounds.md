# Topic 1: Hydrogen Chloride and Hydrochloric Acid

In the previous chapter, we identified unknown salts using chemical tests, and HCl appeared again and again as a key laboratory acid. Now, in Chapter 9, we will explore the four powerhouse industrial chemicals manufactured in millions of tonnes worldwide: **HCl, ammonia, nitric acid, and sulphuric acid**. For each one, we ask three core questions: how is it prepared in the lab, how is it manufactured in industry, and what makes its chemistry so distinctive? Everything in this chapter is **strictly for reading or for watching as a teacher demonstration**: students never prepare or handle these substances themselves. Let's start with HCl.

## A Bottle of Toilet Cleaner and Stomach Acid

Dr. Chen was standing in the bathroom at home with his niece Mia. On the high shelf sat a bottle of heavy-duty toilet bowl cleaner. Mia noticed two hazard symbols printed on the label: one showing a skull and crossbones, and another showing droplets of liquid eating into a hand and a bar of metal. Underneath, in bold letters, it read: "For adult use only."

"Uncle, what do those symbols mean?" Mia asked. Without taking the bottle off the shelf, Dr. Chen read the label aloud. "The first symbol means toxic or seriously harmful. The second means **corrosive**—capable of burning skin, eyes, and metal. It also says: 'Keep out of reach of children' and 'Never mix with bleach or any other household cleaner.'" "And what's inside it?" "The label names an acid: **hydrochloric acid**. We aren't going to open this bottle, and you should never open it either."

Mia thought for a moment, then frowned. "Wait, Uncle—we learned in biology class that our own stomach contains hydrochloric acid! The same chemical that scrubs a toilet bowl is inside me right now? Why doesn't it burn through my stomach?" Dr. Chen smiled. "That is a brilliant question. There are two parts to the answer: first, the acid in this bottle is dozens of times more **concentrated** than stomach acid, and second, your stomach wall is lined with a protective coat of mucus. But there's another fascinating twist: what chemists call 'HCl' is both a **gas** and an **aqueous solution**. Let's look at the full story of both."

## Safety First

- **HCl gas** severely irritates the throat, nose, and eyes, and causes choking. The white "fumes" rising from concentrated hydrochloric acid contain this same gas.
- **Concentrated H₂SO₄** (used to generate HCl in the lab) is intensely corrosive and destroys skin, fabric, and paper.
- All of these procedures are carried out **in a school lab by a trained teacher inside a fume cupboard, wearing safety goggles and gloves**. Every preparation and demonstration in this topic is **for reading only or for observation as a teacher demo**. No student ever prepares, sniffs, or touches these chemicals.
- **Never mix toilet cleaner or any acidic cleaner with bleach**: doing so releases deadly chlorine gas. Only adults should handle such bottles.
- If any acid or gas ever contacts skin or eyes, inform a teacher or adult immediately. Do not attempt self-treatment—seek medical attention right away.

## Part A: HCl Gas and Hydrochloric Acid: Two Forms of One Substance

**Hydrogen chloride** is a gas whose molecule consists of one H atom and one Cl atom joined by a covalent bond: H–Cl. When this gas dissolves in water, it forms **hydrochloric acid**. In Class 9, we saw the synthesis H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl ([hydrogen-part4.md](../../../class9/chemistry/hydrogen/hydrogen-part4.md): "dissolves in water to form hydrochloric acid"). Let's examine both forms side by side.

**Properties of the gas (for reading only):**
- **Colourless**, with a **sharp, choking (pungent)** odour (even a teacher never sniffs it directly).
- **Denser than air:** its vapour density is 18.25, compared to about 14.5 for air (Chapter 5, Topic 2).
- **Extremely soluble in water:** about 450 litres of HCl gas can dissolve in just 1 litre of water.
- **Misty fumes in moist air:** when HCl gas escapes into open air, it dissolves in atmospheric moisture to form microscopic droplets of hydrochloric acid, which appear as a white mist. That mist is not smoke—it is tiny liquid droplets of acid.
- **Turns moist blue litmus red** (but has no effect on dry litmus—we will see why in Common Pitfalls).

**Properties of the aqueous solution:** As we saw in Chapter 1, hydrochloric acid is a **strong acid**: in water, it ionises almost completely into H₃O⁺ and Cl⁻ ions. Because of these H₃O⁺ ions, it shows all the classic reactions of an acid: releasing H₂ with active metals (Chapter 2), releasing CO₂ with carbonates and bicarbonates (Chapter 1), and forming salt and water with bases. We have already covered those reactions, so we won't repeat them here. Among the metal chlorides formed is **ZnCl₂**, which we need to know by name only.

Commercial **concentrated** hydrochloric acid is about 36 percent HCl by mass and gives off misty white fumes when the bottle is opened.

### Example 1. How much denser is HCl than air?

**Problem:** Calculate the vapour density of HCl and find the ratio of its density to that of air. (H = 1, Cl = 35.5; average molar mass of air = 29.)

**Step 1:** Molar mass of HCl = 1 + 35.5 = **36.5** g/mol.
**Step 2:** Vapour density = molar mass ÷ 2 = 36.5 ÷ 2 = **18.25**. Vapour density of air = 29 ÷ 2 = **14.5** (both appeared in the VD table in Chapter 5; the next step is new).
**Step 3:** Ratio of masses of equal volumes at the same T and P = 36.5 ÷ 29 ≈ **1.26**. Thus, HCl gas is about 1.26 times as dense as air.

**Check:** 18.25 ÷ 14.5 = 1.26 as well ✅ (both routes give the exact same answer).

## Part B: Preparing HCl Gas in the Lab (For Reading Only)

**Raw materials:** common salt (**NaCl**) and **concentrated H₂SO₄**. Concentrated H₂SO₄ is a **non-volatile acid**: it has a very high boiling point, so it does not boil away when heated. When it is warmed with NaCl, the much more volatile **HCl** escapes as a gas.

**With gentle heating (below about 200 °C):** NaCl + H₂SO₄ (concentrated) → NaHSO₄ + HCl

**At higher temperatures, a second step occurs:** NaHSO₄ + NaCl → Na₂SO₄ + HCl

Adding the two steps gives the **overall reaction:** 2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl

In the laboratory, gentle heating is normally used so that mainly the first step takes place. (In the Chapter 1 teacher demo, dry HCl gas was also made from NaCl and conc. H₂SO₄, where we wrote the overall equation directly.)

```
          \       /     <-- thistle funnel (conc. H₂SO₄ is poured in here)
           \     /
            \   /
             : :
       ===cork===: :===cork===.________________  delivery tube: HCl gas travels
       :         : :          :                   through here to the next vessel
       :         : :          :
      /          : :           \
     :           : :            :
     :   ~~~~~~~~: :~~~~~~~~~   :  <-- NaCl + conc. H₂SO₄ (funnel stem dips below acid level)
     :      (NaCl + acid)       :
      \                        /
       \______________________/
            round-bottom flask
                  ^
                  :  gentle heat (burner)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – HCl generator: solid NaCl sits in the flask while conc. H₂SO₄ is added through the thistle funnel (the bottom tip of the funnel stem stays submerged below the liquid surface so gas cannot escape back up the funnel). A delivery tube passes through the cork to carry the gas forward under gentle heating. Because the delivery tube is open at the far end, the apparatus is never sealed.

```
delivery tube       short tube: dry gas exits
from flask          here
      :      +-------------------------------------+
      :      :                                     :
   ===:=cork=:===                                  :            <-- open mouth of jar:
   :  :         :                                  :                lighter air escapes here
   :  :         :                       :          :          :
   :  :         :                       :          :          :
   :~~:~~~~~~~~~:                       :          : tube goes:
   :  :         :                       :HCl gas   : to bottom:
   :  :conc.    :                       :fills jar : of jar   :
   :  :H₂SO₄    :                       :from the  :          :
   :  :         :                       :bottom up :          :
   \____________/                       :          :          :
                                        :          :          :
  wash bottle                           :                     :
  (for drying the gas)                  :                     :
                                        \_____________________/
                                     gas jar (standing upright)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Drying and collecting the gas. The wash bottle holds conc. H₂SO₄: moist gas enters through the **long tube** (submerged in the acid), bubbles through the acid to dry, and exits through the **short tube** (ending just below the cork). From there, the delivery tube reaches the **bottom** of the upright gas jar: since HCl is denser than air, it fills the jar from the bottom up while pushing lighter air out the open mouth. The entire setup stays inside a fume cupboard.

**Why each design choice matters:**
- **Why dry the gas?** Gas leaving the flask carries traces of moisture. **Concentrated H₂SO₄** absorbs water vapour without reacting with HCl, making it the ideal drying agent in the wash bottle. (In Chapter 1, the teacher also used a guard tube packed with **anhydrous CaCl₂**—since it also does not react with HCl, it works too.)
- **Why not use dilute H₂SO₄?** Dilute acid contains plenty of water, and because HCl is extraordinarily soluble in water, it would simply stay dissolved instead of escaping as a gas.
- **Why extend the delivery tube to the bottom of the jar?** So that the heavy HCl gas settles at the bottom and smoothly pushes the lighter air upward and out.

### Example 2. Atom count across the three equations

**Problem:** Verify that all three equations are balanced, and show that the overall reaction is the sum of the two steps.

Step 1: NaCl + H₂SO₄ → NaHSO₄ + HCl. Step 2: NaHSO₄ + NaCl → Na₂SO₄ + HCl.
Add both sides: NaCl + H₂SO₄ + NaHSO₄ + NaCl → NaHSO₄ + HCl + Na₂SO₄ + HCl. Since NaHSO₄ appears once on each side as an intermediate, it cancels out, leaving **2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl**.

**Check:** Step 1: Na 1 = 1, Cl 1 = 1, H 2 = 1 + 1, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4 ✅. Step 2: Na 2 = 2, H 1 = 1, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4, Cl 1 = 1 ✅. Overall: Na 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2, H 2 = 2, S 1 = 1, O 4 = 4 ✅.

### Example 3. How much HCl from 58.5 g of NaCl?

**Problem:** In a demonstration, a teacher gently warms 58.5 g of NaCl with the exact stoichiometric amount of conc. H₂SO₄ required for the first step. Find the mass and STP volume of HCl produced, and the mass of H₂SO₄ consumed. (Na = 23, S = 32, O = 16.)

**Step 1 (moles):** Molar mass of NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 g/mol, so moles = 58.5 ÷ 58.5 = **1 mol**.
**Step 2 (mole ratio):** From NaCl : H₂SO₄ : HCl = 1 : 1 : 1, we get HCl = **1 mol** and H₂SO₄ = **1 mol**.
**Step 3 (mass):** Mass of HCl = 1 × 36.5 = **36.5 g**. Molar mass of H₂SO₄ = 2 + 32 + 64 = 98 g/mol, so mass = **98 g**.
**Step 4 (volume):** At STP, 1 mol of any gas occupies 22.4 L, so volume of HCl = **22.4 L** (Chapter 5, Topic 5).

**Check:** Conservation of mass: reactants = 58.5 + 98 = 156.5 g; products = NaHSO₄ (23 + 1 + 32 + 64 = 120 g) + HCl (36.5 g) = 156.5 g ✅.

## Part C: How to Collect and Dissolve It

The method used to collect any gas in the lab depends on two physical properties: **how dense** it is compared to air, and **how soluble** it is in water.

| Property of the Gas | Collection Method | Applies to HCl? |
|---|---|---|
| **Denser** than air | Upward displacement of air: upright jar, delivery tube to the bottom | **Yes**: HCl is about 1.26 times as dense as air |
| **Lighter** than air | Downward displacement of air: inverted jar, delivery tube to the top | No |
| **Very slightly** soluble in water | Over water (jar inverted in a water trough) | **No**: HCl is extremely soluble in water |

If you tried to collect HCl over water, the gas would dissolve into the water almost instantly and the jar would never fill. That is why HCl is always collected by **upward displacement of air**, and never over water.

### Making Hydrochloric Acid: The Inverted Funnel Arrangement

To dissolve HCl gas in water and make hydrochloric acid, you must never dip a bare delivery tube straight into water. Because HCl dissolves so fast, the pressure inside the tube drops suddenly and water gets sucked backward up the tube into the hot generating flask (**suck-back**), which can crack the hot glass. To prevent this, an **inverted funnel** is attached to the end of the delivery tube:

```
               :   :   <-- HCl gas arrives through rubber tubing
               :   :
              _:___:_
              \     /
               \   /         <-- inverted funnel: narrow stem on top, wide rim below
                \ /
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  <-- water surface: the funnel rim just touches the water
   :                            :
   :     water (in beaker)      :
   :    HCl dissolves here      :
   :____________________________:
              beaker
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Dissolving HCl in water: the wide rim of the inverted funnel is positioned so that it just touches the surface of the water. If water begins to rise inside the funnel, the water level in the beaker drops slightly, leaving an air gap below the rim. Air enters the funnel and the water falls right back down, completely preventing suck-back.

## Part D: The Fountain Experiment (Teacher Demo Only)

A classic teacher demonstration vividly proves just how soluble HCl gas is in water. The teacher fills a **completely dry**, thick-walled round-bottom flask with dry HCl gas (behind a safety screen) and clamps it upside down. Its rubber stopper carries two tubes: a long glass tube ending in a fine jet near the top inside the flask and dipping into a beaker of blue litmus solution below, and a short dropper filled with a little water.

```
            _____________
          /               \
         :          ^      :    <-- jet: upper tip of the long tube (near top of flask)
         :          :      :        where the fountain sprays out
         : dry      :      :
         : HCl gas  :      :
          \         :     /
            \_      :   _/
              :     : :
              : :   : :         <-- inverted flask: bulb on top, neck pointing down
              : :   : :
          ======:===:======     <-- stopper (===) with two holes
                :   :
              (     )
              (water)           <-- dropper: squeezing injects a few drops of water
              (     )
             rubber bulb
                    :
      ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~:~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
      :             :     <-- lower end of long tube submerged in solution
      :             :                         :
      :   water + blue litmus                 :
      :                                       :
      :                                       :
      :_______________________________________:
                      beaker
```

> **Figure 1.4** – The HCl fountain demonstration. The inverted flask holds dry HCl gas. When the teacher squeezes the dropper, a few drops of water enter the flask and dissolve a large volume of HCl gas almost instantly. The pressure inside the flask plummets, so external atmospheric pressure pushes the blue litmus solution up the long tube, spraying into the flask as a dramatic **red** fountain.

**What you observe:** the moment the dropper is squeezed, liquid rushes up the long tube and bursts out of the jet as a fountain, turning **blue litmus red** because aqueous HCl is an acid. The flask must be **completely dry** at the start—if it were damp, the gas would dissolve prematurely before the demonstration even began, and no sharp pressure drop would occur.

### Example 4. How little water is enough?

**Problem:** A dry 500 mL flask is filled with HCl gas. Assuming 1 mL of water can dissolve 450 mL of HCl gas, estimate the minimum volume of water needed to dissolve all the gas in the flask.

**Step 1:** 450 mL of gas requires 1 mL of water.
**Step 2:** Water needed for 500 mL of gas = 500 ÷ 450 ≈ **1.1 mL**.
**Step 3:** That means just a squeeze of the dropper (about 1 mL) is enough to absorb nearly the entire flask of gas, causing an immediate drop in pressure.

**Check:** 1.1 × 450 = 495 mL, which is right around 500 mL ✅.

## Part E: Identifying HCl and the White Fumes with Ammonia

There are three straightforward ways to identify HCl (all performed as teacher demos or studied for reading):

1. **Moist blue litmus turns red.**
2. **Dense white fumes with ammonia:** when a glass rod dipped in conc. HCl is held near a glass rod dipped in conc. aqueous ammonia, thick white fumes appear in the air between them: NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl (tiny suspended solid particles of ammonium chloride).
3. **Curdy white precipitate with silver nitrate:** aqueous HCl contains Cl⁻ ions, which react with AgNO₃ solution to form AgCl: HCl + AgNO₃ → AgCl↓ + HNO₃ (silver nitrate is toxic and stains skin, so this is a teacher demo only; see Chapter 8, Topic 4, where we covered acidifying with dilute HNO₃ and dissolving the precipitate in NH₄OH).

```
      glass rod (conc. HCl)             glass rod (conc. ammonia)
                \                                /
                 \       . o   .  o  .          /
                  \    . white fumes   o  .    /
                   \   (solid NH₄Cl specks)   /
```

> **Figure 1.5** – Bringing two moistened glass rods close together produces dense white fumes in the air between them: gaseous HCl and gaseous NH₃ combine to form microscopic solid particles of NH₄Cl. This is strictly a teacher demo, as both vapours are irritating.

### Example 5. Stoichiometry of HCl and NH₃

**Problem:** 18.25 g of HCl reacts completely with ammonia gas (NH₃ + HCl → NH₄Cl). Calculate the mass of NH₃ consumed and the mass of NH₄Cl formed. (N = 14, H = 1, Cl = 35.5.)

**Step 1:** Moles of HCl = 18.25 ÷ 36.5 = **0.5 mol**. The mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 1.
**Step 2:** Mass of NH₃ = 0.5 × 17 = **8.5 g**. Mass of NH₄Cl (molar mass = 14 + 4 + 35.5 = 53.5 g/mol) = 0.5 × 53.5 = **26.75 g**.

**Check:** 18.25 + 8.5 = 26.75 g ✅ (conservation of mass). Atom count: N 1, H 4, Cl 1 on both sides ✅.

## Part F: How HCl Is Made in Industry

In industry, the cleanest and most direct way to manufacture HCl is **direct combination of hydrogen and chlorine**. In Class 9, we saw H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. And in Chapter 4 (Electrolysis, Topic 5), we learned that the chlor-alkali electrolysis of brine produces **both Cl₂ and H₂** at the same plant. Those two gases are fed into a special burner and reacted directly: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. The resulting HCl gas is then absorbed in water to yield concentrated hydrochloric acid. This process is remarkably economical because both raw materials come straight from the same electrolysis plant.

```
   Brine (concentrated NaCl solution)
        :
        v
   Electrolysis (chlor-alkali plant, Chapter 4)
        :
        +--> Cl₂ (at anode) ---------+
        :                            v
        +--> H₂ (at cathode) --> burned in reactor: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (gas)
        :                            :
        +--> NaOH (separate stream)  v
                            absorbed in water (absorption tower)
                                     :
                                     v
                      concentrated hydrochloric acid (~36%)
```

> **Figure 1.6** – Industrial route to HCl: electrolysis of brine yields Cl₂ and H₂ alongside NaOH; burning H₂ in Cl₂ produces HCl gas, which is absorbed in water to make concentrated hydrochloric acid.

### Example 6. By volume and by mass

**Problem:** (a) If 10 L of H₂ and 10 L of Cl₂ react completely at the same temperature and pressure, what volume of HCl gas is formed? (b) What mass of HCl is formed when 2 g of H₂ burns completely in chlorine? (H = 1, Cl = 35.5.)

**Step 1 (volume):** In H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, the mole ratio is 1 : 1 : 2. At the same T and P, gas volumes are in the same ratio as moles (Chapter 5, Topic 5), so 10 L + 10 L → **20 L of HCl** (total gas volume stays 20 L).
**Step 2 (mass):** 2 g of H₂ = 1 mol. This requires 1 mol of Cl₂ = 71 g, producing 2 mol of HCl = 2 × 36.5 = **73 g**.

**Check:** 2 + 71 = 73 g ✅.

## Part G: Where HCl Is Used

| Application | How It Works |
|---|---|
| **Pickling of metals** | Dissolves oxide scale off iron or steel surfaces before electroplating or galvanising (recall from Chapter 4 that electroplating requires a clean metal surface) |
| **Household descalers & toilet cleaners (dilute)** | Dissolves carbonate limescale deposits; bottles carry adult-only safety warnings |
| **Making metal chlorides** | Reacting the acid with bases or carbonates produces salts such as CaCl₂ and ZnCl₂ (names only) |
| **In the human stomach** | Gastric juice contains about 0.5 percent HCl (pH ~1.5 to 2), which activates digestive enzymes and kills harmful bacteria in food |

### Example 7. How dilute is stomach acid?

**Problem:** Gastric juice contains about 0.5 percent HCl by mass. How many grams of HCl are present in 250 g of gastric juice? Roughly how many times more concentrated is commercial concentrated HCl (36 percent) than stomach acid?

**Step 1:** 0.5 percent means 0.5 g per 100 g, so in 250 g there is 250 × 0.5 ÷ 100 = **1.25 g of HCl**.
**Step 2:** Concentration ratio = 36 ÷ 0.5 = **72 times** as concentrated.

**Check:** 1.25 ÷ 250 × 100 = 0.5 percent ✅. That explains why the cleaner on Mia's bathroom shelf is far more hazardous than stomach juice.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake: "Since HCl is an acid, dry HCl gas will also turn dry blue litmus paper red."**
It is easy to fall into this trap because we call HCl an acid. However, acidic behaviour (caused by H₃O⁺ ions) only appears **in the presence of water**. Let's revisit the Chapter 1 teacher demo through the lens of Chapter 7 bonding (teacher demo, for observation only):

1. **Dry HCl gas + dry blue litmus paper:** no colour change at all. Dry HCl gas consists of covalent H–Cl molecules with no free H⁺ or H₃O⁺ ions.
2. **The same gas + a drop of water on the litmus paper:** the paper turns red immediately. Polar water molecules pull apart the polar covalent H–Cl bond: HCl + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + Cl⁻ (Chapter 7, Topic 2). Once H₃O⁺ ions form, acidic properties appear.
3. **White mist of HCl in open air:** this happens for the exact same reason—the dry gas pulls moisture from the air to form tiny liquid droplets of aqueous hydrochloric acid.

```
   Case 1: dry HCl gas + dry blue litmus     :  stays blue
   Case 2: dry HCl gas + moist blue litmus   :  turns red (water forms H₃O⁺)
   Case 3: aqueous HCl + dry blue litmus     :  turns red (H₃O⁺ is already present)
```

> **Figure 1.7** – Dry HCl gas by itself does not turn dry blue litmus red; acidic behaviour requires water to produce H₃O⁺ ions.

Rule to remember: **HCl only acts as an acid when water is present.** In your stomach, in a toilet cleaner, and inside the fountain flask, water is always there.

## Quick Recap

- **HCl gas** is colourless, pungent, about 1.26 times as dense as air (VD 18.25 vs 14.5 for air), and extremely soluble in water; it forms misty white fumes in moist air.
- **Hydrochloric acid** is an aqueous solution of HCl gas; it is a strong acid (~36 percent in commercial concentrated acid, ~0.5 percent in stomach acid).
- **Lab preparation:** NaCl + H₂SO₄ (conc.) → NaHSO₄ + HCl (gentle heat, below ~200 °C); at higher temperatures, NaHSO₄ + NaCl → Na₂SO₄ + HCl; overall 2NaCl + H₂SO₄ → Na₂SO₄ + 2HCl.
- The gas is dried using **conc. H₂SO₄** and collected by **upward displacement of air** (never over water).
- To dissolve HCl in water, use an **inverted funnel** arrangement to prevent suck-back.
- **Fountain experiment:** squeezing a few drops of water into a dry flask of HCl dissolves the gas, drops the internal pressure, and drives blue litmus solution up the jet as a red fountain.
- **Tests:** turns moist blue litmus red; gives dense white fumes of NH₄Cl with NH₃; gives a curdy white precipitate of AgCl with acidified AgNO₃ (Chapter 8).
- **Industry:** H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (using H₂ and Cl₂ from the chlor-alkali process). **Uses:** pickling metals, descaling/cleaning (dilute), making chlorides, and digestion in the stomach.
- **Watch out:** dry HCl gas does not turn dry blue litmus red—water is required for ionisation.
- All preparations and tests are strictly teacher demos or for reading; never mix acidic cleaners with bleach.

6. A teacher adds acidified AgNO₃ to a solution containing 3.33 g of calcium chloride, CaCl₂ (CaCl₂ + 2AgNO₃ → 2AgCl + Ca(NO₃)₂). Find the mass of AgCl and the mass of AgNO₃ used. Why does calcium not affect the test?
7. Gastric juice contains about 0.5 percent HCl by mass. How much HCl is in 800 g of it? If a cleaner contained 10 times that percentage, what would its percentage be?
8. A dry 250 mL flask is filled with HCl in a fountain demo. If 1 mL of water dissolves about 450 mL of HCl, estimate the volume of water that can dissolve all the gas. Why must the flask be dry, and why does the litmus turn red?
9. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Dry HCl gas turns dry blue litmus red. (b) HCl gas is collected by upward displacement of air. (c) HCl is collected over water. (d) Concentrated H₂SO₄ is used to dry HCl. (e) Dilute H₂SO₄ is used to make HCl from NaCl. (f) Dry HCl gas turns moist blue litmus red.
10. NaCl, KCl, CaCl₂, and ZnCl₂ (name only) are four chlorides. How many chloride ions are present in one formula unit of each? What common observation do their solutions give with acidified silver nitrate, and why does the cation not matter?
11. A student draws a set-up for HCl in which the gas is collected in an inverted jar with the tube reaching its top, and the delivery tube is dipped deep in water at the end. Name two errors and the correct method for each.
12. Why is it said that the hydrogen and chlorine for industrial HCl are conveniently available from one plant? Name the process and the third product.

### Answers

1. KCl = 39 + 35.5 = 74.5 g/mol, moles = 44.7 ÷ 74.5 = **0.6 mol**. Ratio 1 : 1 : 1, so HCl = 0.6 mol = 0.6 × 36.5 = **21.9 g**, volume = 0.6 × 22.4 = **13.44 L**. H₂SO₄ = 0.6 × 98 = **58.8 g**. Check: reactants = 44.7 + 58.8 = 103.5 g; products = KHSO₄ (39 + 1 + 32 + 64 = 136; 0.6 × 136 = 81.6 g) + HCl (21.9 g) = 103.5 g ✅.
2. NaCl = 23.4 ÷ 58.5 = **0.4 mol**. From 2NaCl : H₂SO₄ : Na₂SO₄ : 2HCl, HCl = 0.4 mol = 0.4 × 36.5 = **14.6 g**, volume = 0.4 × 22.4 = **8.96 L**. Na₂SO₄ = 0.2 mol × 142 = **28.4 g**. Check: H₂SO₄ = 0.2 × 98 = 19.6 g; reactants = 23.4 + 19.6 = 43.0 g; products = 14.6 + 28.4 = 43.0 g ✅.
3. HCl = 15.68 ÷ 22.4 = **0.7 mol**. Since 2NaCl : 2HCl = 1 : 1, NaCl = 0.7 mol = 0.7 × 58.5 = **40.95 g**. Since H₂SO₄ : 2HCl = 1 : 2, H₂SO₄ = 0.35 mol = 0.35 × 98 = **34.3 g**. Check: reactants = 40.95 + 34.3 = 75.25 g; products = HCl (0.7 × 36.5 = 25.55 g) + Na₂SO₄ (0.35 × 142 = 49.7 g) = 75.25 g ✅.
4. Moles = 3.36 ÷ 22.4 = **0.15 mol**, mass = 0.15 × 36.5 = **5.475 g**. Density of HCl = 36.5 ÷ 22.4 ≈ **1.63 g/L**; density of air = 29 ÷ 22.4 ≈ **1.29 g/L**. Because HCl is denser than air, it is collected in an upright jar with the tube reaching the bottom: **upward displacement of air**. Check: 1.63 ÷ 1.29 = 1.26 (matching Example 1) ✅.
5. H₂ = 67.2 ÷ 22.4 = **3 mol**. From H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl: Cl₂ = 3 mol = **67.2 L**, HCl = 6 mol = **134.4 L**. Masses: H₂ = 3 × 2 = **6 g**, Cl₂ = 3 × 71 = **213 g**, HCl = 6 × 36.5 = **219 g**. Check: 6 + 213 = 219 g ✅.
6. CaCl₂ = 40 + 71 = 111 g/mol, moles = 3.33 ÷ 111 = **0.03 mol**. 1 mol CaCl₂ gives 2 mol AgCl, so AgCl = 0.06 mol = 0.06 × 143.5 = **8.61 g**. AgNO₃ = 0.06 mol × 170 = **10.2 g**. Calcium (Ca²⁺) is a **spectator ion**: the precipitate forms purely from Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓ (Chapter 8). Check: reactants = 3.33 + 10.2 = 13.53 g; products = 8.61 + Ca(NO₃)₂ (0.03 × 164 = 4.92 g) = 13.53 g ✅. (Silver nitrate is toxic: teacher demo only.)
7. 800 × 0.5 ÷ 100 = **4 g** of HCl. Ten times that concentration = **5 percent**. Check: 4 ÷ 800 × 100 = 0.5 ✅.
8. Water needed = 250 ÷ 450 ≈ **0.56 mL**. The flask must be **dry** so the gas does not dissolve prematurely before the demo starts, which would prevent the sudden pressure drop needed to drive the fountain. The litmus turns red because dissolved HCl ionises in water to produce H₃O⁺ ions (acidic). Check: 0.56 × 450 = 252 mL ≈ 250 mL ✅.
9. (a) **False** ❌: dry HCl does not turn dry blue litmus red; water is needed for ionisation. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌: HCl is extremely soluble in water, so it is collected by upward displacement of air. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌: dilute acid contains water in which HCl dissolves; conc. H₂SO₄ is required. (f) **True** ✅: moisture on the litmus provides the water needed to form H₃O⁺.
10. NaCl has 1, KCl has 1, CaCl₂ has 2, and ZnCl₂ has 2 chloride ions per formula unit. All four solutions give a **curdy white precipitate of AgCl** with acidified silver nitrate because the test detects the Cl⁻ ion (Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl↓), while the cations (Na⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺) are spectator ions (Chapter 8, Topic 4). Check: Cl count from formulas: NaCl 1, KCl 1, CaCl₂ 2, ZnCl₂ 2 ✅.
11. **Error 1:** Since HCl is denser than air, the jar must stand upright with the tube reaching the bottom; an inverted jar with a top tube is used for gases lighter than air. **Error 2:** Dipping a bare delivery tube deep into water causes suck-back; the correct method is to attach an inverted funnel whose rim just touches the water surface. (Nor can HCl be collected over water, since it is far too soluble.)
12. In the chlor-alkali process (electrolysis of brine), **both Cl₂ (at the anode) and H₂ (at the cathode)** are produced side by side in the same plant, with **NaOH** as the third product. Thus, both raw materials for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl are immediately available on-site.
