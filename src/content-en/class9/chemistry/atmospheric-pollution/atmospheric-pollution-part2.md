# Topic 2: Acid Rain

In our previous topic, we saw that burning fossil fuels releases **primary pollutants** directly into the atmosphere — such as sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$), nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$), carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$), and soot. We also introduced **secondary pollutants**, which form when primary emissions react chemically inside moving air masses. One of the most destructive secondary phenomena is **acid precipitation**, commonly known as **acid rain**. In this topic, we will follow the complete chemical pathway: how industrial gases turn rain acidic, how this precipitation falls upon the landscape, and how it corrodes marble monuments, acidifies lakes, and damages forest soils. We will also perform a safe home activity modeling marble corrosion and solve stoichiometric mass calculations.

---

## Yellowing Marble and Weathered Statues

Dr. Higgins led Maya, Leo, and their science class on an educational field trip to examine heritage architecture and stone preservation. Standing before historic marble monuments, the students admired the white stone gleaming under the morning sun. Maya walked up to an intricately carved balustrade and examined it closely. 

"Dr. Higgins, from a distance, the marble looks pristine white, but up close parts of the stone appear **dull, pitted, and discoloured with a faint yellow-grey tint**. The delicate floral edges carved into the stone look worn down and rounded. How could such hard, solid stone suffer so much damage?"

Dr. Higgins smiled approvingly. "A keen observation, Maya. Marble is indeed a tough metamorphic rock, but it has a relentless chemical adversary: **acid rain**. Acid precipitation acts like a dilute, airborne chemical solvent that silently etches away the stone over decades."

"Acid in the rain?" asked Maya. "Isn't rain supposed to be pure water?"

"That is precisely what we are here to explore," Dr. Higgins answered. "First, we will discover why even natural rain is slightly acidic, and what chemical reactions make polluted rain severely acidic. Then, we will look at how this acid attacks marble, and how environmental engineers are preventing this damage. Later, you can even model this reaction safely at home without heat or danger."

Historical monuments worldwide — including the Taj Mahal in Agra, the Parthenon in Athens, and medieval cathedrals across Europe — have faced this exact crisis. Strict environmental buffer zones have been established around heritage sites, banning polluting industries and heavy diesel vehicles to protect the irreplaceable stone. Today, we examine the chemistry behind this global challenge.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Acid rain** | Precipitation (rain, snow, fog, or dry deposition) with an elevated concentration of dissolved sulfuric and nitric acids, rendering it significantly more acidic than natural rain | Rain falling downwind of industrial thermal power stations |
| **Acid** | A substance that dissolves in water to release hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$), producing a characteristic tart taste and reacting chemically with carbonates and active metals | Citric acid in lemons, acetic acid in vinegar |
| **$\text{SO}_2$ (Sulfur dioxide)** | A pungent, choking gas produced during the combustion of sulfur-bearing fossil fuels | Exhaust gases from coal-fired boilers |
| **$\text{NO}_x$ (Nitrogen oxides)** | A collective term for nitric oxide ($\text{NO}$) and nitrogen dioxide ($\text{NO}_2$) | Gases formed in internal combustion vehicle engines and lightning strikes |
| **Sulfuric acid ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$)** | A strong mineral acid formed when atmospheric $\text{SO}_2$ oxidizes and dissolves in cloud moisture | A major active corrosive component of acid rain |
| **Nitric acid ($\text{HNO}_3$)** | A strong mineral acid formed when atmospheric $\text{NO}_2$ reacts with oxygen and water | The second major active corrosive component of acid rain |
| **Carbonic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$)** | A very weak, unstable acid formed when carbon dioxide dissolves naturally in water | Natural rain, sparkling soda water |
| **pH** | A logarithmic scale measuring the acidity of an aqueous solution; a pH below 5.6 defines acid precipitation | Natural rain has a pH around 5.6 |
| **Scrubber** | An industrial emission-control system that sprays alkaline slurries (e.g., slaked lime or limestone) to capture $\text{SO}_2$ from flue gases | Flue-gas desulfurization units at power plants |
| **Catalytic converter** | An automotive exhaust unit containing precious metals that catalyzes the conversion of toxic $\text{CO}$ and $\text{NO}_x$ into harmless $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{N}_2$ | Exhaust systems in petrol-driven passenger cars |

## Part A: Why Normal Rain is Naturally Slightly Acidic

Here is a surprising scientific fact: **even the cleanest, most pristine rain falling through unpolluted air is naturally slightly acidic.** Why is this so?

As we saw in Topic 1, Earth's clean atmosphere contains approximately 0.04% (~420 ppm) **carbon dioxide** ($\text{CO}_2$). As raindrops condense and fall through the atmosphere, they dissolve a small amount of this ambient carbon dioxide. In doing so, water and carbon dioxide react reversibly to form a very weak acid known as **carbonic acid**:

$$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$$

Let us check the atomic balance:
- **Left side:** $1\text{ C}, 2\text{ O} + 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ O} = 1\text{ C}, 2\text{ H}, 3\text{ O}$
- **Right side:** $2\text{ H}, 1\text{ C}, 3\text{ O}$ ✅ (Perfect balance!)

Because of this dissolved carbonic acid, normal pristine rainwater possesses a pH of approximately **5.6**. This natural acidity is harmless to humans, plants, and aquatic ecosystems — it is an integral part of the geochemical weathering cycle. In fact, bottled sparkling water contains this very same weak carbonic acid.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Acid rain** is defined as precipitation that has a pH **significantly lower than 5.6**, typically between **4.0 and 4.5** (and occasionally as low as 3.0 in severe industrial events). This marked drop in pH is driven by two potent industrial mineral acids: **sulfuric acid** ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) and **nitric acid** ($\text{HNO}_3$).

## Part B: How Sulfur Dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) Transforms into Sulfuric Acid

Raw coal and unrefined petroleum fuels naturally contain small percentages of elemental **sulfur** ($\text{S}$). When these fuels burn inside thermal power stations, industrial boilers, and refineries, the sulfur oxidizes to produce pungent **sulfur dioxide** gas ($\text{SO}_2$), which acts as a primary pollutant:

$$\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$$

Atom count: $1\text{ S}, 2\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅.

Once released into the atmosphere, $\text{SO}_2$ drifts with prevailing winds. Over hours and days, exposed to sunlight, oxygen, and catalytic airborne dust particles, $\text{SO}_2$ is further oxidized into **sulfur trioxide** ($\text{SO}_3$):

$$2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{SO}_3$$

Atom count:
- Left: $2\text{ S}, 4\text{ O} + 2\text{ O} = 6\text{ O}$
- Right: $2\text{ S}, 6\text{ O}$ ✅

Sulfur trioxide reacts eagerly and almost instantaneously with suspended water droplets inside clouds or rain:

$$\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$$

Atom count:
- Left: $1\text{ S}, 3\text{ O} + 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ O} = 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ S}, 4\text{ O}$
- Right: $2\text{ H}, 1\text{ S}, 4\text{ O}$ ✅

Notice the environmental pathway: **$\text{SO}_2$ is emitted as a primary pollutant**, while **$\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ forms dynamically in the air as a secondary pollutant**.

## Part C: How Nitrogen Oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) Form and Transform into Nitric Acid

Nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) comprises 78% of our atmosphere, but its two nitrogen atoms are held together by an exceptionally strong covalent triple bond ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$), making it chemically inert under ordinary ambient conditions.

However, at **extremely elevated temperatures** — exceeding $1{,}200^\circ\text{C}$ inside vehicle combustion cylinders or around instantaneous lightning channels — atmospheric nitrogen and oxygen gain sufficient activation energy to react, forming **nitric oxide** ($\text{NO}$):

$$\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{High Temperature}} 2\text{NO}$$

Atom count: $2\text{ N}, 2\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅.

Natural lightning strikes generate a small baseline of $\text{NO}$, but billions of motor vehicle engines and industrial power plants generate massive quantities continuously. As soon as nitric oxide mixes with ambient air, it reacts rapidly with atmospheric oxygen to form reddish-brown, toxic **nitrogen dioxide** ($\text{NO}_2$):

$$2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2$$

Atom count:
- Left: $2\text{ N}, 2\text{ O} + 2\text{ O} = 4\text{ O}$
- Right: $2\text{ N}, 4\text{ O}$ ✅

Finally, nitrogen dioxide dissolves in cloud water droplets in the presence of atmospheric oxygen to produce **nitric acid** ($\text{HNO}_3$):

$$4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{HNO}_3$$

Let us check the atomic balance:
- **Left side:** $4\text{ N}$, $8\text{ O} + 2\text{ O} + 2\text{ O} = 12\text{ O}$, $4\text{ H}$
- **Right side:** $4\text{ H}, 4\text{ N}, 12\text{ O}$ ✅ (Fully balanced!)

```
   EMISSION SOURCE                  ATMOSPHERIC OXIDATION              IN RAIN DROPLETS
   
   Sulfur in coal & diesel (S)
        |  combustion
        v
   SO₂ (Sulfur dioxide)   ----- O₂ -----> SO₃ ----- H₂O -----> H₂SO₄ (Sulfuric acid)
                                                                      \
                                                                       \   Dissolved in
                                                                        >  clouds & rain
   Extreme heat: engines & lightning                                   /   ACID RAIN
        |  N₂ + O₂                                                    /
        v                                                            /
   NO (Nitric oxide)      ----- O₂ -----> NO₂ ----- H₂O & O₂ -> HNO₃ (Nitric acid)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – The dual formation pathways of acid rain: sulfur in fossil fuels yields $\text{SO}_2$, which oxidizes to $\text{SO}_3$ and hydrates to $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$. High-temperature combustion creates $\text{NO}$, which oxidizes to $\text{NO}_2$ and dissolves with oxygen to form $\text{HNO}_3$. Both acids wash down to Earth in precipitation.

It is worth noting that acidic substances do not fall solely as liquid rain. They also descend as **acid fog, acid snow, and dry deposition** (acidic dust and gases settling directly on surfaces). Collectively, scientists call this phenomenon **acid deposition**. Furthermore, because smokestacks can be over 100 metres tall, these gases can travel **hundreds of kilometres** on prevailing winds before falling as acid precipitation across distant rural forests, lakes, and heritage sites.

> **Check Your Understanding:** "Acid rain falls only in the immediate vicinity of the factory smokestack that emitted the gases." ❌ Incorrect. Sulfur and nitrogen oxides can remain aloft for several days and travel hundreds of kilometres downwind, depositing acid rain over pristine mountain ranges, lakes, and faraway cities.

## Part D: Comparing Normal Rain and Acid Rain on the pH Scale

The pH scale measures how acidic or basic an aqueous solution is. At Class 9 level, we need to know one core rule: **the lower the pH number, the stronger the acidity.** Pure distilled water has a neutral pH of 7.

```
   Stronger Acidity  <-----------------------------------  Weaker Acidity
 
   pH: 3             4             5             6             7
                     [===========]               ^             ^
                      Acid Rain                  |             |
                     (typically                  |             Neutral pure water: 7.0
                      pH 4.0 to 4.5)             |
                                                 Normal clean rain: ~5.6
                                                 (due to natural dissolved CO₂)
 
   Threshold Rule: Any precipitation with pH < 5.6 is classified as "Acid Rain"
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Normal rain versus acid rain on the pH continuum. Clean precipitation sits around pH 5.6 due to dissolved natural $\text{CO}_2$. Rain with a pH below 5.6 is formally classified as acid precipitation.

Because the pH scale is logarithmic, every single integer drop on the scale represents a **tenfold increase in acidity**. A drop from pH 5.6 to pH 4.6 means the rain has become 10 times more acidic; a drop to pH 3.6 represents a 100-fold increase in acid concentration!

## Part E: Chemical Impact of Acid Rain on Marble and Limestone

Natural marble and sedimentary limestone are composed primarily of **calcium carbonate** ($\text{CaCO}_3$) — the same compound explored during our study of chemical decomposition ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)).

Carbonates react readily with strong acids. When rain carrying dissolved sulfuric acid strikes a marble surface, a double-replacement reaction occurs:

$$\text{CaCO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{CaSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$

Let us check the atomic balance:
- **Left side:** $1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 3\text{ O} + 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ S}, 4\text{ O} = 1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 1\text{ S}, 2\text{ H}, 7\text{ O}$
- **Right side:** $1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ S}, 4\text{ O} + 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ O} + 1\text{ C}, 2\text{ O} = 1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 1\text{ S}, 2\text{ H}, 7\text{ O}$ ✅

What happens physically to the monument?
1. The solid, crystalline, insoluble calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) is converted into **calcium sulfate** ($\text{CaSO}_4$).
2. Unlike dense marble, calcium sulfate is brittle, chalky, and slightly soluble in water.
3. Subsequent rain showers wash the loosened $\text{CaSO}_4$ away, stripping away microscopic layers of the stone.
4. Carbon dioxide gas ($\text{CO}_2$) bubbles away into the atmosphere.

A parallel reaction occurs with dissolved nitric acid:

$$\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{HNO}_3 \rightarrow \text{Ca}(\text{NO}_3)_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$

Atom count check:
- Left: $1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 3\text{ O} + 2\text{ H}, 2\text{ N}, 6\text{ O} = 1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 2\text{ N}, 2\text{ H}, 9\text{ O}$
- Right: $1\text{ Ca}, 2\text{ N}, 6\text{ O} + 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ O} + 1\text{ C}, 2\text{ O} = 1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 2\text{ N}, 2\text{ H}, 9\text{ O}$ ✅

Calcium nitrate ($\text{Ca}(\text{NO}_3)_2$) is highly water-soluble and washes away instantly with rainfall.

```
   BEFORE: Crisp, new marble carving       AFTER: Decades of acid precipitation
 
       ___________                               ___________
      /           \                             /  .  _  .  \
     |   ( o  o )  |   Sharp details,          |   ( .  - )  |   Pitted, rough surface;
     |      ^      |   smooth crystalline      |    . ^ .    |   fine facial features
     |    \___/    |   CaCO₃ luster            |  . \_~_/ .  |   eroded; yellowish-grey
     |_____________|                           |_____________|   crust of CaSO₄ washes off
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Weathering of a marble statue: pristine, lustrous calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) with crisp carved contours gradually converts to crumbly, soluble calcium sulfate ($\text{CaSO}_4$) and calcium nitrate, which wash away and leave behind a pitted, discoloured surface. This degradation is often referred to as "stone cancer".

The yellowish discolouration observed on historical monuments is caused by a combined effect: chemical alteration into calcium sulfate crusts, coupled with airborne soot, vehicular hydrocarbons, and dust particles embedding themselves into the porous, etched stone.

### Cumulative Impact Over Decades

Acid rain does not destroy a monument during a single rainstorm. The corrosive reaction is **slow and cumulative**, acting millimetre by millimetre across generations. Much like iron rusting slowly upon exposure to moisture and oxygen ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md](../chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md)), the steady removal of surface stone causes irreversible cultural and architectural loss.

## Part F: Broader Environmental Impacts of Acid Precipitation

Beyond historic stone buildings, acid rain inflicts widespread harm across ecosystems:

1. **Aquatic Ecosystems (Lakes and Streams):** When acidic rain flows into freshwater bodies, it lowers the lake pH. Most freshwater fish eggs cannot hatch at a pH below 5.0, and beneficial aquatic micro-organisms perish. Furthermore, acid rain leaches toxic **aluminium ions** from surrounding clay soils into the water, coating fish gills and leading to mass suffocation.
2. **Agricultural and Forest Soils:** Acidic rainwater dissolves and leaches away vital plant nutrients — including calcium ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$), magnesium ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$), and potassium ($\text{K}^+$) — washing them deep below root zones.
3. **Forest Decline:** Deprived of essential minerals and suffering direct chemical damage to the protective waxy cuticles on their leaves, trees in regions such as the Black Forest in Germany or the Appalachian Mountains experienced widespread leaf death and stunting.
4. **Corrosion of Structural Metals:** Acid precipitation actively attacks iron, steel, and zinc architectural structures, dramatically accelerating corrosion and rusting:
   $$\text{Fe} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\uparrow$$
   Atom count: $1\text{ Fe}, 2\text{ H}, 1\text{ S}, 4\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅. (Notice the release of hydrogen gas, connecting back to [hydrogen-part6.md](../hydrogen/hydrogen-part6.md)!) Metal bridges, railway tracks, wire fences, and vehicle paint suffer severe deterioration.
5. **Human Health:** While walking through acid rain does not burn skin directly, the fine sulfate and nitrate aerosols that form acid rain (PM2.5) enter human airways, causing asthma attacks, bronchitis, and long-term lung inflammation (Topic 5).

## Part G: Safe Activity: Modeling Marble Dissolution in Vinegar (At Home with an Adult)

Can we demonstrate how acid dissolves marble inside a safe home setting? Yes! While domestic household vinegar (acetic acid) is far more concentrated than acid rain, it exhibits the exact same chemical principle: **acid + metal carbonate $\rightarrow$ salt + water + carbon dioxide gas**.

**Materials Needed:** Two clear glass tumblers, standard white vinegar, a small marble chip (or a piece of blackboard chalk, which is made of calcium carbonate/sulfate), plain tap water, and a shallow tray. **Always conduct this activity under adult supervision.**

**Procedure:**
1. Place both glasses on the tray. Pour plain tap water into **Glass A** until half-full, and an equal volume of white vinegar into **Glass B**.
2. Gently drop a small piece of marble chip or chalk into each glass simultaneously. Keep your face at a comfortable distance from the rims.
3. Observe and record changes over the next 5 to 10 minutes.

**Observations:**
- **Glass A (Control):** The chalk or marble chip rests undisturbed at the bottom; no effervescence occurs.
- **Glass B (Vinegar):** Tiny **bubbles of gas immediately begin fizzing** from the surface of the stone. Over time, the chalk piece softens, shrinks, and partially dissolves.

**Chemical Reaction (Acetic acid in vinegar, $\text{CH}_3\text{COOH}$):**
$$\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{CH}_3\text{COOH} \rightarrow \text{Ca}(\text{CH}_3\text{COO})_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$$

Let us check the atomic balance:
- **Left:** $\text{CaCO}_3$ ($1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ C}, 3\text{ O}$) $+ 2 \times \text{C}_2\text{H}_4\text{O}_2$ ($4\text{ C}, 8\text{ H}, 4\text{ O}$) $= 1\text{ Ca}, 5\text{ C}, 8\text{ H}, 7\text{ O}$
- **Right:** $\text{Ca}(\text{CH}_3\text{COO})_2$ ($1\text{ Ca}, 4\text{ C}, 6\text{ H}, 4\text{ O}$) $+ \text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($2\text{ H}, 1\text{ O}$) $+ \text{CO}_2$ ($1\text{ C}, 2\text{ O}$) $= 1\text{ Ca}, 5\text{ C}, 8\text{ H}, 7\text{ O}$ ✅

**Safety Rules:**
- **Never taste any solutions.** Even though household vinegar is edible, the resulting mixture contains dissolved stone and non-food contaminants.
- Avoid splashing vinegar into the eyes. Wash hands thoroughly with soap after concluding the experiment.
- The neutral liquid can be safely disposed of by rinsing it down the sink with running water under adult supervision.
- Glass A serves as a **scientific control**: it proves that water alone cannot cause the bubbling; the acid is specifically required.

## Part H: Stoichiometry and Mass Calculations

Using the molar masses established in our study of atoms and molecules ([atoms-and-molecules-part6.md](../atoms-and-molecules/atoms-and-molecules-part6.md)):
- $\text{Ca} = 40\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{C} = 12\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{O} = 16\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{H} = 1\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{S} = 32\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{N} = 14\text{ g/mol}$.
- $\text{CaCO}_3 = 40 + 12 + (3 \times 16) = \mathbf{100\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = (2 \times 1) + 32 + (4 \times 16) = \mathbf{98\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\text{CaSO}_4 = 40 + 32 + (4 \times 16) = \mathbf{136\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\text{CO}_2 = 12 + (2 \times 16) = \mathbf{44\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\text{H}_2\text{O} = (2 \times 1) + 16 = \mathbf{18\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\text{SO}_2 = 32 + (2 \times 16) = \mathbf{64\text{ g/mol}}$

**Solved Example 1: How much marble is corroded by sulfuric acid?**
Suppose an acidic rainstorm deposits **$4.9\text{ g}$ of pure dissolved $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$** onto a marble balcony. Calculate the mass of $\text{CaCO}_3$ dissolved, as well as the masses of $\text{CaSO}_4$ and $\text{CO}_2$ produced.

Balanced equation:
$$\text{CaCO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{CaSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2$$
The stoichiometric molar ratio is $1 : 1 : 1 : 1 : 1$.

1. Moles of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:
   $$\text{Moles} = \frac{4.9\text{ g}}{98\text{ g/mol}} = 0.05\text{ mol}$$
2. Moles of $\text{CaCO}_3$ dissolved:
   $$0.05\text{ mol} \times 100\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{5.0\text{ g}}$$
3. Mass of $\text{CaSO}_4$ formed:
   $$0.05\text{ mol} \times 136\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{6.8\text{ g}}$$
4. Mass of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ formed:
   $$0.05\text{ mol} \times 18\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{0.9\text{ g}}$$
5. Mass of $\text{CO}_2$ evolved:
   $$0.05\text{ mol} \times 44\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{2.2\text{ g}}$$

> **Conservation of Mass Check:**
> - Total reactant mass: $5.0\text{ g } (\text{CaCO}_3) + 4.9\text{ g } (\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4) = \mathbf{9.9\text{ g}}$
> - Total product mass: $6.8\text{ g } (\text{CaSO}_4) + 0.9\text{ g } (\text{H}_2\text{O}) + 2.2\text{ g } (\text{CO}_2) = \mathbf{9.9\text{ g}}$ ✅

**Solved Example 2: From coal sulfur to sulfuric acid.**
A thermal power plant combusts coal containing **$10\text{ kg}$ of elemental sulfur**. If all the sulfur burns to $\text{SO}_2$ and is ultimately converted into atmospheric $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, calculate the theoretical mass of $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ generated.

Reaction sequence:
$$\text{S} \rightarrow \text{SO}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_3 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$$
Each atom of sulfur yields exactly one molecule of sulfuric acid ($1 : 1$ mole ratio).

1. Moles of sulfur:
   $$n = \frac{10{,}000\text{ g}}{32\text{ g/mol}} = 312.5\text{ mol}$$
2. Mass of $\text{SO}_2$ produced:
   $$312.5\text{ mol} \times 64\text{ g/mol} = 20{,}000\text{ g} = \mathbf{20\text{ kg}}$$
3. Mass of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ generated:
   $$312.5\text{ mol} \times 98\text{ g/mol} = 30{,}625\text{ g} \approx \mathbf{30.6\text{ kg}}$$
Notice that just $10\text{ kg}$ of sulfur impurities in coal produces over $30\text{ kg}$ of concentrated sulfuric acid in the air due to the addition of oxygen and water!

**Solved Example 3: Flue-gas desulfurization in an industrial scrubber.**
A factory flue stream contains **$640\text{ kg}$ of $\text{SO}_2$**. A wet limestone scrubber traps this gas according to the following reaction:

$$2\text{CaCO}_3 + 2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CaSO}_4 + 2\text{CO}_2$$

Atom count check:
- Left: $2\text{ Ca}, 2\text{ C}, 6\text{ O} + 2\text{ S}, 4\text{ O} + 2\text{ O} = 2\text{ Ca}, 2\text{ C}, 2\text{ S}, 12\text{ O}$
- Right: $2\text{ Ca}, 2\text{ S}, 8\text{ O} + 2\text{ C}, 4\text{ O} = 2\text{ Ca}, 2\text{ S}, 2\text{ C}, 12\text{ O}$ ✅

Calculate the mass of limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3$) required and the mass of gypsum ($\text{CaSO}_4$) recovered.

1. Moles of $\text{SO}_2$:
   $$n_{\text{SO}_2} = \frac{640\text{ kg}}{64\text{ kg/kmol}} = 10\text{ kmol}\ (10{,}000\text{ mol})$$
2. The molar ratio of $\text{CaCO}_3$ to $\text{SO}_2$ is $2 : 2 = 1 : 1$:
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{CaCO}_3 = 10\text{ kmol} \times 100\text{ kg/kmol} = \mathbf{1{,}000\text{ kg}}\ (1\text{ metric tonne})$$
3. Mass of $\text{CaSO}_4$ formed:
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{CaSO}_4 = 10\text{ kmol} \times 136\text{ kg/kmol} = \mathbf{1{,}360\text{ kg}}$$

> **Mass Balance Verification:**
> Reactants: $1{,}000\text{ kg } (\text{CaCO}_3) + 640\text{ kg } (\text{SO}_2) + [5\text{ kmol} \times 32 = 160\text{ kg } (\text{O}_2)] = \mathbf{1{,}800\text{ kg}}$
> Products: $1{,}360\text{ kg } (\text{CaSO}_4) + [10\text{ kmol} \times 44 = 440\text{ kg } (\text{CO}_2)] = \mathbf{1{,}800\text{ kg}}$ ✅
> The captured synthetic calcium sulfate (gypsum) is sold to manufacture drywall and cement!

## Part I: Strategies to Prevent and Mitigate Acid Rain

The only permanent solution to acid rain is to **capture or eliminate emissions of $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{NO}_x$ at their source before they escape into the atmosphere**.

| Technological Solution | Operating Principle | Real-World Application |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel Desulfurization** | Removing sulfur compounds from fossil fuels during refining | Ultra-low-sulfur diesel and petrol (e.g., Euro VI / BS-VI standards) |
| **Flue-Gas Scrubbers** | Spraying alkaline slurries (powdered limestone or slaked lime) inside industrial chimneys to trap $\text{SO}_2$ as solid $\text{CaSO}_4$ | Modern coal-fired thermal power stations |
| **Catalytic Converters** | Precious metal catalysts (platinum, palladium, rhodium) oxidize $\text{CO}$ to $\text{CO}_2$ and reduce $\text{NO}$ to inert $\text{N}_2$ gas | Vehicle exhaust systems ($2\text{NO} + 2\text{CO} \rightarrow \text{N}_2 + 2\text{CO}_2$) |
| **Clean Alternative Fuels** | Transitioning to fuels devoid of sulfur impurities | Compressed Natural Gas (CNG), Liquefied Petroleum Gas (LPG), green hydrogen, and electric vehicles (EVs) |
| **Renewable Energy** | Eliminating combustion entirely | Solar photovoltaic, wind turbines, and hydroelectric power generation |
| **Lake Liming (Emergency Remediation)** | Adding crushed agricultural limestone ($\text{CaCO}_3$) or lime directly into acidified lakes to neutralize water pH | Environmental recovery programs in Scandinavia and North America |

Chemical equations for emissions control:
- Oxidation in converter: $2\text{CO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}_2$ (Balanced: $2\text{ C}, 4\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅)
- Simultaneous reduction-oxidation: $2\text{NO} + 2\text{CO} \rightarrow \text{N}_2 + 2\text{CO}_2$ (Balanced: $2\text{ N}, 2\text{ C}, 4\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅)
- Lake liming: $\text{CaO} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{CaSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$ (Balanced: $1\text{ Ca}, 1\text{ S}, 5\text{ O}, 2\text{ H}$ on both sides ✅)

The history of acid rain offers tremendous environmental optimism: strict regional agreements, such as the Clean Air Act amendments in North America and Europe, mandated scrubbers and low-sulfur fuels. Consequently, **sulfur emissions plummeted by over 80% between 1980 and 2020**, allowing damaged lakes and forests to stage remarkable ecological recoveries. Human technological problems can be resolved through scientific understanding and disciplined action.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing natural unpolluted rain is completely neutral with a pH of 7.0**
   Natural clean rainwater dissolves ambient atmospheric carbon dioxide, forming dilute carbonic acid ($\text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$) that gives clean rain a natural pH of approximately **5.6**.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming acid rain will instantaneously burn skin on contact like battery acid**
   Acid rain is a very dilute acid solution (typically pH 4.0 to 4.5). It does not burn human skin upon direct contact; rather, its damage is cumulative and environmental, eroding stone and disrupting aquatic biomes over decades.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing acid rain with the greenhouse effect**
   Acid rain is caused by sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) and nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x$) producing acidic precipitation. The greenhouse effect is driven primarily by carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$), methane ($\text{CH}_4$), and water vapour trapping infrared heat.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing only sulfur dioxide contributes to acid precipitation**
   Both sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$) and nitrogen oxides ($\text{NO}_x \rightarrow \text{HNO}_3$) are major contributors. In many modern urban centers, $\text{NO}_x$ from vehicle exhaust is now the leading contributor.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming nitrogen and oxygen react spontaneously in normal ambient air**
   Nitrogen is held by a very stable triple bond and will not react with oxygen at room temperature. The reaction occurs exclusively under extreme heat ($>1{,}200^\circ\text{C}$), such as inside combustion engines or during lightning discharges.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking marble simply "vanishes into nothing" when corroded by acid**
   Matter is strictly conserved: the solid calcium carbonate ($\text{CaCO}_3$) reacts chemically to produce calcium sulfate ($\text{CaSO}_4$), water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), and gaseous carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$). The mass of reactants equals the mass of products.

## Quick Recap

- **Natural Rain Acidity:** Natural unpolluted rain is slightly acidic (pH ~5.6) due to dissolved atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ forming carbonic acid: $\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$.
- **Definition of Acid Rain:** Precipitation exhibiting a pH below 5.6 (typically 4.0–4.5), driven by sulfuric and nitric acids.
- **Sulfur Reaction Sequence:** $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$; $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{SO}_3$; $\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.
- **Nitrogen Reaction Sequence:** High temperatures induce $\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}$; $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2$; $4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{HNO}_3$.
- **Damage to Marble ($\text{CaCO}_3$):** Corrosive reaction converts insoluble stone into crumbly, water-soluble calcium sulfate: $\text{CaCO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{CaSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{CO}_2\uparrow$.
- **Broader Environmental Damage:** Kills aquatic fish eggs, leaches aluminum into lakes, strips vital nutrients ($\text{Ca}^{2+}, \text{Mg}^{2+}$) from forest soils, and corrodes structural metals ($\text{Fe} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{FeSO}_4 + \text{H}_2\uparrow$).
- **Technological Solutions:** Low-sulfur fuels, industrial scrubbers (producing useful synthetic gypsum), catalytic converters, transition to renewable power, and temporary lake liming.

### Answers

1. Clean rain absorbs natural atmospheric carbon dioxide, forming weak carbonic acid:
   $$\text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{CO}_3$$
   *Verification:* Left: $1\text{ C}, 2\text{ H}, 3\text{ O}$; Right: $2\text{ H}, 1\text{ C}, 3\text{ O}$ ✅. The resulting acid is **carbonic acid**, giving unpolluted rain a natural pH of ~5.6.

2. Balanced equations:
   - $\text{S} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{SO}_2$ ($1\text{ S}, 2\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅)
   - $2\text{SO}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{SO}_3$ ($2\text{ S}, 6\text{ O}$ on both sides ✅)
   - $\text{SO}_3 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ ($1\text{ S}, 4\text{ O}, 2\text{ H}$ on both sides ✅)
   **$\text{SO}_2$ is a primary pollutant** (emitted directly from combustion), while **$\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is a secondary pollutant** (synthesized within the atmosphere). $\text{SO}_3$ is a transient intermediate.

3. Molecular nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$) has a strong covalent triple bond ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$) requiring intense activation energy to dissociate. Inside engines and lightning arcs, temperatures exceed $1{,}200^\circ\text{C}$, providing sufficient thermal energy for oxidation:
   - $\text{N}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}$ (Balanced: $2\text{ N}, 2\text{ O}$ ✅)
   - $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2$ (Balanced: $2\text{ N}, 4\text{ O}$ ✅)
   - $4\text{NO}_2 + \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 4\text{HNO}_3$ (Balanced: $4\text{ N}, 12\text{ O}, 4\text{ H}$ ✅)

4. Molar masses: $\text{CaCO}_3 = 100\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = 98\text{ g/mol}$.
   $$\text{Moles of }\text{CaCO}_3 = \frac{250\text{ g}}{100\text{ g/mol}} = 2.5\text{ mol}$$
   From the $1 : 1$ stoichiometric ratio, $2.5\text{ mol}$ of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ is consumed:
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = 2.5\text{ mol} \times 98\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{245\text{ g}}$$

5. Molar mass of $\text{HNO}_3 = 1 + 14 + (3 \times 16) = 63\text{ g/mol}$.
   $$\text{Moles of }\text{HNO}_3 = \frac{31.5\text{ g}}{63\text{ g/mol}} = 0.5\text{ mol}$$
   The mole ratio is $2\text{ HNO}_3 : 1\text{ CaCO}_3 : 1\text{ CO}_2$.
   - $\text{Moles of }\text{CaCO}_3 = \frac{0.5}{2} = 0.25\text{ mol}$
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{CaCO}_3 = 0.25\text{ mol} \times 100\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{25\text{ g}}$$
   - $\text{Moles of }\text{CO}_2 = 0.25\text{ mol}$
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{CO}_2 = 0.25\text{ mol} \times 44\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{11\text{ g}}$$
   *Verification:* $\text{Ca}(\text{NO}_3)_2 = 0.25 \times 164 = 41\text{ g}$; $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 0.25 \times 18 = 4.5\text{ g}$. Reactants: $31.5 + 25 = 56.5\text{ g}$; Products: $41 + 4.5 + 11 = 56.5\text{ g}$ ✅.

6. Molar mass of $\text{CaCO}_3 = 100\text{ g/mol}$.
   $$\text{Moles of }\text{CaCO}_3 = \frac{20\text{ g}}{100\text{ g/mol}} = 0.2\text{ mol}$$
   Mole ratio with $\text{CO}_2$ is $1 : 1$:
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{CO}_2 = 0.2\text{ mol} \times 44\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{8.8\text{ g}}$$
   **Observations:** Vigorous effervescence (bubbling) on the chalk surface, and gradual reduction/dissolution of the solid chalk.

7. (a) Sulfur mass: $3\% \text{ of } 400\text{ kg} = 12\text{ kg} = 12{,}000\text{ g}$.
   $$\text{Moles of S} = \frac{12{,}000\text{ g}}{32\text{ g/mol}} = 375\text{ mol}$$
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{SO}_2 = 375\text{ mol} \times 64\text{ g/mol} = 24{,}000\text{ g} = \mathbf{24\text{ kg}}$$
   (b) Each mole of sulfur yields one mole of $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$:
   $$\text{Mass of }\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = 375\text{ mol} \times 98\text{ g/mol} = 36{,}750\text{ g} = \mathbf{36.75\text{ kg}}$$

8. Moles of $\text{SO}_2$:
   $$n = \frac{160\text{ kg}}{64\text{ kg/kmol}} = 2.5\text{ kmol}$$
   From $2\text{CaCO}_3 : 2\text{SO}_2$, ratio is $1 : 1$:
   - $\text{Mass of }\text{CaCO}_3 = 2.5\text{ kmol} \times 100\text{ kg/kmol} = \mathbf{250\text{ kg}}$
   - $\text{Mass of }\text{CaSO}_4 = 2.5\text{ kmol} \times 136\text{ kg/kmol} = \mathbf{340\text{ kg}}$
   *Mass Conservation Check:*
   - Reactants: $250\text{ kg } (\text{CaCO}_3) + 160\text{ kg } (\text{SO}_2) + [1.25\text{ kmol} \times 32 = 40\text{ kg } (\text{O}_2)] = \mathbf{450\text{ kg}}$
   - Products: $340\text{ kg } (\text{CaSO}_4) + [2.5\text{ kmol} \times 44 = 110\text{ kg } (\text{CO}_2)] = \mathbf{450\text{ kg}}$ ✅.

9. (a) **Plain tap water** with a matched piece of chalk/marble.
   (b) **Carbon dioxide** ($\text{CO}_2$).
   (c) Household vinegar is much more acidic (pH ~2.5) than actual acid rain (pH 4.0–4.5), but it demonstrates the exact same chemical mechanism ($\text{acid} + \text{carbonate} \rightarrow \text{salt} + \text{water} + \text{CO}_2$).
   (d) Never taste the liquid; wear eye protection and wash hands thoroughly after handling.

10. (a) **Sample Q (pH 4.2)** is acid rain because its pH is well below the 5.6 threshold.
    (b) **Sample Q** is more acidic and possesses a significantly higher concentration of hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$).
    (c) Sample P represents natural rain; its mild acidity arises from natural atmospheric carbon dioxide dissolving into carbonic acid.

11. (i) Industrial chimneys emit gases high into the troposphere where high-velocity prevailing winds transport them hundreds of kilometres.
    (ii) The oxidation of $\text{SO}_2$ and $\text{NO}_x$ into sulfuric and nitric acids takes hours to days, allowing the cloud masses to drift across borders before precipitation occurs.

12. (a) A wet scrubber captures $\text{SO}_2$ gas in an alkaline lime slurry, preventing it from ever reaching the sky.
    (b) A catalytic converter converts harmful $\text{NO}_x$ and $\text{CO}$ into harmless atmospheric $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{CO}_2$.
    (c) Solar power generates electricity using sunlight without burning coal or releasing any sulfur or nitrogen oxides.
    (d) Powdered agricultural lime ($\text{CaO}/\text{CaCO}_3$) acts as an alkaline neutralizer, restoring lake pH toward 6.5–7.0 and protecting aquatic life.

13. (a) **False** ❌. Acid precipitation occurs as liquid rain, snow, sleet, fog, and dry particle deposition.
    (b) **True** ✅. Calcium sulfate is significantly more porous and water-soluble than crystalline calcium carbonate, so it gradually washes off during rain.
    (c) **True** ✅. The immense heat of lightning provides the activation energy for atmospheric $\text{N}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ to combine into $\text{NO}$.
    (d) **False** ❌. According to the Law of Conservation of Mass, the total mass of reactants strictly equals the total mass of products in any closed chemical reaction.
