# Topic 5: Smog, Particulate Matter, and Respiratory Health

In our previous topic, we looked high into the stratosphere to understand how ozone shields life from cosmic ultraviolet rays, and noted that ozone at ground level acts as a hazardous pollutant. Now, we return to the ground-level air where humans live, study, and breathe every minute of the day. In this topic, we examine **what smog is**, **why winter temperature inversions trap polluted air beneath a rigid atmospheric "thermal ceiling"**, **how microscopic PM2.5 and carbon monoxide compromise the human cardiovascular system**, the hidden dangers of **indoor air pollution**, practical **health defense strategies**, a safe **greased-card dust deposition test**, and how to interpret AQI readings for public health safety. Our goal is scientific empowerment: understanding air chemistry enables us to take practical, life-saving precautions.

## Part A: What is Smog? Two Distinct Types of Smog

The term **smog** was coined in early 20th-century London as a portmanteau of **sm**oke and f**og**. While pure fog is merely a harmless meteorological suspension of condensed liquid water droplets, smog represents a hazardous chemical cocktail of suspended water droplets, particulate soot, fly ash, and acidic gases.

From our study of colloidal mixtures ([is-matter-around-us-pure-part3.md](../is-matter-around-us-pure-part3.md)), we know that smoke and fog are **colloids** — suspensions whose particles are small enough to remain suspended against gravity, yet large enough to scatter visible light rays (the **Tyndall effect**). This optical scattering creates opacity, turning distant buildings hazy and transforming the bright midday sun into a feeble orange glow.

Environmental scientists distinguish between two major types of smog:

**1. Classical / Sulfurous (Winter) Smog**
Also known as London-type smog, this forms in cold, calm, high-humidity winter weather. It results from heavy combustion of sulfur-bearing coal, biomass, and heavy fuels. The air becomes laden with soot, unburnt carbon particles, fly ash, and sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$). This forms the basis of the winter smog crises observed across continental river plains and valleys worldwide.

**2. Photochemical (Summer) Smog**
Also known as Los Angeles-type smog, this occurs in hot, sunny, traffic-dense urban basins. Internal combustion engines produce nitric oxide ($\text{NO}$), which oxidizes to nitrogen dioxide ($\text{NO}_2$). When bombarded by intense solar ultraviolet rays, $\text{NO}_2$ photolyzes into nitric oxide and reactive nascent oxygen atoms, which combine with molecular oxygen to generate **toxic ground-level ozone ($\text{O}_3$)**:

$$\text{NO}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Solar UV}} \text{NO} + \text{O}$$
$$\text{O} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{O}_3$$

This ground-level ozone reacts with unburnt vehicular hydrocarbons and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) to generate eye-stinging peroxyacyl nitrates (PAN) and secondary organic aerosols. This is a classic **secondary photochemical pollutant**; it does not emerge directly from vehicle tailpipes, but synthesizes within sunlit urban air.

> **Check Your Understanding:** "Summer photochemical smog and winter sulfurous smog form through identical mechanisms." ❌ Incorrect. Winter smog relies on cold, damp, stagnant air trapping primary particulate soot and $\text{SO}_2$; photochemical smog requires hot, intense solar irradiation driving secondary chemical reactions that generate ground-level ozone.

## Part B: Winter Smog in Metropolitan Basins: The Multi-Source Challenge

During late autumn and winter (October through January), major metropolitan basins across northern India, East Asia, and geographic valleys globally experience severe smog crises. This is never the result of a single isolated source; rather, it represents a convergence of multiple emission streams:

| Pollution Source | Contribution to Atmospheric Haze |
|---|---|
| **Agricultural Crop Stubble Burning** | Post-harvest open-field burning of crop residue releases massive plumes of carbonaceous soot, volatile organic gases, and PM2.5. |
| **Vehicular Emissions** | Millions of automobiles, diesel delivery trucks, and motor scooters continuously emit $\text{NO}_2$, $\text{CO}$, unburnt hydrocarbons, and fine exhaust soot. |
| **Industrial Plants & Thermal Power Stations** | Heavy coal combustion emits sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$), nitrogen oxides, and industrial fly ash. |
| **Construction and Demolition Dust** | Mechanical grinding, cement mixing, and unpaved roads generate coarse inhalable dust (PM10). |
| **Open Waste & Domestic Solid-Fuel Burning** | Incineration of municipal trash, fallen leaves, and biomass cooking fires emit dense low-altitude particulate plumes. |
| **Meteorological Trap** | Calm surface winds, low ambient temperatures, and **temperature inversions** prevent pollutants from dispersing. |

These human emission sources operate throughout the year. Why then does air quality plunge so catastrophically during the winter months? The fundamental culprit is an atmospheric physical trap: **a temperature inversion**.

### Temperature Inversion: Atmospheric "Thermal Ceiling"

Under normal atmospheric conditions in the troposphere (Topic 1), **air temperature decreases steadily with increasing altitude** (at roughly $6.5^\circ\text{C}$ per kilometre). Sunlight warms the ground, and warm ground air expands, becomes less dense, and **rises buoyantly upward** into cooler air aloft. This vertical movement is known as **thermal convection** ([heat-and-temperature-part6.md](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part6.md)). As warm air rises, it carries urban smoke, dust, and vehicle exhaust high into the upper troposphere, where strong horizontal winds disperse and dilute the contaminants safely away.

During long, clear, calm winter nights, this natural ventilation mechanism reverses:
1. **Rapid Radiative Cooling:** The ground continuously radiates its accumulated thermal heat out into clear night skies, cooling rapidly.
2. **Cold Ground Air:** The layer of air directly in contact with the chilly earth becomes cold, dense, and heavy.
3. **Warm Air Overhead:** A layer of warmer, lighter air remains stationary higher up, creating an inverted thermal profile: **cold, heavy air trapped underneath warm, lighter air**.

Because cold air is denser than warm air, the cold ground air cannot rise. Vertical convection is completely suppressed! The warm boundary layer acts as an impenetrable, invisible **atmospheric thermal ceiling** or "lid", trapping all vehicle exhaust, factory fumes, and agricultural smoke within a few hundred metres of the city streets.

```
   Altitude
     ^
     |   Cool upper air         (Lower temperature)
     |   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
     |   WARM AIR INVERSION LAYER (Higher temperature) <- Invisible "Thermal Ceiling"
     |   =============================================
     |   This warm boundary completely halts convection!
     |
     |   Cold, dense surface air (Trapped near ground)
     |   . : . : . : . : . : . : . : . : . : . : . : .
     |   Vehicular exhaust, soot, smoke, dust TRAPPED!
     +------------------------------------------------>
                 City Streets and Buildings
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Meteorological temperature inversion: a warm air layer aloft acts like an impenetrable lid over cold, dense surface air. Normal vertical convection currents cease, locking particulate soot, vehicle emissions, and industrial gases close to the ground to form dense winter smog.

As the sun climbs high in the afternoon, solar radiation gradually reheats the ground surface, warming the cold air layer and eroding the inversion ceiling. Convection resumes, and surface smog thins out. This explains why winter smog readings are frequently severe at dawn and late evening, but ease somewhat during sunny afternoons.

> **Check Your Understanding:** "During a temperature inversion, air near the ground is warmer than air aloft." ❌ Incorrect. In an inversion, air near the ground is **colder and denser** than the warm air layer sitting directly above it.

## Part C: How Particulate Matter Penetrates the Human Respiratory System

In Topic 1, we defined PM10 as particles with diameters $\le 10\ \mu\text{m}$ and PM2.5 as particles $\le 2.5\ \mu\text{m}$. Consider how human anatomy interacts with these sizes:

```
   Inhaled Ambient Air with Suspended Particulates
            |
            v
      +-----------+
      |   NASAL   |  Nasal hair and viscous mucous membranes trap coarse
      |  PASSAGES |  dust particles (PM10).
      +-----+-----+
            |
            v
      +-----------+
      |  TRACHEA  |  Ciliated epithelial cells sweep out intermediate particles
      |  & BRONCHI|  via cough reflexes.
      +-----+-----+
            |
            v
      +-----------+
      |  BRONCH-  |  Ultrafine PM2.5 bypasses upper physical barriers,
      |   IOLES   |  drifting deep into the lower bronchial tree.
      +-----+-----+
            |
            v
      +-----------+
      | PULMONARY |  Grape-like terminal air sacs where gas exchange occurs.
      |  ALVEOLI  |  PM2.5 embeds into alveolar walls, causing inflammation.
      +-----+-----+
            |
            v
      +-----------+
      |  BLOOD-   |  The finest nanoparticles (<0.1 µm) cross alveolar
      |  STREAM   |  capillary walls, entering systemic circulation.
      +-----------+
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Inhalation pathway of particulate matter: natural biological filters (nasal hairs, mucosal lining) effectively capture coarse particles (PM10), but ultrafine combustion particles (PM2.5) bypass these defenses to penetrate deep into pulmonary alveoli, where the smallest fractions can cross into the bloodstream.

**Example 1: Hair diameter comparison.**
An average human hair measures $70\ \mu\text{m}$ across; a PM2.5 particle measures $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$.
$$\frac{70}{2.5} = 28$$
A PM2.5 particle is approximately **28 to 30 times thinner** than a strand of hair. Roughly 28 fine particles could sit side by side across the width of a single hair strand!

**Example 2: Unit conversions.**
Express $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$ in nanometres and metres:
- Nanometres: $2.5\ \mu\text{m} \times 1{,}000 = \mathbf{2{,}500\text{ nm}}$
- Metres: $2.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$

### Measuring Airborne Particulate Mass: Micrograms Per Cubic Metre ($\mu\text{g/m}^3$)

Environmental agencies quantify airborne particulate density in **micrograms per cubic metre ($\mu\text{g/m}^3$)**. One microgram is one-millionth of a gram ($1\ \mu\text{g} = 10^{-6}\text{ g}$).

**Example 3: Particulate mass in an indoor classroom.**
Consider a classroom measuring $4\text{ m} \times 5\text{ m} \times 3\text{ m}$.
$$\text{Volume} = 4 \times 5 \times 3 = \mathbf{60\text{ m}^3}$$
If ambient PM2.5 pollution reaches a severe concentration of **$150\ \mu\text{g/m}^3$**, the total mass of suspended PM2.5 inside the room is:
$$\text{Total Mass} = 60\text{ m}^3 \times 150\ \mu\text{g/m}^3 = 9{,}000\ \mu\text{g} = \mathbf{9.0\text{ mg}}$$
In clean air meeting the World Health Organization (WHO) 24-hour guideline of $15\ \mu\text{g/m}^3$, the total mass would be:
$$60\text{ m}^3 \times 15\ \mu\text{g/m}^3 = 900\ \mu\text{g} = \mathbf{0.9\text{ mg}}\quad (\text{a 10-fold reduction!})$$

**Example 4: Daily human particulate inhalation.**
An adult at resting activity breathes roughly **$7.5\text{ litres}$ of air per minute**.
Over a 24-hour period ($1{,}440\text{ minutes}$):
$$\text{Daily Inhaled Volume} = 7.5\text{ L/min} \times 1{,}440\text{ min} = 10{,}800\text{ L} = \mathbf{10.8\text{ m}^3}$$
At a smoggy ambient level of $150\ \mu\text{g/m}^3$:
$$\text{Inhaled PM2.5 Mass} = 10.8\text{ m}^3 \times 150\ \mu\text{g/m}^3 = 1{,}620\ \mu\text{g} = \mathbf{1.62\text{ mg per day}}$$
Under clean air conditions ($15\ \mu\text{g/m}^3$), the daily inhaled mass is just $162\ \mu\text{g}$ ($0.162\text{ mg}$). On a severe smog day, the lungs are exposed to ten times the particulate burden!

## Part D: Carbon Monoxide: The Silent Toxic Hazard

In our study of chemical reactions ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md](../chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md)), we learned that when carbon burns with an insufficient supply of oxygen, it undergoes **incomplete combustion** to form **carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$)**:

$$2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Limited Oxygen}} 2\text{CO}$$
$$\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{Excess Oxygen}} \text{CO}_2$$

Carbon monoxide is a **colourless, odourless, and tasteless gas**. It gives no warning of its presence.

**The Biological Mechanism of CO Poisoning**
Inside the lungs, molecular oxygen normally binds reversibly to the iron atoms in blood **haemoglobin** to form oxyhaemoglobin, which circulates oxygen throughout the body. Carbon monoxide has a biochemical affinity for haemoglobin that is **over 200 times stronger than that of oxygen**!

When inhaled, $\text{CO}$ out-competes oxygen and binds irreversibly to form **carboxyhaemoglobin ($\text{COHb}$)**. This starves vital organs — especially the brain and heart — of oxygen. Early symptoms include dull headaches, fatigue, dizziness, and nausea. At high concentrations, it induces disorientation, loss of consciousness, and fatal asphyxiation.

**Example 5: Incomplete vs. complete combustion stoichiometry.**
Consider the combustion of $12\text{ g}$ of elemental carbon ($\text{C}=12\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{CO}=28\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{CO}_2=44\text{ g/mol}$).
- Incomplete combustion: $1\text{ mol C } (12\text{ g}) \rightarrow 1\text{ mol CO } (\mathbf{28\text{ g of toxic CO}})$.
- Complete combustion: $1\text{ mol C } (12\text{ g}) \rightarrow 1\text{ mol CO}_2\ (\mathbf{44\text{ g of non-toxic CO}_2})$.

> [!CAUTION]
> **Never burn charcoal braziers, coal stoves, or unvented fuel heaters inside a closed room or tent.** In a sealed space, burning coals steadily deplete oxygen, accelerating the production of carbon monoxide while occupants sleep unaware.

## Part E: Indoor Air Pollution Hazards

Many people assume air pollution is strictly an outdoor phenomenon. In reality, indoor air can be significantly more toxic than outdoor air due to confined volumes and poor ventilation:

1. **Biomass Cookstoves:** In many developing rural households, burning firewood, dried dung, or raw coal in unvented stoves releases choking soot, fine PM2.5, and carbon monoxide directly into living quarters. Women and young children face the greatest chronic exposure. Transitioning to clean cooking fuels (LPG, biogas, electric induction) is a paramount global health priority.
2. **Unvented Space Heating:** Operating kerosene heaters or coal braziers indoors without flues creates lethal carbon monoxide accumulation.
3. **Incense and Mosquito Coils:** Burning scented incense sticks or mosquito coils inside closed bedrooms generates high concentrations of aromatic hydrocarbons and fine particulate matter.
4. **Cooking Fumes:** Deep frying at high temperatures aerosolizes oil droplets and volatile organic compounds; running a kitchen exhaust hood or opening windows is essential.
5. **Dampness and Mold:** Biological mold spores, dust mites, and pet dander aggravate chronic allergic asthma.

## Part F: Health Impacts and Personal Protection Strategies

The combination of fine particulates (PM2.5) and toxic gases ($\text{CO}$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{NO}_2$, ground-level $\text{O}_3$) causes both acute and chronic health effects:
- **Acute Symptoms:** Eye irritation, dry scratchy throat, persistent coughing, sinus congestion, and tension headaches.
- **Respiratory Aggravation:** Severe asthma attacks, acute bronchitis, and reduced lung capacity.
- **Long-Term Cardiovascular Damage:** Chronic deep PM2.5 exposure promotes arterial plaque inflammation, elevated blood pressure, strokes, heart attacks, and pulmonary cancer.
- **Vulnerable Groups:** Children (whose lungs are still developing), the elderly, pregnant individuals, and outdoor workers (traffic police, delivery couriers, construction crews).

**Actionable Protective Guidelines:**
1. **Monitor the Daily AQI:** Check your local environmental agency app each morning.
2. **Adjust Outdoor Physical Activity:** When the AQI enters **Poor (201–300)** or **Very Poor (301–400)**, cancel outdoor sports, morning jogging, and vigorous playground games. Exercise indoors instead.
3. **Wear Certified Particulate Respirators:** Standard loose cloth masks or surgical paper masks leak around the edges and trap fewer than 40% of fine particles. A well-fitted **N95 or FFP2 respirator**, sealed firmly around the nose and chin, traps at least **95% of fine particles down to $0.3\ \mu\text{m}$**.
4. **Indoor Air Purifiers:** High-Efficiency Particulate Air (HEPA) purifiers effectively recirculate and scrub indoor room air, capturing fine PM2.5 particles on dense fibrous mesh filters.
5. **Strategic Ventilation:** On severe smog days, keep windows sealed during early morning and late evening inversion peaks; ventilate briefly during sunny mid-afternoons when ground inversion layers disperse.

**Example 6: N95 filtration performance.**
Suppose 20,000 fine particles ($0.3\ \mu\text{m}$) approach a person's breathing zone:
- With an N95 respirator (95% filtration efficiency):
  $$\text{Particles penetrating} = 20{,}000 \times (1 - 0.95) = 20{,}000 \times 0.05 = \mathbf{1{,}000\text{ particles}}$$
- With a loose cloth mask (40% filtration efficiency):
  $$\text{Particles penetrating} = 20{,}000 \times (1 - 0.40) = 20{,}000 \times 0.60 = \mathbf{12{,}000\text{ particles}}$$
The N95 respirator allows **12 times fewer particles** into the respiratory system!

## Part G: Hands-on Activity: The Greased-Card Dust Deposition Test

Students can observe particulate deposition visually using a simple, non-hazardous household setup. This activity requires no flames or hazardous chemicals.

> [!NOTE]
> This home activity captures **coarse settleable dust particles and soot specks** ($\text{PM}>10\ \mu\text{m}$). Ultrafine PM2.5 particles remain airborne and are invisible without an electron microscope.

**Materials:** White card stock paper (e.g., $10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$), pencil, ruler, transparent petroleum jelly (Vaseline), a magnifying glass, and masking tape. **Conduct with adult supervision.**

**Procedure:**
1. Using a pencil and ruler, draw a grid of $1\text{ cm} \times 1\text{ cm}$ squares across each white card.
2. Smear a very thin, uniform layer of petroleum jelly across the gridded surface using a clean finger. Wipe hands with soap and water immediately afterward.
3. Prepare two identical cards:
   - **Card A:** Secure horizontally outdoors near a busy roadway or street-facing balcony railing.
   - **Card B:** Secure horizontally in a sheltered garden, park, or quiet interior courtyard.
4. Leave both cards exposed for exactly **24 hours**.
5. Retrieve the cards carefully with an adult. Using a magnifying glass, count the dark specks adhering within 3 or 4 representative $1\text{ cm}^2$ squares on each card. Calculate the average particle count per square centimetre.

```
   Greased Card (10 cm x 10 cm) After 24 Hours
 
   +----+----+----+----+----+
   | .. | .  | :  | .: | .  |   Count visible dark specks of soot
   +----+----+----+----+----+   and road dust trapped on the sticky
   | :. | .  | .. | .  | :  |   grease layer inside each 1 cm² square
   +----+----+----+----+----+
   | .  | :: | .  | .: | .. |   Magnifying glass reveals coarse dust;
   +----+----+----+----+----+   invisible PM2.5 remains airborne.
```

> **Figure 5.3** – The greased-card dust deposition test: sticky petroleum jelly captures falling airborne particulate matter over 24 hours, providing an intuitive, comparative visual index of particulate pollution across different environments.

**Example 7: Card calculation.**
On Card A (roadside), three squares contain 12, 15, and 9 specks.
$$\text{Average roadside density} = \frac{12 + 15 + 9}{3} = \mathbf{12\text{ particles/cm}^2}$$
Across the full $10\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$ ($100\text{ cm}^2$) card:
$$\text{Estimated total particles} = 12 \times 100 = \mathbf{1{,}200\text{ particles}}$$

## Part H: Interpreting AQI for Public Health Decision-Making

Recall the standardized AQI spectrum: **Good (0–50), Satisfactory (51–100), Moderate (101–200), Poor (201–300), Very Poor (301–400), and Severe (401–500).**

**Example 8: Health categories for municipal readings.**
- Day 1: AQI **462** $\rightarrow$ Bracket 401–500 $\rightarrow$ **Severe** (all outdoor exertion banned).
- Day 2: AQI **187** $\rightarrow$ Bracket 101–200 $\rightarrow$ **Moderate** (sensitive individuals take precautions).
- Day 3: AQI **52** $\rightarrow$ Bracket 51–100 $\rightarrow$ **Satisfactory** (normal outdoor activity).
- Day 4: AQI **300** $\rightarrow$ Upper threshold of 201–300 $\rightarrow$ **Poor**; a single-point rise to **301** enters **Very Poor**!

**Example 9: School administrative health protocols.**
At an AQI of 462 (Severe), children must not participate in outdoor sports or assemblies, and municipal authorities may order temporary campus closures to prevent daily commuting through toxic smog. At an AQI of 187 (Moderate), standard indoor schooling proceeds safely, while students with diagnosed asthma are advised to keep inhalers handy and limit high-intensity outdoor sprinting.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing smog is merely natural morning fog**
   Natural fog is composed of pure condensed water droplets that evaporate harmlessly in the sun. Smog is a toxic colloidal suspension of water droplets loaded with particulate soot, acidic sulfates, nitrates, and toxic reactive gases.

2. **Mistake 2: Blaming winter smog solely on agricultural stubble burning**
   Stubble burning is a significant seasonal trigger, but urban vehicle exhaust, thermal coal power plants, industrial emissions, construction dust, and municipal waste burning generate continuous baseline pollution.

3. **Mistake 3: Believing cold air rises buoyantly above warm air**
   Cold air is denser and heavier than warm air. During a temperature inversion, cold air remains trapped at ground level beneath warm air, halting normal convective mixing.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing PM2.5 particles are visible specks floating in the air**
   At $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$ across, PM2.5 particles are microscopic and completely invisible individually. The haze you see is the cumulative light-scattering effect of billions of suspended particles.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming carbon monoxide has a pungent smell that alerts you to danger**
   Carbon monoxide is completely colourless, odourless, and tasteless. Never rely on smell; install certified electronic $\text{CO}$ alarms and never burn charcoal or run engines in unvented rooms.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming a standard cloth mask offers full protection against PM2.5**
   Cloth masks have large weave openings that allow over 60% of fine combustion particles to pass through. Only a certified, tightly fitted N95/FFP2 respirator reliably blocks 95% of fine particles.

7. **Mistake 7: Believing the greased-card test measures official PM2.5 concentrations**
   The test is a simple qualitative and comparative tool that captures coarse, settleable dust ($\text{PM}>10\ \mu\text{m}$).

8. **Mistake 8: Conflating photochemical smog with stratospheric ozone depletion**
   Photochemical smog produces **bad ozone** at ground level that damages lungs. The ozone hole involves the destruction of **good ozone** high in the stratosphere.

## Quick Recap

- **Nature of Smog:** A hazardous colloidal mixture of smoke and fog. **Classical/sulfurous smog** occurs in cold, damp winter conditions driven by soot and $\text{SO}_2$. **Photochemical smog** occurs in hot, sunny conditions driven by vehicular $\text{NO}_x$, VOCs, and ground-level ozone ($\text{O}_3$).
- **Temperature Inversion:** Radiative night-time surface cooling traps cold, dense air near the ground beneath a stationary warm air layer aloft. This suppresses vertical convection, locking pollutants near ground level like an invisible ceiling.
- **Respiratory Penetration of PM:** Coarse PM10 particles are caught in nasal passages and the throat; fine PM2.5 particles penetrate deep into alveolar sacs, where ultrafine components cross into the bloodstream.
- **Concentration Units:** Particulate density is measured in $\mu\text{g/m}^3$ ($1\ \mu\text{g} = 10^{-6}\text{ g}$).
- **Carbon Monoxide Toxicity:** Formed by incomplete combustion ($2\text{C} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{CO}$). Binds irreversibly to blood haemoglobin with over 200 times the affinity of oxygen, causing rapid tissue hypoxia and death. Never operate unvented fuel heaters or charcoal stoves indoors.
- **Indoor Air Pollution:** Domestic cookstoves, mosquito coils, unvented heaters, and tobacco smoke create severe indoor health hazards.
- **Protective Countermeasures:** Check daily AQI; restrict outdoor athletic exertion when AQI exceeds 200; wear tightly sealed N95 respirators (blocking $\ge 95\%$ of $0.3\ \mu\text{m}$ particles); operate indoor HEPA purifiers.
- **AQI Framework:** 0–50 Good, 51–100 Satisfactory, 101–200 Moderate, 201–300 Poor, 301–400 Very Poor, and 401–500 Severe.

   The equation is strictly balanced ✅.
   (b) Moles of methane: $\frac{8\text{ g}}{16\text{ g/mol}} = 0.5\text{ mol}$.
   Mole ratio is $2\text{ CH}_4 : 2\text{ CO} = 1 : 1$.

8. During clear winter nights, the ground rapidly loses heat via infrared radiation, chilling the air immediately adjacent to the surface. After sunrise, sunlight warms the ground, heating the cold surface air, restoring convective buoyancy, breaking the inversion ceiling, and allowing the trapped smoke to disperse upward.
