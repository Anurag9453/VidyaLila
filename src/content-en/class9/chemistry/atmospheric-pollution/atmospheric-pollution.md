# Topic 1: Air and Pollution: Introduction and the Air Quality Index (AQI)

In our previous chapter, we observed that burning hydrogen produces only water vapour, making it an exceptionally clean fuel. But what do we mean when we describe something as "clean"? Cleanliness is measured against a fundamental benchmark: **clean air**. As the final chapter of Class 9 Chemistry, this study directly concerns the very air we breathe every second of our lives. In this topic, we begin by examining the exact composition of pristine air (expressed in percentages and parts per million), explore the layered structure of Earth's atmosphere, and investigate what constitutes **air pollution**, where pollutants originate, and how the **Air Quality Index (AQI)** quantifies air quality. This topic sets the foundation for our upcoming explorations of acid rain, the greenhouse effect, the ozone layer, urban smog, and practical technological remedies.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atmosphere** | The thick blanket of gases surrounding planet Earth | All atmospheric layers taken together |
| **ppm (parts per million)** | A unit expressing very small concentrations: the number of parts of a substance per million ($10^6$) parts of the mixture | 420 ppm $\text{CO}_2$ |

## Part A: What is in Clean Air?

In our study of matter, we recognized that dry air is not a pure chemical compound, but a homogeneous **mixture** of distinct gaseous elements and compounds. Let us examine the composition of clean, dry air at sea level **by volume**. The values below represent close approximations, as natural air varies slightly depending on geographic location and atmospheric conditions.

| Gas | Approximate Proportion (by volume) |
|---|---|
| Nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$) | 78% |
| Oxygen ($\text{O}_2$) | 21% |
| Argon ($\text{Ar}$) | 0.93% |
| Carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) | ~0.04% (~420 ppm) |
| Neon, helium, methane, and other noble/trace gases | Trace amounts |
| Water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) | Variable: near zero in arid deserts, up to 4% in humid tropical conditions |

To visualize this composition, imagine 100 identical small blocks representing the total volume of dry air, where each block accounts for exactly 1%.

```
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N N N
   N N N N N N N N O O
   O O O O O O O O O O
   O O O O O O O O O A

   N = Nitrogen (78 blocks)     O = Oxygen (21 blocks)
   A = Argon, carbon dioxide, and all trace gases combined (~1 block)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – A 100-block volume model of clean, dry air: 78 blocks of nitrogen, 21 blocks of oxygen, and approximately 1 single block comprising argon, carbon dioxide, and all trace gases. Water vapour is omitted here because its concentration fluctuates continually with weather.

Notice that carbon dioxide represents only **0.04%** by volume — a tiny fraction of a single block out of a hundred. Yet, as we will explore in Topic 3, even this tiny fraction exerts a tremendous influence on global surface temperatures. This highlights a crucial principle of atmospheric science: **substances present in extremely small concentrations can have massive planetary consequences**. Because percentages become awkward when dealing with such minuscule quantities, scientists use an alternative concentration unit: **parts per million (ppm)**.

---

### What is ppm (Parts Per Million)?

**1 ppm = 1 part in 1,000,000 (one million) parts.** Just as **percentage (%)** denotes parts per hundred ($1 \div 100$), **ppm** denotes parts per million ($1 \div 1{,}000{,}000$). The mathematical relationship between them is straightforward:

$$\text{1\%} = \frac{1}{100} = \frac{10{,}000}{1{,}000{,}000} = \text{10,000 ppm}$$

Therefore:
- To convert **ppm to percentage**, **divide by 10,000**.
- To convert **percentage to ppm**, **multiply by 10,000**.

**Example 1: Convert an atmospheric $\text{CO}_2$ concentration of 420 ppm into a percentage.**
$$\text{Percentage} = \frac{420}{10{,}000} = 0.042\%$$
This matches the approximate value of 0.04% noted in our composition table ✅.

**Example 2: Convert the concentration of argon (0.93% by volume) into ppm.**
$$\text{Concentration in ppm} = 0.93 \times 10{,}000 = 9{,}300\text{ ppm}$$

**Example 3: Calculate the combined concentration of all trace gases in ppm.**
After accounting for nitrogen (78%) and oxygen (21%), the remaining fraction of air is:
$$100\% - 78\% - 21\% = 1\% = 10{,}000\text{ ppm}$$
Within this 10,000 ppm slice, argon contributes 9,300 ppm and carbon dioxide contributes approximately 420 ppm. The remaining noble gases (neon, helium, krypton), methane, and other traces account for:
$$10{,}000 - 9{,}300 - 420 \approx 280\text{ ppm}$$

> **Verification:**
> $78 + 21 + 0.93 + 0.04 = 99.97\%$, leaving an unallocated residual of $0.03\%$ ($300\text{ ppm}$) ✅. The calculated value of $280\text{ ppm}$ aligns comfortably within rounding margins.

**Example 4: How much oxygen is present in an average room?**
Consider a bedroom measuring $3\text{ m} \times 4\text{ m} \times 2.5\text{ m}$.
$$\text{Volume} = 3 \times 4 \times 2.5 = 30\text{ m}^3$$
Since $1\text{ m}^3 = 1{,}000\text{ L}$, the room contains $30 \times 1{,}000 = 30{,}000\text{ L}$ of air.
- Oxygen volume (21%): $30{,}000 \times \frac{21}{100} = 6{,}300\text{ L}$
- Nitrogen volume (78%): $30{,}000 \times 0.78 = 23{,}400\text{ L}$

> **Verification:**
> $6{,}300 + 23{,}400 = 29{,}700\text{ L}$, leaving $300\text{ L}$ ($1\%$) for argon and trace gases ($6{,}300 + 23{,}400 + 300 = 30{,}000\text{ L}$) ✅.

## Part B: Layers of the Atmosphere

Earth's atmosphere is not a uniform ocean of gas; its density, pressure, temperature, and chemical composition change dramatically with altitude. Atmospheric scientists divide the atmosphere into **five concentric vertical layers**, summarized below with approximate altitude boundaries.

```
   Altitude
   (approximate)

   Above 600 km
   +----------------------------------+
   |  EXOSPHERE: Extremely tenuous    |
   |  fringe merging into outer space |
   +----------------------------------+
   85 km to ~600 km
   +----------------------------------+
   |  THERMOSPHERE: Very thin air;    |
   |  satellites and auroras occur    |
   +----------------------------------+
   50 km to 85 km
   +----------------------------------+
   |  MESOSPHERE: Very cold; meteors  |
   |  burn up here as shooting stars  |
   +----------------------------------+
   12 km to 50 km
   +----------------------------------+
   |  STRATOSPHERE: OZONE LAYER       |
   |  (concentrated mostly 15–35 km)  |
   +----------------------------------+
   0 to 12 km
   +----------------------------------+
   |  TROPOSPHERE: Where we live;     |
   |  weather, clouds, convection     |
   +----------------------------------+
   =========== Earth's Surface ========
```

> **Figure 1.2** – The five major layers of Earth's atmosphere (approximate boundaries, not drawn to linear scale): troposphere (0 to 12 km), stratosphere (12 to 50 km, enclosing the ozone layer), mesosphere (50 to 85 km), thermosphere (85 to ~600 km), and the exosphere merging into space.

Key features of each layer include:
- **Troposphere:** The lowest and densest atmospheric layer, containing roughly 75–80% of the entire mass of the atmosphere. Temperature steadily **decreases with altitude** in this layer (which is why mountain summits are much colder than sea-level valleys). Almost all clouds, weather systems, atmospheric convection currents, and surface biological life exist here. Commercial passenger aircraft generally cruise near the top of the troposphere, around 10 to 12 km.
- **Stratosphere:** Extends from 12 km to roughly 50 km. It houses the critical **stratospheric ozone layer** (primarily between 15 km and 35 km), which absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV) solar radiation (studied in detail in Topic 4).
- **Mesosphere:** Extends from 50 km to 85 km. Temperatures drop to extreme lows (reaching $-90^\circ\text{C}$), and incoming space debris (meteoroids) burns up upon collision with gas molecules, creating visible meteors.
- **Thermosphere and Exosphere:** The uppermost zones where gas molecules are spaced vastly apart and air density approaches a vacuum, gradually blending into interplanetary space.

It is vital to recognize that the air we breathe, pollute, and live in belongs entirely to the **troposphere**. Vertical air movement via thermal convection ([heat-and-temperature-part6.md](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part6.md)) occurs within this active layer, which also plays an essential role in winter temperature inversions and smog formation (Topic 5).

> **Check Your Understanding:** "The protective ozone layer resides in the troposphere." ❌ Incorrect. The protective ozone layer resides in the **stratosphere** (concentrated mostly between 15 km and 35 km).

## Part C: What is Air Pollution? Primary and Secondary Pollutants

Air naturally contains small concentrations of substances like carbon dioxide and suspended dust. What then defines pollution?

> **An air pollutant** is any substance introduced into the atmosphere in a location or at a concentration that causes undesirable effects, damaging living organisms, ecosystems, human health, or material structures.

Carbon dioxide is a natural, necessary component of pristine air; however, when human activities dramatically inflate its concentration, it drives global climate disruption. Conversely, substances like carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) or sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$) are fundamentally toxic contaminants that have no place in healthy breathing air. Therefore, a pollutant is characterized not merely by its intrinsic toxicity, but by its presence in **harmful concentrations and misplaced contexts**.

---

### Primary and Secondary Pollutants

Atmospheric pollutants are classified into two major categories depending on how they enter the atmosphere:

| Category | Formation Mechanism | Typical Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary Pollutant** | Emitted directly from an identifiable source into the atmosphere | Carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$), sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$), nitric oxide ($\text{NO}$), soot / elemental carbon |
| **Secondary Pollutant** | Formed within the atmosphere when primary pollutants chemically react with one another, with sunlight, or with natural atmospheric components | Ground-level ozone ($\text{O}_3$), peroxyacyl nitrates (PAN), acid precipitation droplets ($\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, $\text{HNO}_3$) |

A helpful cooking analogy: adding raw sugar directly to warm tea is like emitting a **primary** substance — it enters the cup directly as sugar. Baking a golden-brown caramel cake from flour, milk, and sugar involves complex chemical transformations that produce a entirely **new** compound — akin to a **secondary** pollutant. Because secondary pollutants form dynamically in moving air masses under solar irradiation, identifying and regulating their precise origin is often a formidable scientific challenge.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Ground-level ozone** is a toxic secondary pollutant that damages respiratory tissues and crops. It must **never** be confused with the beneficial **stratospheric ozone** that shields Earth from harmful solar UV rays. Both possess the exact same chemical formula ($\text{O}_3$), but their environmental role depends entirely on their altitude. As atmospheric scientists often state: *"Good up high, bad nearby."*

## Part D: Where Does Air Pollution Come From?

Sources of atmospheric pollution fall into two broad classifications: **natural sources** and **anthropogenic (human-made) sources**.

| Source Type | Description and Examples |
|---|---|
| **Natural Sources** | Volcanic eruptions (emitting volcanic ash, $\text{SO}_2$, and $\text{CO}_2$), desert dust storms, biogenic marsh gases (natural methane), pollen grains, and lightning-ignited forest fires. |
| **Anthropogenic Sources** | Motor vehicle exhaust (automobiles, trucks, aircraft), fossil-fuel power plants (burning coal, fuel oil, natural gas), industrial manufacturing facilities, crop residue (stubble) burning, construction and demolition dust, domestic solid-fuel stoves, and municipal open-waste incineration. |

While natural pollution events have occurred throughout geological history, **anthropogenic emissions have drastically outpaced natural background levels over the past two centuries**. The encouraging takeaway is that because human activities generate the majority of modern air pollution, deliberate human choices, cleaner technologies, and sensible regulations can effectively eliminate it.

A critical chemical insight: many common combustion processes generate multiple pollutants simultaneously. When fossil fuels (such as coal, diesel, or petrol) burn:
- **Complete combustion** produces carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) and water vapour ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
- **Incomplete combustion** (due to insufficient oxygen) yields lethal carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$) and fine unburnt carbon particles known as soot ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md](../chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part6.md)).
- Inherent chemical impurities, such as sulfur in raw coal or diesel, oxidize directly into sulfur dioxide ($\text{SO}_2$).

> **Check Your Understanding:** "Air pollution is caused entirely by human activities." ❌ Incorrect. Natural phenomena such as volcanic eruptions, dust storms, and wildfires generate considerable emissions. However, modern persistent urban and industrial pollution is predominantly human-made.

## Part E: Particulate Matter: PM10 and PM2.5

The visible component of airborne haze consists largely of **particulate matter (PM)**: microscopic solid particles and liquid droplets suspended stably in the atmosphere. Scientists categorize particulate matter by its **aerodynamic diameter**, measured in **micrometres ($\mu\text{m}$)**.

$$1\text{ micrometre } (\mu\text{m}) = 1{,}000\text{ nanometres } (\text{nm}) = 10^{-6}\text{ m} = 0.001\text{ mm}$$

- **PM10:** Inhalable coarse particles with an aerodynamic diameter of **$10\ \mu\text{m}$ or less**. Examples include crushed road dust, pollen grains, and mechanical grinding debris.
- **PM2.5:** Fine inhalable particles with an aerodynamic diameter of **$2.5\ \mu\text{m}$ or less**. These originate primarily from combustion sources, such as vehicle exhaust, power station emissions, and forest fires. PM2.5 is a subset of PM10.

To comprehend just how tiny these particles are, consider that an average human hair has a diameter of roughly **$70\ \mu\text{m}$**. The diagram below illustrates this scale, where each `#` symbol represents an increment of $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$.

```
   Human hair (70 µm):
   [############################]   28 markers

   PM10 (10 µm):
   [####]                            4 markers

   PM2.5 (2.5 µm):
   [#]                               1 marker
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Size comparison of PM2.5 and PM10 relative to the cross-section of a typical human hair ($~70\ \mu\text{m}$). While a human hair spans 28 markers, a PM10 particle spans only 4 markers, and a PM2.5 particle spans just 1 single marker. Roughly 28 PM2.5 particles placed side by side would span the width of a single strand of hair. (Scale: 1 marker `#` = $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$.)

**Example 5: How many times thinner than a human hair is a PM2.5 particle?**
$$\frac{70\ \mu\text{m}}{2.5\ \mu\text{m}} = 28\text{ times thinner}$$
A single PM2.5 particle is approximately **28 to 30 times thinner** than a strand of human hair.

**Example 6: How many times thinner than a human hair is a PM10 particle?**
$$\frac{70\ \mu\text{m}}{10\ \mu\text{m}} = 7\text{ times thinner}$$

**Example 7: Express the diameter of a $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$ particle in nanometres and metres.**
- In nanometres: $2.5\ \mu\text{m} \times 1{,}000 = 2{,}500\text{ nm}$
- In metres: $2.5 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}$

> **Verification:**
> $28 \times 2.5\ \mu\text{m} = 70\ \mu\text{m}$ ✅ and $7 \times 10\ \mu\text{m} = 70\ \mu\text{m}$ ✅.

Because these particles are so microscopic, gravity acts on them very weakly compared to air resistance. They remain **suspended in ambient air for days or weeks**, drifting over hundreds of kilometres. When inhaled, coarse particles are trapped by nasal hair and mucus, but ultrafine PM2.5 particles penetrate deep into lung alveoli and can even cross directly into the bloodstream (explored further in Topic 5).

## Part F: Reading the Air Quality Index (AQI)

Returning to the digital advisory board Leo observed at his school entrance: the **Air Quality Index (AQI)** is a standardized numerical scale that translates complex atmospheric measurements of multiple pollutants into a simple, intuitive public health score. Environmental protection agencies monitor concentrations of major pollutants — including PM2.5, PM10, ground-level ozone, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{NO}_2$, and $\text{CO}$ — and compute the AQI based on the pollutant exhibiting the highest relative health risk.

A fundamental rule to remember: **the higher the AQI number, the greater the air pollution and the higher the health hazard.** The standard international/national AQI categories are structured as follows:

| AQI Range | Health Category | Public Health Implications |
|---|---|---|
| **0 to 50** | **Good** | Air quality is considered satisfactory; air pollution poses little or no risk. |
| **51 to 100** | **Satisfactory / Moderate** | Acceptable air quality; a very small number of unusually sensitive individuals may experience minor respiratory irritation. |
| **101 to 200** | **Moderate / Unhealthy for Sensitive Groups** | May cause breathing discomfort to children, the elderly, and individuals with chronic lung or heart ailments (e.g., asthma). |
| **201 to 300** | **Poor / Unhealthy** | Prolonged outdoor exposure may cause breathing discomfort to the general public and more noticeable discomfort to sensitive groups. |
| **301 to 400** | **Very Poor / Very Unhealthy** | Respiratory illness warnings triggered; prolonged exertion outdoors carries substantial risk for everyone. |
| **401 to 500** | **Severe / Hazardous** | Emergency health advisory conditions; entire population is likely to be affected. Outdoor physical exertion should be strictly avoided. |

**Example 8: Classify the AQI reading of 312 displayed on Leo's school monitor.**
An AQI reading of 312 falls within the bracket of **301 to 400**, placing it squarely in the **Very Poor** category.

**Example 9: Identify the category for the following regional AQI readings:**
- **AQI 56:** Falls between 51 and 100 $\rightarrow$ **Satisfactory**.
- **AQI 187:** Falls between 101 and 200 $\rightarrow$ **Moderate**.
- **AQI 265:** Falls between 201 and 300 $\rightarrow$ **Poor**.
- **AQI 412:** Falls between 401 and 500 $\rightarrow$ **Severe**.

**Example 10: Yesterday's AQI was 156; today's AQI is 312. By what factor did it change, and how did the category shift?**
$$\text{Factor of change} = \frac{312}{156} = 2.0\text{ (doubled)}$$
The index value doubled, shifting the health classification from **Moderate** (101–200) to **Very Poor** (301–400). Note that because AQI formulas use segmented piecewise linear calculations, a doubled index does not necessarily mean pollutant mass concentrations doubled exactly; it signifies that public health risk has escalated severely.

> **Verification:**
> $156 \times 2 = 312$ ✅. The six category spans sum to $50 + 50 + 100 + 100 + 100 + 100 = 500$ ✅.

> [!WARNING]
> Pay close attention to boundary thresholds. An AQI reading of 200 is classified as **Moderate**, but an increase of just one point to 201 pushes the category into **Poor**. Similarly, 50 is **Good**, while 51 enters **Satisfactory**.

Dr. Higgins concluded: "Make it a daily habit to check the AQI board, Leo. Knowing the number empowers us to adjust our daily outdoor sports, wear appropriate protective masks when necessary, and make informed choices to safeguard our health."

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing oxygen is the most abundant gas in Earth's atmosphere**
   Nitrogen is by far the most abundant gas, accounting for roughly **78%** of clean, dry air by volume. Oxygen makes up approximately **21%**.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming carbon dioxide is solely a pollutant and completely absent from clean air**
   Natural clean air contains roughly 0.04% (~420 ppm) carbon dioxide, which is vital for plant photosynthesis and maintaining Earth's habitable temperature. It becomes a critical environmental threat when anthropogenic emissions elevate its concentration far above historical ecological baselines.

3. **Mistake 3: Treating ppm and percentage as interchangeable quantities**
   While both express fractions of a whole, their magnitudes differ by four orders of magnitude: $1\% = 10{,}000\text{ ppm}$. Always divide ppm by 10,000 to convert to percentage.

4. **Mistake 4: Imagining PM2.5 particles are visible specks just slightly smaller than human hair**
   PM2.5 particles are microscopic and completely invisible as individual specks to the naked eye. At $2.5\ \mu\text{m}$, they are approximately 28 to 30 times thinner than an average strand of human hair.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming ozone is uniformly hazardous wherever it exists**
   Ozone in the stratosphere forms an indispensable natural shield against lethal solar ultraviolet radiation. Conversely, ozone at ground level in the troposphere is a hazardous secondary pollutant that damages lung tissue.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming a higher AQI value indicates better air quality**
   The AQI scale operates in reverse of school test marks: a **higher number indicates dirtier, more hazardous air**. An AQI below 50 represents clean air; an AQI above 300 represents dangerous pollution.

7. **Mistake 7: Assuming air pollution arises exclusively from industrial factory chimneys**
   Air pollution stems from diverse sources including motor vehicles, power stations, construction dust, agricultural burning, household biomass combustion, and natural events like wildfires and volcanoes.

## Quick Recap

- **Composition of Clean Dry Air (by volume):** Approximately 78% nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$), 21% oxygen ($\text{O}_2$), 0.93% argon ($\text{Ar}$), ~0.04% (~420 ppm) carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$), trace noble gases, and variable water vapour.
- **Concentration Conversion:** $1\% = 10{,}000\text{ ppm}$. To convert ppm to %, divide by 10,000; to convert % to ppm, multiply by 10,000.
- **Atmospheric Layers:** Troposphere (0–12 km, where weather occurs and humans live), stratosphere (12–50 km, enclosing the protective ozone layer), mesosphere (50–85 km, where meteors burn), thermosphere (85–600 km), and exosphere (>600 km).
- **Pollutants:** Any harmful substance present in the wrong place or at an elevated concentration. **Primary pollutants** are emitted directly from sources ($\text{CO}$, $\text{SO}_2$, $\text{NO}$, soot); **secondary pollutants** form in the air via chemical reactions (ground-level ozone, atmospheric acids).
- **Sources of Air Pollution:** Natural (volcanic eruptions, desert dust storms, forest fires) and anthropogenic (automobiles, thermal power stations, factories, agricultural burning, construction).
- **Particulate Matter:** PM10 ($\le 10\ \mu\text{m}$) and PM2.5 ($\le 2.5\ \mu\text{m}$). PM2.5 particles are ~28 times thinner than a human hair (~$70\ \mu\text{m}$) and penetrate deep into pulmonary alveoli.
- **Air Quality Index (AQI):** A standardized scale from 0 to 500: 0–50 (Good), 51–100 (Satisfactory), 101–200 (Moderate), 201–300 (Poor), 301–400 (Very Poor), and 401–500 (Severe). Higher values indicate dirtier air.
