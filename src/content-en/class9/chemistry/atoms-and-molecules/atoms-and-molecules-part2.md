# Topic 2: Fixed Ratios: The Law of Constant Proportions

In Topic 1, we saw that **total mass remains constant** in every chemical reaction — nothing vanishes out of existence. Now let's ask a new question. Suppose two elements combine to form a compound. Can they combine in **any mass ratio at all**, or are they locked into a **fixed proportion by mass**? Back in Chapter 2, we mentioned that pure water always contains hydrogen and oxygen in a **1 : 8** ratio by mass, and promised that we would explore the full scientific law in this chapter. Today we meet that law: the **Law of Constant Proportions** (also called the Law of Definite Proportions).

---

## The Mountain Stream and the Kitchen Tap

Mr. Brooks and his nephew Liam were hiking along a high alpine trail. At a crystal-clear mountain stream fed by melting snow, they paused to refill their water bottles. Liam took a sip and said, "Uncle Brooks, this mountain water tastes so crisp and fresh compared to the tap water back home! Do you think its hydrogen-to-oxygen ratio is different too? Maybe mountain water has extra oxygen in it!"

Mr. Brooks smiled. "The taste and chill can differ because natural stream water has tiny traces of dissolved minerals and dissolved air. But if we purify both samples so we have **pure water (H₂O)** alone, their hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio is 100% identical — whether the water came from a mountain glacier, a kitchen tap, or a laboratory reaction. Around 1799, a French chemist tested this exact question across dozens of compounds."

"Who was that?" Liam asked.

"**Joseph Proust**," Mr. Brooks replied. "For years he analyzed pure compounds collected from different places around the world and prepared by different methods in the lab. Every single time, he found that a given compound always contains the **exact same elements in the exact same proportion by mass**. Let's look at the numbers and see how it works."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Law of Constant Proportions** | A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined together in the same fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation | In pure water, H : O = 1 : 8 by mass |
| **Mass ratio** | The comparison of the masses of two (or more) elements in a compound, simplified to the smallest whole numbers | H : O = 2 g : 16 g = 1 : 8 |
| **Pure compound** | A single chemical compound free of any dissolved impurities or other substances | Pure H₂O (distilled water), not salt water |
| **Mixture** | Two or more substances blended together in any proportion without a fixed ratio | Sugar dissolved in water |
| **Law of Multiple Proportions** | A related law studied in higher classes when two elements form more than one compound | Comparing H₂O and H₂O₂ (Part G) |

## Part A: Proust's Law of Constant Proportions

The **Law of Constant Proportions** (proposed by Joseph Proust) states:

> **In any pure chemical compound, the elements are always present in a definite, fixed proportion by mass, regardless of where the compound comes from or how it is prepared.**

Think of every compound as having an unchangeable **master recipe** by mass. Let's compare three samples of pure water obtained from three completely different sources and decomposed into hydrogen and oxygen gas in the laboratory:

| Source of Pure Water | Mass of Water Sample | Mass of Hydrogen | Mass of Oxygen | Mass Ratio (H : O) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen tap (distilled) | 36 g | 4 g | 32 g | 4 : 32 = **1 : 8** |
| Alpine mountain stream (distilled) | 27 g | 3 g | 24 g | 3 : 24 = **1 : 8** |
| Synthesized in the lab (burning H₂ in O₂) | 45 g | 5 g | 40 g | 5 : 40 = **1 : 8** |

Every single sample simplifies to the exact same mass ratio: **1 : 8**!

```
   Kitchen tap water  Alpine stream water    Lab-synthesized water
   36 g sample        27 g sample            45 g sample

   H = 4 g            H = 3 g                H = 5 g
   O = 32 g           O = 24 g               O = 40 g

   H : O = 1 : 8      H : O = 1 : 8          H : O = 1 : 8
        \                  |                  /
         \                 |                 /
          +------>   Identical ratio: 1 : 8  <------+
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Three pure water samples of different total masses from three different sources (tap, alpine stream, and lab synthesis) all yield the exact same hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio: 1 : 8.

**Check:** 4 + 32 = 36 ✅; 3 + 24 = 27 ✅; 5 + 40 = 45 ✅. And in every case, (mass of O) ÷ (mass of H) = 8 ✅.

Notice that the **total sample mass can vary** (36 g, 27 g, 45 g), but the **mass ratio H : O never changes**. Taking a larger sample of pure water simply scales both hydrogen and oxygen by the exact same factor.

## Part B: How to Find the Mass Ratio

**Step-by-step method:**

1. Write down the mass of each element in the sample (in grams).
2. Divide both masses by their greatest common factor (or by the smaller mass) to simplify to the smallest whole numbers.
3. Write the simplified ratio as **A : B**.
4. **Check:** Verify that the sum of the individual element masses equals the total mass of the compound sample (Conservation of Mass from Topic 1!).

### Example 1: Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

When 12 g of carbon is burned completely in oxygen, 44 g of carbon dioxide is formed. What is the mass ratio of carbon to oxygen in carbon dioxide?

- Mass of carbon = 12 g. Mass of oxygen = 44 − 12 = 32 g (by Conservation of Mass).
- Ratio C : O = 12 : 32. Divide both numbers by 4: **3 : 8**.

Check: 12 + 32 = 44 ✅; 12 ÷ 4 = 3 and 32 ÷ 4 = 8 ✅.

This is the exact **C : O = 3 : 8** mass ratio for carbon dioxide that we first met in Chapter 2!

### Example 2: Ammonia (NH₃)

A 17 g sample of pure ammonia gas contains 14 g of nitrogen. What mass of hydrogen does it contain, and what is the N : H mass ratio?

- Mass of hydrogen = 17 − 14 = 3 g.
- Ratio N : H = **14 : 3** (14 and 3 have no common factor greater than 1).

Check: 14 + 3 = 17 ✅; **N : H = 14 : 3**.

### Example 3: Sodium chloride (table salt, NaCl)

An 11.7 g sample of pure sodium chloride contains 4.6 g of sodium and 7.1 g of chlorine. Find the mass ratio Na : Cl.

- Check total mass first: 4.6 + 7.1 = 11.7 g ✅.
- Ratio Na : Cl = 4.6 : 7.1. Multiply both by 10 to clear the decimal: **46 : 71**.

From this we can also see the mass percentages: Na = (4.6 ÷ 11.7) × 100 ≈ **39.3%**, and Cl = (7.1 ÷ 11.7) × 100 ≈ **60.7%** — the exact figures we saw in Chapter 2! (We will cover percentage composition in full detail in Topic 7. Also notice that with rounded atomic masses Na = 23 and Cl = 35.5, the ratio 23 : 35.5 is identical to 46 : 71 ✅.)

## Part C: Finding Unknown Masses From a Known Ratio

Once you know the fixed mass ratio of a compound, you can calculate how much of each element is inside **any sample size** of that compound.

**Method:** Add the ratio parts to find the **total parts**, divide the sample mass by total parts to find the mass of **1 part**, and multiply by each element's parts.

### Example 4: How much H and O are in 9 g of water?

In water, H : O = 1 : 8, so total parts = 1 + 8 = **9 parts**.

- Mass of hydrogen = 9 g × (1 / 9) = **1 g**
- Mass of oxygen = 9 g × (8 / 9) = **8 g**

Check: 1 + 8 = 9 g ✅, and 8 ÷ 1 = 8 ✅.

### Example 5: How much H and O are in 72 g of water?

Total parts = 9. Mass of 1 part = 72 g ÷ 9 = 8 g.

- Mass of hydrogen = 1 × 8 g = **8 g**
- Mass of oxygen = 8 × 8 g = **64 g**

Check: 8 + 64 = 72 g ✅, and 64 ÷ 8 = 8 ✅.

### Example 6: How much carbon and oxygen are in 40 g of carbon dioxide?

In CO₂, C : O = 3 : 8, so total parts = 3 + 8 = **11 parts**. Mass of 1 part = 40 ÷ 11 ≈ 3.636 g.

- Mass of carbon = 3 × (40 / 11) ≈ **10.9 g**
- Mass of oxygen = 8 × (40 / 11) ≈ **29.1 g**

Check: 10.9 + 29.1 = 40.0 g ✅, and 29.1 ÷ 10.9 ≈ 2.67 = 8 / 3 ✅.

### Example 7: How much nitrogen and hydrogen are in 3.4 g of ammonia?

In NH₃, N : H = 14 : 3, so total parts = 14 + 3 = **17 parts**. Mass of 1 part = 3.4 g ÷ 17 = 0.2 g.

- Mass of nitrogen = 14 × 0.2 g = **2.8 g**
- Mass of hydrogen = 3 × 0.2 g = **0.6 g**

Check: 2.8 + 0.6 = 3.4 g ✅, and 2.8 : 0.6 = 28 : 6 = 14 : 3 ✅.

## Part D: Given One Element, How Much of the Other Reacts?

### Example 8: How much oxygen is needed to burn 6 g of carbon completely, and how much CO₂ forms?

In CO₂, C : O = 3 : 8. Since 6 g of carbon is **2 times** the 3 parts of carbon (6 = 3 × 2), we need **2 times** the 8 parts of oxygen:

- Mass of oxygen needed = 8 × 2 = **16 g**
- Mass of CO₂ formed = 6 g + 16 g = **22 g**

Check: 6 : 16 = 3 : 8 ✅, and 6 + 16 = 22 g ✅.

### Example 9: How much oxygen is needed to burn 5 g of hydrogen completely, and how much water forms?

In H₂O, H : O = 1 : 8. For 5 g of hydrogen, oxygen needed = 5 × 8 = **40 g**, and water formed = 5 g + 40 g = **45 g**.

Check: 5 : 40 = 1 : 8 ✅, and 5 + 40 = 45 g ✅ (matching row 3 of our table in Part A!).

### Example 10: What happens if one element is supplied in excess?

Suppose you mix **4 g of hydrogen** with **16 g of oxygen** and spark the reaction. Does the water change its ratio to 4 : 16 (1 : 4)? **Never!** Water can only form in the fixed 1 : 8 ratio: the 16 g of oxygen reacts with exactly **2 g of hydrogen** to form **18 g of water**, and the remaining **2 g of extra hydrogen simply stays behind unreacted**. You cannot force a compound to change its mass ratio!

### Example 11: Testing whether a sample is pure water

A student analyzes a 27 g sample of a clear liquid and finds 3 g of hydrogen and 24 g of oxygen. Could this be pure water?

- H : O = 3 : 24 = **1 : 8** ✅. Yes, this matches the fixed ratio of pure water.

A second student analyzes a 23 g sample of another liquid and finds 3 g of hydrogen and 20 g of oxygen. Could this be pure water?

- H : O = 3 : 20, which is **not** 1 : 8 (3 g of hydrogen would require 24 g of oxygen in pure water).
- Therefore, this sample is **not pure water** — it is either an impure mixture or a different substance altogether. (In fact, hydrogen and oxygen also form a second compound, hydrogen peroxide H₂O₂, in which the H : O mass ratio is 1 : 16.)

Check: 3 + 20 = 23 g ✅ (the masses add up, but the ratio proves it is not pure water).

## Part E: Compounds vs Mixtures

The Law of Constant Proportions applies **only to pure compounds**, never to **mixtures**. As we learned in Chapter 2, the components of a mixture can be blended in **any ratio**.

| Feature | Pure Compound (water, CO₂, NaCl) | Mixture (sugar water, salt and sand) |
|---|---|---|
| Proportion of components | Strictly fixed by mass | Can vary in any ratio |
| Example | H : O in water is always 1 : 8 | 5 g or 50 g of sugar in a glass of water |
| Separation | Only by chemical or electrochemical methods | By physical methods (evaporation, filtration) |
| Properties | Completely new properties, different from constituent elements | Retains the properties of its individual components |

```
   COMPOUND: pure water                 MIXTURE: sugar water

   H : O = 1 : 8                        1 glass water + 1 spoon sugar
   H : O = 1 : 8                        1 glass water + 3 spoons sugar
   H : O = 1 : 8                        1 glass water + 5 spoons sugar

   Ratio is identical every time.       Ratio can be anything you choose,
                                        and all three are "sugar water".
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Left: Water is a compound, so its H : O mass ratio is always 1 : 8. Right: Sugar dissolved in water is a mixture, so the ratio of sugar to water can be varied freely.

**Safe kitchen activity:** Dissolve 1 spoonful of sugar in one glass of clean drinking water and 3 spoonfuls in a second glass. Both are sugar-water solutions (one mildly sweet, one very sweet), proving that a mixture has no fixed ratio. Pure water itself, however, always has H : O = 1 : 8.

## Part F: Where Does the Fixed Mass Ratio Come From?

Why do pure compounds have such an unbreakable mass ratio? The answer lies in **atoms**! In the previous chapter, we saw that every molecule of water has the formula **H₂O** — **2 hydrogen atoms bonded to 1 oxygen atom** — and every atom of a given element has its own fixed mass. Because both the atom ratio and the atomic masses are fixed, the mass ratio of the compound must be fixed too!

Here is a quick preview using rounded atomic masses (H = 1, O = 16):

- Mass of 2 hydrogen atoms = 2 × 1 = **2 units**
- Mass of 1 oxygen atom = 1 × 16 = **16 units**
- Mass ratio H : O = 2 : 16 = **1 : 8** ✅

That is the exact **1 : 8** ratio Joseph Proust measured in the laboratory! In the same way:
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂):** 1 C (12) : 2 O (2 × 16 = 32) = 12 : 32 = **3 : 8** ✅.
- **Ammonia (NH₃):** 1 N (14) : 3 H (3 × 1 = 3) = **14 : 3** ✅.

```
   One molecule of H₂O:

        O (16 mass units)
       / \
      H   H   (1 unit + 1 unit = 2 mass units)

   H : O = 2 : 16 = 1 : 8   (by mass)
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Every molecule of water contains 2 H atoms (1 unit each) and 1 O atom (16 units), giving a mass ratio of 2 : 16 = 1 : 8. Whether you have 1 molecule or a trillion molecules, the mass ratio stays 1 : 8.

*(We will study atomic and molecular masses in detail in Topic 4.)*

## Part G: Another Law to Know by Name

In water (**H₂O**), the mass ratio H : O is **1 : 8**, whereas in hydrogen peroxide (**H₂O₂**), the mass ratio H : O is 2 : 32 = **1 : 16**. Notice that **the same two elements can combine in different ways to form more than one compound, and each compound has its own fixed mass ratio**. Moreover, the oxygen masses that combine with 1 g of hydrogen (8 g and 16 g) stand in a simple whole-number ratio (8 : 16 = 1 : 2). This relationship is called the **Law of Multiple Proportions**, which you will study in detail in higher classes.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing a sample's mass in grams with the simplified mass ratio.**
   "4 g of hydrogen" is a mass; "**1 : 8**" is a ratio. In 36 g of water there are 4 g of H and 32 g of O, which simplifies from 4 : 32 to **1 : 8**.

2. **Mistake 2: Leaving a ratio unsimplified.**
   In CO₂, 12 : 32 should be divided by 4 and written in its simplest whole-number form: **3 : 8**.

3. **Mistake 3: Dividing by one element's part instead of the sum of all parts.**
   To find the mass of hydrogen in 9 g of water (H : O = 1 : 8), do not multiply 9 × (1 / 8)! The total number of parts is 1 + 8 = **9**, so hydrogen is 9 × (1 / 9) = 1 g.

4. **Mistake 4: Applying the Law of Constant Proportions to mixtures.**
   Sugar water, salt water, and air are mixtures and can have variable proportions. The law applies **only to pure chemical compounds**.

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking that adding extra reactant changes the compound's ratio.**
   If you mix 4 g of H₂ with 16 g of O₂, the water formed still has H : O = 1 : 8 (2 g H + 16 g O = 18 g H₂O), and the extra 2 g of H₂ remains unreacted (Example 10).

6. **Mistake 6: Confusing two different compounds made of the same elements.**
   H₂O (1 : 8) and H₂O₂ (1 : 16) are two different compounds with different formulas and different properties; each one has its own constant ratio.

---

## Quick Recap

- **Law of Constant Proportions** (Joseph Proust): A pure chemical compound always contains the same elements combined in the same fixed proportion by mass, regardless of its source or method of preparation.
- Key ratios by mass: Water (**H₂O**): **H : O = 1 : 8**. Carbon dioxide (**CO₂**): **C : O = 3 : 8**. Ammonia (**NH₃**): **N : H = 14 : 3**. Sodium chloride (**NaCl**): **Na : Cl = 46 : 71** (≈ 39.3% Na and 60.7% Cl).
- To find a mass ratio: write the masses of each element, check that they add up to the total sample mass, and divide by their greatest common factor.
- To split a sample mass using a ratio: add the ratio parts, divide the total sample mass by the sum of parts, and multiply by each element's parts.
- Mixtures have variable composition; pure compounds have fixed composition.
- The fixed mass ratio comes from the compound's fixed formula and the fixed masses of its atoms (explored further in Topic 4).
- **Law of Multiple Proportions:** When two elements form more than one compound (like H₂O and H₂O₂), each compound has its own fixed ratio (studied in higher classes).

## Practice Questions

1. State the Law of Constant Proportions in your own words. Who proposed it?

2. A sample of pure water from a river has 6 g of hydrogen and 48 g of oxygen. Find the mass ratio H : O and the mass of the sample. Is this consistent with the law?

3. 24 g of carbon is completely burnt in oxygen to give 88 g of carbon dioxide. Find the mass of oxygen used and the C : O ratio.

4. How much hydrogen and oxygen are present in 54 g of water?

5. How much carbon and oxygen are present in 66 g of carbon dioxide?

6. In 34 g of ammonia, find the mass of nitrogen and hydrogen (N : H = 14 : 3).

7. How much oxygen is needed to burn 10 g of hydrogen completely, and how much water forms?

8. 8 g of hydrogen is mixed with 16 g of oxygen. The water formed still has H : O = 1 : 8 by mass. How much water forms, and how much hydrogen simply stays unreacted?

9. Two samples of a "pure compound of hydrogen and oxygen" give H : O = 1 : 8 and 1 : 16. Can both be water? Explain.

10. State True or False, and correct the false ones:
    (a) The ratio of hydrogen to oxygen in water depends on where the water comes from.
    (b) Sugar solution always has the same sugar to water ratio.
    (c) The Law of Constant Proportions holds for pure compounds.
    (d) The mass of carbon in 44 g of CO₂ is 32 g.

### Answers

1. **Law of Constant Proportions:** In any pure chemical compound, the constituent elements are always present in a fixed proportion by mass, regardless of the source or method of preparation of the compound. It was proposed by **Joseph Proust**.

2. H : O = 6 : 48 = **1 : 8**. Total mass of the sample = 6 g + 48 g = **54 g** ✅. Since the ratio simplifies to 1 : 8, it is completely consistent with the law ✅.

3. Mass of oxygen = 88 g − 24 g = **64 g**. Ratio C : O = 24 : 64; dividing both by 8 gives **3 : 8** ✅.

4. Total parts = 1 + 8 = 9. One part = 54 g ÷ 9 = 6 g. Hydrogen = 1 × 6 g = **6 g**; Oxygen = 8 × 6 g = **48 g**. Check: 6 + 48 = 54 g ✅.

5. In CO₂, C : O = 3 : 8, so total parts = 3 + 8 = 11. One part = 66 g ÷ 11 = 6 g. Carbon = 3 × 6 g = **18 g**; Oxygen = 8 × 6 g = **48 g**. Check: 18 + 48 = 66 g and 18 : 48 = 3 : 8 ✅.

6. Total parts = 14 + 3 = 17. One part = 34 g ÷ 17 = 2 g. Nitrogen = 14 × 2 g = **28 g**; Hydrogen = 3 × 2 g = **6 g**. Check: 28 + 6 = 34 g and 28 : 6 = 14 : 3 ✅.

7. Oxygen needed = 10 g × 8 = **80 g**. Water formed = 10 g + 80 g = **90 g**. Check: 80 ÷ 10 = 8 and 10 + 80 = 90 g ✅.

8. 16 g of oxygen requires 16 ÷ 8 = **2 g of hydrogen** to react in the 1 : 8 ratio. Thus, **18 g of water** (2 g + 16 g) forms, and 8 g − 2 g = **6 g of hydrogen stays unreacted**. Check: total mass before = 8 + 16 = 24 g; total mass after = 18 g water + 6 g unreacted H₂ = 24 g ✅.

9. No, both cannot be water. Pure water always has H : O = 1 : 8 by mass. The sample with H : O = 1 : 16 must be a different chemical compound of hydrogen and oxygen (such as hydrogen peroxide, H₂O₂).

10. (a) **False** ❌. Pure water always has H : O = 1 : 8 regardless of its source. (b) **False** ❌. Sugar solution is a mixture, so its sugar-to-water ratio can vary freely. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌. In 44 g of CO₂ (C : O = 3 : 8), there are 12 g of carbon and 32 g of oxygen.
