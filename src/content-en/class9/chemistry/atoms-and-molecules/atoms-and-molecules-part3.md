# Topic 3: Dalton's Atomic Theory

In the first two topics of this chapter, we learned two rock-solid experimental laws of chemistry. First: **total mass never changes in a chemical reaction** (Law of Conservation of Mass). Second: **every pure compound always contains the same elements in the same fixed proportion by mass**, regardless of its source (Law of Constant Proportions). Both laws were proved by careful weighing in the laboratory, yet a deeper question remained: **why does nature behave this way?** Is there one simple physical picture of matter that explains both laws at once? In this topic, we will discover that picture: **Dalton's Atomic Theory**.

---

## Cutting a Tray of Fudge: Where Does Division Stop?

Mr. Sullivan took his niece Maya to a neighborhood bakery where the confectioner had just set out a fresh square tray of chocolate fudge. Maya watched as the baker sliced the tray in half, then cut each half in half again, and then divided those pieces into smaller squares.

"Uncle Sullivan," Maya asked, "if someone kept slicing a piece of fudge in half over and over again with the sharpest knife imaginable, could they keep dividing it forever?"

Mr. Sullivan smiled. "Think about the two possibilities. Either you can keep halving the piece forever without ever hitting a limit, or eventually you reach a **smallest possible particle** that cannot be cut any further without ceasing to be what it is."

Maya squinted thoughtfully. "We couldn't actually slice something that small with a kitchen knife — it's a thought experiment!"

"Exactly," Mr. Sullivan said. "And more than two thousand years ago, thinkers in both ancient India and ancient Greece asked that exact same thought-experiment question. They didn't have modern microscopes or laboratory balances, but they reasoned about the ultimate building blocks of matter. Then, two hundred years ago, a scientist in England connected that ancient idea to laboratory weighing laws and turned it into a testable scientific theory. Let's trace the story."

```
   Whole tray      Half            Quarter           Small pieces      Smallest particle
   +---------+     +----+----+     +----+----+       +-+-+-+-+-+
   :         :     :    :    :     :    :    :       +-+-+-+-+-+       +-+
   :         : --> :    :    : --> +----+----+  -->  +-+-+-+-+-+  -->  +-+
   :         :     :    :    :     :    :    :       +-+-+-+-+-+   (cannot be cut further)
   +---------+     +----+----+     +----+----+       +-+-+-+-+-+
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Repeatedly dividing a piece of matter: whole tray → half → quarter → tiny pieces → an ultimate indivisible particle. The question of whether division stops at a smallest unit gave rise to the concept of the atom.

## Part A: A Long History of Thought: Kanada, Democritus, and Dalton

The question *"What is matter ultimately made of?"* has fascinated human beings for millennia.

**Kanada (Ancient India).** Around the 6th century BCE, the Indian philosopher **Maharishi Kanada** (founder of the *Vaisheshika* school of philosophy) reasoned that if you keep dividing matter into smaller and smaller pieces, you must eventually reach tiny particles that cannot be divided any further. He called these ultimate particles **parmanu** (and combinations of them **anu**), and proposed that different combinations of these particles give rise to the different forms of matter we see around us. Notice that these insights came from **philosophical reasoning and logic**, long before quantitative laboratory experiments existed.

**Democritus (Ancient Greece).** Around the 5th century BCE in Greece, philosophers **Leucippus and Democritus** arrived at a similar idea: if you divide matter repeatedly, you eventually reach a limit that cannot be cut further. Democritus called these ultimate particles ***atomos***, which in Greek means **"uncuttable" or "indivisible"**. Our modern English word **atom** comes directly from *atomos*.

Both traditions had a brilliant insight, but for over two thousand years the idea remained philosophical because no one had linked it to quantitative measurements in a laboratory.

**John Dalton (England, 1808).** By the early 1800s, chemists had discovered crisp quantitative laws by weighing chemical reactions — especially Lavoisier's **Law of Conservation of Mass** (Topic 1) and Proust's **Law of Constant Proportions** (Topic 2). In **1808**, the English schoolteacher and scientist **John Dalton** published his **Atomic Theory**, which transformed the ancient idea of indivisible particles into a **quantitative scientific theory** whose postulates directly explained the laws of chemical combination.

Remember: science builds step by step — from ancient philosophical questions (Kanada and Democritus) to careful laboratory weighing laws (Lavoisier and Proust) to a unifying scientific theory (Dalton).

## Part B: Dalton's Five Postulates in Plain Language

We can state the core ideas of Dalton's atomic theory as **five clear postulates**, labeled **P1 through P5** so we can refer to each one easily:

| No. | Postulate | In Plain Language |
|---|---|---|
| **P1** | All matter is made of very tiny particles called **atoms**, which are indivisible (cannot be broken down in chemical reactions). | The "smallest uncuttable piece" of matter |
| **P2** | All atoms of a **given element** are identical in mass and in chemical properties. | Every hydrogen atom is just like every other hydrogen atom |
| **P3** | Atoms of **different elements** have different masses and different chemical properties. | A hydrogen atom and an oxygen atom have different masses and behaviors |
| **P4** | Atoms of different elements combine in **small whole-number ratios** to form compounds. | 1 carbon atom + 2 oxygen atoms = CO₂ (never half an atom!) |
| **P5** | In a chemical reaction, atoms are **neither created nor destroyed**; they only **rearrange** how they are combined with one another. | Same LEGO bricks, rearranged into a new model |

Now let's see how these five postulates immediately explain the laws from Topics 1 and 2!

---

## Part C: Postulate P5 and the Law of Conservation of Mass

In Topic 1, we saw that total mass before a chemical reaction always equals total mass after the reaction. **Why?**

Postulate **P5** states that in a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed — they simply switch partners and rearrange. And Postulates **P2 and P3** tell us that each kind of atom has its own fixed mass. If the exact same atoms are present before and after the reaction, and each atom keeps its mass, then **the total mass cannot possibly change!** It is just like taking apart a LEGO house made of 50 bricks and using those exact same 50 bricks to build a LEGO spaceship — the total weight on a scale stays identical.

**Example 1:** Look at **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**. Suppose one H atom has a mass of 1 unit and one O atom has a mass of 16 units (we will define these mass units formally in Topic 4):

- **Before (Reactants):** 4 H atoms = 4 × 1 = 4 units; 2 O atoms = 2 × 16 = 32 units. Total = 4 + 32 = **36 units**.
- **After (Products):** Two H₂O molecules contain 4 H atoms (4 units) and 2 O atoms (32 units). Total = 4 + 32 = **36 units**.

Check: **36 = 36** ✅.

```
   Before (Reactants):             After (Products):

   (H)(H)   (H)(H)   (O)(O)        (H)(O)(H)    (H)(O)(H)
    H₂       H₂       O₂              H₂O          H₂O

   4 H atoms + 2 O atoms           4 H atoms + 2 O atoms
          The exact same 6 atoms, simply rearranged!
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Visualizing Postulate P5 in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: the left side has 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms, and the right side has the exact same 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms in new partnerships. Because no atoms are gained or lost, total mass is conserved.

**Example 2:** In **C + O₂ → CO₂** (Topic 1), 1 carbon atom and 2 oxygen atoms rearrange to form 1 molecule of CO₂ (containing 1 C and 2 O). Because the atoms are identical on both sides, 12 g of C + 32 g of O₂ must yield **44 g of CO₂**.

And remember: when an open bottle of fizzing water or burning paper seems to lose weight on a scale (Topic 1), Postulate P5 is not violated — the atoms simply floated away into the room as gas molecules!

## Part D: Postulates P2, P3, P4 and the Law of Constant Proportions

In Topic 2, we saw that pure water always has a hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio of **1 : 8**, whether it comes from an alpine stream, a tap, or a lab flame. **Why?**

Follow the logical chain from Dalton's postulates:

- Every molecule of pure water is formed by combining hydrogen and oxygen atoms in the same fixed whole-number ratio: **2 H atoms to 1 O atom** (**P4**).
- Every H atom has the same mass, and every O atom has the same mass (**P2** and **P3**).
- Therefore, every single molecule of water has the exact same mass ratio of H to O — and whether you collect 10 molecules or 10 trillion molecules, the overall mass ratio of pure water must always be **1 : 8**!

**Example 3 (Carbon dioxide, CO₂):** Take C = 12 units and O = 16 units.
- In **1 molecule** of CO₂ (1 C and 2 O): C = 12, O = 2 × 16 = 32 → mass ratio C : O = 12 : 32 = **3 : 8**.
- In **5 molecules** of CO₂: C = 5 × 12 = 60, O = 10 × 16 = 160 → ratio = 60 : 160 = **3 : 8**.
- In **100 molecules** of CO₂: C = 1200, O = 3200 → ratio = 1200 : 3200 = **3 : 8** ✅.

No matter how many molecules you take, the C : O mass ratio is always **3 : 8** — matching Topic 2!

**Example 4 (Water, H₂O):** In 1000 molecules of H₂O, mass of H = 2000 × 1 = 2000 units, and mass of O = 1000 × 16 = 16000 units. Ratio H : O = 2000 : 16000 = **1 : 8** ✅.

**Example 5 (Ammonia, NH₃):** Take N = 14 units and H = 1 unit. In 1 molecule of NH₃ (1 N and 3 H), N : H = **14 : 3**. In 100 molecules, N = 1400 and H = 300 → ratio = 1400 : 300 = **14 : 3** ✅.

**Why mixtures don't have a fixed ratio:** In a mixture like salt water or sugar water, the salt and water molecules do not bond together into a new compound formula; you can mix any number of sugar molecules with any number of water molecules. That is why the Law of Constant Proportions applies only to **pure compounds**.

*(Also note: Dalton's Postulate P4 — that atoms combine in simple whole-number ratios — predicted the **Law of Multiple Proportions** when two elements form more than one compound, such as CO and CO₂, which you will study in higher classes.)*

## Part E: Dalton's Picture Symbols vs Modern Letter Symbols

As we learned in the previous chapter, Dalton drew **circular picture symbols** for each element. Soon after, **Berzelius** replaced those drawings with **letter symbols** (H, O, C, N, S...), which are much easier to write, print, and combine into formulas like H₂O and CO₂.

```
   Element      Dalton's picture sketch    Modern Berzelius symbol

   Hydrogen        (.)                          H
   Oxygen          ( )                          O
   Carbon          (●)                          C
   Nitrogen        (|)                          N
   Sulphur         (+)                          S
```

> **Figure 3.3** – A simplified comparison of Dalton's circular picture symbols and modern letter symbols. Letter symbols made writing chemical formulas (such as H₂O and CO₂) fast and universal across all languages.

## Part F: What We Know Today: Where Dalton Was Right and Where His Theory Was Incomplete

Dalton's atomic theory was a towering achievement that laid the foundation for modern chemistry. Over the two centuries since 1808, however, new experimental discoveries have refined two of his postulates. Good science is honest about what a theory gets right and where it needs updating:

| Dalton's Postulate | Modern Scientific View |
|---|---|
| **P1:** Atoms are indivisible | **Modified.** Atoms are not indivisible; they contain even smaller subatomic particles (protons, neutrons, and electrons), which we will study in **Chapter 5 (Structure of the Atom)**. However, in ordinary *chemical reactions*, an atom's nucleus stays intact. |
| **P2:** All atoms of a given element have the exact same mass | **Modified.** Atoms of the same element can have slightly different masses (called **isotopes**, such as carbon-12 and carbon-14), which we will also study in **Chapter 5**. |
| **P3:** Atoms of different elements have different properties | **Holds true** (each element has its own unique atomic number and chemical behavior; brief exceptions in mass called *isobars* are covered in Chapter 5). |
| **P4:** Atoms combine in small whole-number ratios | **Holds true** for ordinary molecular and ionic compounds. |
| **P5:** Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions | **Holds true** for all chemical reactions (nuclear reactions, which change one element into another, are a separate process studied in physics). |

Updating P1 and P2 does not diminish Dalton's genius in the slightest — that is how science progresses! Today, specialized scanning tunneling microscopes can even produce images showing individual atoms arranged on a surface.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Saying "Dalton was the first person ever to think of atoms."**
   Philosophers like Kanada (*anu/parmanu*) and Democritus (*atomos*) proposed the concept of indivisible particles over two thousand years earlier. Dalton's breakthrough in **1808** was turning the atomic idea into a **quantitative scientific theory** that explained the mass laws of chemical reactions.

2. **Mistake 2: Treating "atoms cannot be divided at all" as modern fact.**
   Indivisibility was Dalton's original postulate (**P1**). Today we know atoms are made of protons, neutrons, and electrons (Chapter 5), though atoms still act as intact units during chemical reactions.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking an open-container weight loss disproves Postulate P5.**
   When gas escapes from an open container (Topic 1), those atoms were not destroyed — they simply moved into the surrounding air.

4. **Mistake 4: Applying the Law of Constant Proportions to mixtures.**
   A salt solution can have any concentration of salt because it is a mixture, not a chemical compound formed by P4.

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking half-atoms can take part in a reaction.**
   Postulate **P4** reminds us that only whole numbers of atoms bond together — you can never have 1.5 oxygen atoms in a molecule!

## Quick Recap

- In ancient India, **Kanada** (*Vaisheshika* philosophy) proposed indivisible particles called **parmanu / anu**, and in ancient Greece, **Democritus** used the word ***atomos*** ("uncuttable") — both based on philosophical reasoning.
- In **1808**, **John Dalton** published his **Atomic Theory**, connecting atoms to experimental laws of mass through five postulates (**P1 to P5**):
  - **P1:** Matter consists of tiny indivisible atoms.
  - **P2:** All atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties.
  - **P3:** Atoms of different elements have different masses and properties.
  - **P4:** Atoms combine in small whole-number ratios to form compounds.
  - **P5:** Atoms are neither created nor destroyed in chemical reactions; they only rearrange.
- **P5 (with P2 and P3)** explains the **Law of Conservation of Mass**: same atoms before and after means the exact same total mass (e.g., 36 units = 36 units in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O).
- **P2, P3, and P4** explain the **Law of Constant Proportions**: fixed whole-number atom ratios with fixed atomic masses produce fixed mass ratios (H₂O = 1 : 8, CO₂ = 3 : 8, NH₃ = 14 : 3).
- Dalton's circular picture symbols were replaced by Berzelius's universal **letter symbols**.
- Modern updates (explored in **Chapter 5**): atoms contain subatomic particles (updating P1) and can have isotopes with slightly different masses (updating P2), while P3, P4, and P5 (for chemical reactions) continue to hold.
