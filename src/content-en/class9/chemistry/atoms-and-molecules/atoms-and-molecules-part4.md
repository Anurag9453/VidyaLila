# Topic 4: Atomic Mass, Molecular Mass, and Formula Unit Mass

In the previous topic, Dalton told us that atoms of each element have a characteristic, **fixed mass**, and this explained why elements combine in fixed proportions by mass. But a fundamental question immediately arises: exactly **how much** does a single atom weigh? And how could scientists possibly weigh a particle that is far too tiny to see? In this topic, we will learn how chemists express the masses of microscopic atoms and molecules using a convenient **relative comparison scale**, and how reading a chemical formula lets us calculate both the overall mass of a molecule and the exact mass ratio of its constituent elements.

---

## The Grain Market Scale and Standard Reference Weights

Maya accompanied her uncle, Mr. Carter, to an agricultural wholesale grain market. In the middle of the warehouse stood a large two-pan beam balance. On one side rested a heavy sack of grain; on the other sat solid iron **standard weights**: 1 kg, 5 kg, and 10 kg.

Maya watched the merchant balance sack after sack using the exact same iron weights. She asked, "Uncle Carter, how do we know how heavy the iron weight itself is? And what would happen if every shopkeeper used a different chunk of iron?"

Mr. Carter smiled. "That is precisely why international standards exist. Everyone agrees on a **standard reference mass** — like the standard kilogram. When we weigh the sack of grain, we are not measuring some isolated, magical number out of thin air; we are **comparing** the grain against our chosen standard: 'this sack has the mass of ten 1-kilogram reference weights.' Now, think about this: how could you ever place a single **atom** on a balance like that?"

Maya considered the problem: a single grain of rice is barely noticeable on a heavy platform balance, and an atom is **trillions upon trillions of times smaller** than a grain of rice! Mr. Carter nodded. "Exactly. No balance on Earth can directly weigh a single atom. So chemists do what grain merchants do: they choose a **standard reference atom**, and then express the mass of every other atom as a **comparison** against that standard — telling us how many times heavier or lighter it is. Let's see what that standard is."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atomic mass unit (u)** | The standard unit for expressing atomic masses; defined as exactly 1/12 the mass of one carbon-12 atom | 1 u |
| **Reference atom** | The chosen standard atom against which the masses of all other atoms are compared | Carbon-12 (¹²C) |
| **Relative atomic mass** | How many times heavier an atom of an element is compared to 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom | O = 16, Na = 23 |
| **Molecular mass** | The sum of the relative atomic masses of all the atoms present in one molecule of a substance (in u) | H₂O = 18 u |
| **Formula unit** | The simplest whole-number ratio of ions represented in an ionic compound's formula | NaCl, CaCO₃ |
| **Formula unit mass** | The sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in one formula unit of an ionic compound (in u) | NaCl = 58.5 u |

## Part A: Why Weighing a Single Atom Directly Is Impossible

A single hydrogen atom has an actual mass of approximately:

$$0.00000000000000000000000167\text{ g}\quad (1.67 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g})$$

Writing out twenty-three zeroes before every calculation is horribly clumsy and impractical, and no laboratory balance could ever register such a miniscule mass. Expressing atomic masses directly in everyday grams is therefore completely impractical for working chemistry.

The elegant alternative is **relative comparison**. Just as at the grain market, chemists ask: *"How many times heavier is an oxygen atom than a hydrogen atom?"* This relative comparison does not require grams at all — it gives a simple, clean, dimensionless ratio.

---

## Part B: The Carbon Standard and the Atomic Mass Unit (u)

For over a century, chemists experimented with different standards (first hydrogen, then oxygen). In 1961, international scientific bodies agreed upon the universal standard used today: the **carbon-12 atom** (an isotope of carbon whose nucleus contains 6 protons and 6 neutrons, as we will explore in Chapter 5). Carbon was chosen because it forms millions of stable compounds and provides an exceptionally precise, practical standard across the periodic table.

The fundamental unit of atomic mass was defined as follows:

> **1 atomic mass unit (1 u) = exactly 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom**

Under this standard, **one carbon-12 atom has a mass of exactly 12 u**. To find the mass of any other atom, we simply ask: *"How many times heavier is this atom than 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom?"* That resulting number is called the element's **relative atomic mass**.

For example, hydrogen has a relative atomic mass of 1, and oxygen has 16. This means a single oxygen atom is **16 times heavier** than a hydrogen atom (and 16 times heavier than 1 u).

```
   Relative atomic mass (each . = 1 u)

   H   1    .
   C   12   ............  <- reference standard (carbon-12)
   N   14   ..............
   O   16   ................
   Na  23   .......................
   Cl  35.5 ...................................
   Fe  56   ........................................................
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Relative atomic masses of several common elements compared to the carbon-12 reference standard (12 u); each dot represents 1 u (chlorine's fractional half-unit is not shown as a dot). This is a scale of **relative mass**, comparing atoms to one another just like weights on a beam balance.

An important note: because relative atomic mass is a comparison ratio, it is fundamentally a pure number. When we assign it a physical unit for calculation, we write it in unified atomic mass units, **u** (e.g., oxygen = 16 u).

## Part D: Molecular Mass: Calculating the Mass of a Molecule

The **molecular mass** of a substance is the sum of the atomic masses of all the individual atoms present in one molecule of that substance.

The calculation method is straightforward:
1. Write down the chemical formula.
2. Check the subscript of each element to count its atoms.
3. Multiply each atom count by that element's atomic mass.
4. Add all the values together.

### Example 1: Water (H₂O)

Water contains 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.
- H: 2 × 1 = 2 u
- O: 1 × 16 = 16 u
- Total: 2 + 16 = **18 u**

Check: (2 × 1) + (1 × 16) = 18 u ✅

### Example 2: Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

- C: 1 × 12 = 12 u
- O: 2 × 16 = 32 u
- Total: 12 + 32 = **44 u**

Check: 12 + 32 = 44 u ✅

### Example 3: Ammonia (NH₃)

- N: 1 × 14 = 14 u
- H: 3 × 1 = 3 u
- Total: 14 + 3 = **17 u**

Check: 14 + 3 = 17 u ✅

### Example 4: Methane (CH₄)

- C: 1 × 12 = 12 u
- H: 4 × 1 = 4 u
- Total: 12 + 4 = **16 u**

Check: 12 + 4 = 16 u ✅

### Example 5: Sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄)

- H: 2 × 1 = 2 u
- S: 1 × 32 = 32 u
- O: 4 × 16 = 64 u
- Total: 2 + 32 + 64 = **98 u**

Check: 2 + 32 + 64 = 98 u ✅

### Example 6: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)

- C: 6 × 12 = 72 u
- H: 12 × 1 = 12 u
- O: 6 × 16 = 96 u
- Total: 72 + 12 + 96 = **180 u**

Check: 72 + 12 + 96 = 180 u ✅

---

## Part E: Molecular Mass of Diatomic and Polyatomic Elements

Recall from our study of atomicity that several non-metallic elements do not exist as isolated single atoms in nature; they exist as diatomic or polyatomic **molecules**. Their molecular mass is calculated in the exact same way:

### Example 7: Oxygen gas (O₂) and ozone (O₃)

- Oxygen gas, O₂: 2 × 16 = **32 u**
- Ozone gas, O₃: 3 × 16 = **48 u**

Both substances are made solely of oxygen, but because they have different molecular structures (atomicity of 2 vs 3), their molecular masses are distinct.

Check: 2 × 16 = 32 u ✅, 3 × 16 = 48 u ✅

### Example 8: Phosphorus (P₄) and sulphur (S₈)

- White phosphorus, P₄: 4 × 31 = **124 u**
- Octa-atomic sulphur, S₈: 8 × 32 = **256 u**

Check: 4 × 31 = 124 u ✅, 8 × 32 = 256 u ✅

> **Important distinction:** The **atomic mass** of oxygen is 16 u (the mass of one single O atom), while the **molecular mass** of oxygen gas (O₂) is 32 u. Whenever a problem mentions "oxygen," always read carefully to see whether it refers to the element's individual atoms or the diatomic gas molecule!

## Part F: Formula Unit Mass: For Ionic Compounds

In ionic compounds such as sodium chloride (NaCl) or calcium carbonate (CaCO₃), there are no separate, isolated, discrete "molecules." Instead, positively charged ions (cations) and negatively charged ions (anions) are packed in an alternating, continuous three-dimensional **crystal lattice**. The formula simply shows the simplest ratio in which these ions combine.

```
   Sodium Chloride Crystal (simplified cross-section)

   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺
   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻
   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺

   Formula NaCl = simplest ratio of 1 : 1
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Simplified schematic of a sodium chloride crystal lattice: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions alternate continuously throughout the crystal. There is no isolated, standalone "NaCl molecule"; the formula NaCl represents the simplest ratio of 1 : 1 between sodium and chloride ions.

Because no distinct molecules exist in ionic lattices, chemists use the term **formula unit** rather than "molecule." The formula unit is the simplest set of ions indicated by the chemical formula: for table salt, one formula unit is 1 Na⁺ ion and 1 Cl⁻ ion.

The **formula unit mass** is the sum of the atomic masses of all the atoms in one formula unit of the compound. The arithmetic is identical to calculating molecular mass; only the terminology changes to reflect the ionic nature of the substance.

### Example 9: Table salt (NaCl)

- Na: 1 × 23 = 23 u
- Cl: 1 × 35.5 = 35.5 u
- Total: 23 + 35.5 = **58.5 u**

Check: 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u ✅

### Example 10: Limestone (CaCO₃)

- Ca: 1 × 40 = 40 u
- C: 1 × 12 = 12 u
- O: 3 × 16 = 48 u
- Total: 40 + 12 + 48 = **100 u**

Check: 40 + 12 + 48 = 100 u ✅

### Example 11: Potassium carbonate (K₂CO₃)

- K: 2 × 39 = 78 u
- C: 1 × 12 = 12 u
- O: 3 × 16 = 48 u
- Total: 78 + 12 + 48 = **138 u**

Check: 78 + 12 + 48 = 138 u ✅

## Part G: Dealing with Brackets and Subscripts

When a formula contains parentheses enclosing a polyatomic ion, the subscript outside the closing bracket multiplies **every atom inside the bracket**. Always expand the bracket carefully before computing the mass.

### Example 12: Calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂)

The OH (hydroxide) group is taken twice, so we have: 1 Ca atom, 2 O atoms, and 2 H atoms.
- Ca: 1 × 40 = 40 u
- O: 2 × 16 = 32 u
- H: 2 × 1 = 2 u
- Total: 40 + 32 + 2 = **74 u**

Check: 40 + 32 + 2 = 74 u ✅

### Example 13: Ammonium sulphate ((NH₄)₂SO₄)

The ammonium group (NH₄) is taken twice: 2 × 1 = 2 N atoms, and 2 × 4 = 8 H atoms. Then 1 S atom and 4 O atoms.
- N: 2 × 14 = 28 u
- H: 8 × 1 = 8 u
- S: 1 × 32 = 32 u
- O: 4 × 16 = 64 u
- Total: 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = **132 u**

Check: 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = 132 u ✅

### Example 14: Aluminium sulphate (Al₂(SO₄)₃)

The sulphate group (SO₄) is taken 3 times: 3 × 1 = 3 S atoms, and 3 × 4 = 12 O atoms. Aluminium has a subscript of 2.
- Al: 2 × 27 = 54 u
- S: 3 × 32 = 96 u
- O: 12 × 16 = 192 u
- Total: 54 + 96 + 192 = **342 u**

Check: 54 + 96 + 192 = 342 u ✅

```
   Step-by-step calculation for Al₂(SO₄)₃

   Step 1: Expand brackets and count each atom
           Al₂(SO₄)₃  -->  Al = 2,  S = 3,  O = 4 × 3 = 12

   Step 2: Multiply by atomic masses
           Al: 2  × 27  =  54 u
           S : 3  × 32  =  96 u
           O : 12 × 16  = 192 u
                          -----
   Step 3: Sum the terms
           54 + 96 + 192 = 342 u

           +-------+------------+------------------------+
           | Al 54 |    S 96    |         O 192          |
           +-------+------------+------------------------+
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Step-by-step breakdown of Al₂(SO₄)₃: first expand the brackets to find the total atom count (2 Al, 3 S, 12 O), multiply each by its respective atomic mass, and sum them to obtain 342 u. The lower proportion bar illustrates the contribution of each element to the total mass.

## Part H: Finding Mass Ratios Directly From Chemical Formulas

In Topic 2, we learned through Proust's experiments that pure water always has a hydrogen-to-oxygen mass ratio of 1 : 8. Back then, scientists determined that ratio by lab measurements. Now that we understand atomic masses, **we can deduce the exact mass ratio directly from the chemical formula**, without doing any wet chemistry!

The procedure:
1. Write down the total mass contributed by each element: $\text{number of atoms} \times \text{atomic mass}$.
2. Form the ratio of those masses and simplify to simplest whole numbers.

### Example 15: Water (H₂O)

- Total mass of H: 2 × 1 = 2 u
- Total mass of O: 1 × 16 = 16 u
- Mass ratio H : O = 2 : 16 = **1 : 8**

Check: 2 / 16 simplifies to 1 / 8 ✅. This confirms Proust's law directly from the formula!

### Example 16: Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

- Total mass of C: 1 × 12 = 12 u
- Total mass of O: 2 × 16 = 32 u
- Mass ratio C : O = 12 : 32 = **3 : 8** (dividing both by 4)

Check: 12 ÷ 4 = 3 and 32 ÷ 4 = 8 ✅

### Example 17: Ammonia (NH₃)

- Total mass of N: 1 × 14 = 14 u
- Total mass of H: 3 × 1 = 3 u
- Mass ratio N : H = **14 : 3**

Check: Matches the empirical 14 : 3 ratio found in Topic 2 ✅

### Example 18: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)

- C: 6 × 12 = 72 u
- H: 12 × 1 = 12 u
- O: 6 × 16 = 96 u
- Ratio C : H : O = 72 : 12 : 96 = **6 : 1 : 8** (dividing all terms by 12)

Check: 72 ÷ 12 = 6, 12 ÷ 12 = 1, 96 ÷ 12 = 8 ✅

### Example 19: Working backward — finding component masses from total mass

A laboratory sample contains 36 g of pure water. How many grams of hydrogen and how many grams of oxygen does it contain?

- Molecular mass of water = 18 u, where H contributes 2 units and O contributes 16 units.
- Mass of hydrogen = $36\text{ g} \times \frac{2}{18} = \mathbf{4\text{ g}}$
- Mass of oxygen = $36\text{ g} \times \frac{16}{18} = \mathbf{32\text{ g}}$

Check: 4 g + 32 g = 36 g ✅. Ratio 4 : 32 = 1 : 8 ✅

Now the deeper truth behind the Law of Constant Proportions is crystal clear: the mass proportion is constant because **the chemical formula is constant** (2 H atoms for every 1 O atom) and **the atomic masses are constant** (H = 1 u, O = 16 u). Consequently, every pure sample of water anywhere in the universe must yield the exact same 1 : 8 ratio!

## Part I: A Crucial Distinction: Mass vs Atom Count

Notice that the molecular mass of H₂O is 18 u. This does **not** mean that a water molecule contains 18 atoms! One molecule of water contains only **3 atoms** (two hydrogen and one oxygen). The number 18 u represents its **mass**, not its count of particles.

Consider an everyday analogy: a 1 kg bag of large oranges and a 1 kg bag of tiny blueberries weigh the exact same amount on a scale, but the count of individual fruits inside is vastly different! Similarly, a heavier atom and a lighter atom have different masses, even though each is a single atom.

This brings up a fascinating puzzle: if we take 12 g of carbon and 16 g of oxygen, do both piles contain the exact same number of atoms? The answer is profound, and it forms the gateway to our next topic.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Forgetting to multiply subscripts inside brackets**
   In $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$, the count of oxygen atoms is not 4; it is $4 \times 3 = \mathbf{12}$. Always distribute the outer subscript to every atom within the parentheses before summing.

2. **Mistake 2: Confusing unified atomic mass units (u) with grams (g)**
   1 u is an unimaginably tiny fraction of a gram ($1.66 \times 10^{-24}\text{ g}$). One molecule of water weighs 18 u, not 18 grams!

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing atomic mass with molecular mass for diatomic elements**
   An oxygen atom has an atomic mass of 16 u, but an oxygen gas molecule ($\text{O}_2$) has a molecular mass of $\mathbf{32\text{ u}}$. This is one of the most frequent examination mistakes.

4. **Mistake 4: Calling ionic lattices "molecules"**
   Table salt ($\text{NaCl}$) does not form discrete molecules; it forms an extended ionic lattice. The correct scientific term is **formula unit mass**, though the numerical calculation method is identical.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating mass as atom count**
   $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18\text{ u}$ means one molecule has a mass of 18 u; it contains only 3 atoms.

6. **Mistake 6: Forgetting to multiply by subscripts in formulas**
   For $\text{CO}_2$, the mass is not $12 + 16 = 28\text{ u}$; it is $12 + (2 \times 16) = \mathbf{44\text{ u}}$.

## Quick Recap

- Single atoms cannot be placed on a weighing balance; chemists determine their masses by **relative comparison**, just as bulk goods are weighed against standard brass weights.
- The internationally accepted reference standard is the **carbon-12 atom**: exactly 1/12 of its mass equals **1 atomic mass unit (1 u)**. One carbon-12 atom has a mass of 12 u.
- **Relative atomic mass** indicates how many times heavier an atom is than 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom. Common rounded values: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, Al = 27, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, K = 39, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Cu = 63.5, Zn = 65, Ag = 108.
- **Molecular mass** is the sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a molecule: $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18\text{ u}$, $\text{CO}_2 = 44\text{ u}$, $\text{NH}_3 = 17\text{ u}$, $\text{CH}_4 = 16\text{ u}$, $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 = 98\text{ u}$, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 = 180\text{ u}$, $\text{O}_2 = 32\text{ u}$, $\text{O}_3 = 48\text{ u}$, $\text{P}_4 = 124\text{ u}$, $\text{S}_8 = 256\text{ u}$.
- **Formula unit mass** is used for ionic compounds that form extended lattices without discrete molecules: $\text{NaCl} = 58.5\text{ u}$, $\text{CaCO}_3 = 100\text{ u}$, $\text{Ca(OH)}_2 = 74\text{ u}$, $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3 = 342\text{ u}$, $(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4 = 132\text{ u}$, $\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3 = 138\text{ u}$.
- Subscripts outside brackets multiply every atom inside the bracket.
- Chemical formulas directly yield mass ratios: in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{H} : \text{O} = 2 : 16 = 1 : 8$; in $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{C} : \text{O} = 12 : 32 = 3 : 8$; in $\text{NH}_3$, $\text{N} : \text{H} = 14 : 3$.
- Particle mass (in u) and particle count are fundamentally different concepts.

9. A chemical sample contains 90 g of pure water. How many grams of hydrogen and how many grams of oxygen does it contain in total?

10. State True or False, and correct the false statements:
    (a) The molecular mass of $\text{O}_2$ gas is 16 u.
    (b) $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18\text{ u}$ means a water molecule contains 18 atoms.
    (c) Relative atomic mass is a comparison ratio and is fundamentally dimensionless, though we write particle masses with the unit "u".
    (d) 1 u is defined as 1/12 the mass of a carbon-12 atom.

### Answers

1. A single atom is unimaginably tiny (having an actual mass of around $10^{-24}\text{ g}$), which is far beyond the detection limit of any balance. Chemists use **relative comparison**: they select a reference atom (carbon-12) and determine how many times heavier each atom is relative to 1/12 of that reference standard, just like comparing grain sacks to standard reference weights.

2. 1 atomic mass unit (1 u) is defined as exactly 1/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom. Therefore, the mass of one carbon-12 atom is **12 u**.

3. It means that one oxygen atom is 16 times heavier than 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom (1 u). Because a hydrogen atom has a mass of 1 u, an oxygen atom is **16 times heavier** than a hydrogen atom.

4. (a) $\text{CH}_4$: $(1 \times 12) + (4 \times 1) = 12 + 4 = \mathbf{16\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (b) $\text{NH}_3$: $(1 \times 14) + (3 \times 1) = 14 + 3 = \mathbf{17\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (c) $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$: $(2 \times 1) + (1 \times 32) + (4 \times 16) = 2 + 32 + 64 = \mathbf{98\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (d) $\text{O}_3$: $3 \times 16 = \mathbf{48\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (e) $\text{P}_4$: $4 \times 31 = \mathbf{124\text{ u}}$ ✅

5. (a) $\text{NaCl}$: $23 + 35.5 = \mathbf{58.5\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (b) $\text{K}_2\text{CO}_3$: $(2 \times 39) + 12 + (3 \times 16) = 78 + 12 + 48 = \mathbf{138\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (c) $\text{Ca(OH)}_2$: $40 + 2 \times (16 + 1) = 40 + 32 + 2 = \mathbf{74\text{ u}}$ ✅
   (d) $(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4$: $(2 \times 14) + (8 \times 1) + 32 + (4 \times 16) = 28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = \mathbf{132\text{ u}}$ ✅

6. For $\text{Al}_2(\text{SO}_4)_3$:
   $\text{Al}: 2 \times 27 = 54\text{ u}$
   $\text{S}: 3 \times 32 = 96\text{ u}$
   $\text{O}: 12 \times 16 = 192\text{ u}$
   Total = $54 + 96 + 192 = \mathbf{342\text{ u}}$ ✅
   The student failed to distribute the outer subscript 3 to the oxygen atoms inside the bracket. The correct oxygen count is $4 \times 3 = \mathbf{12}$.

7. $\text{NaCl}$ does not exist as separate, isolated molecules. Instead, $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions are arranged in an infinite, alternating 3D crystal lattice in a 1 : 1 ratio. Therefore, chemists refer to the simplest repeating unit as a **formula unit** rather than a molecule.

8. (a) $\text{CO}_2$: $\text{C} = 12$, $\text{O} = 2 \times 16 = 32$. Ratio $\text{C} : \text{O} = 12 : 32 = \mathbf{3 : 8}$ ✅
   (b) $\text{NH}_3$: $\text{N} = 14$, $\text{H} = 3 \times 1 = 3$. Ratio $\text{N} : \text{H} = \mathbf{14 : 3}$ ✅
   Because the chemical formula is fixed and the atomic masses of the elements are fixed, the resulting mass ratio is always constant, which is the foundational basis of the Law of Constant Proportions.

9. In water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), the molecular mass is 18 u, where H contributes 2 parts and O contributes 16 parts.
   Mass of hydrogen = $90 \times \frac{2}{18} = \mathbf{10\text{ g}}$
   Mass of oxygen = $90 \times \frac{16}{18} = \mathbf{80\text{ g}}$
   Check: $10\text{ g} + 80\text{ g} = 90\text{ g}$ ✅; ratio $10 : 80 = 1 : 8$ ✅

10. (a) **False** ❌. An individual oxygen atom has a mass of 16 u, but diatomic oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$) has a molecular mass of $2 \times 16 = \mathbf{32\text{ u}}$.
    (b) **False** ❌. 18 u is the molecular mass of one water molecule; it contains only **3 atoms** (2 H and 1 O).
    (c) **True** ✅.
    (d) **True** ✅.
