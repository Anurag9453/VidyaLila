# Topic 5: The Mole: Counting Particles by Weighing

In the previous topic, we learned how to express the mass of individual atoms and molecules on the atomic scale: a carbon atom has a mass of 12 u, a water molecule has 18 u, and a sodium chloride formula unit has 58.5 u. (We use standard rounded atomic masses throughout: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, Cl = 35.5.)

Yet a massive practical puzzle remains. In school laboratories and industrial manufacturing plants, chemists never pick up single atoms with microscopic tweezers! We weigh substances out in **grams** and **kilograms** on ordinary balances. What, then, is the **bridge** connecting the macroscopic mass in grams on our bench scale to the microscopic count of atoms inside the beaker? At the end of Topic 4, we posed this exact challenge: how many molecules are in 18 g of water, and do 12 g of carbon and 16 g of oxygen contain the exact same count of atoms? That indispensable bridge is called the **mole**, and we will discover how it works here.

---

## The Hardware Store and Counting by Weighing

Leo accompanied his uncle, Mr. Adams, to a large hardware and fasteners warehouse. Mr. Adams needed tiny brass machine screws for a cabinetry project. Walking up to the counter, he told the storekeeper, "Good morning! I need exactly 2,500 of these brass screws, please."

Leo watched in amazement, expecting the storekeeper to sit down and tediously count out two thousand five hundred screws one by one! Instead, the storekeeper pulled out a sensitive digital electronic balance. He counted out just **50 screws**, placed them on the pan, and noted the reading: **100 g**.

He quickly did the math in his head: "Fifty screws weigh 100 grams, so one screw averages 2 grams. For 2,500 screws, that is $2500 \times 2\text{ g} = 5000\text{ g}$, or **5 kilograms**." The storekeeper placed an empty box on the scale, scooped screws until the scale read exactly 5.00 kg, and handed the box over. The entire transaction took less than a minute!

Leo was astonished: "Uncle Adams, he just counted out 2,500 screws without counting them individually!"

Mr. Adams laughed. "That is the power of **counting by weighing**. Whenever individual objects are identical in mass, you can determine their count directly from their total mass on a scale. In chemistry, atoms and molecules are far too microscopic to ever see, let alone count one by one. So chemists do the exact same thing: **we count atoms by weighing them**. And the master tool for that job is the mole."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mole (mol)** | The SI unit for measuring the amount of substance; a counting package representing $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles | $1\text{ mol} = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles |
| **Avogadro constant ($N_A$)** | The exact number of defined elementary entities contained in one mole of any substance | $6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ particles/mol}$ |
| **Molar mass ($M$)** | The mass of one mole of a substance expressed in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$) | C: $12\text{ g/mol}$; $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $18\text{ g/mol}$ |
| **$n$** | The symbol representing the number of moles | $n = 2\text{ mol}$ |
| **$m$** | The given or measured mass of a chemical sample in grams | $m = 36\text{ g}$ |
| **$M$** | The molar mass of the substance in $\text{g/mol}$ | $M = 18\text{ g/mol}$ |
| **$N$** | The actual count of individual particles (atoms, molecules, or ions) | $N = 1.204 \times 10^{24}$ molecules |

## Part A: Converting Particle Count to Mass

Just like the screws at the hardware store, the underlying logic is simple: **if you know the mass of one particle and the total mass of the collection, you can determine the count.**

However, a single carbon atom has a mass of only about $2 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$. You could never place a single atom on an electronic scale! Therefore, chemists assemble an **enormous, fixed collection of particles** — a macroscopic package — and weigh the entire package at once.

```
   Hardware Store Counter                 Chemistry Laboratory

   50 screws   =  100 g                   6.022 × 10²³ carbon atoms  =  12 g
   1 screw     =  2 g                     (one standard chemical package, or 1 mole)
   5000 g      =  2500 screws

   Scale  ──►  Count of screws            Scale  ──►  Count of atoms
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Counting by weighing: at the hardware store, weighing 50 screws (100 g) reveals that one screw is 2 g, so a 5 kg box contains 2,500 screws. In chemistry, weighing 12 g of carbon yields exactly $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms. The laboratory scale directly measures the count of microscopic particles!

The big question now is: exactly how large must this standard chemical counting package be, and can the exact same package number work for every element and compound in the universe? Let's begin with familiar everyday counting units.

## Part B: The Pair, the Dozen, the Gross, and the Mole

In daily life, we use convenient grouping words to count items:
- **1 pair** = 2 items (shoes, socks, gloves)
- **1 dozen** = 12 items (eggs, bananas, baked rolls)
- **1 gross** = 144 items, or 12 dozen (pencils, buttons)

Notice that a "dozen" is strictly a **count**, not a mass. A dozen chicken eggs and a dozen full-grown elephants are both collections of exactly 12 items, but their masses are completely different! A dozen elephants weigh tens of thousands of kilograms, while a dozen eggs weigh less than one kilogram.

**The mole is simply the chemist's version of a dozen.** The only difference is that because atoms are unimaginably tiny, the chemist's "dozen" has to be unimaginably huge:

$$\mathbf{1\text{ mole} = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ particles}}$$

These elementary particles can be atoms, molecules, or formula units (for ionic lattices). Just as saying "one dozen" requires you to specify what you are counting (a dozen eggs, a dozen roses), saying "one mole" requires you to specify the particle:
- 1 mole of carbon atoms
- 1 mole of water molecules
- 1 mole of sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) formula units

This number is named the **Avogadro constant** (symbol $N_A$), named in honor of the Italian scientist **Amedeo Avogadro**, whose early insights into gases laid the foundation for molecular theory. Written out in full, it is:

$$\mathbf{602,200,000,000,000,000,000,000}$$

Why this particular number? Because it was deliberately defined so that the numerical mass of a single particle in atomic mass units (u) matches the mass of one entire mole of those particles in **grams**! We will explore this beautiful match in Part D.

## Part C: How Big Is Avogadro's Number?

To truly appreciate the sheer scale of $6.022 \times 10^{23}$, consider these illuminating analogies:

1. **Counting one particle per second:**
   Suppose you sat down and counted individual particles at a speed of one particle per second, without ever pausing to eat or sleep. In one year, there are approximately $3.15 \times 10^7$ seconds. To reach $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles would take:
   $$\frac{6.022 \times 10^{23}}{3.15 \times 10^7\text{ s/year}} \approx \mathbf{1.9 \times 10^{16}\text{ years}}$$
   The estimated age of the entire universe is approximately $1.4 \times 10^{10}$ years. You would need to count for **over one million times the age of the universe** just to count out a single mole of atoms!

2. **Distributed among the human population:**
   Earth's human population is approximately 8 billion ($8 \times 10^9$) people. If a single mole of marbles were shared equally among every human on Earth, each person would receive roughly **$7.5 \times 10^{13}$ marbles** — about 75 trillion marbles for every man, woman, and child on the planet!

3. **A single drop of water:**
   A standard small droplet of water has a mass of approximately 0.05 g. That single droplet contains approximately **$1.7 \times 10^{21}$ water molecules** — which is less than 0.3% of one mole! A full mole of water is only **18 grams** — a modest single sip that easily fits inside a small paper cup!

All of these comparisons demonstrate one central truth: **atoms and molecules are so mind-bogglingly small that an astronomical count of them easily fits in the palm of your hand.**

## Part D: The Mass of One Mole: Molar Mass

Here is the brilliant core of the mole concept: **the mass of one mole of any substance in grams has the exact same numerical value as its atomic, molecular, or formula unit mass in unified atomic mass units (u)!**

- The atomic mass of carbon is 12 u $\rightarrow$ **1 mole of carbon atoms = 12 g**.
- The molecular mass of water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$) is 18 u $\rightarrow$ **1 mole of water molecules = 18 g**.
- The formula unit mass of sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$) is 58.5 u $\rightarrow$ **1 mole of $\text{NaCl}$ formula units = 58.5 g**.

The mass of one mole of a substance is called its **molar mass**, denoted by the capital letter **$M$**. Its standard unit is **grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$)**.
- For carbon: $M = 12\text{ g/mol}$
- For water: $M = 18\text{ g/mol}$
- For table salt: $M = 58.5\text{ g/mol}$
- For oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$): $M = 32\text{ g/mol}$

```
   The mass of 1 mole of different substances (Molar Mass)

   Carbon (C)       Water (H₂O)      Table Salt (NaCl)   Oxygen gas (O₂)
   +-------+        +-------+        +-------+           +-------+
   | 12 g  |        | 18 g  |        | 58.5 g|           | 32 g  |
   +-------+        +-------+        +-------+           +-------+
   6.022×10²³       6.022×10²³       6.022×10²³          6.022×10²³
   atoms of C       molecules        formula units       molecules
                    of H₂O           of NaCl             of O₂
```

> **Figure 5.2** – One mole of four different chemical substances: their masses on a balance differ considerably (12 g, 18 g, 58.5 g, 32 g), but the total count of elementary entities in each sample is identical: exactly $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles. Just like a dozen eggs and a dozen elephants: identical count, different mass!

Why does this seamless conversion work? Consider the ratio: an individual oxygen atom has a mass of 16 u, while an individual carbon atom has 12 u. Thus, an oxygen atom is $16/12$ times heavier than a carbon atom. If you assemble equal bunches ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms) of each, the overall mass ratio of the two piles must remain exactly $16 : 12$. Because Avogadro's number was defined so that 12 g of carbon-12 contains exactly one mole of atoms, one mole of oxygen atoms must weigh exactly 16 g!

> **Warning:** Always specify the exact entity! The atomic mass of oxygen is 16 u, so 1 mole of oxygen **atoms** (O) weighs 16 g. However, common oxygen gas exists as diatomic **molecules** ($\text{O}_2$), with a molecular mass of 32 u. Therefore, 1 mole of $\text{O}_2$ **molecules** weighs **32 g**, and it contains $2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \approx 1.204 \times 10^{24}$ oxygen atoms!

## Part E: The Actual Mass of a Single Particle in Grams

By combining the molar mass with Avogadro's number, we can calculate the actual mass of an individual atom or molecule in grams:

$$\text{Mass of 1 particle (g)} = \frac{\text{Molar mass } M}{\text{Avogadro constant } N_A}$$

- **One carbon atom:**
  $$\frac{12\text{ g}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \approx \mathbf{2.0 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}}$$

- **One water molecule ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$):**
  $$\frac{18\text{ g}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \approx \mathbf{3.0 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}}$$

- **One $\text{NaCl}$ formula unit:**
  $$\frac{58.5\text{ g}}{6.022 \times 10^{23}} \approx \mathbf{9.7 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}}$$

These numbers reveal just how light atoms are ($0.00000000000000000000002\text{ g}$). You could never register an isolated atom on any laboratory scale, but one mole of them forms a tidy, tangible 12 g sample that you can easily hold in your hand.

## Part F: The Fundamental Relationships of the Mole

Every quantitative problem involving moles connects three central quantities:
1. **Measured sample mass ($m$, in grams)**
2. **Number of moles ($n$, in mol)**
3. **Count of individual particles ($N$, dimensionless count)**

**Relationship 1: From Mass to Moles**

$$\mathbf{n = \frac{m}{M}}$$

$$\text{Number of moles } (n) = \frac{\text{Given mass in grams } (m)}{\text{Molar mass in g/mol } (M)}$$

This is just like the hardware store calculation: $\text{total mass} \div \text{mass of one unit} = \text{count}$. The only difference is that our unit here is one mole instead of one screw.

**Relationship 2: From Moles to Particle Count**

$$\mathbf{N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}}$$

$$\text{Total particle count } (N) = \text{Number of moles } (n) \times N_A$$

This is identical to converting dozens to individual count: $3\text{ dozen} = 3 \times 12 = 36$ items.

**The Mole Formula Triangle:**

We can summarize these relationships in a handy memory triangle. Cover the variable you wish to calculate, and the required formula appears:

```
                 +-------+
                 |   m   |
                 +-------+
                /    ×    \
               /           \
        +-------+       +-------+
        |   n   |───────|   M   |
        +-------+       +-------+

   m = n × M        n = m / M        M = m / n
```

> **Figure 5.3** – The mass-mole formula triangle: mass ($m$) is at the top; moles ($n$) and molar mass ($M$) are at the bottom. Cover the quantity you want to find: $n = m / M$, $m = n \times M$, or $M = m / n$.

**The Mole Conversion Bridge:**

Moles serve as the universal crossroads between laboratory mass and microscopic particle count:

```
        Measured Mass m (g)
            │
            ▼   ÷ M   (n = m / M)
        Moles n (mol)
            │
            ▼   × 6.022 × 10²³   (N = n × 6.022 × 10²³)
        Particle Count N
```

> **Figure 5.4** – The mole conversion bridge: divide mass by molar mass ($M$) to find moles; multiply moles by $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ to find the total count of particles. Reversing direction simply flips multiplication and division!

**Unit check:**
- $m$ is in grams ($\text{g}$)
- $M$ is in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$)
- $n = m / M$ has units of $\text{g} \div (\text{g/mol}) = \mathbf{\text{mol}}$ ✅

## Part G: Worked Introductory Examples

Let's work through four foundational examples. (More varied, multi-step problems follow in Topic 6.) We use rounded atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, Cl = 35.5. We express particle counts to **4 significant figures**, matching $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ (e.g., $2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 12.044 \times 10^{23} \approx 1.204 \times 10^{24}$).

### Example 1: How many moles and molecules are in 36 g of water?

For water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), molar mass $M = (2 \times 1) + 16 = 18\text{ g/mol}$.
- $n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{36}{18} = \mathbf{2\text{ mol}}$
- $N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{1.204 \times 10^{24}\text{ molecules}}$

Check: 36 g is twice the 18 g molar mass, so it must be 2 moles ✅. The count of molecules is also double Avogadro's number: $2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ ✅

### Example 2: How many moles and atoms are in 6 g of carbon?

For carbon (C), molar mass $M = 12\text{ g/mol}$.
- $n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{6}{12} = \mathbf{0.5\text{ mol}}$
- $N = 0.5 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{3.011 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms}}$

Check: 6 g is half of 12 g, so it represents exactly 0.5 mole, containing half of Avogadro's number of atoms ✅

### Example 3: What is the mass of 0.25 mole of NaCl?

For sodium chloride ($\text{NaCl}$), molar mass $M = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5\text{ g/mol}$.
- $m = n \times M = 0.25 \times 58.5 = \mathbf{14.625\text{ g}} \approx \mathbf{14.6\text{ g}}$

Check: 1 mole is 58.5 g, so one-quarter mole is $58.5 \div 4 = 14.625\text{ g}$ ✅

### Example 4: How many moles of formula units are in 117 g of NaCl?

- $n = \frac{m}{M} = \frac{117}{58.5} = \mathbf{2\text{ mol}}$
- $N = 2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{1.204 \times 10^{24}\text{ formula units}}$

Check: $2\text{ mol} \times 58.5\text{ g/mol} = 117\text{ g}$ ✅

## Part H: Three Different Substances, One Universal Number

A classic conceptual question in chemistry is: **Which sample contains more particles: 18 g of water, 12 g of carbon, or 32 g of oxygen gas?**

The answer: **all three contain the exact same count of particles!** Each sample contains exactly 1 mole of its constituent entities:
- $18\text{ g of }\text{H}_2\text{O} = \frac{18}{18} = 1\text{ mol of molecules}$
- $12\text{ g of C} = \frac{12}{12} = 1\text{ mol of atoms}$
- $32\text{ g of }\text{O}_2 = \frac{32}{32} = 1\text{ mol of molecules}$

Every one of these samples contains exactly $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles. Their measured masses differ because the individual particles have different masses. This illustrates the true power of the mole concept: it allows chemists to compare equal particle counts regardless of how heavy each particle is.

Now consider our earlier question: do 12 g of carbon and 16 g of oxygen **atoms** contain equal counts of atoms?
- Carbon: $12\text{ g} \div 12\text{ g/mol} = 1\text{ mol of C atoms}$
- Oxygen atoms: $16\text{ g} \div 16\text{ g/mol} = 1\text{ mol of O atoms}$

**Yes! Both contain exactly $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ atoms.** (Note: If you have 16 g of oxygen *gas* ($\text{O}_2$), that represents $16 / 32 = 0.5\text{ mol}$ of molecules, which still contains $0.5 \times 2 = 1\text{ mol}$ of individual oxygen atoms!)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Treating the mole as a unit of mass**
   A mole is a counting package for particles, not a mass. 1 mole of carbon weighs 12 g, while 1 mole of water weighs 18 g. The mole count is identical (1 mol), but the mass is different.

2. **Mistake 2: Confusing molecules with constituent atoms**
   1 mole of $\text{O}_2$ gas contains $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ **molecules**, but it contains twice as many **atoms** ($2 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} \approx 1.204 \times 10^{24}$ atoms) because each molecule contains 2 atoms. Always ask: *what entity is being counted?*

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing atomic mass units (u) with grams per mole (g/mol)**
   A single atom or molecule is measured in **u** ($18\text{ u}$ for one $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecule). One mole of those molecules is measured in **g/mol** or grams ($18\text{ g}$ for $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ molecules). The number is the same, but the units and scale are completely different!

4. **Mistake 4: Inverting the division when calculating moles**
   The formula is $n = m / M$, never $M / m$. For 36 g of water ($M = 18\text{ g/mol}$), $n = 36 / 18 = 2\text{ mol}$. Writing $18 / 36 = 0.5$ is incorrect. Check your units: $\text{g} \div (\text{g/mol}) = \text{mol}$.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming the mole concept applies only to gases**
   The mole concept applies to all states of matter: solids, liquids, gases, individual atoms, molecules, and ions.

## Quick Recap

- Atoms are far too microscopic to count directly; chemists **count particles by weighing them**, just as a hardware store counts screws by weighing bulk packages.
- **1 mole = $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles** (the Avogadro constant, $N_A$). It is a universal counting package, just like 1 pair = 2, 1 dozen = 12, and 1 gross = 144. Particles can be atoms, molecules, or formula units.
- $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ is astronomically large: counting at 1 particle per second would take roughly $1.9 \times 10^{16}$ years (over a million times the age of the universe); yet a single droplet of water contains about $1.7 \times 10^{21}$ molecules.
- **Molar mass ($M$)** in $\text{g/mol}$ is numerically identical to the atomic or molecular mass in u: C = $12\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{NaCl} = 58.5\text{ g/mol}$, $\text{O}_2 = 32\text{ g/mol}$.
- The actual mass of a single particle in grams is given by $M / N_A$ (e.g., one carbon atom $\approx 2.0 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$, one water molecule $\approx 3.0 \times 10^{-23}\text{ g}$).
- **The two fundamental formulas:**
  $$n = \frac{m}{M} \quad \text{and} \quad N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$$
- 18 g of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, 12 g of C, and 32 g of $\text{O}_2$ all contain the exact same count of particles: 1 mole ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$ entities).
