# Topic 7: Percentage Composition and Chapter Summary

In the previous topic, we mastered quantitative calculations with the mole: determining the number of moles and particles from a measured sample mass, and calculating the mass from a given particle count. In every calculation, molar mass served as our central bridge.

Now we put molar mass to another powerful, practical use: calculating the **percentage composition by mass** of each element inside a chemical compound directly from its formula. Finally, we will bring our entire journey across all seven topics together into one unified chapter summary table.

Throughout this topic, we continue using standard **rounded atomic masses**: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Na = 23, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40. Minor variations in the final decimal place reflect standard rounding.

## The Agricultural Supply Store and Fertilizer Bags

Before the spring planting season, Sophia visited her uncle, Mr. Evans, at his agricultural farm supply warehouse. Stacked high along the warehouse walls were large **50 kg bags** of three different nitrogen fertilizers: **urea**, **ammonium nitrate**, and **ammonium sulphate**.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Percentage composition** | The percentage by mass of each individual element present in a pure chemical compound | In $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $\text{H} = 11.11\%$, $\text{O} = 88.89\%$ |
| **Mass percent of an element** | $(\text{Total mass of that element in formula} \div \text{Molar mass of compound}) \times 100$ | Ca in $\text{CaCO}_3$: $40\%$ |
| **Molar mass ($M$)** | The mass of one mole of a compound in grams per mole ($\text{g/mol}$) | $M(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 18\text{ g/mol}$ |
| **Fertilizer** | A chemical compound added to soil to supply essential plant nutrients (such as nitrogen, phosphorus, or potassium) | Urea, ammonium nitrate |
| **Nitrogen content** | The mass percentage of elemental nitrogen contained in a fertilizer | $46.7\%$ in urea |
| **Rounding** | Adjusting a calculated decimal value to the nearest tenths or hundredths place | $46.666\dots\% \approx 46.7\%$ |

## Part A: The Formula for Percentage Composition

In Topic 4, we calculated the molecular or formula unit mass of compounds from their formulas, and in Topic 2, we learned that elements in a pure compound always combine in a fixed mass ratio. Percentage composition combines both concepts into a simple percentage formula:

$$\mathbf{\text{Mass percent of an element} = \frac{\text{Total mass of that element in the formula}}{\text{Molar or formula mass of the compound}} \times 100}$$

**Step-by-Step Procedure:**
1. Write down the chemical formula and count the atoms of each element (paying close attention to subscripts and brackets).
2. Calculate the total mass contribution of each element: $\text{atom count} \times \text{atomic mass}$.
3. Sum all elemental masses to determine the total molar mass of the compound.
4. Divide the mass of each element by the total molar mass and multiply by 100.
5. **Check:** The sum of all individual percentages must equal $100\%$ (allowing for slight rounding differences like $99.9\%$ or $100.1\%$).

### Example 1: Water (H₂O)

- H: $2 \times 1 = 2\text{ u}$
- O: $1 \times 16 = 16\text{ u}$
- Total molar mass = $2 + 16 = \mathbf{18\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\% \text{ H} = \frac{2}{18} \times 100 = \mathbf{11.11\%}$
- $\% \text{ O} = \frac{16}{18} \times 100 = \mathbf{88.89\%}$

Check: $11.11\% + 88.89\% = 100.00\%$ ✅

*Notice the connection to Topic 2:* The mass ratio of $\text{H} : \text{O}$ in water was $1 : 8$. In percentages, $11.11 : 88.89 = 1 : 8$ (since $11.11 \times 8 = 88.88$). It expresses the exact same constant ratio in the language of percentages!

### Example 2: Calcium carbonate (CaCO₃, chalk and marble)

- Ca: $1 \times 40 = 40\text{ u}$
- C: $1 \times 12 = 12\text{ u}$
- O: $3 \times 16 = 48\text{ u}$
- Total formula unit mass = $40 + 12 + 48 = \mathbf{100\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\% \text{ Ca} = \frac{40}{100} \times 100 = \mathbf{40.0\%}$
- $\% \text{ C} = \frac{12}{100} \times 100 = \mathbf{12.0\%}$
- $\% \text{ O} = \frac{48}{100} \times 100 = \mathbf{48.0\%}$

Check: $40.0 + 12.0 + 48.0 = 100.0\%$ ✅

```
   100 units of CaCO₃ by mass (each block = 4%)

   Ca: ##########   (10 blocks = 40%)
   C : ###          (3 blocks  = 12%)
   O : ############ (12 blocks = 48%)
```

> **Figure 7.1** – Proportional bar diagram of $\text{CaCO}_3$: 1 block represents 4% by mass. Calcium accounts for 10 blocks (40%), carbon accounts for 3 blocks (12%), and oxygen accounts for 12 blocks (48%), totaling 25 blocks (100%).

### Example 3: Table salt (NaCl)

- Na: $23\text{ u}$, Cl: $35.5\text{ u}$. Total = $\mathbf{58.5\text{ g/mol}}$.
- $\% \text{ Na} = \frac{23}{58.5} \times 100 = 39.32\% \approx \mathbf{39.3\%}$
- $\% \text{ Cl} = \frac{35.5}{58.5} \times 100 = 60.68\% \approx \mathbf{60.7\%}$

Check: $39.3\% + 60.7\% = 100.0\%$ ✅

### Example 4: Carbon dioxide (CO₂)

- C: $1 \times 12 = 12\text{ u}$, O: $2 \times 16 = 32\text{ u}$. Total = $\mathbf{44\text{ g/mol}}$.
- $\% \text{ C} = \frac{12}{44} \times 100 = 27.27\% \approx \mathbf{27.3\%}$
- $\% \text{ O} = \frac{32}{44} \times 100 = 72.73\% \approx \mathbf{72.7\%}$

Check: $27.3\% + 72.7\% = 100.0\%$ ✅ (Notice that $27.3 : 72.7$ simplifies directly to the empirical $3 : 8$ ratio from Topic 2!)

### Example 5: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆)

- C: $6 \times 12 = 72\text{ u}$
- H: $12 \times 1 = 12\text{ u}$
- O: $6 \times 16 = 96\text{ u}$
- Total molecular mass = $72 + 12 + 96 = \mathbf{180\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\% \text{ C} = \frac{72}{180} \times 100 = \mathbf{40.0\%}$
- $\% \text{ H} = \frac{12}{180} \times 100 = 6.67\% \approx \mathbf{6.7\%}$
- $\% \text{ O} = \frac{96}{180} \times 100 = 53.33\% \approx \mathbf{53.3\%}$

Check: $40.0\% + 6.7\% + 53.3\% = 100.0\%$ ✅

## Part B: Working Backward: Finding Element Mass in a Given Sample

Percentage composition is far more than an academic exercise; it allows you to determine the exact mass of any constituent element in a measured bulk sample:

$$\mathbf{\text{Mass of element} = \left(\frac{\text{Mass percent}}{100}\right) \times \text{Sample mass} = \left(\frac{\text{Total element mass in formula}}{\text{Molar mass of compound}}\right) \times \text{Sample mass}}$$

### Example 6: Calcium in 250 g of CaCO₃

Since calcium makes up $40.0\%$ of $\text{CaCO}_3$:
$$\text{Mass of Ca} = \frac{40.0}{100} \times 250\text{ g} = \mathbf{100\text{ g of calcium}}$$

Check: 100 g of $\text{CaCO}_3$ contains 40 g of Ca; so 250 g ($2.5 \times 100\text{ g}$) contains $40 \times 2.5 = 100\text{ g}$ ✅

### Example 7: Oxygen and hydrogen in 36 g of water

- $\text{Mass of oxygen} = 36\text{ g} \times \frac{16}{18} = \mathbf{32\text{ g of oxygen}}$
- $\text{Mass of hydrogen} = 36\text{ g} - 32\text{ g} = \mathbf{4\text{ g of hydrogen}}$

Check: $4\text{ g} + 32\text{ g} = 36\text{ g}$ ✅; mass ratio $4 : 32 = 1 : 8$ ✅

### Example 8: Sodium in 117 g of table salt (NaCl)

- $\text{Mass of sodium} = 117\text{ g} \times \frac{23}{58.5} = \mathbf{46\text{ g of sodium}}$

Check: $117\text{ g} = 2\text{ moles of NaCl}$ ($2 \times 58.5\text{ g}$), so it contains 2 moles of sodium: $2 \times 23\text{ g} = 46\text{ g}$ ✅; $46 \div 117 \times 100 = 39.3\%$ ✅

### Example 9: Carbon in 200 g of CO₂

- Carbon makes up $\frac{12}{44} = \frac{3}{11}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ by mass:
  $$\text{Mass of carbon} = 200\text{ g} \times \frac{3}{11} \approx \mathbf{54.5\text{ g of carbon}}$$

Check: $54.5 \div 200 \times 100 = 27.25\% \approx 27.3\%$ ✅

## Part C: Why Percentage Composition Is Always Constant

Take any pure sample of water on Earth: from a melting polar ice cap, an underground spring, or synthesized in a laboratory combustion tube. Every single sample contains exactly **$11.11\%$ hydrogen** and **$88.89\%$ oxygen** by mass.

This constancy is guaranteed by the **Law of Constant Proportions** (Topic 2). Because every pure compound has a fixed formula and fixed atomic masses, its mass percentages are invariant.

**Example 10: Hydrogen and oxygen in a 900 g water sample**

- $\text{Hydrogen} = 900\text{ g} \times \frac{2}{18} = \mathbf{100\text{ g of hydrogen}}$
- $\text{Oxygen} = 900\text{ g} - 100\text{ g} = \mathbf{800\text{ g of oxygen}}$
- Ratio $100 : 800 = 1 : 8$ ✅

Does the percentage change if you analyze 5 grams of water versus 5,000 liters? **Never.** Percentage composition depends solely on chemical identity, not on sample size.

Because of this constancy, percentage composition serves as a reliable **chemical fingerprint**: if a clear liquid purported to be water yields $14\%$ hydrogen upon elemental analysis, it cannot be pure water — it must be contaminated or an entirely different chemical compound!

## Part D: Returning to the Fertilizer Store: Which Delivers the Most Nitrogen?

Sophia analyzed the formulas of the three fertilizers to find their elemental nitrogen percentages:

### Urea (CO(NH₂)₂)

The formula can be written as $\text{CH}_4\text{N}_2\text{O}$:
- C: $1 \times 12 = 12\text{ u}$
- O: $1 \times 16 = 16\text{ u}$
- N: $2 \times 14 = 28\text{ u}$
- H: $4 \times 1 = 4\text{ u}$
- Total molar mass = $12 + 16 + 28 + 4 = \mathbf{60\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\% \text{ N in urea} = \frac{28}{60} \times 100 = 46.67\% \approx \mathbf{46.7\%}$

### Ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃)

Contains two nitrogen atoms (one in $\text{NH}_4^+$, one in $\text{NO}_3^-$):
- N: $2 \times 14 = 28\text{ u}$
- H: $4 \times 1 = 4\text{ u}$
- O: $3 \times 16 = 48\text{ u}$
- Total molar mass = $28 + 4 + 48 = \mathbf{80\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\% \text{ N in ammonium nitrate} = \frac{28}{80} \times 100 = \mathbf{35.0\%}$

### Ammonium sulphate ((NH₄)₂SO₄)

The ammonium ion is taken twice:
- N: $2 \times 14 = 28\text{ u}$
- H: $8 \times 1 = 8\text{ u}$
- S: $1 \times 32 = 32\text{ u}$
- O: $4 \times 16 = 64\text{ u}$
- Total molar mass = $28 + 8 + 32 + 64 = \mathbf{132\text{ g/mol}}$
- $\% \text{ N in ammonium sulphate} = \frac{28}{132} \times 100 = 21.21\% \approx \mathbf{21.2\%}$

Notice that each formula unit contains the exact same mass of nitrogen ($28\text{ u}$), but because the accompanying carrier atoms have different masses, the resulting nitrogen percentages differ drastically!

```
   Nitrogen Content in 100 kg of Fertilizer (1 block = 2 kg)

   Urea             : #######################  46.7 kg
   Ammonium nitrate : ##################       35.0 kg
   Ammonium sulphate: ###########              21.2 kg
```

> **Figure 7.2** – Visual comparison of nitrogen content per 100 kg of fertilizer: urea contains 46.7 kg of N, ammonium nitrate contains 35.0 kg of N, and ammonium sulphate contains 21.2 kg of N. Urea is by far the most nitrogen-dense fertilizer.

Check: Urea delivers $\frac{46.7}{35.0} \approx 1.33$ times as much nitrogen as ammonium nitrate. Directly from molar masses: $\frac{80}{60} = 1.33$ ✅

### Actual nitrogen per 50 kg bag

- **Urea:** $50\text{ kg} \times \frac{28}{60} = \mathbf{23.33\text{ kg of active nitrogen}}$
- **Ammonium nitrate:** $50\text{ kg} \times \frac{28}{80} = \mathbf{17.50\text{ kg of active nitrogen}}$
- **Ammonium sulphate:** $50\text{ kg} \times \frac{28}{132} = \mathbf{10.61\text{ kg of active nitrogen}}$

### Cost analysis: Getting the best value per kilogram of nitrogen

> *(Note: The monetary values below are purely illustrative practice figures, not actual commercial market prices.)*

Suppose the agricultural warehouse sells 50 kg bags at the following prices:

| Fertilizer | Illustrative Bag Price (50 kg) | Pure Nitrogen Content | Cost per kg of Nitrogen |
|---|---|---|---|
| Urea | \$30.00 | 23.33 kg | $\$30.00 \div 23.33 = \mathbf{\$1.29\text{ /kg}}$ |
| Ammonium nitrate | \$25.00 | 17.50 kg | $\$25.00 \div 17.50 = \mathbf{\$1.43\text{ /kg}}$ |
| Ammonium sulphate | \$18.00 | 10.61 kg | $\$18.00 \div 10.61 = \mathbf{\$1.70\text{ /kg}}$ |

Look at the surprising result: **The ammonium sulphate bag has the lowest sticker price (\$18), yet its nitrogen is the most expensive (\$1.70 per kg)!** Conversely, the urea bag has the highest sticker price (\$30), yet it delivers the cheapest nitrogen (\$1.29 per kg).

This was Sophia's crucial insight for Mr. Harris: **never buy agricultural fertilizers based solely on bag price — always calculate the cost per kilogram of active nutrient!**

**Example 11: Supplying 14 kg of nitrogen**

Mr. Harris needs exactly 14 kg of elemental nitrogen for his field. How many kilograms of each fertilizer would be required, and what would it cost (at illustrative prices of \$0.60/kg for urea, \$0.50/kg for ammonium nitrate, and \$0.36/kg for ammonium sulphate)?

- **Urea:** $\text{Mass} = 14 \div \left(\frac{28}{60}\right) = 14 \times \frac{60}{28} = \mathbf{30\text{ kg}}$.
  $$\text{Cost} = 30\text{ kg} \times \$0.60 = \mathbf{\$18.00}$$
- **Ammonium nitrate:** $\text{Mass} = 14 \times \frac{80}{28} = \mathbf{40\text{ kg}}$.
  $$\text{Cost} = 40\text{ kg} \times \$0.50 = \mathbf{\$20.00}$$
- **Ammonium sulphate:** $\text{Mass} = 14 \times \frac{132}{28} = \mathbf{66\text{ kg}}$.
  $$\text{Cost} = 66\text{ kg} \times \$0.36 = \mathbf{\$23.76}$$

Check: $30\text{ kg of urea} \times \frac{28}{60} = 14\text{ kg of N}$ ✅; $40\text{ kg of AN} \times \frac{28}{80} = 14\text{ kg of N}$ ✅; $66\text{ kg of AS} \times \frac{28}{132} = 14\text{ kg of N}$ ✅

Urea supplies the required nitrogen with the lowest total mass to transport (30 kg) and at the lowest cost (\$18.00).

> **Practical note:** In real-world agriculture, farmers also evaluate soil pH, moisture, and the need for secondary nutrients (e.g., ammonium sulphate also provides essential sulphur). Professional agronomists should always be consulted for real field management. Our exercise demonstrates chemical percentage analysis.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Ignoring subscripts inside or outside brackets**
   In $(\text{NH}_4)_2\text{SO}_4$, the nitrogen count is $2 \times 1 = 2$ and hydrogen is $2 \times 4 = 8$. Forgetting to distribute the 2 results in an incorrect molar mass. In $\text{CO(NH}_2)_2$, the subscript 2 outside the parentheses applies to both N and H.

2. **Mistake 2: Taking the mass of only one atom when multiple atoms are present**
   In water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$), the mass of hydrogen is $2 \times 1 = 2\text{ u}$, not 1 u! Dividing $1 / 18 \times 100 = 5.56\%$ is incorrect; the true hydrogen percentage is $2 / 18 \times 100 = \mathbf{11.11\%}$.

3. **Mistake 3: Forgetting to verify that percentages sum to 100%**
   Always add your individual calculated percentages together. If they sum to $87\%$ or $115\%$, an arithmetic error has occurred in calculating the molar mass or individual contributions.

4. **Mistake 4: Confusing mass percentage with actual mass in grams**
   Stating that calcium is $40\%$ in $\text{CaCO}_3$ does not mean there are 40 g of calcium in every sample. It means 40 g of Ca in a **100 g sample**; a 250 g sample contains 100 g of Ca.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming the cheapest container provides the cheapest nutrient**
   A low container price often reflects lower active ingredient concentration. Always divide container price by total nutrient mass to compare true unit cost.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming rounded percentages violate integer mass ratios**
   $11.11\%$ and $88.89\%$ represent the exact $1 : 8$ ratio of water; the fractional hundredths arise solely from decimal rounding.

## Quick Recap

- **Mass percentage formula:**
  $$\text{Mass percent} = \frac{\text{Total mass of the element in formula}}{\text{Molar mass of the compound}} \times 100$$
  The sum of all elemental mass percentages must equal $100\%$.
- **Standard values:**
  - $\text{H}_2\text{O}$: $\text{H} = 11.11\%$, $\text{O} = 88.89\%$
  - $\text{CaCO}_3$: $\text{Ca} = 40.0\%$, $\text{C} = 12.0\%$, $\text{O} = 48.0\%$
  - $\text{NaCl}$: $\text{Na} = 39.3\%$, $\text{Cl} = 60.7\%$
  - $\text{CO}_2$: $\text{C} = 27.3\%$, $\text{O} = 72.7\%$
  - Glucose ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$): $\text{C} = 40.0\%$, $\text{H} = 6.7\%$, $\text{O} = 53.3\%$
- **Finding element mass in a sample:** $\text{Mass of element} = (\text{percent} / 100) \times \text{sample mass}$. For example, 250 g of $\text{CaCO}_3$ contains 100 g of Ca.
- Percentage composition is **constant** for every pure sample of a compound, regardless of origin or sample size, directly confirming the Law of Constant Proportions.
- **Fertilizer nitrogen content:** Urea ($46.7\%$) $>$ Ammonium nitrate ($35.0\%$) $>$ Ammonium sulphate ($21.2\%$). Always compare fertilizers by cost per kilogram of nitrogen rather than container price.
- Maintain careful tracking of brackets and subscripts.

## Chapter Summary: Atoms and Molecules

Here is a panoramic review of our journey across all seven topics of Chapter 4:

| Topic | Key Scientific Concepts | One-Line Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic 1** | **Law of Conservation of Mass** | In any chemical reaction, total mass is conserved (mass of reactants = mass of products); closed systems preserve mass visibly, whereas open systems can lose mass to escaping gases. |
| **Topic 2** | **Law of Constant Proportions** | In any pure chemical compound, the constituent elements are always combined in a fixed proportion by mass (e.g., $\text{H} : \text{O} = 1 : 8$ in water), regardless of source or method of preparation. |
| **Topic 3** | **Dalton's Atomic Theory** | Dalton's 5 postulates (matter is composed of indivisible atoms, atoms of an element are identical in mass, compounds form from whole-number ratios, reactions are rearrangements) provided the first unified physical explanation for both chemical laws. |
| **Topic 4** | **Atomic Mass, Molecular Mass, and Formula Unit Mass** | Microscopic atoms cannot be weighed individually; their masses are measured relative to carbon-12 (where $1\text{ u} = \frac{1}{12}\text{ mass of }{}^{12}\text{C}$). Formula unit masses are computed by summing the constituent atomic masses. |
| **Topic 5** | **The Mole Concept** | The mole is the chemist's counting package ($1\text{ mol} = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ entities}$, the Avogadro constant). The molar mass ($M$) in $\text{g/mol}$ is numerically identical to the particle's atomic mass in u. |
| **Topic 6** | **Mole Calculations** | Conversions between mass ($m$), moles ($n$), and particle count ($N$) are conducted systematically via the mole crossroads bridge using $n = m / M$ and $N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$. |
| **Topic 7** | **Percentage Composition** | The mass percentage of each element in a compound is calculated from its formula, remains constant for any pure sample, and enables practical evaluation of active nutrients in products like agricultural fertilizers. |
