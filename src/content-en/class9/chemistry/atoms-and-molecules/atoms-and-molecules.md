# Topic 1: The Law of Conservation of Mass

At the end of the previous chapter, we wrote chemical reactions as **balanced equations** — such as 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O — following the rule that **every element must have the exact same number of atoms on both sides of the arrow**. We also promised to explore the formal scientific law behind that rule and the scientist who demonstrated it experimentally. And we left two big questions open: how heavy is a single atom, and how can chemists count trillions of microscopic atoms by weighing a sample in grams? In this chapter, we will answer all of these questions. We begin with the foundational principle on which all chemical accounting rests: **in a chemical reaction, total mass never changes.**

---

## The Bottle, the Effervescent Tablet, and the Kitchen Scale

During the school holidays, Zara was visiting her aunt, Ms. Davis. On the kitchen counter sat a tube of fizzing vitamin C tablets — the kind that bubble vigorously when dropped into water. Zara asked, "Aunt Davis, when the tablet fizzes away in water, where does it go? Does some of its mass disappear?"

Ms. Davis smiled. "Let's test that right now on our digital kitchen scale. Remember our safety rule: always do experiments with an adult, and never taste lab materials."

She poured some water into a plastic bottle, tucked a fizzing tablet inside a small dry tissue wedged in the bottle neck so it wouldn't touch the water yet, and screwed the cap on **tightly**. She placed the sealed bottle on the digital scale: **250.0 g**. Then she tipped the bottle so the tablet dropped into the water while keeping the cap tightly sealed. Bubbles fizzed up and the plastic walls felt firm with trapped gas. When the fizzing stopped, Zara looked at the display: **250.0 g** — unchanged to the tenth of a gram!

"The tablet dissolved and made gas," Zara said, "yet the total mass didn't change at all!"

Next, Ms. Davis repeated the exact same test with an **open bottle** (leaving the cap off the top). Before the tablet dropped into the water, the total reading was **250.0 g**. Once the fizzing stopped, the scale read **249.6 g**. Zara's eyes widened. "Now it went down by 0.4 g! Did mass get destroyed this time?"

"Not at all," Ms. Davis explained. "In the open bottle, the carbon dioxide gas escaped into the room, so our kitchen scale was no longer weighing that gas. When we kept the cap sealed, the gas stayed inside and the mass remained 250.0 g. **Nothing vanished** — in the open bottle, some of the product simply floated off the scale!"

*(These numbers are illustrative; different tablets and bottles will give slightly different readings, but the pattern is always the same.)*

More than two centuries ago, a French scientist investigated this exact puzzle with extraordinary care. Let's meet him and see what he discovered.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mass** | The amount of matter in an object or sample, measured on a balance in grams (g) or kilograms (kg) | 12 g of carbon |
| **Reactants** | The starting substances that undergo a chemical reaction | Carbon and oxygen |
| **Products** | The new substances formed by a chemical reaction | Carbon dioxide |
| **Closed system** | A sealed container or setup from which no matter (not even gas) can enter or escape | A tightly capped bottle |
| **Open system** | An unsealed container or setup where gases or smoke can enter from or escape into the surrounding air | An uncapped bottle, burning paper in an open dish |
| **Law of Conservation of Mass** | Mass is neither created nor destroyed in a chemical reaction | Total mass of reactants = total mass of products |
| **Conservation** | Remaining constant in total amount before and after a process | 250.0 g before = 250.0 g after |

## Part A: Lavoisier and His Precision Balance

Around 250 years ago in France, the chemist **Antoine Lavoisier** set out to solve a puzzle that had confused scientists for centuries. When wood burned, the leftover ash weighed much less than the original wood. Yet when metals like iron or tin were heated in air, the flaky solid formed weighed **more** than the original metal! People wondered whether some mysterious substance was being destroyed or created during heating.

Lavoisier's breakthrough came from two things: **highly accurate balances** and one strict rule — **weigh everything before and after the reaction, including all gases.** By heating substances inside **sealed glass vessels** so that no gas could enter or escape, Lavoisier found the exact same result every single time: **the total mass of the sealed vessel and its contents never changed.**

He also showed why open-air experiments had fooled people: when wood burns in open air, invisible gases (carbon dioxide and water vapor) escape into the room; when a metal is heated in air, oxygen gas from the air combines with the metal, adding its mass to the solid. Earlier experimenters had simply forgotten to weigh the gases!

From these experiments came a fundamental law of chemistry:

> **Law of Conservation of Mass:** In any chemical reaction, mass can neither be created nor destroyed. **The total mass of all the reactants is always equal to the total mass of all the products.**

In one crisp equation: **Total mass before reaction = Total mass after reaction** (when all reactants and products, including gases, are accounted for).

## Part B: Closed Bottle vs Open Bottle

This explains Zara's two kitchen-scale readings completely. The exact same chemical reaction happened in both bottles, but in one bottle the gas was trapped and weighed, while in the other it escaped into the room.

```
   Closed bottle (cap sealed)             Open bottle (cap off)

        +-----+                              +-----+
        │ cap │                              │     │   CO₂ escapes
        +-----+                              │     │  ─────────►
        │ o o │                              │ o o │
        │ o   │  gas stays trapped           │ o   │
        │~~~~~│  inside                      │~~~~~│
        │water│                              │water│
        +-----+                              +-----+
       ┌───────┐                            ┌───────┐
       │ scale │                            │ scale │
       │250.0 g│                            │249.6 g│
       └───────┘                            └───────┘
       before = after                       gas escaped into air,
       mass unchanged                       so scale reads less
```

> **Figure 1.1** – In the closed bottle, all products remain inside, so the scale reading stays constant at 250.0 g. In the open bottle, 0.4 g of CO₂ gas escapes into the air, so the scale reads 249.6 g — mass was not destroyed, it simply left the container.

**Check:** In the open bottle, 250.0 − 249.6 = **0.4 g** of gas escaped into the air ✅. Adding the 0.4 g of escaped gas back to the 249.6 g remaining in the bottle gives 249.6 + 0.4 = **250.0 g** ✅.

Therefore, **to verify the Law of Conservation of Mass directly on a scale, the reaction must be carried out in a closed system** (or any escaping/entering gases must be captured and weighed).

## Part C: Why Mass Sometimes Seems to Increase or Decrease

Let's look at three everyday situations that appear at first glance to break the law, and see why they actually obey it:

1. **Burning paper or wood in open air.** After a 5 g sheet of paper burns in an open dish, the leftover ash might weigh only 1 g. Where did the rest of the mass go? As the paper reacted with oxygen from the air, most of the products were **carbon dioxide gas and water vapor (steam)**, which floated away into the air. If you burned the paper inside a strong, sealed chamber containing oxygen, the total mass of the chamber before and after burning would be identical.

2. **An effervescent tablet or an opened bottle of fizzy soda.** Just as Zara observed, when bubbles of carbon dioxide gas escape from an open container, the container gets lighter by the exact mass of the gas that left.

3. **Rusting of iron.** When an iron nail is exposed to moist air for several weeks, it forms reddish-brown **rust**, and the rusted nail actually weighs **more** than the fresh iron nail! Why? Because **oxygen** (and water) from the air chemically combined with the iron. Nothing was created out of nowhere: the mass of the iron plus the mass of the oxygen (and water) that joined it equals the exact mass of the rust.

**Thought-experiment check:** Suppose 5.6 g of pure iron reacts completely with oxygen from the air to form **8.0 g** of iron oxide. How much oxygen joined the iron?
- Mass of oxygen = 8.0 − 5.6 = **2.4 g**.
- Check in reverse: 5.6 g iron + 2.4 g oxygen = **8.0 g** iron oxide ✅. The extra 2.4 g came from oxygen in the air!

## Part D: How Balanced Equations Express Conservation of Mass

In the previous chapter, we balanced equations by counting atoms on both sides. Now we see that **balancing a chemical equation is the direct application of the Law of Conservation of Mass**:

- In a chemical reaction, atoms are neither created nor destroyed — they simply **rearrange their bonds** into new combinations.
- Every atom has its own fixed mass.
- Because the **exact same atoms** are present before and after the reaction, the **total mass** before and after the reaction must be identical.

```
   Before (Reactants)                 After (Products)

   H  H      O  O      H  H           H  O  H      H  O  H
   (H₂)      (O₂)      (H₂)           (H₂O)        (H₂O)

   4 H atoms + 2 O atoms      ──►     4 H atoms + 2 O atoms
   Same atoms, new partnerships: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Atoms rearrange during a reaction: 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 oxygen atoms are present both before and after the arrow. Because no atoms are gained or lost, total mass is conserved.

**Numerical check on 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.** Using H = 1 and O = 16 (these are relative atomic masses, which we will study in Topic 4):

- **Reactants:** 2 × H₂ = 2 × (2 × 1) = 4, and O₂ = 2 × 16 = 32. Total = 4 + 32 = **36**.
- **Products:** 2 × H₂O = 2 × (2 + 16) = 2 × 18 = **36**.
- **36 = 36** ✅ — mass is conserved!

Let's check four more balanced equations from the previous chapter in the same way:

| Balanced Equation | Total Mass of Reactants | Total Mass of Products |
|---|---|---|
| 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO | (2 × 24) + 32 = **80** | 2 × (24 + 16) = **80** |
| CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O | 16 + (2 × 32) = **80** | 44 + (2 × 18) = **80** |
| Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ | 65 + (2 × 36.5) = **138** | (65 + 71) + 2 = **138** |
| 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ | (4 × 56) + (3 × 32) = **320** | 2 × 160 = **320** |

*(Using rounded atomic masses: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Mg = 24, Cl = 35.5, Fe = 56, Zn = 65.)*

This shows why balancing equations is not merely a formatting exercise: **an unbalanced equation violates the Law of Conservation of Mass.**

## Part E: Worked Numericals

### Example 1: Burning carbon to form CO₂

When 12 g of carbon is burned completely in 32 g of oxygen (C + O₂ → CO₂), what mass of carbon dioxide is formed?

- Total mass of reactants = 12 g + 32 g = **44 g**.
- By the Law of Conservation of Mass, total mass of products = **44 g**.
- Since CO₂ is the only product, **mass of CO₂ = 44 g**.

Check: 12 + 32 = 44 ✅.

### Example 2: Burning magnesium ribbon

When 24 g of magnesium is burned completely in air, 40 g of magnesium oxide is produced. What mass of oxygen combined with the magnesium?

- Reactants: Mg (24 g) + O₂ (*x* g). Product: MgO (40 g).
- 24 + *x* = 40 → ***x* = 16 g of oxygen**.

Check: 24 + 16 = 40 ✅. Notice why the white magnesium oxide powder weighs more (40 g) than the starting magnesium ribbon (24 g): 16 g of oxygen gas from the air combined with the metal.

### Example 3: Thermal decomposition — finding a missing product by difference

When 50 g of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃, limestone) is heated strongly, 28 g of solid calcium oxide (CaO) remains behind: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. What mass of CO₂ gas was released?

- Mass of reactant = 50 g. Mass of products = CaO (28 g) + CO₂ (*x* g).
- 28 + *x* = 50 → ***x* = 22 g of CO₂**.

Check: 28 + 22 = 50 ✅. (If this heating were done in an open dish, the 22 g of CO₂ would escape into the air, leaving 28 g of solid on the scale.)

### Example 4: Forming water from hydrogen and oxygen

4 g of hydrogen reacts completely with 32 g of oxygen (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O). What mass of water is formed?

- Total mass of reactants = 4 g + 32 g = 36 g.
- Total mass of product = **36 g of water**.

Check: 2 × 18 = 36 ✅.

### Example 5: Reaction with two reactants and two products

In the reaction Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂, 65 g of zinc reacts completely with 73 g of hydrochloric acid (HCl) to form 136 g of zinc chloride (ZnCl₂) and hydrogen gas (H₂). What mass of hydrogen gas is produced?

- Total mass of reactants = 65 g + 73 g = 138 g.
- Total mass of products = 136 g + *x* = 138 g → ***x* = 2 g of H₂**.

Check: 136 + 2 = 138 ✅.

## Part F: What This Law Does Not Say

1. **Atom counts and total mass are conserved, but the total number of molecules does not have to stay the same.** Look at 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: on the left there are 2 + 1 = **3 molecules**, while on the right there are **2 molecules**. Total atoms (4 H and 2 O) and total mass (36 g) stay constant, even when the number of molecules changes!
2. **Physical changes also conserve mass.** When 100 g of ice melts into liquid water, you still have 100 g of water — no new substance forms, and no atoms are gained or lost.
3. **The law applies to mass, not to heat energy alone.** A reaction may release or absorb heat, while the mass of all chemical reactants and products remains equal in ordinary laboratory chemistry.
4. **Scope of the law:** In all chemical reactions, conservation of mass holds to extreme precision. (In nuclear reactions — such as inside stars or nuclear reactors — a tiny fraction of mass is converted into energy, which you will study in advanced physics.)

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Concluding that mass was destroyed when an open container loses weight.**
   No. If a gas escapes from an open container into the room, the scale simply stops weighing that gas. In a closed container, the mass stays constant.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking that burning paper violates the law because the ash is lighter.**
   Burning paper uses oxygen from the air and produces carbon dioxide gas and water vapor in addition to ash. When all gases are included, total mass before equals total mass after.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking that rusting creates new mass out of nothing.**
   Rust is heavier than the original iron nail because oxygen (and water) from the air has bonded to the iron: iron + oxygen → iron oxide.

4. **Mistake 4: Assuming the number of molecules must be equal on both sides of an equation.**
   No. **Atoms of each element** and **total mass** are always equal on both sides, but the number of molecules can change (e.g., 3 molecules on the left vs 2 molecules on the right in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O).

5. **Mistake 5: Forgetting to include all reactants or all products when adding masses.**
   Always sum **all** reactants that actually reacted on the left and set that equal to the sum of **all** products formed on the right.

## Quick Recap

- **Law of Conservation of Mass** (Antoine Lavoisier): In a chemical reaction, mass is neither created nor destroyed. **Total mass of reactants = total mass of products.**
- Lavoisier demonstrated this law using **sealed vessels** and precision balances, showing that apparent mass gains or losses in open air are caused by gases entering from or escaping into the air.
- In a **closed system**, the scale reading stays constant; in an **open system**, escaping gas makes the scale reading drop even though no mass was destroyed.
- Burning wood, fizzing tablets, and rusting iron all obey conservation of mass once atmospheric oxygen and escaping gases are included.
- A **balanced chemical equation** reflects conservation of mass because both sides contain the exact same number of atoms of every element (e.g., in 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, 36 = 36).
- In numerical problems, you can find any one unknown reactant or product mass by setting (sum of reactant masses) = (sum of product masses).
- Atoms and mass are always conserved in a chemical reaction, whereas the total number of molecules may change.
