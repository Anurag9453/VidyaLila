# Topic 2: Ionic Bond: Transfer of Electrons

In Topic 1, we learned that atoms seek a completely filled outer shell—an octet or a duplet—by **losing**, **gaining**, or **sharing** electrons. We also noted that metals (having 1 to 3 outer electrons) tend to donate electrons, while non-metals (having 5 to 7 outer electrons) tend to accept them. What happens when a metal atom encounters a non-metal atom? The metal transfers its valence electron(s) directly to the non-metal. This electron transfer gives rise to an **ionic bond**. In earlier chapters, we noted that substances like table salt ($\text{NaCl}$) do not consist of isolated single "molecules." Today, we fulfill our promise to explain why.

---

## The Crayon Box Dilemma

During art class, Leo sat next to his classmate Maya. Leo had a custom wooden crayon case designed with slots for **exactly 8 crayons**—no more, no less. However, Leo had 9 crayons in his set. Because there was no free slot for the ninth crayon, it kept rolling off the lid and clattering onto the floor.

Maya, meanwhile, had an identical 8-slot crayon case, but she possessed only **7 crayons**. One slot sat completely empty, and she kept glancing at it.

Their teacher, Mr. Vance, noticed the predicament and smiled. "Leo, why don't you give your extra crayon to Maya?"

Leo handed the spare crayon across the table. Instantly, both cases held **exactly 8 crayons**. Neither case had an awkward overflow, and neither had an empty vacancy.

"Notice how neatly that solved the problem?" Mr. Vance asked. "One person had one too many, and the other needed exactly one. Their needs matched perfectly in opposite directions. Atoms do the exact same thing: a sodium atom has 1 troublesome extra electron in its outermost shell, while a chlorine atom is 1 electron short of a full octet. Transferring that electron completes the outer shells of both atoms."

Leo frowned slightly. "Wait, Mr. Vance—after I gave Maya my crayon, we were still separate people. Why do the sodium and chlorine atoms stick together after the transfer?"

Mr. Vance's eyes lit up. "Because unlike crayons, electrons carry an **electric charge**. Giving away or gaining an electron turns neutral atoms into charged ions. And opposite charges attract each other with immense strength! Let's take a closer look."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain English) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ion** | An atom (or group of atoms) that has acquired an electric charge by losing or gaining electrons | $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$ |
| **Cation** | A positively charged ion formed when an atom **loses** electrons | $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ |
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion formed when an atom **gains** electrons | $\text{Cl}^-$, $\text{O}^{2-}$ |
| **Ionic (electrovalent) bond** | The strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by the transfer of electrons from a metal to a non-metal | The bond holding $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ in $\text{NaCl}$ |
| **Ionic compound** | A chemical compound held together by ionic bonds | $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{MgO}$, $\text{MgCl}_2$ |
| **Crystal lattice** | A regular, repeating three-dimensional framework of alternating positive and negative ions | The crystal structure of table salt |
| **Formula unit** | The simplest whole-number ratio of ions represented in an ionic compound (not a discrete molecule) | $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{MgCl}_2$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ |

## Part A: How an Ionic Bond Forms: Na and Cl

Recall our Lewis symbols from Topic 1. When modeling electron transfer, we represent the **metal's valence electrons with crosses ($\mathbf{x}$)** and the **non-metal's valence electrons with dots ($\bullet$)**. All electrons in nature are identical; these symbols merely track where each electron originates.

**Sodium ($\text{Na}$):** Atomic number $Z = 11$, electron configuration $(2, 8, \mathbf{1})$. It has 1 valence electron. Losing this electron leaves a stable second shell of 8: neon-like $(2, 8)$.

**Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$):** Atomic number $Z = 17$, electron configuration $(2, 8, \mathbf{7})$. It has 7 valence electrons, exactly 1 short of an octet. Gaining 1 electron completes its third shell to 8: argon-like $(2, 8, 8)$.

The step-by-step mechanism:

1. **Loss of an electron:** Sodium transfers its 1 valence electron to chlorine. Sodium now has 11 protons ($+11$) and 10 electrons ($-10$), giving a net charge of **$+1$**. It becomes a sodium cation, **$\text{Na}^+$** $(2, 8)$.
2. **Gain of an electron:** Chlorine receives this electron. Chlorine now has 17 protons ($+17$) and 18 electrons ($-18$), giving a net charge of **$-1$**. It becomes a chloride anion, **$\text{Cl}^-$** $(2, 8, 8)$.
3. **Electrostatic attraction:** The positive $\text{Na}^+$ and negative $\text{Cl}^-$ attract each other with powerful electrostatic force, just like opposite magnetic poles. This strong electrostatic attraction is the **ionic bond**.

```
   Before Transfer: Neutral Atoms           After Transfer: Oppositely Charged Ions

                         ..                                    ..
   Na x  ---------->   : Cl .          [Na]⁺               [ : Cl .x ]⁻
                         ..                                    ..

   Na: 2, 8, 1          Cl: 2, 8, 7       Na⁺: 2, 8          Cl⁻: 2, 8, 8
   outer e⁻: 1          outer e⁻: 7       outer e⁻: 8        outer e⁻: 8
   net charge: 0        net charge: 0     net charge: +1     net charge: -1
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Transfer of an electron from sodium to chlorine. Sodium's single valence electron ($\text{x}$) moves into chlorine's valence shell, complementing chlorine's 7 dots to form a complete octet ($\text{.x}$). The resulting ions, $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$, are enclosed in brackets with their net charges indicated outside.

Notice that the nuclei remain completely unchanged: sodium still contains 11 protons, and chlorine still contains 17 protons. Their elemental identities remain intact. Chemists summarize this reaction cleanly: $\text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \text{NaCl}$ (sodium chloride, ordinary table salt).

## Part B: Mg and O: Exchanging Two Electrons

In this case, both atoms exchange **two electrons**.

**Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$):** $Z = 12$, configuration $(2, 8, \mathbf{2})$. Losing its 2 valence electrons leaves a stable neon-like configuration $(2, 8)$.

**Oxygen ($\text{O}$):** $Z = 8$, configuration $(2, \mathbf{6})$. Gaining 2 electrons completes its octet to $(2, 8)$.

Magnesium transfers **both** of its outer electrons directly to oxygen.
- Magnesium: 12 protons, 10 electrons $\rightarrow$ net charge **$+2$**, forming **$\text{Mg}^{2+}$**.
- Oxygen: 8 protons, 10 electrons $\rightarrow$ net charge **$-2$**, forming the oxide ion, **$\text{O}^{2-}$**.

```
        Mg                         O                       Mg²⁺               O²⁻

                                  ..                                          ..
     x Mg x   ------------>      : O                     [Mg]²⁺           [ : O xx ]²⁻
                                  ..                                          ..

   Mg: 2, 8, 2               O: 2, 6                   Mg²⁺: 2, 8         O²⁻: 2, 8
   outer e⁻: 2               outer e⁻: 6               outer e⁻: 8        outer e⁻: 8
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Magnesium transfers both of its valence electrons ($\text{xx}$) to an oxygen atom. The 2 transferred electrons join oxygen's 6 valence electrons to form a complete octet. Both $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ achieve a stable neon configuration $(2, 8)$. One $\text{Mg}$ pairs with one $\text{O}$, giving the formula **$\text{MgO}$** (magnesium oxide).

Here, one magnesium atom donates 2 electrons, and one oxygen atom accepts 2 electrons: **electrons donated = electrons accepted = 2**. Thus, the atoms combine in a simple $1:1$ ratio.

## Part C: Mg and Two Cl: When the Ratio Is Unequal

Now consider magnesium combining with chlorine. Magnesium wants to donate **2 electrons**, but a single chlorine atom can accept only **1 electron** (since it already possesses 7 valence electrons). Even after donating 1 electron to one chlorine atom, magnesium still has 1 leftover electron.

To achieve stability, magnesium must interact with **two separate chlorine atoms**: donating 1 electron to the first, and 1 electron to the second.

```
       ..                               ..                 ..                     ..
     : Cl .  <------- x Mg x -------> . Cl :           [ : Cl .x ]⁻  [Mg]²⁺  [ x. Cl : ]⁻
       ..                               ..                 ..                     ..

   Cl: 2, 8, 7              Mg: 2, 8, 2                Cl⁻: 2, 8, 8   Mg²⁺: 2, 8   Cl⁻: 2, 8, 8
   outer e⁻: 7              outer e⁻: 2                outer e⁻: 8    outer e⁻: 8   outer e⁻: 8
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Formation of magnesium chloride. Magnesium's 2 valence electrons ($\text{x}$) are transferred to two separate chlorine atoms. Each chlorine atom receives 1 electron to complete its octet. Total inventory: one $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ cation balances two $\text{Cl}^-$ anions, resulting in the chemical formula **$\text{MgCl}_2$**.

Electron bookkeeping check: Magnesium donates 2 electrons; two chlorine atoms accept $1 + 1 = 2$ electrons. **Electrons donated = electrons accepted.** The compound is electrically neutral overall: $(+2) + 2(-1) = 0$.

## Part D: Why the Criss-Cross Method Works

In earlier chemistry topics, you learned the mechanical **criss-cross method**: the valency of one element becomes the subscript of the other. At the time, it felt like an arbitrary trick. Now you see the fundamental physical reality behind it:

> **The Fundamental Law of Ionic Bonding:** In any ionic compound, **total electrons lost by metal atoms must equal total electrons gained by non-metal atoms**. This ensures that the compound is electrically neutral (net charge $= 0$).

| Metal | Non-metal | Electrons Lost per Metal Atom | Electrons Gained per Non-metal Atom | Atomic Combining Ratio | Electrons Lost = Gained | Chemical Formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na (1) | Cl (1) | 1 | 1 | 1 Na : 1 Cl | $1 = 1$ | **$\text{NaCl}$** |
| Mg (2) | O (2) | 2 | 2 | 1 Mg : 1 O | $2 = 2$ | **$\text{MgO}$** |
| Mg (2) | Cl (1) | 2 | 1 | 1 Mg : 2 Cl | $2 = 1 + 1$ | **$\text{MgCl}_2$** |
| Na (1) | O (2) | 1 | 2 | 2 Na : 1 O | $1 + 1 = 2$ | **$\text{Na}_2\text{O}$** |
| Ca (2) | F (1) | 2 | 1 | 1 Ca : 2 F | $2 = 1 + 1$ | **$\text{CaF}_2$** |
| Al (3) | O (2) | 3 | 2 | 2 Al : 3 O | $2 \times 3 = 3 \times 2 = 6$ | **$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$** |

Look closely at $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$: Criss-crossing gives a subscript of 2 for Al and 3 for O. This reflects the Lowest Common Multiple (LCM) of their valencies:
- 2 aluminium atoms donate a total of $2 \times 3 = \mathbf{6\text{ electrons}}$.
- 3 oxygen atoms accept a total of $3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6\text{ electrons}}$.
The criss-cross method is simply the arithmetic LCM ensuring electron balance.

*Rule on Simplification:* If both subscripts share a common factor (such as $\text{Mg}_2\text{O}_2$), we always reduce them to the simplest whole-number ratio: **$\text{MgO}$**.

## Part E: Crystal Lattice and the Formula Unit

Up to this point, we have illustrated one sodium cation paired with one chloride anion as if they form an isolated duo. In reality, a tiny grain of table salt contains **billions upon billions** of ions. A sodium cation does not attract just one chloride ion; its positive charge radiates outward in all directions, attracting **all adjacent $\text{Cl}^-$ ions**. Similarly, each chloride ion attracts all surrounding $\text{Na}^+$ ions.

As a result, the ions organize themselves into a vast, highly ordered, three-dimensional framework known as a **crystal lattice**.

```
   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺
   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻
   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺
   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻   Na⁺   Cl⁻

   A central Na⁺ ion is surrounded directly by Cl⁻ ions in all planar directions.
```

> **Figure 2.4** – A two-dimensional slice of a sodium chloride crystal lattice. Every $\text{Na}^+$ ion is bordered by $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, and every $\text{Cl}^-$ ion is bordered by $\text{Na}^+$ ions. In the true three-dimensional crystal, each $\text{Na}^+$ is coordinated by **6 $\text{Cl}^-$ ions** (top, bottom, left, right, front, back), and each $\text{Cl}^-$ is coordinated by **6 $\text{Na}^+$ ions**. Because the overall ratio throughout the crystal is strictly $1:1$, the empirical formula is written as $\text{NaCl}$.

**Why "Formula Unit" instead of "Molecule"?**
In this continuous lattice, no individual $\text{Na}^+$ is exclusively paired with a single $\text{Cl}^-$. There are no discrete, independent units wandering around. Therefore, calling $\text{NaCl}$ a "molecule" is scientifically incorrect. Instead, chemists use the term **formula unit**, which denotes the **simplest whole-number ratio of ions** in the compound ($1:1$ for $\text{NaCl}$, $1:2$ for $\text{MgCl}_2$).

This explains why we calculate **formula unit mass** rather than molecular mass for ionic compounds:
- $\text{NaCl}$: $23 + 35.5 = \mathbf{58.5\text{ u}}$
- $\text{MgO}$: $24 + 16 = \mathbf{40\text{ u}}$
- $\text{MgCl}_2$: $24 + (2 \times 35.5) = \mathbf{95\text{ u}}$

**Naming Ionic Compounds:**
The standard IUPAC rule is straightforward: **state the metal's name first, followed by the non-metal's root with the suffix "-ide"**:
- Sodium + Chlorine $\rightarrow$ **Sodium chloride**
- Magnesium + Oxygen $\rightarrow$ **Magnesium oxide**
- Calcium + Fluorine $\rightarrow$ **Calcium fluoride**
- Aluminium + Oxygen $\rightarrow$ **Aluminium oxide**

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Formation of $\text{NaCl}$ from sodium and chlorine.

- Sodium: $Z = 11$, configuration $(2, 8, 1)$. Donates 1 electron.
- Chlorine: $Z = 17$, configuration $(2, 8, 7)$. Accepts 1 electron.
- Cation: $\text{Na}^+$ (11 protons, 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$, charge $+1$).
- Anion: $\text{Cl}^-$ (17 protons, 18 electrons, configuration $2, 8, 8$, charge $-1$).
- Lewis representation: $\text{Na}\ \text{x} \rightarrow [\text{Na}]^+$ and $:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\cdot\ + \text{x} \rightarrow [:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\mathbf{x}:]^-$.
- Formula: $1\text{ }\text{Na}^+ + 1\text{ }\text{Cl}^- \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{NaCl}}$.

*Verification:* Electrons lost ($1$) = Electrons gained ($1$) ✅. Net charge $= +1 - 1 = 0$ ✅.

### Example 2. Formation of $\text{MgO}$ from magnesium and oxygen.

- $\text{Mg}$: $(2, 8, 2)$ donates 2 electrons $\rightarrow \text{Mg}^{2+}$ (12 protons, 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$).
- $\text{O}$: $(2, 6)$ accepts 2 electrons $\rightarrow \text{O}^{2-}$ (8 protons, 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$).
- Lewis representation:
```
                      ..
       [Mg]²⁺      [ : O : ]²⁻
                      ..
```
- Formula: $\mathbf{\text{MgO}}$ (reduced from $1:1$ ratio).

*Verification:* Electrons transferred $= 2$ ✅. Net charge $= +2 - 2 = 0$ ✅. Both ions have neon-like electron configurations $(2, 8)$ ✅.

### Example 3. Formation of $\text{MgCl}_2$.

- $\text{Mg}$ loses 2 electrons. Each $\text{Cl}$ can accept only 1 electron, requiring 2 chlorine atoms.
- $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has 10 electrons; each $\text{Cl}^-$ has 18 electrons.
```
        ..                   ..
    [ : Cl : ]⁻  [Mg]²⁺  [ : Cl : ]⁻
        ..                   ..
```
- Formula: $\mathbf{\text{MgCl}_2}$ (magnesium chloride).

*Verification:* Electrons donated $= 2$; electrons accepted $= 1 + 1 = 2$ ✅. Net charge $= (+2) + 2(-1) = 0$ ✅.

### Example 4. Formation of aluminium oxide ($\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$).

- Aluminium: $(2, 8, 3)$ loses 3 electrons $\rightarrow \text{Al}^{3+}$ (10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$).
- Oxygen: $(2, 6)$ gains 2 electrons $\rightarrow \text{O}^{2-}$ (10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$).
- $\text{LCM}(3, 2) = 6$. Therefore, $6 \div 3 = \mathbf{2\text{ Al atoms}}$ and $6 \div 2 = \mathbf{3\text{ O atoms}}$.
- Two $\text{Al}^{3+}$ cations carry total charge $+6$; three $\text{O}^{2-}$ anions carry total charge $-6$.
- Formula: $\mathbf{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}$.

*Verification:* $2 \times 3 = 6$ electrons donated $= 3 \times 2 = 6$ electrons accepted ✅. Net charge $= +6 - 6 = 0$ ✅.

### Example 5. Formation of calcium fluoride ($\text{CaF}_2$).

- $\text{Ca}$: $(2, 8, 8, 2)$ loses 2 electrons $\rightarrow \text{Ca}^{2+}$ (20 protons, 18 electrons, configuration $2, 8, 8$, argon-like).
- $\text{F}$: $(2, 7)$ gains 1 electron $\rightarrow \text{F}^-$ (9 protons, 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$, neon-like).
- 1 calcium atom requires 2 fluorine atoms. Formula: $\mathbf{\text{CaF}_2}$ (calcium fluoride).

*Verification:* Electrons lost $= 2$; electrons gained $= 1 + 1 = 2$ ✅. Net charge $= +2 + 2(-1) = 0$ ✅.

### Example 6. Formation of sodium oxide ($\text{Na}_2\text{O}$).

- Each sodium atom loses 1 electron. One oxygen atom requires 2 electrons.
- Therefore, **2 sodium atoms** must combine with **1 oxygen atom**.
- Result: $2\text{ }\text{Na}^+$ cations and $1\text{ }\text{O}^{2-}$ anion. Formula: $\mathbf{\text{Na}_2\text{O}}$ (sodium oxide).

*Verification:* Electrons lost $= 2 \times 1 = 2$; electrons gained $= 1 \times 2 = 2$ ✅. Net charge $= 2(+1) - 2 = 0$ ✅.

### Example 7. Formation of lithium fluoride ($\text{LiF}$) involving a helium-like duplet.

- $\text{Li}$: $(2, 1)$ loses 1 electron $\rightarrow \text{Li}^+$ (3 protons, 2 electrons, helium-like duplet configuration).
- $\text{F}$: $(2, 7)$ gains 1 electron $\rightarrow \text{F}^-$ (9 protons, 10 electrons, neon-like octet configuration $2, 8$).
- Combining ratio: $1:1$. Formula: $\mathbf{\text{LiF}}$ (lithium fluoride).

*Verification:* Notice that $\text{Li}^+$ achieves stability via a duplet, not an octet, which is completely valid for period 2's smallest element ✅.

### Example 8. Formation of calcium oxide ($\text{CaO}$).

- $\text{Ca}$ loses 2 electrons; $\text{O}$ gains 2 electrons.
- $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ $(2, 8, 8)$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ $(2, 8)$.
- Simplest ratio is $1:1$. Formula: $\mathbf{\text{CaO}}$ (calcium oxide, commonly known as quicklime).

*Verification:* Electrons lost $= 2$, electrons gained $= 2$ ✅. Net charge $= +2 - 2 = 0$ ✅.

### Example 9. Predicting compound formation from electron configurations.

A metal atom has configuration $(2, 8, 8, 1)$ and a non-metal atom has configuration $(2, 8, 6)$. What compound will they form?

- Metal: Total electrons $= 19 \rightarrow$ Potassium ($\text{K}$). Valence electrons $= 1$, so it loses 1 electron.
- Non-metal: Total electrons $= 16 \rightarrow$ Sulphur ($\text{S}$). Valence electrons $= 6$, so it needs 2 electrons.
- One sulphur atom requires 2 potassium atoms to supply 2 electrons.
- Formula: $\mathbf{\text{K}_2\text{S}}$ (potassium sulphide).

*Verification:* $2 \times 1 = 2$ electrons donated $= 1 \times 2 = 2$ electrons accepted ✅.

### Example 10. Counting protons and electrons in ions.

State the number of protons and electrons in $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cl}^-$. Are they different elements?

- $\text{Mg}^{2+}$: Atomic number $Z = 12 \rightarrow \mathbf{12\text{ protons}}$. Electrons $= 12 - 2 = \mathbf{10\text{ electrons}}$.
- $\text{Cl}^-$: Atomic number $Z = 17 \rightarrow \mathbf{17\text{ protons}}$. Electrons $= 17 + 1 = \mathbf{18\text{ electrons}}$.
- They remain completely different elements because their proton counts (12 vs. 17) remain unchanged.

*Verification:* Net charge on $\text{Mg}^{2+} = +12 - 10 = +2$ ✅. Net charge on $\text{Cl}^- = +17 - 18 = -1$ ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Treating atoms in an ionic compound as neutral atoms**
   In solid $\text{NaCl}$, there are no neutral sodium or chlorine atoms; they exist exclusively as $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions. This is why table salt does not exhibit the explosive reactivity of metallic sodium or the choking toxicity of chlorine gas.

2. **Mistake 2: Referring to an ionic compound as a "molecule"**
   Ionic compounds form extensive 3D crystal lattices, not isolated molecules. The correct scientific term for $\text{NaCl}$ or $\text{MgCl}_2$ is a **formula unit**.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing cation and anion charge signs**
   Losing negatively charged electrons makes an ion **positive** (cation). Gaining electrons makes it **negative** (anion). Magnesium losing 2 electrons becomes $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, never $\text{Mg}^{2-}$.

4. **Mistake 4: Failing to balance donated and accepted electrons**
   Always check that: Total electrons lost $=$ Total electrons gained. If this condition is violated, the chemical formula is incorrect.

5. **Mistake 5: Believing an ion turns into a noble gas atom**
   $\text{Na}^+$ has 10 electrons (just like neon), but it is **not** neon. It still contains 11 protons in its nucleus and remains a charged sodium ion.

6. **Mistake 6: Writing ionic charges inside final chemical formulas**
   Write the formula of magnesium chloride as $\text{MgCl}_2$, never as $\text{Mg}^{2+}\text{Cl}^-_2$. Charges cancel out overall and are omitted in standard chemical formulas.

7. **Mistake 7: Reversing the roles of metals and non-metals**
   In an ionic bond, the metal is always the electron donor (forming the cation) and the non-metal is always the electron acceptor (forming the anion).

## Quick Recap

- An **ionic bond** is formed when a metal atom transfers one or more electrons to a non-metal atom, held together by electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
- The metal loses electrons to form a positive **cation**, while the non-metal gains electrons to form a negative **anion**. Both achieve stable electronic configurations (octets or duplets).
- Reaction summary: $\text{Na} (2, 8, 1) + \text{Cl} (2, 8, 7) \rightarrow \text{Na}^+ (2, 8) + \text{Cl}^- (2, 8, 8) \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{NaCl}}$.
- Key formulas: $\text{MgO}$ ($2$ donated $= 2$ accepted), $\text{MgCl}_2$ ($2$ donated $= 2 \times 1$ accepted), $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ ($6$ donated $= 6$ accepted), $\text{CaF}_2$ ($2$ donated $= 2 \times 1$ accepted).
- The **criss-cross method** works because it balances the total electrons lost with total electrons gained, ensuring net electric neutrality.
- Ionic compounds exist as a continuous **crystal lattice**. In $\text{NaCl}$, each $\text{Na}^+$ is surrounded by 6 $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, and each $\text{Cl}^-$ by 6 $\text{Na}^+$ ions.
- Because no discrete molecules exist, we use the term **formula unit** (and formula unit mass: $\text{NaCl} = 58.5\text{ u}$).
- Naming rule: State the metal first, followed by the non-metal root ending in **-ide** (e.g., sodium chloride, calcium fluoride).

### Answers

1. $\text{Na} (2, 8, 1)$ loses 1 electron to become $\mathbf{\text{Na}^+}$ (11 protons, 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$, net charge $+1$). $\text{Cl} (2, 8, 7)$ gains 1 electron to become $\mathbf{\text{Cl}^-}$ (17 protons, 18 electrons, configuration $2, 8, 8$, net charge $-1$).
   Lewis diagram: $\text{Na}\ \text{x} \rightarrow [\text{Na}]^+$ and $:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\cdot\ + \text{x} \rightarrow [:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\mathbf{x}:]^-$. The resulting electrostatic attraction produces the ionic compound **$\text{NaCl}$**.

2. Magnesium loses 2 electrons ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$: 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$); oxygen gains 2 electrons ($\text{O}^{2-}$: 10 electrons, configuration $2, 8$). Electrons transferred $= 2$. Chemical formulas always express the **simplest whole-number ratio** of ions, so the $1:1$ ratio is written as **$\text{MgO}$**, never $\text{Mg}_2\text{O}_2$.

3. Magnesium must lose 2 valence electrons, but each chlorine atom has capacity for only 1 electron. Therefore, 2 chlorine atoms are required.
   Diagram: $[:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\mathbf{x}:]^- \ [\text{Mg}]^{2+} \ [:\ddot{\text{Cl}}\mathbf{x}:]^-$.
   Check: Electrons lost $= 2$; electrons gained $= 1 + 1 = 2$ ✅. Formula: **$\text{MgCl}_2$**.

4. Each $\text{Al}$ loses 3 electrons; each $\text{S}$ $(2, 8, 6)$ gains 2 electrons. The lowest common multiple is 6.
   - 2 $\text{Al}$ atoms $\times 3\text{ e}^- = 6\text{ e}^-$ lost.
   - 3 $\text{S}$ atoms $\times 2\text{ e}^- = 6\text{ e}^-$ gained.
   Charge: $2(+3) = +6$; $3(-2) = -6$; net charge $= 0$ ✅. Formula: **$\text{Al}_2\text{S}_3$** (aluminium sulphide).

5. (a) $\text{K}$ loses 1, $\text{F}$ gains 1 $\rightarrow$ **$\text{KF}$**.
   (b) $\text{Ca}$ loses 2, each $\text{Cl}$ gains 1 $\rightarrow$ **$\text{CaCl}_2$**.
   (c) Each $\text{Li}$ loses 1, $\text{O}$ gains 2 $\rightarrow$ **$\text{Li}_2\text{O}$**.
   (d) $\text{Al}$ loses 3, each $\text{F}$ gains 1 $\rightarrow$ **$\text{AlF}_3$**.
   In each case, electrons lost equal electrons gained ✅.

6. (a) $\text{Ca}^{2+}$: **20 protons, 18 electrons** $\rightarrow$ shares argon's configuration $(2, 8, 8)$.
   (b) $\text{F}^-$: **9 protons, 10 electrons** $\rightarrow$ shares neon's configuration $(2, 8)$.
   (c) $\text{Li}^+$: **3 protons, 2 electrons** $\rightarrow$ shares helium's duplet configuration $(2)$.
   (d) $\text{S}^{2-}$: **16 protons, 18 electrons** $\rightarrow$ shares argon's configuration $(2, 8, 8)$.

7. In the criss-cross method, aluminium's valency (3) becomes the subscript of oxygen, and oxygen's valency (2) becomes the subscript of aluminium: **$\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$**. This means 2 aluminium atoms donate $2 \times 3 = 6$ electrons, while 3 oxygen atoms accept $3 \times 2 = 6$ electrons. The criss-cross method is essentially a mathematical shortcut ensuring that total electrons lost equal total electrons gained.

8. In sodium chloride, each $\text{Na}^+$ ion is coordinated by **6 $\text{Cl}^-$ ions** (and vice versa). Because no single $\text{Na}^+$ is uniquely bound to one $\text{Cl}^-$, there are no isolated molecules; hence, the ratio is called a **formula unit** ($\text{NaCl}$, $1:1$).

9. - $\text{Na}_2\text{O} = (2 \times 23) + 16 = 46 + 16 = \mathbf{62\text{ u}}$.
   - $\text{CaO} = 40 + 16 = \mathbf{56\text{ u}}$.
   - $\text{CaF}_2 = 40 + (2 \times 19) = 40 + 38 = \mathbf{78\text{ u}}$.

10. The particles in $\text{NaCl}$ are not neutral atoms; they are stable **ions** ($\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$) with completely filled valence shells. Because their electron configurations are entirely different from neutral sodium and chlorine atoms, their chemical and physical properties are completely different.

   Formula: **$\text{MgF}_2$** (magnesium fluoride). Electrons lost $= 2 = 1 + 1$ gained ✅.
