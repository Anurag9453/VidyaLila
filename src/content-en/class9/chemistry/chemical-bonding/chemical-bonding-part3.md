# Topic 3: Covalent Bond: Sharing Electrons

In the previous topic, a metal atom and a non-metal atom engaged in an outright transfer of electrons: sodium gave up an electron, chlorine accepted it, and the resulting oppositely charged ions bonded tightly. What happens, however, when **both reacting atoms are non-metals**—such as two chlorine atoms, or hydrogen and oxygen? Both atoms have nearly full valence shells and need electrons to reach an octet or duplet; neither is willing to give electrons away. How can a bond form when neither partner can donate? Today we discover nature's ingenious solution: **mutual electron sharing**.

---

## The Story of Shared Earphones

During a school bus trip to the science museum, two classmates, Liam and Leo, sat next to each other. Liam had a set of wired earphones plugged into his phone and was eager to listen to a new album. Leo did not have any earphones with him, but he was just as eager to listen to the music.

They faced an obvious dilemma: if Liam kept both earbuds, Leo heard nothing. If Liam handed both earbuds to Leo, Liam heard nothing.

"Why don't we share?" Liam suggested. "You take the right earbud, and I'll take the left."

They popped the earbuds into their respective ears. Instantly, both heard the music in full stereo, and both were thoroughly entertained throughout the journey.

Their science teacher, Ms. Carter, watched the interaction and smiled. "Notice what just happened," she observed. "Liam did not permanently give his earphones away to Leo, and Leo did not take them from Liam. They **shared a single pair**. Because the pair rested between them, it met the needs of both friends simultaneously."

"And notice something else," Ms. Carter added. "The music only plays as long as both friends remain connected to that shared pair of earbuds. If either moves away, the listening session ends. Atoms behave in the exact same manner: when two atoms both need electrons and neither can afford to surrender any, they **share pairs of electrons**. The shared electron pair holds both atomic nuclei together. That mutual bond is called a **covalent bond**."

```
      Liam                          Leo
     (ear)                         (ear)
      (o)                           (o)
       \                            /
        \____ one set of earphones __/
             left earbud   right earbud
             Liam's ear    Leo's ear

       Shared pair: neither loses, neither takes; both benefit
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The shared earphones analogy. One set of earphones with two earbuds serves two listeners simultaneously. In a covalent bond, a shared pair of valence electrons sits between two atomic nuclei, counting toward the stable outer shell of both atoms and holding them together in a chemical bond.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain English) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Covalent bond** | A chemical bond formed by the mutual sharing of one or more pairs of electrons between two atoms | The $\text{H}-\text{H}$ bond in $\text{H}_2$ |
| **Shared pair (bonding pair)** | A pair of electrons shared between two atoms that counts toward the valence shell of both | 1 shared pair in $\text{H}_2$ |
| **Single covalent bond** | A bond consisting of 1 shared pair of electrons, represented by a single dash ($-$) | $\text{H}-\text{Cl}$ |
| **Double covalent bond** | A bond consisting of 2 shared pairs of electrons, represented by a double dash ($=$) | $\text{O}=\text{O}$ |
| **Triple covalent bond** | A bond consisting of 3 shared pairs of electrons, represented by a triple dash ($\equiv$) | $\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$ |
| **Molecule** | A distinct, electrically neutral group of atoms held together firmly by covalent bonds | $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CO}_2$, $\text{CH}_4$ |
| **Lone pair (non-bonding pair)** | A pair of valence electrons that is not shared in bonding and remains localized on one atom | Each $\text{Cl}$ atom in $\text{Cl}_2$ retains 3 lone pairs |
| **Dot-and-cross diagram** | A representation tracking valence electrons from different atoms using dots ($\bullet$) and crosses ($\mathbf{x}$) | See Part C diagrams below |

## Part A: Why Don't Two Non-Metals Transfer Electrons?

In Topic 1, we learned that metals (with 1 to 3 valence electrons) readily lose electrons, whereas non-metals (with 4 to 7 valence electrons) have a strong tendency to gain or share electrons.

Imagine two chlorine atoms coming together. Each chlorine atom has 7 valence electrons and requires 1 electron to complete its octet. Neither chlorine atom can surrender an electron: if one did, its valence count would drop to 6, moving it further away from stability! Stripping an electron from a chlorine atom requires an enormous input of energy. It is like two children who both crave the exact same chocolate bar, but only one bar exists. The only harmonious solution is to **share**.

Similarly, when hydrogen bonds with chlorine, carbon bonds with hydrogen, or nitrogen bonds with hydrogen, both partners require additional electrons to complete their valence shells. Transfer is energetically impossible, so sharing is the only pathway.

> **Golden Rule of Bonding:**
> - **Metal + Non-Metal $\rightarrow$ Ionic Bond** (electron transfer).
> - **Non-Metal + Non-Metal $\rightarrow$ Covalent Bond** (electron sharing).

## Part B: The Rule of Sharing: Verify by Counting

In a covalent bond, **one shared pair contains 2 electrons**. These two electrons are attracted simultaneously to the positive nuclei of both bonding atoms, and they **count fully toward the valence shell of both atoms**.

Just like the shared earphones, which provide music to both Liam and Leo, a shared electron pair contributes 2 electrons to the octet or duplet of each participating atom.

**The Universal Electron Counting Formula for Any Atom:**

$$\text{Total Valence Electrons around Atom} = (\text{Electrons in Lone Pairs}) + 2 \times (\text{Number of Covalent Bonds})$$

For Hydrogen, the target count is **2 electrons (duplet)**. For all other main-group non-metals, the target count is **8 electrons (octet)**.

To make our diagrams completely clear, we represent the valence electrons of one atom with **dots ($\bullet$)** and those of the partner atom with **crosses ($\mathbf{x}$)**. Let us examine the molecules step by step, starting with the simplest.

## Part C: Single Covalent Bonds: H₂, Cl₂, and HCl

### Hydrogen Molecule ($\text{H}_2$): The Simplest Molecule

A hydrogen atom has only 1 electron in its K shell. It needs 1 additional electron to complete its duplet (achieving helium's configuration). When two hydrogen atoms meet, **each contributes its single electron to form a shared pair**.

```
     H .   +   x H      ---->      H .x H      ---->      H - H

   (electron • from       (electron x from       shared pair         single bond
    first H atom)          second H atom)        (2 electrons)       (one dash)
```

**Verification:** The first hydrogen atom now counts 2 electrons (the shared pair), and the second hydrogen atom also counts 2 electrons. Both atoms achieve a stable **duplet**, identical to helium.

### Chlorine Molecule ($\text{Cl}_2$)

Chlorine has configuration $(2, 8, \mathbf{7})$. Each chlorine atom has 7 valence electrons and is **1 electron short** of an octet. Two chlorine atoms share **1 pair of electrons**. The remaining 6 valence electrons on each atom remain unshared as **3 lone pairs**.

```
        ..         xx                 ..    xx
      : Cl .  +  x Cl xx     --->   : Cl .x Cl xx     --->   Cl - Cl
        ..         xx                 ..    xx
    (7 valence)  (7 valence)       shared pair in center      single bond
```

**Verification:** Each chlorine atom holds 3 lone pairs ($3 \times 2 = 6\text{ electrons}$) plus 1 shared pair ($2\text{ electrons}$):
$$6 + 2 = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$$
Both chlorine atoms successfully complete their octet.

### Hydrogen Chloride ($\text{HCl}$): Two Different Non-Metals

Hydrogen has 1 electron (needs 1 for a duplet). Chlorine has 7 valence electrons (needs 1 for an octet). Both atoms require exactly 1 electron, so they share **1 pair**:

```
                  ..                 ..                    ..
      H x   +   . Cl :   --->   H x. Cl :     --->     H - Cl :
                  ..                 ..                    ..
   (1 valence)  (7 valence)     shared pair in center      single bond
```

**Verification:**
- Hydrogen counts 2 electrons (the shared pair) $\rightarrow$ stable **duplet** ✅.
- Chlorine counts 6 unshared electrons (3 lone pairs) $+ 2$ shared electrons $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ $\rightarrow$ stable **octet** ✅.

```
   H-H            Cl-Cl           H-Cl
   H₂             Cl₂             HCl
   (1 bond)       (1 bond,        (1 bond,
                   6 unshared e⁻)  6 unshared e⁻ on Cl)
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Single covalent bonds in $\text{H}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$, and $\text{HCl}$. Each line represents one shared pair (2 electrons). While hydrogen has no lone pairs, chlorine retains three non-bonding lone pairs (6 electrons).

## Part D: Solved Examples: Single Bonds

### Example 1. Construct the Lewis structure for $\text{H}_2$ and verify the electron count.

- Each $\text{H}$ atom brings 1 electron.
- Two $\text{H}$ atoms share their electrons: $\text{H}\bullet\mathbf{x}\text{H}$, drawn as $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{H}}$.
- Molecular formula: $\mathbf{\text{H}_2}$.

*Verification:* First $\text{H} = 2$ electrons (duplet) ✅. Second $\text{H} = 2$ electrons (duplet) ✅. Total valence electrons $= 1 + 1 = 2$ ✅.

### Example 2. Construct the Lewis structure for $\text{Cl}_2$ and count lone pairs.

- Each $\text{Cl}$ atom has configuration $(2, 8, 7)$ and needs 1 electron.
- They form 1 shared pair: $\mathbf{\text{Cl}-\text{Cl}}$.
- Each $\text{Cl}$ has $7 - 1 = 6$ unshared electrons, which form **3 lone pairs per chlorine atom**.

*Verification:* Each $\text{Cl}: 6\text{ (lone pairs)} + 2\text{ (bond)} = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Total valence electrons $= 7 + 7 = 14$; bonding electrons ($2$) $+$ non-bonding electrons ($12$) $= 14$ ✅.

### Example 3. Construct the Lewis structure for $\text{HCl}$.

- $\text{H}$ ($1$ valence electron) shares 1 pair with $\text{Cl}$ ($7$ valence electrons): $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{Cl}}$.
- $\text{H}$ achieves a duplet ($2$ electrons) ✅.
- $\text{Cl}$ retains 3 lone pairs ($6$ electrons) plus the shared pair ($2$ electrons) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅.
- Total valence electrons $= 1 + 7 = 8$ ✅.
- Note that $\text{HCl}$ is a **compound molecule** (composed of two different elements), whereas $\text{H}_2$ and $\text{Cl}_2$ are **elemental molecules**.

## Part E: Atoms with Multiple Bonds: H₂O, NH₃, and CH₄

Recall the valencies from atomic structure: **Hydrogen = 1, Oxygen = 2, Nitrogen = 3, Carbon = 4**. In covalent molecules:

$$\mathbf{\text{Valency}} = \mathbf{\text{Number of Covalent Bonds Formed by that Atom}}$$

### Water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)

Oxygen has configuration $(2, \mathbf{6})$. It requires **2 electrons** to complete its octet. Hydrogen needs 1 electron. Therefore, **one oxygen atom bonds with two hydrogen atoms**, sharing 1 pair with each:

```
   Dot (O electrons) and cross (H electrons):

             ..
        H x. O .x H       <-- 2 lone pairs on O (top and bottom)
             ..

   Structural formula:   H - O - H      (O has 2 lone pairs)
```

**Verification:**
- Oxygen has 2 shared pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $+ 2$ lone pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.
- Each hydrogen atom has 1 shared pair $= \mathbf{2\text{ electrons}}$ (duplet) ✅.

### Ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$)

Nitrogen has configuration $(2, \mathbf{5})$. It requires **3 electrons** to complete its octet. It bonds with **three hydrogen atoms**, forming 3 single covalent bonds. One pair of valence electrons remains unshared as a **lone pair** on nitrogen:

```
   Dot (N electrons) and cross (H electrons) for NH₃:

            H
            x
            .
       H x. N .x H         <-- 1 lone pair on N (bottom)
            ..

   Structural formula:     H - N - H       (N has 1 lone pair)
                               |
                               H
```

**Verification:**
- Nitrogen: 3 shared pairs ($6\text{ electrons}$) $+ 1$ lone pair ($2\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.
- Each hydrogen: 1 shared pair $= \mathbf{2\text{ electrons}}$ (duplet) ✅.

```
   Water, H₂O                    Ammonia, NH₃

          ..                          ..
        H-O-H    (2 bonds,          H-N-H     (3 bonds,
          ..      2 lone pairs)       |        1 lone pair)
                                      H
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Molecular structures of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{NH}_3$. Oxygen forms 2 single bonds and retains 2 lone pairs. Nitrogen forms 3 single bonds and retains 1 lone pair. In both molecules, every atom achieves an octet or duplet. (Advanced geometric shapes, such as the bent shape of water or the pyramidal shape of ammonia, will be explored in higher classes.)

### Methane ($\text{CH}_4$)

Carbon has configuration $(2, \mathbf{4})$. It requires **4 electrons** to complete its octet. Carbon bonds with **four hydrogen atoms**, sharing 1 pair with each. Because all 4 of carbon's valence electrons participate in bonding, carbon retains **zero lone pairs**:

```
             H
             x                        H
             .                        |
        H x. C .x H     ---->     H - C - H
             .                        |
             x                        H
             H

        Carbon's 4 valence electrons (dots) share with 4 H electrons (crosses):
        4 shared pairs, 0 lone pairs.
```

**Verification:**
- Carbon: 4 shared pairs $= 4 \times 2 = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.
- Each hydrogen: 1 shared pair $= \mathbf{2\text{ electrons}}$ (duplet) ✅.

> **Figure 3.4** – Methane ($\text{CH}_4$): a central carbon atom bonded to four hydrogen atoms via four single covalent bonds. Carbon has zero lone pairs.

## Part F: Solved Examples: H₂O, NH₃, and CH₄

### Example 4. Determine bonds and lone pairs in water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$).

- $\text{O}$ $(2, 6)$ needs 2 electrons $\rightarrow$ forms 2 single bonds with two $\text{H}$ atoms.
- Unshared electrons on oxygen $= 6 - 2 = 4\text{ electrons} = \mathbf{2\text{ lone pairs}}$.
- Structural representation: $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{O}-\text{H}}$.

*Verification:* Oxygen has $2 \times 2 = 4$ bonding electrons $+ 4$ non-bonding electrons $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Each $\text{H}$ has $2$ electrons ✅. Total valence count $= 6 + 1 + 1 = 8$ ✅.

### Example 5. Determine bonds and lone pairs in ammonia ($\text{NH}_3$).

- $\text{N}$ $(2, 5)$ needs 3 electrons $\rightarrow$ forms 3 single bonds with three $\text{H}$ atoms.
- Unshared electrons on nitrogen $= 5 - 3 = 2\text{ electrons} = \mathbf{1\text{ lone pair}}$.

*Verification:* Nitrogen has $3 \times 2 = 6$ bonding electrons $+ 2$ non-bonding electrons $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Each $\text{H}$ has $2$ electrons ✅. Total valence count $= 5 + 3(1) = 8$ ✅.

### Example 6. Determine bonds and lone pairs in methane ($\text{CH}_4$).

- $\text{C}$ $(2, 4)$ needs 4 electrons $\rightarrow$ forms 4 single bonds with four $\text{H}$ atoms.
- Unshared electrons on carbon $= 4 - 4 = 0$ lone pairs.

*Verification:* Carbon has $4 \times 2 = \mathbf{8\text{ bonding electrons}}$ ✅. Each $\text{H}$ has $2$ electrons ✅. Total valence count $= 4 + 4(1) = 8$ ✅.

Notice the consistent trend: **Number of covalent bonds = Valency**.
- Oxygen: valency 2 $\rightarrow$ 2 bonds ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)
- Nitrogen: valency 3 $\rightarrow$ 3 bonds ($\text{NH}_3$)
- Carbon: valency 4 $\rightarrow$ 4 bonds ($\text{CH}_4$)

## Part G: Double and Triple Bonds

Sometimes, sharing a single pair of electrons between two atoms is insufficient to fulfill their octets. In such cases, two atoms share **two or three pairs of electrons**.

Returning to our analogy: if Liam and Leo had two sets of earphones and shared both simultaneously, the physical link connecting them would be twice as strong. That is exactly what happens in double and triple covalent bonds.

### Oxygen Molecule ($\text{O}_2$): A Double Covalent Bond

Oxygen $(2, 6)$ requires **2 electrons**. If two oxygen atoms shared only 1 pair, each atom would reach only 7 valence electrons ($6 + 1 = 7$). Therefore, the two oxygen atoms share **2 pairs of electrons** (4 electrons in total), forming a **double bond**:

```
       ..       xx                 ..      ..
       O  .x.x  O       ---->      O   =   O        (each O retains 2 lone pairs)
       ..       xx                 ..      ..
```

**Verification:** Each oxygen atom counts 2 shared pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $+ 2$ lone pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.

### Nitrogen Molecule ($\text{N}_2$): A Triple Covalent Bond

Nitrogen $(2, 5)$ requires **3 electrons**. Two nitrogen atoms share **3 pairs of electrons** (6 electrons in total), forming an exceptionally strong **triple bond**:

```
       : N .x.x.x N xx     ---->     : N ≡ N :      (each N retains 1 lone pair)
```

**Verification:** Each nitrogen atom counts 3 shared pairs ($6\text{ electrons}$) $+ 1$ lone pair ($2\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅. This triple bond is extremely strong and difficult to break, which is why nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$), making up 78% of Earth's atmosphere, is remarkably unreactive.

### Carbon Dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$): Two Double Bonds

Carbon $(2, 4)$ requires 4 electrons. Each oxygen atom $(2, 6)$ requires 2 electrons. Carbon shares **2 pairs with one oxygen atom** and **2 pairs with the other oxygen atom**:

```
       ..              ..              ..          ..
       O  ..xx C xx..  O      ---->    O  =  C  =  O
       ..              ..              ..          ..
```

**Verification:**
- Carbon forms 2 double bonds $= 4$ shared pairs $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.
- Each oxygen atom has 2 shared pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $+ 2$ lone pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.

```
   O=O  (O₂)            N≡N  (N₂)           O=C=O  (CO₂)

   2 shared pairs       3 shared pairs      2 shared pairs between C
   each O retains 2     each N retains 1    and each O (4 pairs total);
   lone pairs           lone pair           each O retains 2 lone pairs
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Double and triple covalent bonds in $\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$, and $\text{CO}_2$. Each dash represents one shared pair of electrons. Every atom achieves an octet. Notice that in $\text{CO}_2$, carbon satisfies its valency of 4 by forming two double bonds.

## Part H: Solved Examples: Double and Triple Bonds

### Example 7. Why does oxygen form a double bond in $\text{O}_2$?

- Each $\text{O}$ atom has 6 valence electrons and needs 2 electrons to reach 8.
- If they shared 1 pair, each would reach $6 + 1 = 7$ electrons (incomplete).
- By sharing **2 pairs** (4 electrons), each atom counts $4\text{ (shared)} + 4\text{ (lone pairs)} = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅.
- Structural formula: $\mathbf{\text{O}=\text{O}}$.

*Verification:* Total valence electrons $= 6 + 6 = 12$. In the molecule: 4 bonding electrons $+ 8$ non-bonding electrons $= 12$ ✅.

### Example 8. Triple bond in the nitrogen molecule ($\text{N}_2$).

- Each $\text{N}$ atom has 5 valence electrons and needs 3 electrons.
- The two atoms share **3 pairs** of electrons: $\mathbf{\text{N}\equiv\text{N}}$.
- Each nitrogen atom retains 1 lone pair ($2$ electrons).

*Verification:* Each $\text{N}: 6\text{ (shared)} + 2\text{ (lone pair)} = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Total valence electrons $= 5 + 5 = 10$; 6 bonding $+ 4$ non-bonding $= 10$ ✅.

### Example 9. Covalent bonding in carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$).

- $\text{C}$ $(2, 4)$ needs 4 electrons; each $\text{O}$ $(2, 6)$ needs 2 electrons.
- Carbon shares 2 electron pairs with each oxygen atom, yielding two double bonds: $\mathbf{\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O}}$.

*Verification:* Carbon has $4 \times 2 = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Each oxygen has $2 \times 2 + 4 = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Total valence electrons $= 4 + 2(6) = 16$; 8 bonding $+ 8$ non-bonding $= 16$ ✅.

### Example 10. Predict the bond type in hydrogen fluoride ($\text{HF}$).

- $\text{H}$ ($1$ valence electron, needs 1) and $\text{F}$ ($(2, 7)$, needs 1) are both non-metals.
- Both need 1 electron, so they share **1 pair**: single covalent bond, $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{F}}$.
- Fluorine retains 3 lone pairs (6 unshared electrons).

*Verification:* $\text{H}$ reaches a duplet ($2$ electrons) ✅. $\text{F}$ reaches an octet ($6 + 2 = 8$ electrons) ✅. The bonding pattern matches $\text{HCl}$ exactly because fluorine and chlorine are both halogens with 7 valence electrons.

## Part I: Covalent Compounds Form Discrete Molecules

In Topic 2, we emphasized that ionic compounds like $\text{NaCl}$ do not form individual molecules; they exist as vast, continuous crystal lattices characterized by **formula units**.

Covalent substances behave completely differently. A water molecule consists of **two hydrogen atoms bonded to one oxygen atom**. This three-atom group forms a **discrete, self-contained, independent unit: a molecule**. In a glass of water, billions of these individual $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ molecules move alongside one another, like tennis balls in a bin.

Because these units exist independently, the term **molecular mass** applies directly to covalent compounds:

| Molecule | Constituent Atoms | Molecular Mass in $\text{u}$ ($\text{H}=1, \text{C}=12, \text{N}=14, \text{O}=16, \text{Cl}=35.5$) |
|---|---|---|
| $\text{H}_2$ | $2\text{ H}$ | $2 \times 1 = \mathbf{2}$ |
| $\text{Cl}_2$ | $2\text{ Cl}$ | $2 \times 35.5 = \mathbf{71}$ |
| $\text{HCl}$ | $1\text{ H}, 1\text{ Cl}$ | $1 + 35.5 = \mathbf{36.5}$ |
| $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ | $2\text{ H}, 1\text{ O}$ | $(2 \times 1) + 16 = \mathbf{18}$ |
| $\text{NH}_3$ | $1\text{ N}, 3\text{ H}$ | $14 + (3 \times 1) = \mathbf{17}$ |
| $\text{CH}_4$ | $1\text{ C}, 4\text{ H}$ | $12 + (4 \times 1) = \mathbf{16}$ |
| $\text{O}_2$ | $2\text{ O}$ | $2 \times 16 = \mathbf{32}$ |
| $\text{N}_2$ | $2\text{ N}$ | $2 \times 14 = \mathbf{28}$ |
| $\text{CO}_2$ | $1\text{ C}, 2\text{ O}$ | $12 + (2 \times 16) = \mathbf{44}$ |

**A Vital Distinction for Upcoming Topics:**
- **Intramolecular forces:** The covalent bonds holding atoms together *inside* a molecule are **extremely strong**.
- **Intermolecular forces:** The attractive forces operating *between* separate molecules are **relatively weak**.

This crucial difference governs why covalent compounds melt, boil, and behave so differently from ionic compounds, as we will explore in Topic 4.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming all valence electrons are shared**
   In $\text{Cl}_2$, each chlorine atom shares only **1 electron** out of its 7 valence electrons. The remaining 6 electrons stay with the atom as 3 lone pairs. Only the electrons needed to reach an octet are shared.

2. **Mistake 2: Forgetting to count lone pairs when checking octets**
   When verifying an atom's electron count, you must count both bonding pairs and lone pairs. For example, each oxygen atom in $\text{O}_2$ has 4 bonding electrons plus 4 non-bonding electrons $= 8$ electrons.

3. **Mistake 3: Forcing an octet onto hydrogen**
   Hydrogen's valence shell is the K shell, which holds a maximum of **2 electrons (duplet)**. Hydrogen never forms an octet.

4. **Mistake 4: Drawing incorrect bond counts inconsistent with valency**
   The number of dashes around an atom must match its bonding capacity: 1 for H, 2 for O, 3 for N, and 4 for C. Drawing 3 or 5 bonds around carbon in $\text{CH}_4$ violates valence rules.

5. **Mistake 5: Depicting ions in covalent molecules**
   A water molecule does not contain $\text{H}^+$ or $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions. The electrons are mutually shared, not transferred, leaving the molecule electrically neutral overall.

6. **Mistake 6: Assigning covalent bonding to metals**
   When a metal combines with a non-metal (e.g., $\text{NaCl}$), electron transfer occurs $\rightarrow$ **ionic bond**. Covalent bonds occur primarily between non-metals.

## Quick Recap

- When two **non-metal** atoms combine, neither donates electrons; they **share pairs of electrons** to complete their valence shells, forming **covalent bonds**.
- One shared pair $= 2\text{ electrons}$, counting toward the stable configuration of both bonding atoms.
- **Single bonds (1 shared pair):** $\text{H}_2$ ($\text{H}-\text{H}$), $\text{Cl}_2$ ($\text{Cl}-\text{Cl}$), $\text{HCl}$ ($\text{H}-\text{Cl}$), $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ ($\text{H}-\text{O}-\text{H}$), $\text{NH}_3$ (3 single bonds), $\text{CH}_4$ (4 single bonds).
- **Double bonds (2 shared pairs):** $\text{O}_2$ ($\text{O}=\text{O}$), $\text{CO}_2$ ($\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O}$).
- **Triple bonds (3 shared pairs):** $\text{N}_2$ ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$).
- **Counting rule:** $(\text{Electrons in lone pairs}) + 2 \times (\text{Number of bonds}) = \mathbf{8}$ (or $2$ for hydrogen).
- Number of covalent bonds formed equals the element's valency: $\text{H} = 1, \text{O} = 2, \text{N} = 3, \text{C} = 4$.
- Covalent compounds exist as **discrete, independent molecules**, which have well-defined **molecular masses** (e.g., $\text{H}_2\text{O} = 18\text{ u}$, $\text{CO}_2 = 44\text{ u}$).

    (c) In $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, each hydrogen atom achieves a stable duplet.
    (d) $\text{CO}_2$ contains two double covalent bonds.

11. Fluorine gas exists as diatomic molecules, $\text{F}_2$ ($Z = 9$). Which type of chemical bond joins the two fluorine atoms, and why? Draw its Lewis structure and verify the electron count.

### Answers

1. Both chlorine atoms have 7 valence electrons and need 1 electron to complete their octet. Because both have high attraction for electrons, neither atom can donate an electron without becoming less stable. By sharing 1 pair of electrons, both atoms attain 8 electrons ($6\text{ unshared} + 2\text{ shared} = 8$), satisfying the octet rule simultaneously.

2. Diagram: $\text{H}\bullet\mathbf{x}\text{H}$, structural formula: $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{H}}$. Each hydrogen atom is surrounded by **2 electrons** (the shared pair). This stable two-electron arrangement is called a **duplet** (identical to helium). The bond is a **single covalent bond**.

3. $\text{H}$ ($1$ valence electron) and $\text{Cl}$ ($7$ valence electrons) share 1 pair: $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{Cl}}$.
   - $\text{H}$: 1 shared pair $= \mathbf{2\text{ electrons}}$ (duplet) ✅.
   - $\text{Cl}$: 3 lone pairs ($6\text{ electrons}$) $+ 1$ shared pair ($2\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.

4. Diagram: $\text{H}\mathbf{x}\bullet\ddot{\text{O}}\bullet\mathbf{x}\text{H}$. Oxygen has **2 shared pairs** (forming 2 single bonds with two $\text{H}$ atoms) and **2 lone pairs** (4 unshared electrons).
   Octet check for oxygen: $2\text{ shared pairs (4)} + 2\text{ lone pairs (4)} = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅. Each $\text{H}$ has $2$ electrons ✅.

5. - $\text{NH}_3$: Nitrogen forms 3 single bonds and possesses **1 lone pair** (2 unshared electrons).
   - $\text{CH}_4$: Carbon forms 4 single bonds and has **0 lone pairs**. Because carbon has 4 valence electrons and all 4 participate in bonding pairs ($4 \times 2 = 8$), no unshared valence electrons remain.

6. An oxygen atom has 6 valence electrons and needs 2 electrons; by sharing **2 pairs** of electrons, two oxygen atoms satisfy both octets ($\text{O}=\text{O}$, double bond). A nitrogen atom has 5 valence electrons and needs 3 electrons; by sharing **3 pairs** of electrons, two nitrogen atoms satisfy both octets ($\text{N}\equiv\text{N}$, triple bond). In each case, the number of shared pairs equals the electron deficit.

7. Structural formula: $\mathbf{\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O}}$.
   - Carbon: 2 double bonds $= 4$ shared pairs $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅.
   - Each oxygen: 2 shared pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $+ 2$ lone pairs ($4\text{ electrons}$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ ✅.

8. A carbon atom has 4 valence electrons and needs 4 additional electrons to achieve an octet. If it forms only 3 bonds, it would count only $3 \times 2 = 6$ electrons, leaving an incomplete, unstable shell. Carbon must form **4 bonds** (with 4 hydrogen atoms) to reach a full octet of 8 electrons ($4 \times 2 = 8$).

9. - $\text{Cl}_2 = 2 \times 35.5 = \mathbf{71\text{ u}}$
   - $\text{NH}_3 = 14 + (3 \times 1) = \mathbf{17\text{ u}}$
   - $\text{CH}_4 = 12 + (4 \times 1) = \mathbf{16\text{ u}}$
   - $\text{CO}_2 = 12 + (2 \times 16) = \mathbf{44\text{ u}}$

10. (a) **False** ❌. Each chlorine atom shares only **1 electron** (forming 1 shared pair), keeping 6 electrons unshared as 3 lone pairs.
    (b) **False** ❌. $\text{H}_2$ contains a **single covalent bond** ($\text{H}-\text{H}$).
    (c) **True** ✅. Each hydrogen atom is surrounded by 2 electrons (duplet).
    (d) **True** ✅. Carbon dioxide has two double bonds ($\text{O}=\text{C}=\text{O}$).

11. The bond is a **single covalent bond** because both reacting atoms are non-metals needing 1 electron each. Fluorine $(2, 7)$ contributes 1 electron to form 1 shared pair: $\mathbf{\text{F}-\text{F}}$, with 3 lone pairs on each fluorine atom.
    Check: Each $\text{F}: 6\text{ unshared} + 2\text{ shared} = \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅. Total valence count $= 7 + 7 = 14$; 2 bonding $+ 12$ non-bonding $= 14$ ✅.
