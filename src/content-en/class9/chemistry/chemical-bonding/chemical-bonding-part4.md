# Topic 4: Properties of Ionic and Covalent Compounds

So far, we have mastered how bonds form at the atomic level: ionic bonds arise from the **transfer** of electrons between metals and non-metals, while covalent bonds arise from the **sharing** of electrons between non-metals. But why does this microscopic distinction matter in the real world? The answer is profound: **the type of chemical bond dictates virtually every physical property of a substance**. It determines whether a material is a rigid solid, a fluid liquid, or a gas; whether it shatters or bends; how hot it must be heated to melt; whether it dissolves in water; and whether it conducts an electric current. Today, we connect the atomic world to everyday observations, backed by a safe, hands-on experiment.

---

## Two Identical Containers

In the pantry of the school science club, Maya noticed two identical stainless-steel containers resting side by side on a shelf. Both contained fine, white, crystalline grains. One held **table salt**, while the other held **granulated sugar**. Unfortunately, moisture had smeared the paper labels, leaving both completely blank.

Maya opened both lids and peered inside. "Uncle Foster," she said to her science mentor, "they look almost identical. Should I just taste a tiny pinch to see which is which?"

"Absolutely not!" Mr. Foster replied firmly. "The very first golden rule of science: **never taste anything in a laboratory or from unlabelled containers**, even if they look like kitchen ingredients. An unlabelled container is an unknown hazard."

Maya smiled sheepishly. "How can we tell them apart without tasting, then?"

Mr. Foster retrieved a small 2-AA battery holder, two copper wires, a small LED, and a 220-ohm resistor from his toolkit. "We can build a simple conductivity tester. We will dissolve a spoonful of each powder into separate cups of water. The solution that lights the LED is **salt**; the one that leaves it dark is **sugar**."

Maya looked puzzled. "Wait—both of them dissolve in water completely. Why would only salt light the bulb?"

"That 'why' is the heart of today's lesson," Mr. Foster smiled. "Salt is an **ionic compound**, while sugar is a **covalent compound**. Every physical property they display springs directly from the forces holding their particles together."

## Part A: A Fundamental Mental Picture

The key to understanding every difference between ionic and covalent compounds lies in visualizing how their particles attract one another:

- **Ionic Compounds:** Ions of opposite charge ($\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$) are bound together by **exceptionally strong electrostatic forces**. This attraction operates in all directions throughout a rigid 3D crystal lattice. To melt or shatter an ionic solid, you must overcome these powerful ion-ion attractions.
  *Analogy:* An ionic solid is like a **solid brick wall**, where every single brick is tightly mortared to its neighbors on every side.
- **Covalent Compounds:** The covalent bonds holding atoms together *within* a molecule are strong. However, the attractive forces operating *between* neighboring molecules (**intermolecular forces**) are **extremely weak**.
  *Analogy:* A covalent solid or liquid is like a **stack of cardboard boxes**. Each box is sturdy and taped securely (the molecule), but the boxes merely rest against one another with very little adhesive force between them.

> **Crucial Insight:** When a covalent substance melts or boils, the strong covalent bonds *inside* the molecules **do not break**. Only the weak attractions *between* molecules are overcome.

Keeping this mental image in mind makes every physical property instantly logical.

## Part B: Safe Hands-on Activity: Homemade Conductivity Tester

### Essential Safety Guidelines

- **Low Voltage Only:** Use **only two AA cells (3 volts total)** with a small LED or torch bulb. **NEVER connect wires to household electrical outlets (mains electricity) or wall sockets.** Mains voltage (120 V or 230 V) through water carries a lethal risk of electrocution.
- **Adult Supervision:** Always perform this activity with an adult present.
- **No Tasting:** Never taste any experimental powders or solutions.
- **No Flammable Liquids:** Never use petrol, kerosene, rubbing alcohol, or paint thinner. We use only **cooking oil** and water.
- **Component Protection:** Always place a **$100\ \Omega$ to $330\ \Omega$ resistor in series with the LED** when using a 3 V battery pack to prevent burning out the LED. Alternatively, use a standard $2.5\text{ V}$ miniature torch bulb, which connects directly without a resistor. Note that LEDs have polarity: if the LED fails to light, reverse its legs.
- **Hygiene:** Do not allow the bare wire ends to touch directly inside the liquid. Wash your hands thoroughly after finishing.

**Apparatus Required:** A 2-AA battery pack with switch, two copper connecting wires, one small LED with a $220\ \Omega$ resistor (or a miniature torch bulb), 4 clean glass cups or beakers, distilled (or purified) water, table salt, white table sugar, cooking oil, and a plastic spoon.

**Procedure:**

1. Connect the battery pack, LED (with resistor), and wires in series to form an **open testing circuit**, leaving two bare wire ends exposed as testing probes (A and B).
2. Pour distilled or clean water into Cup 1. Submerge probes A and B into the water, ensuring they are close together but **not touching**. Observe the LED.
3. Remove the probes, rinse them with clean water, and dry them with a paper towel.
4. Repeat the test sequentially in:
   - (a) Cup 2: Saltwater solution (1 spoonful of salt dissolved in water).
   - (b) Cup 3: Sugar solution (1 spoonful of sugar dissolved in water).
   - (c) Cup 4: Cooking oil.
5. Record your observations in your notebook.

```
      +-------[ 2 AA cells, 3 V ]-------+
      :                                 :
  [LED + resistor]                      :  insulated copper wire
  (or torch bulb)                       :
      :                                 :
      :  wire                           :
     (A)                               (B)
      v                                 v
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~  Test Liquid  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~
    ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Homemade conductivity tester. A simple 3-volt series circuit with two exposed testing probes, A and B. The liquid completes the circuit. If the liquid contains **mobile ions**, electric current flows through the solution and the LED lights up. If no mobile charged particles are present, the circuit remains open and the LED remains dark. Always use 3 V batteries; never use mains electricity!

**Expected Results:**

| Liquid Tested | LED Status | Scientific Deduction |
|---|---|---|
| Distilled water | **Does NOT light** | Contains virtually no free ions |
| Saltwater solution | **LIGHTS BRIGHTLY** | Contains abundant mobile $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions |
| Sugar solution | **Does NOT light** | Contains neutral sugar molecules; zero ions |
| Cooking oil | **Does NOT light** | Contains neutral covalent molecules; zero ions |

*A Note on Tap Water:* Pure distilled water does not conduct electricity. Standard tap water contains trace amounts of dissolved mineral salts, so an ultra-sensitive tester might show a very faint glow. If the batteries are slightly depleted or the wire spacing is wide, tap water will show no light at all, whereas adding a pinch of salt instantly causes the LED to shine brightly.

Maya's mystery was solved without tasting: the container whose solution lit the LED was **table salt**, and the other was **sugar**.

## Part C: Why Does Electricity Flow (or Not Flow)?

From physics, we know that an electric current is a **flow of electric charge**. In a metal wire, current is carried by moving free electrons. In liquids and solutions, current must be carried by **mobile charged particles—free ions**.

### 1. Solid Ionic Compounds (e.g., dry salt crystals)

In solid $\text{NaCl}$, abundant $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions exist, but they are **locked tightly into fixed positions within the crystal lattice**. They can vibrate in place, but they cannot travel across the crystal. Because the charged particles cannot move, **solid ionic compounds do not conduct electricity**.

### 2. Molten Ionic Compounds (Liquid State)

When heated to its melting point (approximately $801\ ^\circ\text{C}$ for $\text{NaCl}$), thermal energy breaks the rigid lattice framework. The ions become **free to move and slide past one another**. Because mobile charged ions are now present, **molten ionic compounds conduct electricity very well**. *(Note: Melting table salt requires industrial laboratory equipment; never attempt this at home!)*

### 3. Aqueous Ionic Solutions (Dissolved in Water)

When salt dissolves in water, polar water molecules wedge themselves into the crystal surface, pulling $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions away from the lattice. The ions become surrounded by water molecules and disperse throughout the liquid as **freely mobile ions**. When the testing probes are inserted, $\text{Na}^+$ ions migrate toward the negative probe and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions migrate toward the positive probe. This flow of charge completes the circuit, lighting the LED.

### 4. Covalent Compounds (Sugar, Cooking Oil, Pure Water)

Covalent compounds consist of **discrete, electrically neutral molecules**. When sugar dissolves in water, the individual sugar molecules separate from one another, but they remain intact and **electrically neutral**. There are no positive cations and no negative anions. With zero mobile charged particles available, **covalent compounds cannot conduct electricity in the solid, molten, or dissolved state**.

```
  Solid Ionic (Fixed)        Molten Ionic (Free)       Aqueous Solution (Hydrated)

   +  -  +  -  +               +     -   +               +  (H₂O)  -
   -  +  -  +  -                 -  +        -          (H₂O)  -   +
   +  -  +  -  +               -    +    +                  -  (H₂O) +
   -  +  -  +  -                  +     -   -            +   -   (H₂O)

  Ions locked in lattice     Lattice broken by heat;    Lattice dissolved by water;
  (Non-conductor)            ions free to migrate       ions free to migrate
                             (Conducts electricity)     (Conducts electricity)
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Behavior of ionic particles in three states. **Solid:** Cations and anions are tightly locked in a rigid lattice and cannot carry current. **Molten:** Thermal energy frees the ions to move. **Aqueous solution:** Water molecules dismantle the lattice, liberating free ions to carry charge. Both molten and dissolved states conduct electricity.

*Key Takeaway:* Water and heat do not *create* ions in an ionic compound; the ions were present from the very beginning. Dissolving or melting simply **liberates** them from their fixed lattice positions.

## Part D: Molecules in a Covalent Liquid

Consider a liquid covalent substance, such as water or cooking oil. The strong covalent bonds hold each individual molecule together internally, but adjacent molecules experience only weak intermolecular attractions.

```
     H-O-H          H-O-H
              H-O-H           H-O-H
   H-O-H                H-O-H
          H-O-H                  H-O-H
     H-O-H        H-O-H
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Molecular arrangement in a covalent liquid (e.g., water). Strong covalent bonds bind the atoms inside each individual $\text{H}-\text{O}-\text{H}$ molecule, but the forces between neighboring molecules are relatively weak. The molecules readily slide and tumble past one another. Because every molecule is electrically neutral and no free ions exist, the liquid cannot conduct electricity.

## Part E: Comparison Table: Every Property and Its Underlying Reason

This table is the cornerstone of this topic. Every observable property is paired directly with its underlying scientific reason:

| Physical Property | Ionic Compounds | Covalent Compounds | Underlying Reason |
|---|---|---|---|

| **Electrical Conductivity (Molten or Dissolved)** | **Good conductor** (electrolyte) | **Non-conductor** (even when dissolved) | **Ionic:** Lattice is broken; ions become mobile and carry electric charge. <br>**Covalent:** Molecules remain neutral; no mobile ions exist. |

## Part F: Two Notable Exceptions

In Class 9 science, rules describe general behavior, but two famous exceptions illuminate the deeper principles:

1. **Hydrogen Chloride Gas ($\text{HCl}$):**
   $\text{HCl}$ is a covalent gas consisting of polar molecules. However, when bubbled into water, it reacts chemically with water molecules to produce **hydronium ions ($\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$) and chloride ions ($\text{Cl}^-$)**. As a result, hydrochloric acid conducts electricity vigorously, even though pure $\text{HCl}$ is a covalent gas!
2. **Diamond (an allotrope of Carbon):**
   Diamond is a covalent solid consisting purely of carbon atoms. Unlike typical covalent substances, diamond is not made of small separate molecules. Instead, every carbon atom is covalently bonded to four neighbors in a **giant three-dimensional network**. Because you must break countless strong covalent bonds to alter its structure, diamond is the hardest natural substance known and has an astronomical melting point of over $3500\ ^\circ\text{C}$!

These exceptions do not invalidate our rules; they demonstrate why we carefully say **"generally"** when describing covalent properties.

## Part G: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Resolving Maya's container puzzle.

One dissolved white powder lit the tester LED, while the other did not. Identify which container holds table salt and which holds sugar.

- Table salt ($\text{NaCl}$) is an **ionic compound**. When dissolved in water, its crystal lattice breaks down into mobile $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, which conduct current and light the LED.
- Table sugar (sucrose, $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11}$) is a **covalent compound**. When dissolved, it separates into neutral molecules. Lacking free ions, it cannot complete the circuit.
- **Conclusion:** The powder that lit the LED is table salt; the one that did not is sugar.

*Verification:* Successfully identified without dangerous tasting ✅.

### Example 2. Why does dry table salt fail to light the LED?

- Dry solid $\text{NaCl}$ consists of $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions held rigidly in fixed positions within the crystal lattice.
- Because the charged particles cannot migrate, no current flows, and the circuit remains open.

*Verification:* Placing dry testing probes on dry salt crystals yields zero light; adding a few drops of water immediately illuminates the LED ✅.

### Example 3. Why is the melting point of sodium chloride so high ($801\ ^\circ\text{C}$)?

- In $\text{NaCl}$, each $\text{Na}^+$ ion is surrounded by 6 $\text{Cl}^-$ ions, and each $\text{Cl}^-$ ion is surrounded by 6 $\text{Na}^+$ ions in a 3D lattice.
- The electrostatic attraction between these oppositely charged ions is exceptionally strong.
- Tremendous thermal kinetic energy is required to disrupt this lattice and allow ions to slide into a liquid state.

*Verification:* Contrast with ice, which melts at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ because only weak intermolecular attractions separate water molecules ✅.

### Example 4. Why is methane ($\text{CH}_4$) a gas at room temperature with a boiling point of $-162\ ^\circ\text{C}$?

- Methane consists of discrete $\text{CH}_4$ molecules held together internally by strong covalent bonds.
- However, the intermolecular forces *between* separate methane molecules are extremely weak.
- It requires very little thermal energy to separate the molecules from one another. Thus, at room temperature ($25\ ^\circ\text{C}$), methane already possesses more than enough energy to exist as a gas.

*Verification:* Boiling methane separates $\text{CH}_4$ molecules; it does not break the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ bonds ✅.

### Example 5. Why are salt crystals brittle?

When struck with a hammer, a salt crystal shatters rather than flattening like metal.
- In the resting lattice, alternating positive and negative ions sit side by side in perfect balance ($+ - + -$).
- Mechanical impact forces one layer of ions to shift slightly relative to the layer beneath it.
- This displacement brings **ions of the same charge into direct alignment** ($+$ facing $+$, and $-$ facing $-$).
- Strong electrostatic repulsion instantly drives the layers apart, causing the crystal to cleave and shatter.

*Verification:* Metals flatten because their bonding mechanism allows electron seas to absorb deformation, whereas ionic repulsion shatters the lattice ✅.

### Example 6. Why does table salt dissolve readily in water?

- Water molecules have partially charged ends (polar nature).
- The negative ends of water molecules attract positive $\text{Na}^+$ ions, while the positive ends attract negative $\text{Cl}^-$ ions.
- This hydration energy overcomes the lattice energy, pulling the ions out of the crystal and dispersing them freely into solution.

*Verification:* The presence of these liberated ions is confirmed by our conductivity tester ✅.

### Example 7. Why does table salt not dissolve in cooking oil?

- Cooking oil consists of non-polar covalent molecules.
- Oil molecules have no distinct electrical charges and cannot exert sufficient attractive force to overcome the powerful electrostatic forces holding the ionic lattice together.
- Salt grains remain undissolved and settle to the bottom.

*Verification:* Tested with our circuit: cooking oil displays zero electrical conductivity ✅.

### Example 8. Predict which substance has a higher melting point: $\text{MgO}$ or $\text{CH}_4$.

- $\text{MgO}$ is an ionic compound composed of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions held by strong electrostatic forces.
- $\text{CH}_4$ is a covalent compound composed of discrete molecules held by weak intermolecular forces.
- **Answer:** $\text{MgO}$ has an extraordinarily high melting point (approx. $2850\ ^\circ\text{C}$), while $\text{CH}_4$ melts at $-182\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

*Verification:* $\text{MgO}$ carries doubly charged ions ($+2$ and $-2$), creating even stronger electrostatic attraction than $\text{NaCl}$ ($+1$ and $-1$), explaining its higher melting point ($2850\ ^\circ\text{C}$ vs. $801\ ^\circ\text{C}$) ✅.

### Example 9. Identifying an unknown white solid.

An unknown white solid has a melting point of $780\ ^\circ\text{C}$, shatters when struck with a mallet, dissolves in water, and its aqueous solution conducts electricity. Is it ionic or covalent?

- High melting point, brittleness, water solubility, and electrical conductivity in solution are classic hallmark properties of an **ionic compound**.
- **Conclusion:** It is an ionic compound (such as potassium chloride, $\text{KCl}$).

### Example 10. Identifying an unknown liquid.

A clear liquid boils at $68\ ^\circ\text{C}$, does not mix with water (floats on top), and does not conduct electricity. Is it ionic or covalent?

- Low boiling point, non-conductive, and insoluble in water are hallmark properties of a **covalent liquid** (such as hexane).
- **Conclusion:** It is a covalent compound.

### Example 11. Sugar dissolves in water. Why doesn't its solution conduct electricity?

- Solubility and electrical conductivity are independent phenomena.
- When sugar dissolves, individual sugar molecules disperse throughout the water, but they remain intact and **electrically neutral**.
- Without mobile charged particles (ions), the solution cannot carry an electric current.

### Example 12. Compare electrical conduction in a copper wire vs. saltwater.

- In a metallic copper wire, current is carried by a flow of **free delocalized electrons**.
- In saltwater, current is carried by the physical migration of **free ions** ($\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$).
- Both represent a flow of electric charge, but the charge carriers are different.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that solid salt contains no ions**
   Solid $\text{NaCl}$ is entirely composed of ions. It does not conduct electricity simply because its ions are locked in place, not because ions are absent.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming that whatever dissolves in water conducts electricity**
   Sugar and alcohol dissolve readily in water, yet their solutions do not conduct electricity at all. Electrical conduction requires **mobile ions**, not merely dissolved neutral molecules.

3. **Mistake 3: Claiming that covalent compounds have low melting points because covalent bonds are weak**
   Covalent bonds inside molecules are strong! Covalent compounds melt easily because the **forces between separate molecules (intermolecular forces) are weak**. Melting separates molecules; it does not sever covalent bonds.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing that heat or water "creates" ions in an ionic compound**
   Ions exist inside the ionic crystal from the moment the compound is synthesized. Melting or dissolving merely frees existing ions from their fixed lattice positions.

5. **Mistake 5: Stating that "all covalent compounds are insoluble in water"**
   Table sugar, glucose, and rubbing alcohol are covalent substances that dissolve effortlessly in water. This is why we say covalent compounds are **"generally"** insoluble in water.

6. **Mistake 6: Using dangerous experimental substitutes**
   Never use flammable liquids like petrol, kerosene, or lighter fluid in conductivity testing. Never connect wires to household mains sockets. Stick strictly to 3-volt battery packs and cooking oil.

## Quick Recap

- **Ionic compounds** are held together by strong, omnidirectional electrostatic forces between ions throughout a crystal lattice.
- **Covalent compounds** consist of molecules held together internally by strong covalent bonds, but attracted to neighboring molecules by weak intermolecular forces.
- **Properties of Ionic Compounds:** Hard, crystalline solids; **brittle** (shatter under impact); **very high melting and boiling points** ($\text{NaCl} \approx 801\ ^\circ\text{C}$); generally soluble in water; insoluble in organic solvents.
- **Electrical Conductivity of Ionic Compounds:** Non-conductors in the solid state (ions locked in place); **excellent conductors when molten or dissolved in water** (free mobile ions).
- **Properties of Covalent Compounds:** Gases, liquids, or soft solids; low to moderate melting and boiling points (e.g., ice at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, methane at $-162\ ^\circ\text{C}$); generally insoluble in water (exceptions: sugar, ethanol); soluble in organic solvents.
- **Electrical Conductivity of Covalent Compounds:** Non-conductors in all states (composed of neutral molecules; zero mobile ions).
- **Tester Safety:** Use only 2 AA cells (3 V) with an LED and resistor; never mains electricity; adult supervision required; use cooking oil, never flammable fuels.
- **Notable Exceptions:** $\text{HCl}$ is a covalent gas that forms ions in water and conducts electricity; diamond is a giant network covalent solid that is extremely hard with an enormous melting point.

### Answers

1. - **Melting Point:** Ionic compounds have very high melting points because vast thermal energy is required to sever the strong electrostatic forces holding the 3D crystal lattice together. Covalent compounds have low melting points because only weak intermolecular forces between molecules need to be overcome.
   - **Electrical Conductivity:** Ionic compounds conduct electricity when molten or dissolved in water because their ions become mobile and carry charge. Covalent compounds do not conduct electricity in any state because they consist of neutral molecules with no mobile ions.

2. In solid $\text{NaCl}$, the $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions are locked in fixed positions in the crystal lattice and cannot move; therefore, no current can flow. In the molten state, heat breaks the lattice, freeing the ions to move and carry current. When dissolved in water, polar water molecules dismantle the lattice, liberating free mobile ions throughout the solution, which conduct current.

3. Sugar is a covalent compound composed of neutral molecules. When it dissolves in water, the individual molecules separate and disperse, but they remain whole and electrically neutral. Because no mobile ions are produced, the solution cannot carry an electric current.

4. In an ionic lattice, positive and negative ions are arranged in alternating rows ($+ - + -$). A mechanical blow shifts one layer of ions with respect to another, causing ions of like charge to align directly ($+$ with $+$, and $-$ with $-$). The resulting intense electrostatic repulsion forces the layers apart, causing the crystal to cleave and shatter along planes.

5. In $\text{NaCl}$, melting requires overcoming powerful electrostatic attractions between oppositely charged $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ ions throughout the lattice, requiring temperatures of $801\ ^\circ\text{C}$. In ice, the water molecules are held together only by relatively weak intermolecular forces; minimal thermal energy ($0\ ^\circ\text{C}$) is sufficient to allow molecules to slip past one another into liquid water.

6. **$\text{MgO}$** possesses significantly stronger ionic bonding. In $\text{MgO}$, the ions carry double charges ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$), whereas in $\text{NaCl}$, the ions carry single charges ($\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$). Greater ionic charges exert substantially stronger electrostatic attraction, resulting in a much higher melting point ($2850\ ^\circ\text{C}$ vs. $801\ ^\circ\text{C}$).

7. Methane is a gas at room temperature because the intermolecular attractions between separate $\text{CH}_4$ molecules are extremely weak, so room temperature provides more than enough kinetic energy to keep them in the gaseous state. Boiling methane **does not break the $\text{C}-\text{H}$ covalent bonds**; it merely separates the neutral $\text{CH}_4$ molecules from one another.
