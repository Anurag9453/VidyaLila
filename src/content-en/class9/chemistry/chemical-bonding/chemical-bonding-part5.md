# Topic 5: Identifying Bond Types, Practice, and Chapter Summary

Over the preceding topics, we systematically analyzed both major types of chemical bonds: **ionic bonds** (formed when a metal donates electrons to a non-metal, creating oppositely charged ions held in a crystal lattice) and **covalent bonds** (formed when non-metal atoms mutually share pairs of electrons to create discrete molecules). In Topic 4, we saw how these bonding mechanisms directly produce distinct physical properties: ionic compounds are hard, high-melting, brittle crystalline solids that conduct electricity when melted or dissolved; covalent compounds are typically low-boiling gases, liquids, or soft solids that do not conduct electricity.

Today's focus is complete practical mastery: given any chemical formula, you will be able to **instantly identify its bond type, perform electron bookkeeping, and sketch its Lewis structure without rote memorization**. Finally, we synthesize the entire journey in a comprehensive chapter summary.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain English) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Bond type** | The classification of a chemical bond based on whether electrons are transferred (ionic) or shared (covalent) | $\text{NaCl}$ is ionic; $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is covalent |
| **Metal + Non-metal** | The signature combination that produces an **ionic bond** via electron transfer | $\text{K} + \text{Cl} \rightarrow \text{KCl}$ |
| **Non-metal + Non-metal** | The signature combination that produces a **covalent bond** via mutual electron sharing | $\text{H} + \text{F} \rightarrow \text{HF}$ |
| **Two identical non-metal atoms** | Always covalent bonding; both atoms require electrons, so sharing is mandatory | $\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$ |
| **Formula unit** | The simplest whole-number ratio of ions in an ionic lattice (never an isolated molecule) | $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{CaCl}_2$ |
| **Molecule** | An independent, discrete, electrically neutral unit of covalently bonded atoms | $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{CCl}_4$ |

## Part A: A 3-Step Method to Identify Bond Types

Whenever you encounter a chemical formula, follow this foolproof 3-step sequence:

### Step 1: Classify Constituent Elements as Metal or Non-Metal

Rely on your knowledge from periodic trends and valency tables:
- **Common Metals:** Lithium ($\text{Li}$), Sodium ($\text{Na}$), Potassium ($\text{K}$), Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), Calcium ($\text{Ca}$), Aluminium ($\text{Al}$). *(These elements have 1 to 3 valence electrons and readily donate them.)*
- **Common Non-Metals:** Hydrogen ($\text{H}$), Carbon ($\text{C}$), Nitrogen ($\text{N}$), Oxygen ($\text{O}$), Fluorine ($\text{F}$), Phosphorus ($\text{P}$), Sulphur ($\text{S}$), Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$). *(These elements have 4 to 7 valence electrons and tend to gain or share electrons.)*

> **Critical Reminder:** Hydrogen is a **non-metal**. Although its single valence electron places it in Group 1, hydrogen behaves chemically as a non-metal that forms covalent bonds by sharing electrons.

### Step 2: Determine the Bond Type

- **Metal + Non-Metal $\rightarrow$ Ionic Bond** (electrons are transferred from the metal to the non-metal).
- **Non-Metal + Non-Metal $\rightarrow$ Covalent Bond** (electrons are mutually shared between both atoms).
- **Two atoms of the same non-metal $\rightarrow$ Covalent Bond** (diatomic molecules like $\text{O}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$).
*(Note: Combinations of metal + metal involve metallic bonding, which will be studied in advanced grades.)*

### Step 3: Verify with Electron Bookkeeping

- **For an Ionic Compound:**
  $$\text{Total Electrons Donated by Metal Atoms} = \text{Total Electrons Accepted by Non-Metal Atoms}$$
  Net electric charge must equal zero.
- **For a Covalent Compound:**
  Count valence electrons around every atom:
  $$\text{Electrons in Lone Pairs} + 2 \times (\text{Number of Covalent Bonds}) = \mathbf{8}\ (\text{or } \mathbf{2} \text{ for Hydrogen})$$

```
                   Given: Chemical Formula
                             |
                             v
              Classify elements as Metal or Non-Metal
                             |
              +--------------+--------------+
              |                             |
              v                             v
     1 Metal + 1 Non-Metal         Both are Non-Metals
              |                             |
              v                             v
         IONIC BOND                   COVALENT BOND
    (Metal donates e⁻;           (Both atoms mutually
     Non-metal accepts e⁻)        share electron pairs)
              |                             |
              v                             v
      Electrons Donated            Every atom satisfies
      = Electrons Accepted         octet or duplet
      e.g., Na₂O: 2(1) = 2         e.g., H₂S: S needs 2, each H needs 1
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Decision flowchart for identifying chemical bond types. First, classify constituent elements into metals and non-metals. Second, assign the bond type. Third, confirm with electron counting (electron balance for ionic, octet/duplet satisfaction for covalent).

## Part B: The Same Non-Metal, Two Different Partners

A non-metal's behavior depends entirely on who its bonding partner is. Consider **Sulphur** ($Z = 16$, electron configuration $2, 8, \mathbf{6}$). Sulphur requires **2 electrons** to achieve a stable octet.

Notice how sulphur fulfills its octet in two completely different ways depending on its partner:

```
  With Magnesium (Metal Partner)          With Hydrogen (Non-Metal Partner)

  Mg: 2, 8, 2    S: 2, 8, 6               H: 1 (needs 1 for duplet)
                                          S: 2, 8, 6 (needs 2 for octet)
  Mg transfers 2 electrons to S
  (Complete electron transfer)             S shares 2 electron pairs with two H atoms

                 ..                                     ..
  [Mg]²⁺      [ :S: ]²⁻                            H x. S .x H
                 ..                                     ..
  Mg²⁺: 2, 8   S²⁻: 2, 8, 8               S has: 6 valence + 2 shared = 8 (octet)
  (8 outer)    (8 outer)                  Each H has: 1 valence + 1 shared = 2 (duplet)

  Formula: MgS                            Formula: H₂S
  IONIC BOND                              COVALENT BOND
  (2 donated = 2 accepted)                (2 shared pairs, 2 lone pairs)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – A single non-metal, two different bonding mechanisms. With a metal partner ($\text{Mg}$), sulphur forms ionic $\text{MgS}$ by accepting 2 electrons. With a non-metal partner ($\text{H}$), sulphur forms covalent $\text{H}_2\text{S}$ by sharing 2 pairs of electrons. In both compounds, sulphur satisfies its octet; the chosen pathway depends entirely on the partner's electronegativity.

> **Key Principle:** The ultimate goal is always the same: **achieving a stable octet (or duplet)**. The pathway chosen (transfer vs. sharing) is dictated by the nature of the bonding partner!

## Part C: 12 Solved Examples

Let us classify all 12 reagent bottles from Dr. Davis's storeroom, performing electron verification for each.

### Group 1: Metal + Non-Metal (Ionic Shelf)

**Example 1: Potassium Chloride ($\text{KCl}$)**
- Elements: $\text{K}$ ($Z = 19$, configuration $2, 8, 8, \mathbf{1}$, metal) and $\text{Cl}$ ($Z = 17$, configuration $2, 8, \mathbf{7}$, non-metal).
- Potassium donates 1 electron; chlorine accepts 1 electron.
- Electron check: $1\text{ donated} = 1\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
- Ions formed: $\text{K}^+$ $(2, 8, 8)$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ $(2, 8, 8)$.
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**. Placed on the **IONIC** shelf.

**Example 2: Calcium Chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$)**
- Elements: $\text{Ca}$ ($Z = 20$, configuration $2, 8, 8, \mathbf{2}$, metal) and $\text{Cl}$ (non-metal).
- Calcium donates 2 electrons. Each chlorine accepts 1 electron, requiring 2 chlorine atoms.
- Electron check: $2\text{ donated} = 2 \times 1\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
- Formula: $\mathbf{\text{CaCl}_2}$ (simplest ratio $1:2$).
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**.

**Example 3: Sodium Oxide ($\text{Na}_2\text{O}$)**
- Elements: $\text{Na}$ ($(2, 8, 1)$, metal) and $\text{O}$ ($(2, 6)$, non-metal).
- Each sodium donates 1 electron; oxygen accepts 2 electrons. Two sodium atoms are required.
- Electron check: $2 \times 1\text{ donated} = 2\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
- Ions formed: $2\text{ }\text{Na}^+$ $(2, 8)$ and $1\text{ }\text{O}^{2-}$ $(2, 8)$.
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**.

**Example 4: Magnesium Sulphide ($\text{MgS}$)**
- Elements: $\text{Mg}$ ($(2, 8, 2)$, metal) and $\text{S}$ ($(2, 8, 6)$, non-metal).
- Magnesium donates 2 electrons; sulphur accepts 2 electrons.
- Electron check: $2\text{ donated} = 2\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
- Ratio is $1:1$, giving the formula $\mathbf{\text{MgS}}$.
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**.

**Example 5: Lithium Fluoride ($\text{LiF}$)**
- Elements: $\text{Li}$ ($(2, 1)$, metal) and $\text{F}$ ($(2, 7)$, non-metal).
- Lithium donates 1 electron; fluorine accepts 1 electron.
- Electron check: $1 = 1$ ✅.
- Ions formed: $\text{Li}^+$ (duplet of 2, helium-like) and $\text{F}^-$ (octet of $2, 8$, neon-like).
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**.

**Example 6: Calcium Oxide ($\text{CaO}$)**
- Elements: $\text{Ca}$ ($(2, 8, 8, 2)$, metal) and $\text{O}$ ($(2, 6)$, non-metal).
- Calcium donates 2 electrons; oxygen accepts 2 electrons: $2 = 2$ ✅.
- Simplest ratio is $1:1 \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaO}}$.
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**.

**Example 7: Potassium Sulphide ($\text{K}_2\text{S}$)**
- Elements: $\text{K}$ ($(2, 8, 8, 1)$, metal) and $\text{S}$ ($(2, 8, 6)$, non-metal).
- Each potassium donates 1 electron; sulphur needs 2 electrons. Two potassium atoms combine with one sulphur atom.
- Electron check: $2 \times 1\text{ donated} = 2\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
- **Bond Type:** **Ionic**.

### Group 2: Non-Metal + Non-Metal (Covalent Shelf)

**Example 8: Hydrogen Fluoride ($\text{HF}$)**
- Elements: $\text{H}$ ($1$ valence electron, non-metal) and $\text{F}$ ($(2, 7)$, non-metal).
- Both require 1 electron to complete their valence shells (duplet for $\text{H}$, octet for $\text{F}$).
- They share 1 pair of electrons: $\mathbf{\text{H}-\text{F}}$.
- Electron check: $\text{H}$ has 2 electrons (duplet) ✅; $\text{F}$ has $2\text{ (shared)} + 6\text{ (lone pairs)} = 8$ (octet) ✅.
- **Bond Type:** **Covalent** (single bond). Placed on the **COVALENT** shelf.

**Example 9: Carbon Tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$)**
- Elements: $\text{C}$ ($(2, 4)$, non-metal) and $\text{Cl}$ ($(2, 8, 7)$, non-metal).
- Carbon needs 4 electrons; each chlorine needs 1 electron. Carbon forms 4 single covalent bonds with four chlorine atoms.
- Electron check: Carbon has $4 \times 2 = 8$ electrons (octet) ✅; each chlorine has $2\text{ (shared)} + 6\text{ (lone pairs)} = 8$ (octet) ✅.
- **Bond Type:** **Covalent**.

**Example 10: Hydrogen Sulphide ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$)**
- Elements: $\text{S}$ ($(2, 8, 6)$, non-metal) and $\text{H}$ ($1$ electron, non-metal).
- Sulphur shares 1 electron pair with each of two hydrogen atoms.
- Electron check: Sulphur has $2 \times 2 = 4\text{ bonding electrons} + 4\text{ non-bonding electrons} = 8$ ✅; each hydrogen has 2 electrons ✅.
- **Bond Type:** **Covalent** (a gas at room temperature).

**Example 11: Phosphine ($\text{PH}_3$)**
- Elements: $\text{P}$ ($Z = 15$, configuration $2, 8, \mathbf{5}$, non-metal) and $\text{H}$ (non-metal).
- Phosphorus needs 3 electrons; each hydrogen needs 1. Phosphorus shares 3 pairs with three hydrogen atoms, retaining 1 lone pair.
- Electron check: $\text{P}$ has $3 \times 2 + 2 = 8$ electrons ✅; each $\text{H}$ has 2 electrons ✅.
- **Bond Type:** **Covalent** (a gas at room temperature).

**Example 12: Chlorine Gas ($\text{Cl}_2$)**
- Two identical non-metal atoms, each with 7 valence electrons.
- Because their electronegativities are identical, neither donates electrons; they share 1 pair: $\mathbf{\text{Cl}-\text{Cl}}$.
- Electron check: Both chlorine atoms count 8 valence electrons ✅.
- **Bond Type:** **Covalent** (single bond).

## Part E: Mixed Reasoning: Connecting All Chapters

### Reasoning 1 (Formula Writing + Bonding)
Determine the chemical formula and bond type for the compound formed between magnesium and chlorine.
- Magnesium has valency 2; chlorine has valency 1.
- By criss-cross: $\mathbf{\text{MgCl}_2}$.
- Magnesium (metal) transfers 2 valence electrons to two chlorine atoms (non-metals).
- **Conclusion:** **Ionic bond**.

### Reasoning 2 (Electron Configuration to Bonding)
An unknown element X has electron configuration $(2, 8, 8, 2)$ and element Y has configuration $(2, 8, 7)$. Deduce the chemical formula, bond type, and ion symbols.
- Element X ($Z = 20$, Calcium): metal with 2 valence electrons $\rightarrow$ donates 2 electrons to form $\text{X}^{2+}$.
- Element Y ($Z = 17$, Chlorine): non-metal with 7 valence electrons $\rightarrow$ accepts 1 electron to form $\text{Y}^-$.
- Combining ratio: $1\text{ }\text{X}^{2+} : 2\text{ }\text{Y}^- \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{XY}_2}$ ($\text{CaCl}_2$).
- **Conclusion:** **Ionic compound**.

### Reasoning 3 (Deducing Bond Type from Physical Properties)
A mystery crystalline white solid melts at $770\ ^\circ\text{C}$. In the solid state, it does not conduct electricity, but its aqueous solution illuminates an LED tester brightly. Identify the bond type and explain the underlying reasons.
- Conduction in aqueous solution proves that the substance releases **free mobile ions**.
- A high melting point indicates strong **electrostatic forces** throughout a rigid lattice.
- Non-conduction in the solid state proves that the ions are locked in fixed positions.
- **Conclusion:** **Ionic compound** (e.g., potassium chloride, $\text{KCl}$).

### Reasoning 4 (Predicting Physical Properties from Molecular Formula)
Predict three physical properties of carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) from its chemical formula.
- $\text{C}$ and $\text{Cl}$ are both non-metals, so $\text{CCl}_4$ is a **covalent compound**.
- Predictions:
  1. Low boiling point (it is a fluid liquid at room temperature).
  2. Non-conductor of electricity (contains neutral molecules, zero ions).
  3. Insoluble in water (forms a separate immiscible liquid layer).

### Reasoning 5 (Electron Balance in Polyatomic Subscripts)
Derive the formula for the ionic compound formed between aluminium $(2, 8, 3)$ and oxygen $(2, 6)$ using electron transfer calculations.
- Each aluminium atom donates 3 electrons. Each oxygen atom accepts 2 electrons.
- The lowest common multiple of 3 and 2 is 6.
- $2\text{ Al atoms} \times 3\text{ e}^- = \mathbf{6\text{ electrons donated}}$.
- $3\text{ O atoms} \times 2\text{ e}^- = \mathbf{6\text{ electrons accepted}}$.
- Ratio: $2\text{ Al} : 3\text{ O} \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3}$ (aluminium oxide). Total charge: $2(+3) + 3(-2) = 0$ ✅.

### Reasoning 6 (Correcting Terminology Errors)
A student writes in an exam: *"A sample of table salt contains billions of $\text{NaCl}$ molecules, while water is an ionic crystal with a formula unit of $\text{H}_2\text{O}$."* Correct the student's statement.
- The student has inverted both definitions:
  - $\text{NaCl}$ is an **ionic compound** forming a continuous crystal lattice; its basic representative unit is a **formula unit**, not a molecule.
  - $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ is a **covalent compound** existing as discrete, independent units called **molecules**, not an ionic crystal.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Mistaking hydrogen for an alkali metal**
   Although hydrogen appears above sodium in Group 1, it is a non-metal with high electronegativity. Compounds like $\text{HF}$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, and $\text{PH}_3$ are **covalent**, not ionic.

2. **Mistake 2: Looking only at the second element in a formula**
   Never categorize a compound by looking only at the non-metal. For instance, both $\text{KCl}$ and $\text{CCl}_4$ contain chlorine, but $\text{KCl}$ is ionic (metal + non-metal) while $\text{CCl}_4$ is covalent (non-metal + non-metal).

3. **Mistake 3: Using "molecule" to describe ionic compounds**
   Ionic compounds do not form separate molecules. The correct term for $\text{NaCl}$ or $\text{CaCl}_2$ is **formula unit**. Reserve "molecule" exclusively for covalent substances.

4. **Mistake 4: Violating electron conservation in ionic formulas**
   In $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$, two sodium atoms supply 2 electrons to one oxygen atom. If total electrons lost do not equal total electrons gained, the formula is chemically invalid.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming all valence electrons are shared in covalent bonds**
   Only the electrons needed to reach an octet or duplet are shared in bonding pairs. The remainder reside as non-bonding lone pairs (e.g., in $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, sulphur retains 2 lone pairs).

6. **Mistake 6: Believing all covalent substances conduct electricity if dissolved**
   Except for rare reactive species like $\text{HCl}$ gas, dissolving a covalent substance (such as sugar or alcohol) does not produce ions and does not conduct electricity.

## Quick Recap

- To identify a bond type: **Classify elements into metals and non-metals**.
  - **Metal + Non-metal $\rightarrow$ Ionic Bond** ($\text{Na}_2\text{O}$, $\text{CaCl}_2$, $\text{MgS}$, $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$).
  - **Non-metal + Non-metal $\rightarrow$ Covalent Bond** ($\text{HF}$, $\text{CCl}_4$, $\text{H}_2\text{S}$, $\text{PH}_3$).
- **Ionic Rule:** Total electrons lost by metal atoms equals total electrons gained by non-metal atoms; net charge $= 0$.
- **Covalent Rule:** Atoms mutually share electron pairs until every atom achieves an octet (or a duplet for hydrogen).
- Diatomic molecules of the same non-metal ($\text{Cl}_2$, $\text{O}_2$, $\text{N}_2$) are always covalent.
- The same non-metal can bond ionically with a metal ($\text{MgS}$) or covalently with a non-metal ($\text{H}_2\text{S}$); the partner determines the pathway.
- **Ionic Compounds:** Formula unit, hard, brittle, high melting point, conductive when molten or aqueous.
- **Covalent Compounds:** Discrete molecules, low to moderate melting points, non-conductors in all states.

The compound has a very high melting point. State its bond type, explain your reasoning, and suggest one plausible chemical formula.

    (a) $\text{Cl}_2$ is an ionic compound because chlorine is a highly reactive non-metal.
    (b) Lithium and fluorine combine to form $\text{LiF}$ through the transfer of one electron.
    (c) Carbon tetrachloride ($\text{CCl}_4$) contains four single covalent bonds.
    (d) The scientifically accurate term for one discrete unit of calcium chloride ($\text{CaCl}_2$) is a "molecule".

### Answers

1. **Step 1:** Classify the constituent elements as metals or non-metals.
   **Step 2:** If metal + non-metal, the bond is **ionic**; if non-metal + non-metal, the bond is **covalent**.
   **Step 3:** Verify using electron counts: for ionic, electrons donated must equal electrons accepted; for covalent, every atom must achieve a complete octet or duplet via shared pairs.

2. (a) $\text{KF}$: $\text{K}$ (metal) + $\text{F}$ (non-metal) $\rightarrow$ **Ionic**.
   (b) $\text{CS}_2$: $\text{C}$ (non-metal) + $\text{S}$ (non-metal) $\rightarrow$ **Covalent**.
   (c) $\text{CaS}$: $\text{Ca}$ (metal) + $\text{S}$ (non-metal) $\rightarrow$ **Ionic**.
   (d) $\text{CF}_4$: $\text{C}$ (non-metal) + $\text{F}$ (non-metal) $\rightarrow$ **Covalent**.
   (e) $\text{Li}_2\text{O}$: $\text{Li}$ (metal) + $\text{O}$ (non-metal) $\rightarrow$ **Ionic**.
   (f) $\text{PCl}_3$: $\text{P}$ (non-metal) + $\text{Cl}$ (non-metal) $\rightarrow$ **Covalent**.

3. $\text{K}$ $(2, 8, 8, 1)$ donates 1 electron $\rightarrow \text{K}^+ (2, 8, 8)$. $\text{O}$ $(2, 6)$ requires 2 electrons $\rightarrow \text{O}^{2-} (2, 8)$.
   Two potassium atoms are needed: $2 \times 1\text{ e}^-\text{ donated} = 2\text{ e}^-\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
   Net charge: $2(+1) + (-2) = 0$ ✅.
   Lewis diagram: $[\text{K}]^+\ [:\ddot{\text{O}}:]^{2-}\ [\text{K}]^+$. Formula: $\mathbf{\text{K}_2\text{O}}$.

4. Nitrogen $(2, 5)$ needs 3 electrons; each chlorine $(2, 8, 7)$ needs 1 electron. Nitrogen forms 3 single covalent bonds with three chlorine atoms:
   - Nitrogen: 3 shared pairs ($6\text{ e}^-$) $+ \mathbf{1\text{ lone pair}}$ ($2\text{ e}^-$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.
   - Each chlorine: 1 shared pair ($2\text{ e}^-$) $+ 3\text{ lone pairs}$ ($6\text{ e}^-$) $= \mathbf{8\text{ electrons}}$ (octet) ✅.
   - Total valence count: $5 + 3(7) = 26$ electrons ($6$ bonding $+ 20$ non-bonding $= 26$) ✅.

5. Lithium $(2, 1)$ donates 1 electron $\rightarrow \text{Li}^+ (2)$. Sulphur $(2, 8, 6)$ accepts 2 electrons $\rightarrow \text{S}^{2-} (2, 8, 8)$.
   Two lithium atoms combine with one sulphur atom: $2 \times 1\text{ donated} = 2\text{ accepted}$ ✅.
   Formula: $\mathbf{\text{Li}_2\text{S}}$ (lithium sulphide), an **ionic compound**.

6. Both oxygen $(2, 6)$ and fluorine $(2, 7)$ are non-metals. Oxygen needs 2 electrons; each fluorine needs 1 electron.
   Oxygen shares 1 electron pair with each of two fluorine atoms: $\mathbf{\text{F}-\text{O}-\text{F}}$.
   - Formula: $\mathbf{\text{OF}_2}$ (oxygen difluoride).
   - Number of shared pairs: **2 shared pairs** (2 single covalent bonds).
   - Bond type: **Covalent bond**.

7. Magnesium is a metal with low electronegativity that readily donates 2 electrons to sulphur, forming the ionic compound $\text{MgS}$. Hydrogen is a non-metal that cannot surrender electrons to sulphur; instead, hydrogen and sulphur mutually share electron pairs, forming the covalent compound $\text{H}_2\text{S}$.

8. **Ionic compound.**
   - Reasoning: High melting point reflects strong electrostatic lattice attractions; electrical conduction in aqueous solution proves the release of mobile ions, while non-conduction in the solid confirms that ions are locked in place.
   - Plausible formula: $\text{NaCl}$, $\text{KCl}$, or $\text{CaCl}_2$.

9. **$\text{CCl}_4$** will have a vastly lower boiling point ($77\ ^\circ\text{C}$ vs. over $2800\ ^\circ\text{C}$ for $\text{CaO}$). $\text{CCl}_4$ is a covalent molecular liquid held only by weak intermolecular forces between molecules. $\text{CaO}$ is an ionic lattice held by exceptionally strong electrostatic attractions between $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions, requiring enormous energy to boil.

10. (a) **False** ❌. $\text{Cl}_2$ is a covalent molecule formed by mutual sharing of an electron pair between two identical non-metal chlorine atoms.
    (b) **True** ✅. Lithium donates 1 electron to fluorine, forming $\text{Li}^+$ and $\text{F}^-$.
    (c) **True** ✅. Carbon shares four separate electron pairs with four chlorine atoms.
    (d) **False** ❌. Calcium chloride is an ionic compound; its fundamental repeating unit is a **formula unit**, not a molecule.
