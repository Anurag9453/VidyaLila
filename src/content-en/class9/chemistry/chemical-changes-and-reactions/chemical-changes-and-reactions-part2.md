# Topic 2: Combination and Decomposition: Joining Together and Breaking Apart

In the previous topic, we saw that in every chemical reaction, old bonds **break**, atoms **rearrange**, and new bonds **form**. We also saw that to make sense of thousands of different reactions, chemists group them into **types**, much like sorting groceries into fruits, vegetables, and grains. Today we look at the two most direct patterns: one in which substances **join together to make a single product**, and another in which **a single substance breaks apart into two or more simpler substances**.

---

## A morning at the neighbourhood bakery

Walking past the corner bakery early one morning with his uncle Mr. Collins, Leo noticed two things happening at once. Outside, the bakery's brick wall was being freshly whitewashed, and a metal bucket of milky-white lime mixture stood near the doorway. Inside, the big oven was hot, and the baker was sliding in a baking tin half-filled with cake batter.

Twenty minutes later, when the baker pulled the tin out, the batter had risen into a **tall, spongy cake filling the entire tin**. "The baker didn't pump any air into it while it was in the oven—so how did it puff up like that?" Leo asked.

Mr. Collins explained, "There was a pinch of **baking soda** mixed into the batter. In the heat of the oven, the baking soda **broke apart** and released carbon dioxide gas, forming thousands of tiny bubbles trapped inside the batter. Those bubbles made the cake rise." Then he pointed to the bucket outside: "And that whitewash is **slaked lime**. Earlier this morning, the mason took white rocky lumps called **quicklime** and added them to water in that bucket. The water hissed and got steaming hot as the two substances **joined together to make one new substance**."

"So inside the oven one substance **broke apart**, and outside in the bucket two substances **joined together**?" Leo asked.

"Spot on," Mr. Collins smiled. "Chemists call those two patterns **decomposition** (breaking apart) and **combination** (joining together). They are two sides of the same coin. Let's look at both today."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Combination reaction** | Two or more reactants join together to form **a single product** | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O |
| **Decomposition reaction** | **A single reactant** breaks down to form two or more simpler products | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ |
| **Thermal decomposition** | Breaking down caused by heat | Baking soda, limestone |
| **Electrolytic decomposition** | Breaking down caused by an electric current (electrolysis) | Splitting water into H₂ and O₂ |
| **Photolytic decomposition** | Breaking down caused by light | Silver bromide, AgBr |
| **Catalytic decomposition** | Rapid breakdown helped along by a catalyst | H₂O₂ with yeast |
| **Quicklime** | CaO, white lumps made by heating limestone in a kiln; reacts vigorously with water, releasing intense heat (caustic) | Lime from a kiln |
| **Slaked lime** | Ca(OH)₂, formed when quicklime reacts with water | Lime whitewash |
| **Exothermic** | A reaction that releases heat to its surroundings (covered fully in Topic 4) | CaO + H₂O |

## Part A: Combination reaction: two or more into one

The name says it all: **combination** means **joining together**. Two or more reactants (elements or compounds) combine to form **only one product**. Keep this simple pattern in mind: **A + B → AB**.

In Topic 1 we talked about bonds. In a combination reaction, old bonds in the reactants break and the atoms bond together into **one larger, new compound**.

```
   Combination:      A    +    B    ───►    A B

                  (separate) (separate)    (joined into one)


   A real example:   2H₂ + O₂ ───► 2H₂O

     H─H   H─H          O═O                H─O─H     H─O─H

    left: H = 4, O = 2                    right: H = 4, O = 2
    (2 molecules of hydrogen, 1 of oxygen)  (2 molecules of water)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Combination: A and B join to form a single product AB. Below, a real example: 2 molecules of hydrogen and 1 molecule of oxygen combine to form 2 molecules of water. Counting atoms gives H = 4 and O = 2 on both sides: no atom is created or lost—only the bonds changed.

### Familiar examples of combination reactions

| Reaction | What combines and what forms | Where you see it |
|---|---|---|
| 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO | Magnesium + oxygen → magnesium oxide | Dazzling white light when Mg ribbon burns |
| C + O₂ → CO₂ | Carbon + oxygen → carbon dioxide | Burning coal or charcoal |
| 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O | Hydrogen + oxygen → water | Burning of hydrogen gas |
| N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ | Nitrogen + hydrogen → ammonia | Industrial manufacture of fertilisers |
| CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ | Quicklime + water → slaked lime | The mason's bucket of whitewash |

Notice that in the first four reactions **two elements** combine, whereas in the last one **two compounds** (CaO and H₂O) combine to form a single new compound. Both count as combination reactions because in every case there is **only one product**. (By contrast, in CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O there are two products, so it is not a combination reaction; we will study it as "combustion" in Topic 6.)

### A closer look: burning magnesium ribbon

**For observation only (teacher demonstration).** In the school lab, a teacher holds a thin strip of cleaned magnesium ribbon with tongs and lights it in a burner flame. The ribbon burns with a **blindingly bright white light**, leaving behind a crumbly **white powder** (MgO). Because the glare is intense enough to harm the eyes, **you must never stare directly at the flame**. Students do not perform this themselves; they observe from a safe distance as instructed by the teacher.

Reaction: **2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO**. Check: Mg: 2 = 2 ✅, O: 2 = 2 ✅.

### Slaking of lime: a steaming-hot combination

The reaction in the mason's bucket outside the bakery is: **CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂**. Quicklime (CaO) comes as hard white lumps. When water is added, the lumps crack and crumble with a loud hiss, **releasing a large amount of heat**—sometimes enough to make the water boil and steam. That is why this process is called **slaking** lime, and the product is called **slaked lime**. Because it gives out heat, this reaction is **exothermic**.

> **Safety:** Quicklime is **caustic**—it can severely burn skin and eyes. Only a trained **adult** wearing protective gloves and safety goggles should handle it. **Students must never touch quicklime.** Slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂, is milder, but it is still irritating to eyes, skin, and mouth, so never touch or taste lime whitewash either.

## Part B: Solved examples: combination

### Example 1. Finding the mass of MgO from the masses of Mg and O₂

Taking atomic masses Mg = 24 u and O = 16 u (from Chapter 4), in 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO: total mass of reactants = 2 × 24 + 2 × 16 = 48 + 32 = **80**. Mass of product: 2 × (24 + 16) = 2 × 40 = **80**.

Check: 80 on both sides ✅. So 48 g of magnesium combines completely with 32 g of oxygen to give **80 g** of MgO. The white ash left after burning is not lighter than the original metal—it is actually **heavier**, because oxygen from the air has bonded to the magnesium.

### Example 2. Checking the ammonia synthesis equation

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃. Is this balanced? Left: N = 2, H = 3 × 2 = 6. Right: N = 2 × 1 = 2, H = 2 × 3 = 6.

Check: N: 2 = 2 ✅, H: 6 = 6 ✅. Every 1 molecule of N₂ requires 3 molecules of H₂—a fixed 1 : 3 recipe ratio.

### Example 3. Is a reaction a combination reaction or not?

Look at three equations:
- (i) C + O₂ → CO₂: 2 reactants, **1** product ✅ combination.
- (ii) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂: 2 reactants, **1** product ✅ combination.
- (iii) CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O: 2 reactants, **2** products ❌ not a combination reaction.

The quickest way to spot a combination reaction: **only one chemical formula appears to the right of the arrow**.

## Part C: Decomposition reaction: one into two or more

Now think in reverse. In a combination reaction, substances join together. In a **decomposition** reaction (the opposite of "composition"), there is **only one reactant**, and it breaks apart into **two or more simpler products**. Pattern: **AB → A + B**.

Here is a natural question: why would a stable compound break apart on its own? Its bonds are strong! To break those bonds, we usually have to **supply energy**. That energy is commonly supplied in one of three ways: **by heat, by electricity, or by light**. That gives us the three main flavours of decomposition.

```
                                      thermal decomposition
                   ┌── heat ────────►  CaCO₃ ──► CaO + CO₂
                   │
                   │                  electrolytic decomposition
   AB ─────────────┼── electricity ──►  2H₂O ──► 2H₂ + O₂
   (one reactant)  │
                   │                  photolytic decomposition
                   └── light ────────►  2AgBr ──► 2Ag + Br₂

   AB ──► A + B     (one substance breaks into two or more)
```

> **Figure 2.2** – The three main flavours of decomposition: by heat (thermal), by electricity (electrolytic), and by light (photolytic). In all three, a single reactant breaks down into two or more products, and the atom count stays equal on both sides.

### 1. Thermal decomposition: breaking down by heat

**(a) Baking soda and a rising cake.** Baking soda has the chemical name **sodium hydrogencarbonate** and the formula NaHCO₃. Inside a hot oven, it breaks down:

**2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂**

Check: Na: 2 = 2 ✅, H: 2 = 2 ✅, C: 2 = 1 + 1 = 2 ✅, O: 6 = 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 ✅.

That **CO₂ gas** forms tiny bubbles throughout the batter and makes the cake rise into a light sponge. (Bakers usually pair baking soda with a mildly acidic ingredient such as buttermilk, lemon juice, or baking powder so the cake does not taste soapy or bitter—you will learn why in a higher grade.) This is a **completely safe, everyday kitchen reaction**.

**(b) Limestone and the lime kiln.** Limestone has the formula CaCO₃. When heated strongly (to around 900 °C), it decomposes:

**CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**

Check: Ca: 1 = 1 ✅, C: 1 = 1 ✅, O: 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ✅.

Reaching 900 °C requires huge industrial furnaces called **lime kilns**. Tonnes of crushed limestone are fed into the kiln, intense heat is applied from below, CO₂ gas escapes from the top, and **quicklime (CaO)** is collected at the bottom. This is a **large-scale industrial process**—not an experiment for home or a school desk.

### 2. Electrolytic decomposition: breaking down by electricity

The bonds in water (H₂O) are so strong that ordinary heating just boils the water into steam without breaking its molecules apart. However, if we pass an **electric current** through water, it decomposes into hydrogen and oxygen gases:

**2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂**

Check: H: 4 = 4 ✅, O: 2 = 2 ✅.

**For observation only (teacher demonstration).** The teacher connects a low-voltage DC battery to two electrodes dipped in water (because pure water barely conducts electricity, the teacher adds a suitable electrolyte). Bubbles stream off both electrodes: **hydrogen** at one electrode and **oxygen** at the other. Notice that **the volume of hydrogen gas collected is twice the volume of oxygen gas**, matching the 2H₂ to 1O₂ ratio in the balanced equation. (At the same temperature and pressure, the volume of a gas is proportional to the number of its molecules—a law you will study in a later grade.) This demonstration proves that water is a compound, not an element (Chapter 2). You can read more about electric current in the Physics chapter [Electricity](../../physics/electricity/electricity.md). **Students must never attempt electrolysis using a wall socket (mains electricity)**—it is only ever demonstrated by a teacher using a low-voltage battery setup.

### 3. Photolytic decomposition: breaking down by light

Some compounds break apart simply when light shines on them. A classic example is **silver bromide (AgBr)**, which formed the light-sensitive coating on traditional **black-and-white photographic film**:

**2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂**

Check: Ag: 2 = 2 ✅, Br: 2 = 2 ✅.

When the camera shutter clicked open, light struck the film and decomposed AgBr into **tiny dark grains of metallic silver (Ag)**. Brighter parts of the scene produced darker patches of silver, capturing the image. **This is for reading only**: silver compounds and bromine are hazardous laboratory chemicals not handled by students.

### A fourth way: breakdown helped by a catalyst (preview)

Sometimes a helper substance speeds up the decomposition of a compound enormously without being used up itself. Such a helper is called a **catalyst**. For example, dilute 3% hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂, sold in pharmacies as a colourless antiseptic liquid) slowly decomposes into water and oxygen:

**2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂**

Check: H: 4 = 4 ✅, O: 4 = 2 + 2 = 4 ✅.

On its own, H₂O₂ breaks down very slowly—and faster in light, which is why it is stored in dark brown bottles. Drop in a pinch of **baker's yeast** or a slice of **raw potato**, however, and oxygen bubbles foam up rapidly. We will see how catalysts work in Topic 4.

### Try this at home: H₂O₂ with yeast or potato (at home with an adult)

- **What you need:** a small plastic or steel bowl, 3% pharmacy hydrogen peroxide, a pinch of dry yeast or a thin slice of raw potato, and a spoon.
- **What to do:** Have an **adult** pour a small splash of 3% H₂O₂ into the open bowl. Wear **eye protection** (safety glasses). Drop in the pinch of yeast or slice of raw potato. **Observe:** a brisk foam of tiny oxygen bubbles rises immediately.
- **What not to do:** **Never drink or taste** anything in this activity, keep it away from your eyes and face, and **never perform it in a closed bottle** (trapped oxygen gas builds up pressure and can burst the container). Wash your hands afterwards.

### Watch a cake rise (at home with an adult)

With an adult in the kitchen, prepare two small ramekins of cake batter: mix a **pinch of baking soda** into the first, and **leave it out** of the second. Have the adult bake both in the oven. **Observe:** the first bakes into a light, spongy, risen mini-cake, while the second stays **flat and dense**. That shows what the CO₂ gas from decomposing baking soda does! **Do not taste raw batter or plain baking soda.**

### Testing the gas from thermal decomposition (in the school lab with a teacher)

**In the school lab with a teacher.** The teacher places a small amount of baking soda in a dry, heat-resistant test tube fitted with a delivery tube whose open end dips into a beaker of clear limewater. That way, the gas pathway is **always open to the air**. **Important safety rule:** a test tube being heated must **never be stoppered shut**, because trapped gas pressure would shatter the glass. **Observe:** as the baking soda is gently heated, bubbles pass through the limewater and turn it **milky white**, confirming that the gas released is **CO₂**. (The milkiness is tiny suspended particles of CaCO₃—the exact same reaction that happens on a whitewashed wall in Part F.)

## Part D: Solved examples: decomposition

### Example 4. How much quicklime do we get from limestone?

Atomic masses: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16. Formula unit mass of CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 16 × 3 = **100**. CaO = 40 + 16 = **56**. CO₂ = 12 + 32 = **44**.

Check: 100 = 56 + 44 ✅. So **100 kg** of pure limestone yields **56 kg** of quicklime and **44 kg** of CO₂ gas. Scaling up, **1 tonne** (1000 kg) of pure limestone produces **560 kg** of quicklime. The mass that seems to "disappear" from the solid rock simply escaped into the air as CO₂ gas, just as we saw in Chapter 4.

### Example 5. Mass check for baking soda decomposition

2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. Atomic masses: Na = 23, H = 1, C = 12, O = 16. NaHCO₃ = 23 + 1 + 12 + 48 = **84**. For 2 formula units: 2 × 84 = **168**.

Right side: Na₂CO₃ = 46 + 12 + 48 = **106**. H₂O = **18**. CO₂ = **44**. Total = 106 + 18 + 44 = **168**.

Check: 168 = 168 ✅. Another clear verification of the conservation of mass (Chapter 4).

### Example 6. Identifying the flavour of decomposition

Identify the type of decomposition in each case below:
- (i) In a hot oven, 2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂: driven by **heat** (thermal).
- (ii) At battery electrodes in water, 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂: driven by **electricity** (electrolytic).
- (iii) On photographic film, 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂: driven by **light** (photolytic).
- (iv) With yeast, 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂: sped up by a **catalyst** (catalytic).

In every case there is **only one reactant**, so all four are decomposition reactions. Whenever you see **just one formula to the left of the arrow**, think decomposition!

## Part E: Two sides of the same coin

Combination and decomposition look like exact **opposites**. Compare the same chemical change running in opposite directions:

| Joining together (combination) | Breaking apart (decomposition) |
|---|---|
| 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (burning hydrogen) | 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (using electric current) |
| CaO + CO₂ → CaCO₃ (forming carbonate rock) | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (heating in a lime kiln) |

Notice carefully: in both directions, **the atoms are identical**—only their bonding partners flip. However, that does not mean the reverse reaction happens automatically under the same conditions! Each direction demands its own **conditions**: decomposing limestone needs a roaring 900 °C kiln, whereas recombining happens slowly in air. Their energy changes are opposite too: if the forward reaction releases heat, the reverse reaction absorbs energy. We will explore that in Topic 4.

Remember the quick spotting rule:

- **A + B → AB**: **one** formula to the right of the arrow = combination.
- **AB → A + B**: **one** formula to the left of the arrow = decomposition.

## Part F: The full lime cycle: from quarry to whitewashed wall

Let us connect the mason's bucket outside the bakery to the complete story of lime. Lime goes through a remarkable **chemical cycle** that includes both **decomposition and combination**.

```
   LIMESTONE (CaCO₃)            natural rock from a quarry
        │
        │  heated in a lime kiln at ~900 °C       (decomposition)
        │  CaCO₃ ──► CaO + CO₂↑
        ▼
   QUICKLIME (CaO)              white caustic lumps: handled only by adults
        │
        │  water added; releases intense heat     (combination)
        │  CaO + H₂O ──► Ca(OH)₂
        ▼
   SLAKED LIME (Ca(OH)₂)        whitewash mixture brushed onto walls
        │
        │  slowly reacts with CO₂ in the air as the wall dries
        │  Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ ──► CaCO₃ + H₂O        (a third reaction: two products, not combination)
        ▼
   Thin layer of CaCO₃          hard, bright white finish—back to calcium carbonate!
```

> **Figure 2.3** – The lime cycle: limestone (CaCO₃) decomposes on strong heating to give quicklime (CaO) and CO₂. Quicklime combines with water to form slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂. After slaked lime is brushed onto a wall, it slowly absorbs CO₂ from the air over a couple of days to form a hard, gleaming white layer of CaCO₃ again. The cycle features one decomposition, one combination, and a third reaction with atmospheric CO₂.

That is why a freshly whitewashed wall takes two or three days to develop its hard, bright sheen: **carbon dioxide from the air** is slowly turning the slaked lime coating back into a thin layer of marble-like calcium carbonate!

Let us check the atom count of that final step: **Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O**. Ca: 1 = 1 ✅, C: 1 = 1 ✅, O: 2 + 2 = 4 and 3 + 1 = 4 ✅, H: 2 = 2 ✅. (Because this step produces two products—CaCO₃ and H₂O—it is not strictly a combination reaction; simply treat it as a partner-rearrangement reaction that completes the cycle.)

## Part G: Balancing practice

Recall the hit-and-trial method from Chapter 3, Topic 6: write the **correct formulas** first, adjust the **coefficients** in front, and finally verify by counting every atom on both sides.

### Example 7. Mg + O₂ → MgO

Left: Mg = 1, O = 2. Right: Mg = 1, O = 1. To balance O, put 2 in front of MgO: now right has Mg = 2, O = 2. Put 2 in front of Mg on the left: **2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO**.

Check: Mg: 2 = 2 ✅, O: 2 = 2 ✅.

### Example 8. NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂

Right has Na = 2, so place 2 in front of NaHCO₃ on the left. Now left has Na = 2, H = 2, C = 2, O = 6. Right has Na = 2, H = 2, C = 1 + 1 = 2, O = 3 + 1 + 2 = 6. **2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂**.

Check: Na: 2 = 2 ✅, H: 2 = 2 ✅, C: 2 = 2 ✅, O: 6 = 6 ✅.

### Example 9. H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂

Left: H = 2, O = 2. Right: H = 2, O = 1 + 2 = 3. Oxygen is odd on the right. Since O₂ and H₂O₂ both supply oxygen in multiples of 2, try 2H₂O₂ on the left (H = 4, O = 4) and 2H₂O on the right (H = 4, O = 2) plus O₂ (O = 2): **2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂**.

Check: H: 4 = 4 ✅, O: 4 = 2 + 2 = 4 ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Thinking "two reactants" automatically means a combination reaction**

Both C + O₂ → CO₂ and CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O have two reactants. However, the first is a combination reaction (**one** product), while the second is not (**two** products). Always check the **number of products**, not just the reactants.

**Mistake 2: Thinking decomposition only happens by heating**

Decomposition comes in three main flavours: by heat (baking soda, limestone), by electricity (electrolysis of water), and by light (AgBr on film), plus catalytic decomposition.

**Mistake 3: Touching quicklime or whitewash with bare hands**

Quicklime and slaked lime are caustic and can burn skin and eyes. Only adults wearing gloves and goggles should handle them.

**Mistake 4: Running a gas-producing reaction inside a sealed tube or bottle**

Never heat baking soda or decompose H₂O₂ inside a tightly closed test tube or bottle: trapped CO₂ or O₂ gas builds up pressure rapidly and can shatter or burst the container. Always keep the vessel open.

**Mistake 5: Thinking mass "vanishes" when limestone is heated**

The solid lump left after heating 100 g of limestone weighs only 56 g. The other 44 g did not vanish—it escaped into the air as CO₂ gas. Total mass is conserved: 56 + 44 = 100.

## Quick Recap

- **Combination:** two or more reactants join to form **a single product** (A + B → AB). Examples: 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, C + O₂ → CO₂, 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂.
- **Decomposition:** **a single reactant** breaks down into two or more simpler products (AB → A + B).
- Flavours of decomposition: **thermal/heat** (CaCO₃, NaHCO₃), **electrolytic/electricity** (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂), **photolytic/light** (2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂), and **catalytic** (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂).
- Combination and decomposition are opposites in pattern, but each direction requires its own specific conditions (such as heat or electricity).
- The lime cycle: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (in a lime kiln), CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (slaking), and Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O (hardening on a whitewashed wall).
- Quicklime is caustic (adult handling only); never run gas-evolving reactions in a sealed container.
- Mass is conserved in every reaction: 100 = 56 + 44, and 168 = 106 + 18 + 44.

   (b) A decomposition reaction needs two reactants.
   (c) Quicklime is safe to touch with bare hands.
   (d) It is safe to heat baking soda in a tightly closed test tube.

10. Classify each of the following as combination or decomposition, and explain: (a) 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (b) CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (c) CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (d) 2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂ (e) C + O₂ → CO₂.

11. Two reactions look like opposites: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O and 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. In what two ways are they the same, and in what way are they different?

### Answers

1. **Combination:** two or more reactants join together to form **a single product**, such as 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO (Mg: 2 = 2 ✅, O: 2 = 2 ✅). **Decomposition:** **a single reactant** breaks down into two or more simpler products, such as CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (Ca 1 = 1 ✅, C 1 = 1 ✅, O 3 = 3 ✅).

2. **No.** It forms two products (CO₂ and H₂O). A combination reaction has only **one** formula to the right of the arrow. This reaction is combustion, which we will study in Topic 6.

3. (a) **2Ca + O₂ → 2CaO** (combination): Ca 2 = 2 ✅, O 2 = 2 ✅. (b) **2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂** (decomposition): K 2 = 2 ✅, Cl 2 = 2 ✅, O 6 = 3 × 2 = 6 ✅ (we balance 3 and 2 oxygens at their lowest common multiple, 6). (c) **2Na + Cl₂ → 2NaCl** (combination): Na 2 = 2 ✅, Cl 2 = 2 ✅. (d) **N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃** (combination): N 2 = 2 ✅, H 6 = 6 ✅.

4. Baking soda decomposes in the hot oven: **2NaHCO₃ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O + CO₂**. Bubbles of **carbon dioxide (CO₂)** gas get trapped in the batter and puff the cake up. This is thermal decomposition.

5. By heat (thermal): **CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂**. By electricity (electrolytic): **2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂**. By light (photolytic): **2AgBr → 2Ag + Br₂**; this photolytic reaction was used in black-and-white photographic film. All three are balanced: Ca/C/O 1,1,3 = 1,1,3 ✅; H/O 4,2 = 4,2 ✅; Ag/Br 2,2 = 2,2 ✅.

6. Formula masses: CaCO₃ = 100, CaO = 56, CO₂ = 44, giving a mass ratio of 100 : 56 : 44. For 500 kg (5 times 100 kg): **quicklime = 5 × 56 = 280 kg**, and **CO₂ = 5 × 44 = 220 kg**. Check: 280 + 220 = 500 kg ✅.

7. **The volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen**, because the balanced equation produces 2 molecules of H₂ for every 1 molecule of O₂ (at the same temperature and pressure, gas volume is proportional to the number of molecules). Check: H: 4 = 4 ✅, O: 2 = 2 ✅.

8. Limestone (CaCO₃) → heated to give quicklime (CaO) + CO₂: **decomposition**. Quicklime + water → slaked lime, Ca(OH)₂: **combination**. As whitewash dries on the wall, it reacts with CO₂ in the air to form CaCO₃ again: **Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O** (Ca 1 = 1 ✅, C 1 = 1 ✅, O 4 = 4 ✅, H 2 = 2 ✅). Because this last step has two products, it is not strictly a combination reaction.

9. (a) **True** ✅. (b) **False** ❌. A decomposition reaction starts with **one** reactant. (c) **False** ❌. Quicklime is caustic and must only be handled by an adult wearing gloves and goggles. (d) **False** ❌. Heating a gas-producing reaction in a sealed tube builds dangerous pressure that can shatter the glass; the tube must stay open.

10. (a) **Combination** (two reactants, one product). (b) **Decomposition** (one reactant, two products). (c) **Combination** (two reactants, one product). (d) **Decomposition** (one reactant, two products). (e) **Combination** (two reactants, one product).

11. Both involve the **exact same atoms** (H = 4, O = 2 on both sides) and both work by breaking old bonds and making new ones. They differ in direction and conditions: the first is a **combination** reaction (burning hydrogen, which releases heat), whereas the second is a **decomposition** reaction that requires **electric current** to proceed.

Well done! You now know how the bakery cake puffed up and where the mason's whitewash came from. In combination reactions substances join together, in decomposition reactions a single substance breaks apart, and in both cases not a single atom is gained or lost.
