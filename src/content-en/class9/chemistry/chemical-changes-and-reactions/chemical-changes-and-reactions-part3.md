# Topic 3: Displacement and Double Displacement

In the previous topic, we studied two types of chemical reactions: **combination** (two or more substances joining into one) and **decomposition** (one substance breaking down into two or more). Both were simply about joining or splitting. Today's two reaction types have a different twist: here, one element **takes the place** of another inside a compound, or two compounds **swap their partners**. Let us start with a story from a vineyard.

---

## The reddish tank at the vineyard

During the school holidays, Hannah visited her uncle Mr. Evans at his family's grape vineyard. Early in the morning, Mr. Evans was using a tank sprayer to mist the grapevines with a **bright blue solution**. This was a solution of **copper sulphate** (CuSO₄, traditionally called blue vitriol), used to protect the vines from fungal disease. Mr. Evans was wearing thick gloves and warned Hannah to step back: "This solution is toxic—never touch it with bare hands, and never let it near your mouth."

Looking at an old steel bucket that had been used to mix the solution, Hannah noticed that the inside of the grey iron wall had turned **reddish-brown**. "Uncle, did the bucket rust on the inside?" Mr. Evans chuckled: "It looks like rust, Hannah, but that coating is actually pure **copper**! The bucket is made of iron, and the solution contains copper sulphate. Iron **pushes the copper out** of the solution and takes its place. That is why we always use plastic sprayer tanks nowadays."

Hannah was amazed. "Iron can push copper out of a liquid? Aren't they both metals?" Mr. Evans nodded. "That is the exact question to ask. Come over to the workbench and I'll show you a **contest between metals**: **which metal is strong enough to push another out of its place.**" Today we will understand that contest.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Displacement reaction** | A more reactive metal pushes a less reactive metal out of its salt solution and takes its place | Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu |
| **Reactivity** | How readily and vigorously a metal takes part in chemical reactions | Na is very reactive; Au is very unreactive |
| **Reactivity series** | A list of metals arranged in order from most reactive at the top to least reactive at the bottom | K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au |
| **Salt solution** | A salt (such as CuSO₄) dissolved in water, where its positive and negative ions move freely | Blue CuSO₄(aq) solution |
| **Double displacement reaction** | A reaction in which two compounds in solution **swap their ionic partners** | CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl |
| **Precipitate** | An **insoluble solid** formed inside a solution during a reaction, appearing as a cloudy suspension that settles down | CaCO₃, AgCl |
| **(aq)** | Aqueous: dissolved in water | NaCl(aq) |
| **(s)** | Solid state | CaCO₃(s) |
| **Neutralisation** | The reaction of an acid and a base to form a salt and water | HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O |

## Part A: What displacement means: taking someone's seat

Think of a simple everyday comparison. Imagine a player sitting on a starting bench. A **stronger, higher-ranked** player walks over to claim that spot on the team, and the first player has to step aside. Now the stronger player is in the lineup and the first player is on the sidelines. A lower-ranked player, however, cannot unseat a stronger starter. Only the **more active** contender can take the spot.

Metals behave in the exact same way. In a salt compound, a metal is paired with a non-metal or polyatomic ion (for example, in copper sulphate, Cu is paired with SO₄). If a **more reactive** metal comes along, it takes that spot in the compound and leaves the first metal behind as a free element. Chemists call this a **displacement reaction**.

General pattern:

```
   A  +  B C   -->   A C  +  B
   (more reactive    (A takes B's place;
    metal)            B is set free)
```

Notice that **only one partner is displaced**: a free element (A) pushes B out of a compound (BC). For that reason, it is also called a **single displacement** reaction.

## Part B: Iron and copper sulphate: the story of the nail

Mr. Evans set up a classic demonstration—one that you can also observe (only with an adult or teacher, following the safety rules below).

> **Activity 3.1 (at home with an adult, or in the school lab with a teacher).** Rub a clean **iron nail** lightly with sandpaper until it shines (with help from an adult or teacher). Have the adult dissolve a few crystals of **copper sulphate** in water in a small glass cup to make a clear blue solution. Suspend or rest the iron nail in the solution and watch over **20 to 30 minutes**. Then lift the nail out onto a paper towel.
>
> **Safety:** **Only an adult should handle copper sulphate.** Never taste it or bring it near your mouth or eyes. **Wash your hands thoroughly with soap** afterwards. Keep the glass out of reach of young children and pets, and have the adult dispose of the solution properly. Never reuse any cup or spoon from this activity for food or drink.

You will observe two clear changes:

1. A **reddish-brown coating** forms on the submerged part of the iron nail. This coating is **copper metal (Cu)**.
2. The **deep blue colour of the solution gradually fades**, turning **pale green** if left long enough.

Both observations come from a single chemical reaction:

> **Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu**

Let us verify the atom count as in Chapter 3: Fe: 1 and 1. Cu: 1 and 1. S: 1 and 1. O: 4 and 4. The equation is balanced.

What happened? **Blue CuSO₄** was dissolved in the water. Iron (Fe) pushed copper (Cu) out of the solution, and that copper deposited onto the nail as a reddish-brown coat. Meanwhile, iron entered the solution as **FeSO₄**. Because **aqueous FeSO₄ is pale green**, the blue colour faded and turned greenish. The exact same reaction had happened inside Mr. Evans's old iron mixing bucket!

Now let us zoom in to the particle level (recalling from Chapter 6 that ionic salts exist as separate ions in water). In the solution, copper sulphate floats around as Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions, while the nail is made of neutral Fe atoms. Tracking two formula units gives us this picture:

```
      BEFORE                                  AFTER

     nail (grey Fe)                          nail coated with reddish-brown Cu
          ||                                   ||
     ___________                          ___________
    /   ||      \                        /   ##      \
   /    ||       \                      /    ##       \
  / blue solution \                    /  pale green   \
  \_______________/                    \_______________/
   blue due to Cu²⁺                     pale green due to Fe²⁺

   Particle count (tracking 2 copies):

   BEFORE
     in the nail:      Fe  Fe
     in the solution:  Cu²⁺  SO₄²⁻  Cu²⁺  SO₄²⁻

   AFTER
     on the nail:      Cu  Cu
     in the solution:  Fe²⁺  SO₄²⁻  Fe²⁺  SO₄²⁻
```

> **Figure 3.1** – An iron nail in copper sulphate solution, before and after. Two Fe atoms displace two Cu²⁺ ions: 2 neutral Cu atoms deposit onto the nail, while 2 Fe²⁺ ions enter the solution. The SO₄²⁻ ions do not change at all—they simply float along as "spectators." Both before and after, there are 2 Fe, 2 Cu, and 2 SO₄²⁻: nothing is created or destroyed, only places are swapped. (Underneath, Fe atoms are transferring electrons to Cu²⁺ ions; we will preview that in Topic 5.)

### A quick mass check

Using atomic masses Fe = 56 u, Cu = 63.5 u, S = 32 u, and O = 16 u, let us find the formula unit masses:

- CuSO₄ = 63.5 + 32 + 4 × 16 = **159.5 u**
- FeSO₄ = 56 + 32 + 4 × 16 = **152 u**

Total mass of reactants: 56 + 159.5 = **215.5 u**. Total mass of products: 152 + 63.5 = **215.5 u**. They match—the law of conservation of mass (Chapter 4) holds true here as well.

Notice a fun detail: for every 56 g of iron that dissolves off the nail, 63.5 g of copper plates onto it—so in theory the nail gains a tiny bit of mass! In practice, the copper coating is loose and flaky and only a thin surface layer reacts, so you cannot easily weigh that tiny difference on a household scale.

**Hannah's question: why does this happen?** Because **iron is more reactive than copper.** To predict which metal can displace which in any pair, we need a ranking ladder. Let us build it.

## Part C: The reactivity series: which metal displaces which

By testing many different metals with salt solutions, water, and acids, chemists arranged metals into a ranked list called the **reactivity series**. Think of it as a **ladder**: the most reactive metals sit at the top, and the least reactive sit at the bottom.

```
      MOST REACTIVE
              ^
         1.   K    Potassium    reacts extremely vigorously
         2.   Na   Sodium
         3.   Ca   Calcium
         4.   Mg   Magnesium
         5.   Al   Aluminium
         6.   Zn   Zinc
         7.   Fe   Iron
         8.   Pb   Lead
         9.   H    Hydrogen     (not a metal, included as a reference)
        10.   Cu   Copper
        11.   Hg   Mercury
        12.   Ag   Silver
        13.   Au   Gold         barely reacts at all, so it stays shiny
              v
      LEAST REACTIVE
```

> **Figure 3.2** – The reactivity ladder (13 positions) from K down to Au, in order of decreasing reactivity. Any metal higher on the ladder can displace a metal lower on the ladder from its salt solution. Hydrogen is not a metal, but it is placed on the ladder as a benchmark for reactions with acids.

**The entire rule in one sentence:**

> **A metal higher on the reactivity ladder can displace a metal lower on the ladder from its salt solution. A lower metal cannot displace a higher metal.**

That is why Fe (position 7) displaces Cu (position 10) from copper sulphate solution: Fe sits above Cu. And that is also why **Cu cannot displace Zn from ZnSO₄ solution**: Cu (10) sits below Zn (6). The ladder also explains why gold jewellery stays bright for centuries without tarnishing: Au sits at the very bottom of the ladder and barely reacts with air or moisture.

**Safety note:** Metals at the very top of the ladder, such as K and Na, react **violently** with water and even moist air (catching fire). They are handled **only by teachers** under special precautions. Lead (Pb) and mercury (Hg) are **toxic heavy metals**, and silver nitrate (AgNO₃) stains skin black and is corrosive to eyes. **Students never perform activities with these substances**; we include them on the ladder purely so you can read and predict reactions on paper.

## Part D: Making predictions: will the reaction happen or not?

The best part about the reactivity ladder is that you can predict right on paper whether a reaction will happen. Simply check: **is the free metal higher or lower on the ladder than the metal inside the salt?**

### Example 1. Zn + CuSO₄: will it react?

- Zn is at position **6**, Cu is at **10**. Zn is higher.
- Therefore Zn will displace Cu. **Yes, the reaction happens.**

```
   Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
```

Check: Zn 1=1, Cu 1=1, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅. If a strip of zinc is placed in blue copper sulphate solution, the blue colour fades to **colourless** (because ZnSO₄ solution is colourless) and reddish-brown copper deposits on the zinc.

### Example 2. Cu + ZnSO₄: will it react?

- Cu is at **10**, Zn is at **6**. Cu is lower.
- A lower metal cannot displace a higher metal. **No reaction.**

```
   Cu + ZnSO₄ → no reaction
```

If you dip a copper wire into colourless zinc sulphate solution, nothing happens at all. Displacement is a one-way street: **Zn displaces Cu, but Cu cannot displace Zn.**

### Example 3. Fe + CuSO₄: will it react?

- Fe is at **7**, Cu is at **10**. Fe is higher. **Yes, the reaction happens.** That is our iron nail experiment from Part B.

### Example 4. Mg + FeSO₄: will it react?

- Mg is at **4**, Fe is at **7**. Mg is higher. **Yes.**

```
   Mg + FeSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Fe
```

Check: Mg 1=1, Fe 1=1, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅.

### Example 5. Fe + ZnSO₄: will it react?

- Fe is at **7**, Zn is at **6**. Fe is **lower** than Zn. **No reaction.** Iron cannot displace zinc.

### Example 6. Ag + CuSO₄: will it react?

- Ag is at **12**, Cu is at **10**. Ag is lower. **No reaction.**

### Example 7. Al + CuSO₄: will it react?

- Al is at **5**, Cu is at **10**. Al is higher. **Yes, the reaction can happen.**

```
   2Al + 3CuSO₄ → Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 3Cu
```

Check: Al 2=2, Cu 3=3, S 3=3, O 12=12 ✅. (In practice, aluminium metal is covered by a tough, thin layer of aluminium oxide formed in air, which delays the start of the reaction; we will look at that protective layer in Topic 6.)

Here is a summary table of all seven predictions:

| Free metal | Salt solution | Is the free metal higher on the ladder? | Reaction? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zn | CuSO₄ | Yes (6 vs 10) | Yes |
| Cu | ZnSO₄ | No (10 vs 6) | No |
| Fe | CuSO₄ | Yes (7 vs 10) | Yes |
| Mg | FeSO₄ | Yes (4 vs 7) | Yes |
| Fe | ZnSO₄ | No (7 vs 6) | No |
| Ag | CuSO₄ | No (12 vs 10) | No |
| Al | CuSO₄ | Yes (5 vs 10) | Yes |

## Part E: Displacement with acids: releasing hydrogen gas

Notice that **H (hydrogen)** sits at position 9 on our ladder. In dilute acids such as hydrochloric acid (HCl) or dilute sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄), hydrogen is the positive partner. Any metal **above H on the ladder** (such as Mg, Al, Zn, or Fe) can **displace hydrogen from dilute acid**, releasing bubbles of hydrogen gas (H₂):

```
   Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
```

Check: Zn 1=1, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅.

> **Activity 3.2 (for observation only, teacher demonstration).** In the school lab, the teacher places a few granules of zinc in a test tube and adds a little **dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl)**. Brisk bubbles of hydrogen gas immediately stream up. The test tube is kept away from open flames. **Students do not perform this themselves**: acids are corrosive and hydrogen gas is flammable.

Now compare copper: Cu (position 10) sits **below H (9)** on the ladder. So if copper turnings are placed in dilute HCl, **no reaction occurs** and no hydrogen bubbles appear.

## Part F: Double displacement: swapping partners

Now for our second reaction type today. In single displacement, **one free element** pushes a partner out of a compound. In **double displacement**, **two compounds** in solution meet, and their ions **swap partners with each other**. Because two displacements happen simultaneously, we call it **double displacement**.

Recall the school dance from Topic 1. Two couples are on the dance floor: Sam–Maya and Liam–Zoe. Mid-song, the two couples **swap partners**: Sam pairs with Zoe, and Liam pairs with Maya. Everyone on the floor is the same person as before—only the partnerships have been exchanged.

General pattern:

```
   AB  +  CD   -->   AD  +  CB
   (A and C swap their partners B and D)
```

Let us look at a clear example: **calcium chloride and washing soda (sodium carbonate)**. Calcium chloride (CaCl₂) and sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃) are both white solids that dissolve in water to form clear, colourless solutions. Watch what happens when the two clear solutions are poured together:

```
   CaCl₂(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq)
```

Check: Ca 1=1, Cl 2=2, Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 3=3 ✅.

```
   BEFORE:  Ca²⁺  Cl⁻  Cl⁻   +   Na⁺  Na⁺  CO₃²⁻
               \_______ partners swap _______/
   AFTER:   Ca²⁺  CO₃²⁻  (CaCO₃, solid)   +   Na⁺  Cl⁻   +   Na⁺  Cl⁻  (2 NaCl, in solution)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Partner swapping in double displacement. Before mixing, Ca²⁺ is paired with two Cl⁻ ions, and two Na⁺ ions are paired with CO₃²⁻. Upon mixing, Ca²⁺ pairs up with CO₃²⁻, leaving the two Na⁺ ions paired with the two Cl⁻ ions. Both before and after, there are 1 Ca²⁺, 2 Cl⁻, 2 Na⁺, and 1 CO₃²⁻: nothing is lost, they simply swapped partners. Because the newly formed CaCO₃ is insoluble in water, it comes out as a solid **precipitate**.

### Precipitate: when a "milky cloud" appears in a clear liquid

The instant those two clear solutions mix, a **chalky white cloud** appears, turning the liquid milky and gradually settling to the bottom as a white powder. That solid is **CaCO₃** (calcium carbonate, the same substance found in chalk, limestone, and marble). Because CaCO₃ **does not dissolve in water**, it separates out as a solid. We call an insoluble solid formed in a solution a **precipitate**, and in chemical equations we mark it with **(s)** or a downward arrow **↓**.

Meanwhile, the other product, sodium chloride (NaCl), stays dissolved in the water, so we mark it **(aq)**.

**Note:** At this stage you do not need to memorise solubility rules for every compound—just learn the specific precipitate examples covered in this chapter. You will study general solubility rules in higher grades.

### Example 8. AgNO₃ and NaCl: a curdy white precipitate

When clear solutions of silver nitrate (AgNO₃) and sodium chloride (NaCl) are mixed, a **curdy white precipitate** of silver chloride (AgCl) forms immediately:

```
   AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s)↓ + NaNO₃(aq)
```

Check: Ag 1=1, N 1=1, O 3=3, Na 1=1, Cl 1=1 ✅. Partners swapped: Ag traded NO₃ for Cl, and Na traded Cl for NO₃. Because AgCl is insoluble in water, it precipitates out as a white solid.

> **Activity 3.3 (for observation only, teacher demonstration).** The teacher mixes a few drops of dilute silver nitrate solution with sodium chloride solution in a test tube while students observe from their seats. **Students do not handle silver nitrate**: it leaves stubborn dark stains on skin and can damage the eyes. The teacher wears gloves and safety goggles and disposes of the waste safely.

In another classic teacher demonstration, mixing solutions of lead nitrate and potassium iodide produces a brilliant yellow precipitate of lead iodide ("golden rain"). Because **lead compounds are toxic**, that demonstration is strictly performed by a teacher in a laboratory; we mention it here for reading only.

## Part G: Neutralisation: another double displacement reaction

There is another famous everyday reaction that follows the exact same partner-swapping pattern: when an **acid and a base** react to form a **salt and water**. Chemists call this **neutralisation** (you will study acids and bases in detail later; for now, just know that HCl is an acid and NaOH is a base):

```
   HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H₂O(l)
```

Check: H 2=2 (1 from HCl + 1 from NaOH = 2; 2 in H₂O), Cl 1=1, Na 1=1, O 1=1 ✅.

Look at the partner swap: **H** (originally with Cl) joins with **OH** to form HOH = **H₂O (water)**, while **Na** (originally with OH) pairs with **Cl** to form **NaCl**. No solid precipitate forms here because NaCl stays dissolved and H₂O is water itself—yet a genuine chemical reaction has occurred because neutral **water molecules** have formed from H⁺ and OH⁻ ions. Keep this principle in mind:

> A double displacement reaction actually takes place when **something leaves the free-ion mix**—such as an insoluble precipitate or neutral water molecules (as in neutralisation). If two salt solutions are mixed and all four ions simply stay dissolved without forming any precipitate, water, or gas, then you just have a mixture of ions, not a chemical reaction.

**Safety note:** Never mix household acids and bases (such as toilet cleaners, drain cleaners, or bleach) at home. Acid–base experiments belong in the school lab with a teacher.

## Part H: How to tell all four reaction types apart

Now we have all four main reaction types in our toolkit. Here is how to identify them just by looking at the equation:

| Type | General pattern | Key question to ask | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Combination | A + B → AB | Do **two or more substances join to make one**? | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O |
| Decomposition | AB → A + B | Does **one substance break down into two or more**? | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ |
| Displacement | A + BC → AC + B | Does **one free element push another out of a compound**? | Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu |
| Double displacement | AB + CD → AD + CB | Do **two compounds swap their partners**? | CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl |

Three quick steps to classify any equation:

1. **Count how many formulas are on the left and on the right.** 1 → 2 or more: decomposition. 2 or more → 1: combination.
2. If the left side has **one free element + one compound**: displacement.
3. If the left side has **two compounds** and they exchange partners to give two compounds on the right: double displacement.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Reading the reactivity ladder upside down**

Metals **higher** on the ladder are **more** reactive. Thus Zn (position 6) can displace Cu (position 10), but Cu cannot displace Zn. Reading the ladder backwards leads you to write a reaction for Cu + ZnSO₄, which never happens.

**Mistake 2: Saying the iron nail "turned into copper"**

Iron atoms **do not turn into copper atoms**. Iron atoms dissolve into the liquid as Fe²⁺ ions (forming FeSO₄), while **copper ions that were already in the blue solution** plate onto the outside of the nail as copper metal. No element ever turns into another element in a chemical reaction—they just trade places.

**Mistake 3: Saying the blue solution "went bad" or "melted"**

The blue colour faded because blue Cu²⁺ ions were **removed** from the solution and pale-green Fe²⁺ ions **entered** it. Nothing melted; the ions in solution changed.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring ionic charges and subscripts during double displacement**

In CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃, each Ca²⁺ brings **two Cl⁻** ions and each CO₃²⁻ brings **two Na⁺** ions, so they form **2 NaCl** on the right side. Writing Na₂Cl₂ is wrong (the formula of sodium chloride is NaCl), and writing just 1 NaCl leaves the equation unbalanced.

**Mistake 5: Assuming every ion mix is a double displacement reaction**

On paper you can swap any two pairs of ions, but a real double displacement reaction happens only when a **precipitate** (or water/gas) forms.

**Mistake 6: Confusing a precipitate with smoke or gas**

A precipitate is an insoluble **solid** that clouds a liquid and settles to the bottom. A gas forms rising bubbles. They are completely different states of matter.

## Quick Recap

- **Displacement:** a free element pushes another element out of its compound and takes its place. A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution.
- **Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu:** a reddish-brown coating of Cu forms on the iron nail, and the deep blue solution turns pale green (FeSO₄). That is why an iron sprayer tank gets a copper coating inside if used for copper sulphate spray.
- **Reactivity series:** K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au (from most reactive to least reactive). Higher displaces lower, never the reverse: Zn displaces Cu, but Cu cannot displace Zn.
- Metals above H on the ladder displace **H₂ gas** from dilute acids (Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂; teacher demonstration).
- **Double displacement:** two compounds in solution swap ionic partners (AB + CD → AD + CB).
- **Precipitate:** an insoluble solid formed in solution, marked with (s) or ↓. Mixing CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ gives a white precipitate of CaCO₃(s); mixing AgNO₃ + NaCl gives a curdy white precipitate of AgCl(s).
- **Neutralisation** (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O) is also a double displacement reaction.
- The four main reaction types: combination (many → one), decomposition (one → many), displacement (element + compound), and double displacement (two compounds swapping partners).
- Silver nitrate, lead salts, and mercury compounds are never handled by students; copper sulphate is handled only by an adult.

11. A student wants to do the iron nail activity at home. List three safety steps she must follow, and say who should handle the copper sulphate.

12. Gold ornaments stay shiny for centuries. Use the reactivity series to explain why.

### Answers

1. A **more reactive** metal displaces a **less reactive** metal from its salt solution and takes its place. A metal higher on the reactivity ladder displaces a metal lower on the ladder, never the reverse.

2. **Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu.** Atom count: Fe 1=1, Cu 1=1, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅. Two observable changes: (i) a **reddish-brown coating of copper (Cu)** forms on the nail because Fe displaces Cu from solution; (ii) the deep blue solution turns **pale green** because blue Cu²⁺ ions are replaced by pale-green FeSO₄ in solution.

3. The bucket is made of iron (Fe) and the spray solution contains **copper sulphate (CuSO₄)**. Because iron is more reactive than copper, **Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu**. Displaced copper plates onto the inside wall of the iron bucket as a reddish-brown layer. It is metallic copper, not rust.

4. (a) **Yes.** Zn (6) is above Fe (7). **Zn + FeSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Fe** (Zn 1=1, Fe 1=1, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅).
   (b) **No.** Cu (10) is below Fe (7).
   (c) **Yes.** Mg (4) is above Zn (6). **Mg + ZnSO₄ → MgSO₄ + Zn** (Mg 1=1, Zn 1=1, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅).
   (d) **No.** Fe (7) is below Mg (4).
   (e) **Yes.** Mg (4) is above H (9), so it displaces hydrogen gas. **Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂** (Mg 1=1, H 2=2, Cl 2=2 ✅).
   (f) **No.** Ag (12) is below H (9).

5. In a double displacement reaction, two compounds in solution **swap their ionic partners**. In CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃, Ca²⁺ starts paired with Cl⁻ and Na⁺ starts paired with CO₃²⁻; after mixing, Ca²⁺ pairs with CO₃²⁻ (forming solid CaCO₃) and Na⁺ pairs with Cl⁻ (forming dissolved NaCl). **CaCl₂(aq) + Na₂CO₃(aq) → CaCO₃(s)↓ + 2NaCl(aq).** Count: Ca 1=1, Cl 2=2, Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 3=3 ✅.

6. A **precipitate** is an insoluble **solid** formed during a reaction in solution that clouds the liquid and settles out. (a) **CaCO₃**: chalky/milky **white** solid. (b) **AgCl**: **curdy white** solid.

7. In HCl the partners are H and Cl; in NaOH they are Na and OH. After mixing, **H pairs with OH to form H₂O** and **Na pairs with Cl to form NaCl**. Because the two compounds swapped partners, it is a double displacement reaction. **No precipitate** forms: NaCl remains dissolved and H₂O is liquid water.

8. Reactants: Fe + CuSO₄ = 56 + 159.5 = **215.5 u**. Products: FeSO₄ + Cu = 152 + 63.5 = **215.5 u**. Equal ✅, confirming conservation of mass.

9. (a) Combination: 2 into 1; S 1=1, O 2=2 ✅. (b) Displacement: Mg displaces H from the acid; Mg 1=1, H 2=2, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅. (c) Double displacement (neutralisation): Na 1=1, O 4=4, H 2=2, N 1=1 ✅. (d) Decomposition: 1 into 2; Cu 1=1, C 1=1, O 3=3 ✅. (e) Displacement: Al (5) displaces Fe (7); Al 2=2, Fe 3=3, S 3=3, O 12=12 ✅. (f) Decomposition: Ag 4=4, O 2=2 ✅. (g) Combination: 2 into 1; S 2=2, O 6=6 ✅. (h) Double displacement: Cu swaps SO₄ for OH, Na swaps OH for SO₄; Cu 1=1, S 1=1, O 6=6, Na 2=2, H 2=2 ✅. (i) Displacement: Fe displaces H; Fe 1=1, H 2=2, S 1=1, O 4=4 ✅. (j) Double displacement (neutralisation): Ca 1=1, O 2=2, H 4=4, Cl 2=2 ✅.

10. (a) **False** ❌. Cu sits below Zn in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace Zn. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌. A precipitate is an insoluble **solid**, not a gas. (d) **False** ❌. In neutralisation (HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O), no precipitate forms—water is formed instead.

11. Three safety steps: (i) **only an adult should handle copper sulphate** and prepare the solution; (ii) **never taste** the crystals or solution, and keep it away from mouth and eyes; (iii) **wash hands thoroughly with soap** after the activity. Also keep the cup away from young children and pets, have the adult dispose of the liquid safely, and never reuse the cup for food.

12. Gold (Au) sits at the **very bottom** of the reactivity series, meaning it is extremely unreactive. Air, moisture, and everyday substances barely react with it, so gold jewellery keeps its shine for centuries.
