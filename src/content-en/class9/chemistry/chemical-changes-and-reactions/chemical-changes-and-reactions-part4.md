# Topic 4: Energy and Rate: Exothermic, Endothermic, and Catalysts

In the previous topics, we saw the four main **types** of chemical reactions: combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement. All four answer the question, "**What new substances** are formed?" Today we ask two new questions: "Where does **heat energy** go or come from during a reaction?" and "**How fast** does a reaction happen—and how can we speed it up or slow it down?" Let us start on a chilly mountain trail with a small pocket pouch.

---

## The warm pouch on the mountain trail

It was a frosty December morning. Mr. Brooks had taken his nephew Sam on an early hike up a windy ridge. The air was so biting cold that Sam's fingers were going numb even inside his gloves. "Uncle, my hands are freezing!" Sam said.

Mr. Brooks pulled a flat, sealed plastic packet out of his backpack. Printed on the wrapper were the words: *"Hand Warmer — tear open and expose to air."* He tore open the outer seal, gave the little inner pouch a gentle shake, and slipped it into Sam's gloved hands. Within a couple of minutes, the pouch began to grow **pleasantly warm**, thawing Sam's chilly fingers right out. "There's no battery inside and no flame—where is all this heat coming from?" Sam asked in surprise.

"Before I tore the wrapper, the pouch was completely cold; the moment air reached it, it started warming up," Mr. Brooks replied. "That means whatever is inside the pouch started **reacting with the air**, and that chemical reaction is **releasing heat**. That is our big idea for today: some reactions **give out** heat, while other reactions **take in** heat. And there's a second puzzle too: what makes a reaction go fast or slow? Let's find out."

First let us learn the key terms, then picture how energy changes in a reaction, uncover the secret inside Sam's hand warmer, and finally explore the **rate of reaction**.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Exothermic reaction** | A reaction that releases heat (energy) to the surroundings, making the surroundings warmer | Burning wood, a hand-warmer packet |
| **Endothermic reaction** | A reaction that absorbs heat (energy) from the surroundings, making the surroundings cooler or requiring continuous heating | Photosynthesis, heating CaCO₃ in a kiln |
| **Energy level** | A qualitative high-or-low step on a sketch representing the energy stored in substances | Energy level of reactants vs products |
| **Rate of reaction** | How fast a chemical reaction takes place | A crushed effervescent tablet fizzes faster |
| **Surface area** | The total exposed outer area of a solid that is available to touch and react with another substance | Finely powdered solid has a much larger surface area than a single lump |
| **Concentration** | How crowded the dissolved particles are in a given volume of solution | A concentrated solution vs a dilute solution |
| **Catalyst** | A substance that speeds up a reaction without being used up itself | MnO₂, baker's yeast, or raw potato (for decomposing H₂O₂) |
| **Enzyme** | A biological catalyst made by living cells | Amylase in human saliva |

## Part A: Energy in a reaction: coming out or going in?

In Topic 1, we saw that every chemical reaction involves **breaking** old bonds and **making** new bonds. Keep this qualitative rule (without numbers) firmly in mind:

- **Breaking bonds requires energy input** (like pulling apart two stuck magnets or untying a tight knot).
- **Making new bonds releases energy back** (like two magnets snapping together with a click).

In any reaction, there are only two possible outcomes when we compare the two steps:

1. **Making the new bonds releases more energy than was needed to break the old bonds.** The leftover energy comes out into the surroundings as heat (and sometimes light). The surroundings get **warmer**. This is called an **exothermic reaction** (*"exo"* = out, *"thermic"* = heat).
2. **Breaking the old bonds takes more energy than the new bonds give back.** The extra energy has to be pulled in from the surroundings. Either the surroundings get **cooler**, or we have to keep supplying energy continuously (as heat, light, or electricity). This is called an **endothermic reaction** (*"endo"* = in).

(You studied the difference between heat and temperature and how heat flows from warmer to cooler objects in the Physics chapter *Heat and Temperature*; here we simply use the fact that heat is a form of energy transferred between the reaction and its surroundings.)

A helpful everyday comparison: carrying a heavy backpack **downstairs** is easy and releases energy, whereas carrying it **upstairs** requires you to put energy in. An exothermic reaction is "stepping downhill" in stored chemical energy; an endothermic reaction is "stepping uphill."

### Energy-level sketches

We can picture the stored chemical energy of reactants and products as high or low steps on a diagram. First look at the sketch for an exothermic reaction:

```
Energy
  ^
  :   Reactants  ________
  :                      \  heat released
  :                       \ to surroundings
  :                        v
  :                 Products  ________
  :
  +----------------------------------> progress of reaction
```

Now look at the opposite case: in an endothermic reaction, the products sit at a **higher** energy level than the reactants. Both sketches together make up Figure 4.1.

```
Energy
  ^
  :                 Products  ________
  :                         /
  :                        /  heat absorbed
  :                       /   from surroundings
  :   Reactants  ________/
  :
  +----------------------------------> progress of reaction
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Energy-level sketches. **Top (exothermic):** the reactants start at a **higher** energy level and the products end at a **lower** level; the difference in energy is released to the surroundings as heat (and sometimes light), warming the surroundings. **Bottom (endothermic):** the reactants start **lower** and the products end **higher**; to climb to that higher level, energy must be absorbed from outside (as heat, light, or electricity), cooling the surroundings or requiring a continuous supply of heat. These are qualitative sketches with no numerical scale.

## Part B: Exothermic reactions: reactions that release heat

Exothermic reactions are all around us in daily life. For each one below, notice where the released heat shows up:

| Reaction | Equation | Where you notice the heat |
|---|---|---|
| Burning a fuel (methane / natural gas) | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O + heat | The hot flame of a gas stove |
| Cellular respiration (inside our cells) | C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy | Keeps our body warm (~37 °C) and powers our muscles |
| Slaking of lime | CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + heat | The water gets steaming hot (**handled by adults only**) |
| Neutralisation | HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O + heat | The mixed dilute solution becomes noticeably warm |
| Iron oxidising in a hand warmer | 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ (simplified) + heat | The pocket pouch stays warm for hours |
| Composting of plant waste | Microbes breaking down leaves and food scraps | The centre of a compost heap feels warm |

**Safety reminder:** In the slaking of lime (CaO + H₂O), quicklime is strongly **caustic** and the mixture can get boiling hot. That is strictly handled by teachers or trained adults—students never touch quicklime.

A quick note on cellular respiration: inside our body cells, glucose reacts slowly with oxygen to release energy. That energy keeps our body near 37 °C and powers every heartbeat and movement. We will return to respiration as "slow combustion" in Topic 6.

## Part C: Endothermic reactions: reactions that absorb energy

| Reaction | Equation | Where the energy comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Photosynthesis | 6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ | Sunlight absorbed by green leaves |
| Thermal decomposition of limestone | CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ | Intense heat from a lime kiln |
| Baking soda + lemon juice | 3NaHCO₃ + C₆H₈O₇ → Na₃C₆H₅O₇ + 3H₂O + 3CO₂ | Absorbs heat from the liquid, so the cup feels **cool** |
| Electrolysis of water | 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ | Electrical energy from a battery (teacher demo) |

Without sunlight, plants cannot carry out photosynthesis: light energy is **stored** inside the chemical bonds of glucose. Later, when cells respire glucose, that stored energy is released back! Photosynthesis and respiration are chemical opposites: photosynthesis is endothermic, and respiration is exothermic.

In the third row, C₆H₈O₇ is **citric acid** (the acid in lemon juice). That is the exact cup from our Topic 1 home activity that felt cool to the touch: as CO₂ bubbles fizz out, the reaction pulls heat from the liquid. (In a cake oven, baking soda decomposes by a different equation using oven heat, as we saw in Topic 2.)

### An honest distinction: the instant cold pack

Athletes use "instant cold packs" for sprains: you squeeze the pack to pop an inner water bag, shake it, and the pack turns icy cold in seconds. Inside the pack are solid ammonium nitrate (NH₄NO₃) and water. When the inner bag breaks, ammonium nitrate dissolves in the water and absorbs heat rapidly.

**Honest chemistry note:** Dissolving NH₄NO₃ in water is **not a chemical reaction—it is a physical dissolving process**. No new substance is formed; the NH₄⁺ and NO₃⁻ ions simply separate and disperse in water:

NH₄NO₃(s) → NH₄⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq)

However, **the energy flow works the exact same way**: separating the ions absorbs heat from the water, so the pack cools down via an **endothermic process**. That is why cold packs are often mentioned alongside endothermic reactions, even though we carefully distinguish endothermic *dissolving* from an endothermic *chemical reaction*. (Never cut open a cold pack or swallow its contents, and wash your hands if a pack ever leaks.)

## Part D: The initial "push" needed to start a reaction

Here is a good puzzle: if natural gas (CH₄) and oxygen (O₂) in the air are both present when a stove burner is turned on, and burning is strongly exothermic, why doesn't the gas catch fire all by itself at room temperature? Why do we need a spark or a lit match?

Because most reactions need a small initial **"push"** to get started: before any new bonds can form, some old bonds in the reactants have to be stretched and broken, and that requires an initial input of energy. The spark from a lighter or match provides that starting push to a few molecules. Once those first molecules react, the heat they release gives the push to their neighbours, and the flame sustains itself! (In higher grades you will call this starting push *activation energy*.)

So remember: "exothermic" means the reaction releases heat overall—it does **not** mean the reaction starts without a push.

## Part E: The secret inside the hand warmer

Now let us return to Sam's hand warmer on the mountain trail. Inside the breathable inner pouch is **finely powdered iron (Fe)**, mixed with a little salt, moisture, and an absorbent filler (such as activated carbon and vermiculite). While the outer plastic wrapper is sealed airtight, oxygen from the air cannot get in, so the iron powder **waits unreacted**.

The moment you tear open the outer wrapper, air enters through the porous inner pouch. The iron powder reacts with oxygen to form iron(III) oxide—the **exact same chemical reaction as rusting**, but sped up enormously! In simplified form:

4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ + heat

(We call this equation simplified because real rust also incorporates water molecules; we will cover rusting in full in Topic 6.) Because the iron is a fine powder with a huge surface area, and because the trace of salt and moisture speeds the process along, a reaction that normally takes months on an iron gate finishes in a few hours inside the pouch. The steady release of heat keeps the pouch warm for 6 to 10 hours.

```
   Wrapper sealed: Fe powder waits unreacted
   +------------------------------+
   :  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe      :   pouch is cold
   :  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe      :
   +------------------------------+
                 :
           seal torn open
                 v
   Air (O₂) enters the porous pouch
   +------------------------------+
   : 4Fe + 3O₂ --> 2Fe₂O₃ + heat  :   pouch is warm
   :  O₂ -> Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe  Fe    :
   +------------------------------+
```

> **Figure 4.2** – How an air-activated hand-warmer pouch works. **Top:** while sealed in its airtight wrapper, the fine iron powder (Fe) has no oxygen to react with, so the pouch stays cold. **Bottom:** once the wrapper is torn open, oxygen from the air enters and reacts with the iron powder to form Fe₂O₃, releasing steady heat in an exothermic reaction. (Schematic diagram; real pouches also contain salt, water, and filler.)

**Safety rules for air-activated hand warmers:**
- They are **single-use**. Once all the iron powder has oxidised, the pouch cools down permanently and cannot be re-used.
- Do not press a hot pouch directly against bare skin for long periods (it can cause low-temperature burns); keep it outside a layer of fabric or inside a glove. Never cut open the pouch or put the powder near your mouth.
- Never place a hand warmer in a microwave or near a flame.
- Dispose of the cooled, spent pouch in regular household trash, not in waterways.

## Part F: Rate of reaction: how fast does a reaction go?

Now for our second big question: **how fast** does a chemical reaction happen? Some reactions are explosive (fireworks), while others are slow (iron rusting over months, or milk turning into curd overnight). Chemists can control the **rate of reaction** using four main factors.

### 1. Temperature

When we heat a mixture, its particles move faster and collide with each other more often and with greater force, so the reaction goes **faster**. Cooling slows particles down and makes the reaction **slower**.

### 2. Concentration

A more concentrated solution packs more reactant particles into the same volume, making collisions between particles much more frequent. Higher concentration gives a **faster** reaction.

### 3. Surface area (particle size of a solid)

When a solid lump reacts with a liquid or gas, only the particles on the **outside surface** of the lump can touch the other reactant; particles buried deep inside have to wait. If we **crush or grind** the lump into tiny pieces or a fine powder, we expose a much larger total surface area at once. Therefore, **smaller pieces (larger surface area) give a faster reaction**. Think of how crushed ice melts much faster than a single large ice block of the same weight (melting is a physical change, but the surface-area principle is identical).

### 4. Catalyst

A **catalyst** is a substance that **speeds up** a chemical reaction **without being used up itself**—at the end of the reaction, all the catalyst is still there unchanged. It works by providing an easier pathway for the reactants to rearrange.

Recall the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) from Topic 2:

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

On its own in a beaker, H₂O₂ decomposes very slowly. Add a pinch of **manganese dioxide (MnO₂)** in the school lab, or a pinch of **baker's yeast** or a slice of **raw potato** at home, and oxygen gas bubbles out immediately! The catalyst remains unchanged after the fizzing stops.

**At home with an adult** (revisiting the Topic 2 activity through the lens of reaction rate): pour a small splash of 3% pharmacy hydrogen peroxide into an open bowl, wear eye protection, and drop in a pinch of yeast or a slice of raw potato. Never taste it, and **never use a closed bottle** (pressure buildup!). Watch how the catalyst turns a barely noticeable trickle of decomposition into a rapid foam of oxygen bubbles.

**Enzymes:** Living cells make their own biological catalysts called **enzymes**. For example, saliva in your mouth contains the enzyme *amylase*, which speeds up the breakdown of starch in bread into simple sugars (that is why plain bread tastes slightly sweet if you chew it for a long time). Think of enzymes as nature's catalysts.

### Safe home activity: effervescent tablets (at home with an adult)

This activity lets you see firsthand how **surface area** and **temperature** change the rate of a reaction.

**Safety rules first (read carefully):**

- An **adult must supervise**. Have the adult pour any warm water.
- **Never taste or drink** the tablet solutions used in the experiment.
- **Use the exact same volume of water in both glasses** (for example, 100 mL each) so the comparison is a fair test.
- Always use open glasses, **never a closed bottle** (trapped CO₂ gas builds up pressure).
- Have the adult crush the tablet inside a clean zip-top bag using the back of a spoon; do not inhale the powder or bring it near your eyes.
- Wash your hands afterwards and pour the spent liquid down the sink.

**Materials:** 2 or 4 clean glass cups, a measuring cup, a stopwatch or phone timer, effervescent tablets (such as fizzing vitamin C or antacid tablets, handled with an adult), and a spoon.

**Experiment 1: whole tablet vs crushed tablet (surface area):**

1. Pour **equal volumes** (say, 100 mL) of room-temperature water into two identical glasses.
2. Take two identical tablets; leave one **whole**, and have an adult crush the second into a **fine powder** inside a clean plastic pouch. Drop the whole tablet into Glass 1 and tip the crushed tablet into Glass 2 at the exact same moment.
3. Start the timer and watch how vigorously each glass fizzes and how many seconds it takes for the solid to disappear completely.

**Typical result:** The crushed tablet fizzes much more vigorously and finishes **much faster** than the whole tablet. (Exact times depend on the brand of tablet, but the crushed one always wins.)

**Experiment 2: cold water vs warm water (temperature):**

1. Pour **100 mL of cold water** (from the fridge) into Glass 1, and **100 mL of warm water** (poured by an adult—comfortably warm to the touch, never boiling hot) into Glass 2.
2. Drop an **identical whole tablet** into each glass at the same time and time them.

**Typical result:** The tablet in warm water fizzes much more rapidly and finishes in a fraction of the time taken in cold water.

```
 Whole tablet:
  time -->  0s      20s     40s     60s     80s
            [#####][#####][#####][####]    finished
            steady fizzing, takes longer to finish

 Crushed tablet:
  time -->  0s      20s     40s
            [#####][#####]  finished
            vigorous fizzing, finishes much sooner
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Whole tablet vs crushed tablet (illustrative timeline; actual times depend on the tablet). Both glasses contain the same volume of water at the same temperature. Because the crushed tablet has a much larger exposed surface area, it reacts and finishes much faster. It is the exact same chemical reaction in both glasses—only the rate differs.

Inside an effervescent tablet are dry solid citric acid and sodium hydrogencarbonate (baking soda). While dry in the tablet, the solid particles cannot move around to react; the moment water dissolves them, the ions meet and fizz out CO₂ gas!

## Part G: Everyday reasoning with reaction rates

**Why do we chop vegetables into small pieces before cooking?**
Cutting vegetables into smaller pieces increases their **surface area**, allowing heat and boiling water to reach the inside of every piece much faster so the food cooks quickly.

**Why do we keep milk and leftovers in the refrigerator?**
Spoilage reactions driven by bacteria slow down at low temperatures. Inside a cold fridge, those chemical reactions run **much more slowly**, keeping milk fresh much longer than on a warm kitchen counter.

**Why does bread dough rise faster in a warm kitchen than in a cold room?**
Yeast contains enzymes (biological catalysts) that break down sugars in the flour to release CO₂ gas bubbles, making the dough rise. At a warm room temperature, those enzyme-catalysed reactions run faster than in the cold.

**Why does food cook faster in a pressure cooker?**
Inside a sealed pressure cooker, water boils at a temperature higher than 100 °C, and that higher temperature speeds up the chemical reactions of cooking.

## Part H: Solved examples

### Example 1. Exothermic or endothermic?

Sort the following into two groups: (a) burning natural gas, (b) photosynthesis, (c) heating CaCO₃ in a kiln, (d) an air-activated hand warmer, (e) cellular respiration, (f) CaO + H₂O.

- Release heat: (a), (d), (e), (f).
- Absorb energy (heat or light): (b), (c).

**Answer:** Exothermic: a, d, e, f. Endothermic: b, c.

Check: Photosynthesis requires sunlight, and decomposing CaCO₃ requires continuous kiln heat—both absorb energy ✅.

### Example 2. Reading an energy-level sketch

In a certain reaction, the energy level of the products is **lower** than the energy level of the reactants. Is the reaction exothermic or endothermic, and what happens to the temperature of the surroundings?

**Answer:** Exothermic. Because stored chemical energy stepped down, the difference was released as heat, warming the surroundings.

Check: Matches the top sketch in Figure 4.1 ✅.

### Example 3. Atom count: the hand-warmer equation

Is 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ balanced?

- Left: Fe = 4, O = 3 × 2 = 6.
- Right: Fe = 2 × 2 = 4, O = 2 × 3 = 6.

**Answer:** Yes, it is balanced.

Check: Fe = 4 and O = 6 on both sides ✅.

### Example 4. Is the cellular respiration equation balanced?

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O

- Left: C = 6, H = 12, O = 6 + 12 = 18.
- Right: C = 6, H = 6 × 2 = 12, O = 6 × 2 + 6 = 18.

**Answer:** Balanced.

Check: C 6 = 6, H 12 = 12, O 18 = 18 ✅.

### Example 5. Photosynthesis as the reverse of respiration

6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂

- Left: C = 6, H = 12, O = 12 + 6 = 18.
- Right: C = 6, H = 12, O = 6 + 12 = 18.

**Answer:** Balanced, and the exact reverse of cellular respiration: respiration releases energy (exothermic), while photosynthesis absorbs sunlight energy (endothermic).

Check: Reactants and products are swapped between the two equations ✅.

### Example 6. Whole tablet vs crushed tablet speed ratio

Suppose a whole effervescent tablet takes 90 seconds to finish fizzing, while an identical crushed tablet in an equal volume of water takes 30 seconds. How many times faster did the crushed tablet react?

- 90 ÷ 30 = 3.

**Answer:** 3 times faster (taking one-third of the time means three times the average rate).

Check: 30 × 3 = 90 ✅.

### Example 7. Cold water vs warm water speed ratio

Suppose a whole tablet takes 80 seconds in cold water and 20 seconds in warm water. How many times faster is the reaction in warm water?

- 80 ÷ 20 = 4.

**Answer:** 4 times faster in warm water.

Check: 20 × 4 = 80 ✅ (illustrative numbers; actual times depend on the tablet and water temperatures).

### Example 8. What happens to a catalyst?

Adding a pinch of yeast to H₂O₂ causes rapid bubbling of oxygen gas. What happens to the yeast itself at the end of the reaction?

**Answer:** The yeast (whose enzyme acts as a catalyst) is not used up; it remains unchanged at the end. It only sped up the reaction.

Check: Yeast does not appear as a reactant or product in 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂ ✅.

### Example 9. Verify the H₂O₂ decomposition equation

2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂

- Left: H = 4, O = 4.
- Right: H = 4, O = 2 + 2 = 4.

**Answer:** Balanced.

Check: H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4 ✅.

### Example 10. Lump vs powder

A single 5 g marble chip (CaCO₃) and 5 g of powdered CaCO₃ are dropped into identical beakers of dilute acid at the same temperature. Which reacts faster, and why?

**Answer:** The powder reacts much faster because it has a far larger exposed surface area in contact with the acid.

Check: Smaller particle size → larger surface area → faster reaction ✅.

### Example 11. Why must a lime kiln be heated continuously?

Why do we have to keep burning fuel in a lime kiln to keep CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ going?

- Thermal decomposition of CaCO₃ is an endothermic reaction: breaking the bonds takes more energy than forming the products gives back.
- If the heat supply stops, the reaction stops.

**Answer:** Because the reaction continuously **absorbs** heat energy.

Check: Limestone sits unchanged at room temperature for millions of years without decomposing ✅.

### Example 12. Cold pack vs chemical reaction

Is the cooling of an ammonium nitrate instant cold pack a chemical reaction?

**Answer:** No. Dissolving NH₄NO₃ in water is a physical dissolving process because no new chemical substance is formed. However, it absorbs heat from the water in the same endothermic way.

Check: In NH₄NO₃(s) → NH₄⁺(aq) + NO₃⁻(aq), existing ions simply separate in water ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "An exothermic reaction always starts on its own without any push"**

No. Most exothermic reactions (like burning natural gas or wood) need an initial push—such as a spark or match—to start breaking the first bonds. Once started, the heat released keeps the reaction going.

**Mistake 2: "Endothermic always means the beaker feels cold"**

When an endothermic reaction pulls heat from the surrounding liquid (like baking soda in lemon juice), the cup feels cool. But many endothermic reactions (like decomposing CaCO₃ in a 900 °C kiln) only happen while we **supply strong heat**, so nothing feels cold! "Endothermic" simply means the reaction absorbs energy.

**Mistake 3: "A catalyst gets used up during the reaction"**

No. A catalyst speeds up the reaction without being consumed; it is still present at the end.

**Mistake 4: "An instant cold pack works by a chemical reaction"**

Dissolving ammonium nitrate in water is a physical change (dissolving), not a chemical reaction, though both absorb heat endothermically.

**Mistake 5: "Since higher temperature speeds up reactions, we should use boiling water for the tablet activity"**

Never use boiling water in a student activity—it is a scalding hazard. Only use comfortably warm tap water poured by an adult, and always in an open glass.

**Mistake 6: "A faster reaction always releases more total heat"**

No. **How fast** a reaction goes (rate) and **how much total energy** it releases are two different things. Rusting 1 g of iron slowly over a month and oxidising 1 g of iron powder over a few hours in a hand warmer release the same total amount of heat for the same reaction—just spread over different times.

## Quick Recap

- In every reaction, breaking old bonds requires energy input and making new bonds releases energy back. Whichever is larger decides whether the reaction is **exothermic** or **endothermic**.
- **Exothermic:** releases heat to the surroundings (burning fuels, cellular respiration, slaking of lime CaO + H₂O, neutralisation, iron oxidising in a hand warmer, composting). On an energy-level sketch, products sit lower than reactants.
- **Endothermic:** absorbs energy from outside (photosynthesis, heating CaCO₃, baking soda + citric acid, electrolysis of water). On an energy-level sketch, products sit higher than reactants.
- Dissolving NH₄NO₃ in an instant cold pack is physical dissolving, not a chemical reaction, though it absorbs heat endothermically.
- Most reactions need an initial **push** (like a spark or match) to get started.
- **Hand warmer:** fine iron powder + oxygen from the air → rapid rusting-like oxidation that releases steady heat (4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃). Single-use; do not press directly against bare skin for long periods.
- **Reaction rate increases with:** higher temperature, higher concentration, smaller particle size (larger surface area), and adding a catalyst.
- A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction without being used up itself (MnO₂, yeast, or raw potato for H₂O₂). **Enzymes** are biological catalysts in living cells.
- In the effervescent tablet fair tests, an adult supervises, nothing is tasted, containers stay open, and water volumes are kept equal.

### Answers

1. **Exothermic:** a reaction that releases heat to the surroundings; examples: burning fuels (CH₄ + 2O₂), cellular respiration, slaking of lime (CaO + H₂O), an air-activated hand warmer. **Endothermic:** a reaction that absorbs heat (or light/electrical energy) from the surroundings; examples: photosynthesis, thermal decomposition of CaCO₃, electrolysis of water, baking soda with lemon juice.

2. Breaking old bonds in the reactants requires energy input, while forming new bonds in the products releases energy back. If forming the new bonds releases more energy than breaking the old bonds took, the extra energy comes out as heat (exothermic). If breaking the old bonds takes more energy than the new bonds give back, energy must be absorbed from outside (endothermic).

3. **Exothermic:** reactants sit at a **higher** energy level and products sit **lower**; the difference is released as heat. **Endothermic:** reactants sit **lower** and products sit **higher**; the difference is absorbed from outside (Figure 4.1).

4. While the wrapper is sealed, no oxygen can reach the fine iron powder inside, so no reaction occurs. Tearing open the wrapper lets oxygen from the air enter and react rapidly with the **iron powder** to release heat. Simplified equation: **4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃**. It is called simplified because actual rust also contains water molecules bonded in the solid.

5. When ammonium nitrate dissolves in water, no new substance is formed—its ions simply separate in water, making it a physical dissolving process rather than a chemical reaction. However, separating those ions absorbs heat from the water and cools the pack down, just like an endothermic reaction.

6. Most reactions need an initial push of energy to start breaking the first bonds in the reactants. The matchstick or spark provides that starting push; once burning starts, the heat released by the exothermic reaction keeps neighbouring gas molecules reacting.

7. (i) Increase temperature: an effervescent tablet fizzes faster in warm water. (ii) Increase concentration: a more concentrated solution reacts faster. (iii) Smaller particle size (larger surface area): finely chopped vegetables cook faster than large chunks. (iv) Add a catalyst: yeast or raw potato makes H₂O₂ release oxygen gas rapidly.

8. The crushed tablet finishes faster because crushing exposes a much larger total surface area to the water at once. The volume (and temperature) of water must be kept the same in both glasses so that surface area is the only variable being changed (a fair test).

9. Safety rules: (i) an adult supervises, (ii) never taste or drink the liquid, (iii) use equal volumes of water in open glasses, (iv) have an adult pour warm (never boiling) water, and (v) wash hands afterwards. A closed bottle must never be used because the released CO₂ gas builds up pressure and can burst the container.

10. 150 ÷ 30 = 5, so the crushed tablet reacts **5 times** faster. Check: 30 × 5 = 150 ✅.

11. A catalyst is a substance that speeds up a reaction without being consumed itself. For 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, manganese dioxide (MnO₂), baker's yeast, or raw potato can act as catalysts. The catalyst is not used up—it remains unchanged after the reaction finishes.

12. (a) Cutting vegetables into small pieces increases their surface area so heat and hot water reach the interior faster. (b) The cold temperature inside a refrigerator slows down the chemical reactions that sour milk. (c) In a warm room, the enzymes in yeast work faster and produce CO₂ bubbles more quickly, making the dough rise faster.

13. Left: C = 6, H = 12, O = 6 + 12 = 18. Right: C = 6, H = 12, O = 12 + 6 = 18. Equal on both sides, so balanced ✅. Cellular respiration is **exothermic**—it releases energy that warms our body and powers our cells.

14. (a) **False** ❌. A catalyst is not used up; it remains unchanged at the end of the reaction. (b) **True** ✅. Photosynthesis absorbs light energy from the sun. (c) **False** ❌. Most exothermic reactions still need an initial push (such as a spark) to get started. (d) **False** ❌. Air-activated iron hand warmers are single-use; once the iron powder has oxidised, they cannot be reheated to reverse the reaction.
