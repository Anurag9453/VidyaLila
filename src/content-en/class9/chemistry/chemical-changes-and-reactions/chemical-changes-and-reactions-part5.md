# Topic 5: Oxidation and Reduction

In the previous topic, we saw that chemical reactions either release or absorb **heat**, and that some run **fast** while others run **slowly**. Inside the hand-warmer pouch, iron powder was combining with oxygen from the air. Now think about how often **oxygen** shows up across chemistry—in burning, rusting, and cellular respiration—and how **hydrogen** is also gained or lost in many reactions. Is there a single **unifying story** connecting all of them? Yes: in these reactions, one substance **gains** oxygen while another substance **gives up** oxygen. Today we learn the chemical names for this give-and-take: **oxidation** and **reduction**.

---

## The story of the sliced apple

In science class, Ms. Carter placed two small plates on the demonstration table in front of her student Maya. Each plate held a freshly cut slice of apple. Onto the first slice, Ms. Carter brushed a few drops of **fresh lemon juice**; she left the second slice plain. "Let's check both slices in ten minutes," she said.

Ten minutes later, the plain apple slice had turned **rusty brown**, while the slice brushed with lemon juice was still **crisp and pale white**.

Maya asked, "Ms. Carter, what happened to the plain slice? Why did it only turn brown on the cut surface?"

Ms. Carter explained, "Cutting the apple exposed the inner fruit cells to **oxygen in the air** for the first time. Certain compounds inside the apple react slowly with oxygen to form brown-coloured substances. While the peel was intact, oxygen couldn't reach them."

"And how did the lemon juice stop it?" Maya asked.

"Lemon juice is rich in **vitamin C**," Ms. Carter smiled. "Vitamin C **steps in and reacts with the oxygen first**, before the apple's own compounds get oxidised—like a bodyguard taking the hit. As long as vitamin C is there on the surface, the apple slice stays pale and fresh."

This everyday kitchen change is a classic example of **oxidation**: **a substance gaining oxygen**. **Activity (at home with an adult, who handles the knife):** place two fresh apple slices on a plate, brush one with lemon juice and leave the other plain, and compare their colour after ten minutes.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxidation** | Gain of oxygen by a substance, or loss of hydrogen from a substance | Mg turning into MgO |
| **Reduction** | Loss of oxygen from a substance, or gain of hydrogen by a substance | CuO turning into Cu |
| **Redox reaction** | A reaction in which oxidation and reduction take place **simultaneously** | CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O |
| **Oxidising agent** | A substance that oxidises another substance (by giving it oxygen or removing its hydrogen) | CuO |
| **Reducing agent** | A substance that reduces another substance (by removing its oxygen or giving it hydrogen) | H₂ |
| **Oxide** | A compound of an element combined with oxygen | CuO, MgO, Fe₂O₃ |

## Part A: Two clear definitions (in terms of oxygen and hydrogen)

At this level, we use **two straightforward definitions** based on tracking oxygen and hydrogen atoms:

| | In terms of oxygen | In terms of hydrogen |
|---|---|---|
| **Oxidation** | **Gain** of oxygen | **Loss** of hydrogen |
| **Reduction** | **Loss** of oxygen | **Gain** of hydrogen |

A simple memory aid: **"Gaining Oxygen = Oxidation. Losing Oxygen = Reduction. For Hydrogen, it's the exact opposite."**

```
   OXYGEN
            +O                          -O
   substance ---> oxide          oxide ---> substance
   (oxidation)                   (reduction)

   HYDROGEN
            -H                            +H
   substance ---> (less H)       substance ---> (more H)
   (oxidation)                   (reduction)
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Summary map of oxygen and hydrogen transfer. Gaining oxygen and losing hydrogen are **both oxidation**; losing oxygen and gaining hydrogen are **both reduction**.

---

## Part B: One reaction, two roles

Let us look at a clean, classic laboratory reaction: passing **hydrogen gas** over heated **black copper(II) oxide (CuO)**. The black powder turns into reddish-brown metallic copper, and droplets of water condense on the cooler part of the tube. (**For observation only—teacher demonstration.** Because hydrogen gas is flammable, students do not perform this experiment.)

CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O

First check that the equation is balanced (as in Chapter 3: 1 Cu, 1 O, and 2 H on both sides ✅). Now trace **who loses oxygen and who gains it**:

- **CuO** **loses its oxygen** (turning from CuO into Cu). Loss of oxygen = **reduction**. We say CuO is **reduced**.
- **H₂** **gains oxygen** (turning from H₂ into H₂O). Gain of oxygen = **oxidation**. We say H₂ is **oxidised**.

Both changes happen **in the very same reaction, at the exact same moment**. The oxygen atom that CuO let go of is the exact oxygen atom that H₂ picked up! Oxygen did not vanish into thin air—it simply **changed hands**, like one player passing a basketball to a teammate.

```
          oxygen passes from CuO to H₂O
       +-----------------------------------+
       |                                   v
      CuO   +    H₂    --->    Cu    +    H₂O
      ----                     --         ---
      REDUCED                (formed)   (formed)
      (lost oxygen)

                   H₂ : gained oxygen  --->  OXIDISED
                   CuO: lost oxygen    --->  REDUCED

      Oxidising agent : CuO  (gave oxygen to H₂)
      Reducing agent  : H₂   (took oxygen from CuO)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. Oxygen is transferred from CuO to H₂. CuO is reduced (and acts as the oxidising agent), while H₂ is oxidised (and acts as the reducing agent). Both roles always appear together.

**A fundamental rule:** Oxidation and reduction **never happen alone**. If one substance gains oxygen, some other substance must be supplying it. That is why chemists combine the two words (**red**uction + **ox**idation) and call the whole process a **redox reaction**.

## Part C: Oxidising agent and reducing agent

Recall the sliced apple story: vitamin C took up the oxygen itself so the apple compounds wouldn't be oxidised. In chemistry, a substance that causes another substance to be reduced (or protects it by taking the oxygen itself) acts as a **reducing agent**.

Here is the direct rule:

- The **oxidising agent** is the substance that **oxidises the other substance** (by giving it oxygen or taking away its hydrogen). In doing so, the oxidising agent **gets reduced itself**.
- The **reducing agent** is the substance that **reduces the other substance** (by taking away its oxygen or giving it hydrogen). In doing so, the reducing agent **gets oxidised itself**.

In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O: CuO is the oxidising agent (it gave oxygen to H₂), and H₂ is the reducing agent (it took oxygen away from CuO).

Notice the swap that sounds backwards at first but makes total sense: **the substance that gets oxidised is the reducing agent**, and **the substance that gets reduced is the oxidising agent**. Just remember that an "agent" is named for what it **does to the other reactant** (like a travel agent who books a trip for *you*, not for themselves).

## Part D: Tracing 12 redox equations step by step

Every equation in the table below is balanced (equal atom counts on both sides). Let us identify what is oxidised and what is reduced using our oxygen and hydrogen rules:

| No. | Equation | Oxidised (gained O or lost H) | Reduced (lost O or gained H) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O | H₂ (gained O) | CuO (lost O) |
| 2 | 2Cu + O₂ → 2CuO | Cu (gained O) | Not visible by O/H rule alone; see note below |
| 3 | 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO | Mg (gained O) | Not visible by O/H rule alone; see note below |
| 4 | ZnO + C → Zn + CO | C (gained O) | ZnO (lost O) |
| 5 | Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂ | CO (gained O) | Fe₂O₃ (lost O) |
| 6 | CuO + CO → Cu + CO₂ | CO (gained O) | CuO (lost O) |
| 7 | 2H₂S + O₂ → 2S + 2H₂O | H₂S (lost H) | O₂ (gained H) |
| 8 | CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O | CH₄ (gained O and lost H) | O₂ (gained H) |
| 9 | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O | H₂ (gained O) | O₂ (gained H) |
| 10 | C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O | C₆H₁₂O₆ (gained O, lost H) | O₂ (gained H) |
| 11 | 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ | Fe (gained O) | Not visible by O/H rule alone; see note below |
| 12 | Fe₂O₃ + 2Al → Al₂O₃ + 2Fe | Al (gained O) | Fe₂O₃ (lost O) |

**Note on equations 2, 3, and 11:** In these combination reactions between a metal and oxygen, the metal clearly gains oxygen (so it is oxidised), and O₂ is the **oxidising agent** (since it supplies the oxygen). However, neither oxygen loss nor hydrogen transfer appears in the equation, so the oxygen–hydrogen definition alone cannot show why O₂ is reduced. In Part G, we will see how the electron definition makes O₂'s reduction crystal clear.

Let us walk through five of these examples in detail.

### Example 1. 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

When magnesium ribbon burns in air, it produces a dazzling white light (teacher demonstration only; never look directly at the flame) and leaves behind white MgO powder.

- Mg gains oxygen, so **Mg is oxidised** (and acts as the reducing agent).
- O₂ supplies the oxygen, so **O₂ is the oxidising agent**.
- Why O₂ is itself reduced will become clear in the electron preview in Part G.

Atom count: Mg 2 = 2, O 2 = 2 ✅.

### Example 2. ZnO + C → Zn + CO

When white zinc oxide powder is heated strongly with carbon (coke) in an industrial furnace, metallic zinc is extracted (for reading only).

- ZnO **loses** oxygen (forming Zn): **reduction**; ZnO is reduced and is the oxidising agent.
- C **gains** oxygen (forming CO): **oxidation**; C is oxidised and is the reducing agent.

Atom count: Zn 1 = 1, O 1 = 1, C 1 = 1 ✅.

### Example 3. Fe₂O₃ + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO₂

Iron ore contains iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃). Inside a towering blast furnace, carbon monoxide gas strips the oxygen away from Fe₂O₃ to leave molten iron metal (industrial process, for reading only).

- Fe₂O₃ loses oxygen: **reduced** (oxidising agent).
- CO gains oxygen (forming CO₂): **oxidised** (reducing agent).

Atom count: Fe 2 = 2, C 3 = 3, O: 3 + 3 = 6 on the left and 3 × 2 = 6 on the right ✅.

### Example 4. 2H₂S + O₂ → 2S + 2H₂O

This example showcases the **hydrogen definition**. Hydrogen sulphide (H₂S) is a toxic gas with the smell of rotten eggs. When it reacts with a limited supply of oxygen, yellow sulphur and water are formed (on paper only; H₂S is hazardous).

- H₂S **loses hydrogen** (turning into S): **oxidation**. H₂S is the reducing agent.
- O₂ **gains hydrogen** (turning into H₂O): **reduction**. O₂ is the oxidising agent.

Atom count: H 4 = 4, S 2 = 2, O 2 = 2 ✅.

### Example 5. CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

When methane burns on a gas stove (which we will study further in Topic 6):

- CH₄ gains oxygen (forming CO₂) and loses hydrogen: **CH₄ is oxidised** (reducing agent).
- O₂ gains hydrogen (forming H₂O): **O₂ is reduced** (oxidising agent).

Atom count: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O: 2 × 2 = 4 on the left and 2 (in CO₂) + 2 (in 2H₂O) = 4 on the right ✅.

## Part E: Oxygen "changing hands"

```
   Before (reactants)                    After (products)

   CuO      H₂                           Cu       H₂O
   [Cu][O]  [H][H]    ------>            [Cu]    [H][O][H]

   O was partnered with Cu               O is now partnered with H
   (Cu lost O: reduction)                (H₂ gained O: oxidation)
```

> **Figure 5.3** – In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, the oxygen atom switches partners from Cu to H₂. CuO loses oxygen (reduction) at the exact moment H₂ gains oxygen (oxidation).

## Part F: Everyday redox stories

1. **Burning (combustion):** Wood, candle wax, and stove gas all burn by reacting with oxygen in the air. The fuel gains oxygen, so the fuel is **oxidised** (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O). We will explore combustion fully in Topic 6.
2. **Rusting of iron:** Exposed to air and moisture, iron slowly gains oxygen and **oxidises** into reddish-brown rust (simplified as 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃, which also ran fast inside Sam's hand warmer in Topic 4).
3. **Cellular respiration:** Inside our cells, glucose reacts with oxygen: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy. Glucose is **oxidised** in a controlled, slow process that powers life.
4. **Browning of cut fruit:** As we saw with Maya's apple slice, exposed fruit compounds oxidise in air, while antioxidants like vitamin C act as reducing agents that take up the oxygen first.
5. **Bleaching stains:** Household laundry bleach works by **oxidising** coloured stain molecules into colourless ones. Bleach is a strong **oxidising agent**.

> **Important household safety rule:** **Never mix household bleach with any other cleaning product.** Mixing bleach with toilet bowl cleaners, vinegar/acids, or ammonia releases deadly toxic gases. Bleach must only ever be used by an adult, diluted strictly according to the label, in a well-ventilated room, and never mixed with anything else.

## Part G: Looking ahead to higher grades (the electron view)

Our oxygen-and-hydrogen definitions work wonderfully for most reactions in this chapter, but as we noticed in equations 2, 3, and 11 (such as 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO), they don't show the full picture when no oxygen is lost and no hydrogen is present. **In higher grades**, you will combine Chapter 5 (Structure of the Atom) and Chapter 6 (Chemical Bonding) to learn a deeper definition: **oxidation is the loss of electrons, and reduction is the gain of electrons.** When magnesium burns, each Mg atom loses 2 electrons to become Mg²⁺ (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻, so Mg is oxidised), while each oxygen atom gains 2 electrons to become O²⁻ (O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻, so O₂ is reduced!). That electron view also explains displacement reactions like Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu as redox reactions, which you will explore in later classes.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Thinking oxidation and reduction can happen separately**

They **always happen together**. If one substance gains oxygen, another must lose it (or supply it). That is why the combined process is called **redox**.

**Mistake 2: Thinking "reduction" means "the total mass gets smaller"**

In chemistry, reduction specifically means **loss of oxygen** (or gain of hydrogen / gain of electrons). When CuO turns into Cu, the oxygen is removed from the oxide—that is why chemists originally named it "reduction" (bringing a metal ore back to the pure metal), not because matter disappears.

**Mistake 3: Mixing up the substance oxidised/reduced with the agent**

The substance that **gets oxidised** is the **reducing agent**. The substance that **gets reduced** is the **oxidising agent**. An agent is named for what it does to its reaction partner.

**Mistake 4: Assuming every reaction containing oxygen atoms is a redox reaction**

In CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ or CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl, oxygen atoms are already bonded inside the reactants and simply stay with their groups—no element gains or loses oxygen in the redox sense. Always check whether a substance actually gained/lost oxygen or hydrogen.

**Mistake 5: Mixing bleach with other household cleaners**

Never do this—review the safety box in Part F.

## Quick Recap

- **Oxidation** = gain of oxygen or loss of hydrogen. **Reduction** = loss of oxygen or gain of hydrogen.
- Both always happen **simultaneously** in a **redox reaction**.
- In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O: CuO is **reduced** (and is the oxidising agent); H₂ is **oxidised** (and is the reducing agent).
- An **oxidising agent** oxidises another substance and gets reduced itself; a **reducing agent** reduces another substance and gets oxidised itself.
- Everyday redox examples: burning fuels, rusting of iron, cellular respiration, browning of cut apples, and bleaching stains.
- **Preview for higher grades:** oxidation = loss of electrons (Mg → Mg²⁺ + 2e⁻), reduction = gain of electrons (O + 2e⁻ → O²⁻).

### Answers

1. **Oxidation:** gain of oxygen, or loss of hydrogen. **Reduction:** loss of oxygen, or gain of hydrogen.

2. **H₂** is oxidised (it gains oxygen to form H₂O). **CuO** is reduced (it loses oxygen to form Cu). **CuO is the oxidising agent** (it gives oxygen to H₂). **H₂ is the reducing agent** (it takes oxygen away from CuO).

3. (a) **C** is oxidised (gains oxygen to form CO); **CuO** is reduced (loses oxygen to form Cu). Atom count: Cu 1 = 1, O 1 = 1, C 1 = 1 ✅. (b) **Al** is oxidised (gains oxygen to form Al₂O₃); **Cr₂O₃** is reduced (loses oxygen to form Cr). Atom count: Cr 2 = 2, O 3 = 3, Al 2 = 2 ✅. (c) **H₂S** is oxidised (loses hydrogen to form S); **SO₂** is reduced (loses oxygen to form S). Atom count: H 4 = 4, S 2 + 1 = 3, O 2 = 2 ✅. Notice the neat twist: both reactants end up forming elemental sulphur!

4. Oxygen atoms are never created or destroyed in a reaction—they simply transfer from one reactant to another. Whichever substance receives the oxygen is oxidised, and whichever substance gives it up is reduced. Because both happen together, the reaction is a **redox** reaction.

5. **Mg** is oxidised (it gains oxygen). **O₂** is the oxidising agent (it supplies the oxygen). Because no oxygen is lost from another compound and no hydrogen is involved, the oxygen–hydrogen definition alone cannot show the reduction step; the electron view (in higher grades) shows that O₂ is reduced because it gains electrons from Mg.

6. Left: Fe = 4, O = 3 × 2 = 6. Right: Fe = 2 × 2 = 4, O = 2 × 3 = 6 ✅. **Fe** is oxidised (it gains oxygen).

7. Compounds on the cut surface of the apple react with oxygen in the air and turn brown (oxidation). Lemon juice contains vitamin C, which reacts with the oxygen first so the apple's own compounds are spared. Vitamin C acts as a **reducing agent** because it gets oxidised itself in order to protect the apple slice.

8. **Fast:** burning natural gas or LPG on a stove. **Slow:** rusting of an iron gate, or the browning of a sliced apple.

9. (a) **False** ❌. Reduction means **losing** oxygen (or gaining hydrogen); gaining oxygen is oxidation. (b) **True** ✅. H₂ takes oxygen from CuO. (c) **False** ❌. Oxidation and reduction always occur together. (d) **True** ✅. CH₄ gains oxygen (forming CO₂) and loses hydrogen.

10. Mixing bleach with other cleaners (such as toilet cleaners, acids/vinegar, or ammonia) can release dangerous, toxic gases. Bleach should only be used alone, diluted as directed, in a well-ventilated area, by an adult.

11. Using the electron view, Mg is **oxidised** because it **lost 2 electrons** (oxidation = loss of electrons).
