# Topic 6: Everyday Chemistry: Combustion, Rusting, and Rancidity + Chapter Summary

In the previous topic, we learned that **oxidation** means gaining oxygen (or losing hydrogen), **reduction** means losing oxygen (or gaining hydrogen), and both always happen together. Even the browning of a sliced apple was a small everyday oxidation reaction. Today we see how this same chemistry shapes our daily lives: burning fuel on a stove, iron rusting in damp air, oils and snacks turning stale, and our own cells breathing to keep us alive. At the end, we will bring the entire chapter together in a single summary.

---

## The puffed-up bag of potato chips

Daniel picked up a sealed bag of potato chips at the grocery store and held it up to his uncle Mr. Parker. The bag was puffed up like a pillow. "Uncle, this bag is half air and only half chips! Are they short-changing us?"

Mr. Parker laughed. "That isn't ordinary air inside the bag, Daniel. It's pure **nitrogen gas**. And it has a very important job: keeping those crispy chips from going bad."

When they got home, Daniel opened an old tin of roasted nuts and fried crisps that had been sitting at the back of the pantry with a loose lid. A sharp, stale, unpleasant smell wafted out. "Ugh! These smell awful." Mr. Parker nodded: "And right there is today's chemistry question: why do potato chips stay fresh for months inside a nitrogen-flushed bag, while snacks in a loosely closed tin turn stale and smelly? Let's look at three big oxidation reactions in everyday life: combustion, rusting, and rancidity."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Combustion (burning)** | A reaction in which a fuel burns in oxygen, releasing heat and light | A gas stove flame, burning wood |
| **Fuel** | A substance that is burned to provide heat or power | Natural gas, LPG, wood, coal, petrol |
| **Fire triangle** | The three things needed for a fire: fuel, oxygen, and heat | Remove any one of the three and the fire goes out |
| **Complete combustion** | Burning in plenty of oxygen so that all the carbon in the fuel turns into CO₂ | A clean blue stove flame |
| **Incomplete combustion** | Burning in a limited supply of oxygen, producing toxic carbon monoxide (CO) gas and soot | A charcoal brazier in a closed room |
| **Rusting** | The slow conversion of iron into flaky, reddish-brown hydrated iron(III) oxide in the presence of air and moisture | A rusty bicycle chain |
| **Corrosion** | The gradual deterioration of a metal surface by reaction with air, moisture, or other gases | Green patina on copper, black tarnish on silver |
| **Rancidity** | The spoilage of fats and oils by slow oxidation in air, producing an unpleasant smell and taste | Stale fried snacks or old cooking oil |
| **Antioxidant** | A substance added to food to slow down oxidation | Vitamin E or food-grade preservatives |

## Part A: Combustion (burning)

**Combustion** is a chemical reaction in which a **fuel** reacts rapidly with **oxygen**, giving off **heat and light**. It is both an exothermic reaction (Topic 4) and an oxidation reaction (Topic 5), because the fuel combines with oxygen.

The simplest example of a gaseous fuel burning on a kitchen stove is methane (the main component of natural gas):

CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O  (with heat and light)

Bottled LPG is a **mixture** of hydrocarbon gases (mainly propane and butane); you do not need to memorise their formulas. Simply remember the big pattern: when a hydrocarbon fuel burns in plenty of air, its carbon and hydrogen combine with oxygen to form **carbon dioxide (CO₂)** and **water vapour (H₂O)**. Atom count: left has 1 C, 4 H, 4 O; right has 1 C, 4 H, and 2 + 2 = 4 O ✅.

Solid carbon (such as charcoal or coal) also burns by combining directly with oxygen: C + O₂ → CO₂.

### The three requirements for fire: the fire triangle

For a fire to start and keep burning, **all three** corners of the **fire triangle** must be present: **fuel**, **oxygen**, and **heat** (enough heat to bring the fuel to its ignition temperature). If you remove **even one** of the three, the fire goes out immediately.

```
                      HEAT
                       /\
                      /  \
                     /    \
                    / FIRE \
                   /        \
                  /__________\
               FUEL          OXYGEN

    Three ways to put out a fire: remove any one corner
      remove fuel   : turn off the gas valve; clear away dry brush
      remove oxygen : smother with a fire blanket, lid, or CO₂ extinguisher
      remove heat   : spray water on burning wood or paper to cool it below ignition
```

> **Figure 6.1** – The fire triangle: a fire needs fuel, oxygen, and heat all at once. Removing any one of the three extinguishes the fire.

Let us match everyday fire-safety actions to the fire triangle:

- **Turning off the knob on a gas stove:** cuts off the supply of fuel, so the flame dies instantly. (Fuel removed.)
- **Sliding a metal lid or damp fire blanket over a pan of burning cooking oil:** blocks air from reaching the oil. (Oxygen removed.) **Never pour water onto burning oil**: water sinks beneath hot oil, flashes into steam explosively, and sprays burning oil droplets into a dangerous fireball.
- **Pouring water onto a wood or paper campfire:** cools the burning wood below the temperature needed to sustain combustion. (Heat removed.)
- **Using a CO₂ fire extinguisher:** carbon dioxide is denser than air, so it settles over the fire like an invisible blanket and pushes oxygen away. (Oxygen removed.)

Students should **never experiment with fire**; all combustion demonstrations are conducted only by teachers or adults.

### Complete vs incomplete combustion: the danger of carbon monoxide

When there is **plenty of oxygen**, carbon burns completely to form carbon dioxide (CO₂). When **oxygen is limited** (for example, inside a poorly ventilated room or a clogged burner), carbon does not burn completely and instead forms **carbon monoxide (CO)**:

2C + O₂ → 2CO  (incomplete combustion)

For methane burning in limited oxygen: 2CH₄ + 3O₂ → 2CO + 4H₂O. Atom count: left has 2 C, 8 H, 6 O; right has 2 C, 8 H, and 2 + 4 = 6 O ✅.

**Carbon monoxide (CO) is a deadly poisonous gas.** Worse still, it is **colourless and completely odourless**, so you cannot see it or smell it building up in a room. When inhaled, CO binds tightly to haemoglobin in the blood in place of oxygen, starving the brain and body of oxygen and causing dizziness, headache, unconsciousness, and death. That is why every household should follow these life-saving precautions:

- **Never burn a charcoal grill, coal brazier, or portable camp stove inside a closed room, tent, or garage.** Every winter, tragic poisonings happen when people bring glowing coals indoors for warmth with the windows shut.
- **Ensure proper ventilation for gas water heaters and furnaces:** gas water heaters must be properly vented to the outside and used only where fresh air can circulate freely.
- **Check the colour of your kitchen stove flame:** a healthy gas flame burns **clean blue** (complete combustion). A **yellow, smoky, sooty flame** means incomplete combustion is happening—have an adult get the burner ports cleaned and checked.
- If anyone ever shows signs of CO poisoning, **move them into fresh outdoor air immediately** and call emergency medical services.

(Notice too that carbon monoxide can burn further to CO₂ if given more oxygen: 2CO + O₂ → 2CO₂ ✅, with 2 C and 4 O on both sides.)

> **Quick check:** A gas stove burner is burning with a yellow flame and leaving black soot on the bottom of pots. Is this complete combustion? ❌ No—it is incomplete combustion caused by insufficient air mixing with the gas, which wastes fuel and can produce CO.

## Part B: Rusting of iron

When **iron (Fe)** is left exposed to both **air (oxygen)** and **moisture (water)**, its surface slowly turns into a **flaky, reddish-brown coating** called **rust**. Rust is not metallic iron—it is a brittle new compound that crumbles away instead of holding weight.

Simplified equation:

4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃

Atom count: left has 4 Fe and 6 O; right has 2 × 2 = 4 Fe and 2 × 3 = 6 O ✅.

**Honest chemistry note:** Real rust is *hydrated* iron(III) oxide (meaning water molecules are attached inside the solid crystal), so 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃ is a **simplified** equation. Even so, it clearly shows the core redox process: iron combines with oxygen, meaning iron is **oxidised** (Topic 5).

Rusting requires **both** oxygen and water at the same time. In bone-dry desert air, or submerged in boiled water sealed away from air, iron barely rusts at all. Near the sea, where the air is humid and carries salt spray, iron rusts much faster.

```
          AIR (oxygen)          WATER / MOISTURE
               \                     /
                v                   v
           ,~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~,
           |  thin film of moisture       |
           '~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~'
           ################################   <- iron (Fe)
                   ||        ||
             rust: flaky reddish-brown Fe₂O₃ forms
           ##########xxxxxx################
                    (rust flakes off, exposing fresh
                     iron underneath to air and water)
```

> **Figure 6.2** – When iron, oxygen from the air, and water meet, iron oxidises into rust (Fe₂O₃). Because rust is porous and flaky, it peels off and exposes fresh iron underneath, allowing rusting to eat deeper and deeper into the metal.

### Safe home investigation: what does rusting need? (at home with an adult)

Set up three clean glass jars, each containing one **shiny new iron nail**:

1. **Jar A:** Stand the nail half-submerged in **ordinary tap water** (exposed to both **air and water**).
2. **Jar B:** Submerge the nail completely in **water that an adult has boiled and cooled** (boiling drives out dissolved air), and pour a thin layer of cooking oil on top to keep air out (**water only, no air**).
3. **Jar C:** Place the nail in a **dry jar** (with a small packet of silica gel desiccant from a shoebox if available) and close the lid (**air only, no moisture**).

Have an adult handle the boiled water. Leave the three jars undisturbed for 4 to 7 days and compare. **What you will see:** the nail in Jar A develops obvious reddish-brown rust, while the nails in Jar B and Jar C show little or no rust. **Conclusion:** rusting requires **both** oxygen (air) and water (moisture). Wash your hands after handling the nails.

### How to prevent rusting

The core strategy for stopping rust is simple: **keep air and moisture from reaching the iron surface.**

| Method | How it works | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| **Painting** | Forms a dry barrier coat that blocks air and moisture | Iron gates, park benches, bridges |
| **Oiling or greasing** | Forms a water-repellent film on moving parts | Bicycle chains, garden tools, machine gears |
| **Galvanising (zinc coating)** | Coats iron with a thin layer of zinc; because zinc is more reactive than iron, the zinc oxidises first and protects the iron even if scratched | Galvanised roofing sheets, water pipes, buckets |
| **Tin or chromium plating** | Plates a thin, corrosion-resistant metal layer onto steel | Tin food cans, shiny chrome bicycle handlebars |
| **Alloying (stainless steel)** | Mixes iron with chromium and nickel to make an alloy that resists rusting | Kitchen sinks, cutlery, cooking pots |

> **Quick check:** If the paint on an iron garden gate chips off in a patch, will that spot start to rust? ✅ Yes, because the bare iron is once again exposed to air and rain.

## Part C: Corrosion of other metals

We use the word *rusting* specifically for iron. The gradual wearing away of any metal by reaction with air, moisture, or gases in the environment is called **corrosion**.

- **Green coating on copper:** Over time, copper roofs, statues (like the Statue of Liberty), and old copper vessels react slowly with oxygen, carbon dioxide, and moisture in the air to form a pale **green patina** (basic copper carbonate). (At home with an adult, rubbing tarnished copper with a little lemon juice and salt dissolves that basic coating and restores the bright copper shine.)
- **Black tarnish on silver:** Trace amounts of sulphur compounds in the air (such as hydrogen sulphide, H₂S) react with silver spoons and jewellery to form a thin black coating of silver sulphide (Ag₂S): 4Ag + 2H₂S + O₂ → 2Ag₂S + 2H₂O. Atom count: left has 4 Ag, 4 H, 2 S, 2 O; right has 4 Ag, 2 S, 4 H, 2 O ✅. (That is also why silver tarnishes quickly if left near eggs or onions, which contain sulphur compounds.)
- **Aluminium's self-protecting oxide layer:** Aluminium sits high on the reactivity ladder (above zinc and iron!), yet aluminium window frames, pans, and foil do not crumble away. Why? Because the instant fresh aluminium touches air, it reacts with oxygen to form an ultra-thin, tough, tightly clinging layer of **aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃)**: 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃ ✅ (4 Al and 6 O on both sides). Unlike flaky rust on iron, this Al₂O₃ film seals the metal underneath completely against further air and water attack! Here, surface oxidation actually **protects** the metal.

## Part D: Rancidity (when fats and oils turn stale)

Now let us return to Daniel's pantry tin of stale snacks. Mr. Parker pointed to the tin and said, "Fried crisps and roasted nuts contain fats and oils. When **fats and oils** stay exposed to oxygen in the air, they slowly **oxidise** into smaller compounds that have a sour, stale smell and unpleasant taste. Chemists call this spoilage **rancidity**."

Notice the connection: rancidity is yet another everyday **oxidation** reaction (Topic 5). Heat, sunlight, and moisture all speed it up.

**How we prevent or slow down rancidity:**

- **Airtight containers:** Keeping lids tightly sealed stops fresh oxygen from continuously reaching the food.
- **Refrigeration:** Storing butter, nuts, and leftovers in a cool fridge slows down the rate of oxidation (Topic 4).
- **Cool, dark storage:** Keeping cooking oils in dark bottles or closed cupboards away from sunlight and stove heat slows photolytic and thermal oxidation.
- **Adding antioxidants:** Food manufacturers add small amounts of safe **antioxidants** (such as vitamin E / tocopherols) that react with oxygen first, just like the vitamin C on Maya's apple slice.
- **Flushing packets with nitrogen gas (N₂):** Before sealing bags of potato chips or snack crisps, factories flush out the air (and its reactive O₂) and fill the bag with **nitrogen gas**. Because N₂ has a very strong triple bond (Chapter 6), it is unreactive at room temperature, so the oil in the chips does not oxidise. As a bonus, the puffed-up nitrogen cushion stops the thin chips from getting crushed during shipping!

```
    Loosely closed tin             Nitrogen-flushed chip bag
    (air keeps entering)           (oxygen replaced by nitrogen)

    +----------------+            ,-~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~-.
    |  O₂   O₂   O₂  |           (  N₂   N₂   N₂   N₂  )
    |  oily snacks   |           (   N₂  chips  N₂     )
    | oxidised by O₂ |            ( N₂   N₂   N₂   N₂ )
    +----------------+            '-~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~-'

    oxygen oxidises fats:          no oxygen, so no oxidation:
    stale, rancid smell            chips stay crisp and fresh
```

> **Figure 6.3** – In a loosely closed container, oxygen from the air oxidises the fats and oils in snacks, causing rancidity. In a sealed potato chip bag flushed with nitrogen gas (N₂), oxygen is excluded so the chips stay fresh and uncrushed.

> **Quick check:** If a bottle of cooking oil is left uncapped on a sunny windowsill all summer, will it turn rancid faster? ✅ Yes—oxygen, warmth, and sunlight all speed up the oxidation of oils.

## Part E: Cellular respiration: slow combustion inside our body

Every time we eat carbohydrates (which our body digests into glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆) and breathe in oxygen, our **cells** carry out a controlled oxidation reaction that releases the **energy** we need to live:

C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy

Atom count: left has 6 C, 12 H, and 6 + 12 = 18 O; right has 6 C, 12 H, and 12 + 6 = 18 O ✅.

Chemists often describe **cellular respiration** as **slow combustion**: the fuel (glucose) reacts with oxygen to produce the exact same products as burning—CO₂, water, and energy—but **without flames or smoke**, step by step with the help of enzymes at a gentle 37 °C. That is why our body stays warm and why we breathe out carbon dioxide and water vapour!

## Part F: Everyday reasoning examples

**Example 1: Why are potato chip bags flushed with nitrogen gas?**
Chips are fried in oil, which turns rancid if oxidised by oxygen in the air. Flushing the bag with unreactive nitrogen gas removes oxygen, preventing oxidation and cushioning the chips from breaking. ✅

**Example 2: Why should cooking oil and butter be stored in airtight containers in a cool, dark place?**
Airtight lids keep out oxygen, while cool temperatures and darkness slow down the rate of oxidation (Topic 4), keeping the fats fresh much longer.

**Example 3: Why are kitchen sinks and cutlery made of stainless steel while cast-iron skillets are seasoned with oil?**
Stainless steel is an alloy of iron with chromium and nickel that resists rusting even in constant contact with water and salt. Plain cast iron would rust if left wet, so a thin baked-on film of oil is used to keep air and moisture away from the iron.

**Example 4: How does galvanising protect an iron roof sheet even if the zinc layer gets a scratch?**
Zinc sits higher than iron on the reactivity ladder (Topic 3). Even if a scratch exposes both metals, the more reactive zinc oxidises first in place of the iron, protecting the iron from rusting.

**Example 5: A student says, "Aluminium oxide is just like rust—it destroys the aluminium pan." Is that right?**
No. Iron rust (Fe₂O₃) is flaky and peels off, exposing more iron to corrode. Aluminium oxide (Al₂O₃) forms a tough, microscopic, waterproof skin that seals the aluminium underneath and **protects** it from further corrosion.

**Example 6: Why must a gas water heater or charcoal grill never be used in an unventilated space?**
In a closed space, oxygen runs low and the fuel undergoes incomplete combustion, producing colourless, odourless, deadly carbon monoxide (CO) gas. Proper ventilation and outdoor exhausting are essential.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Fire only needs fuel and heat"**
Fire needs all **three** corners of the fire triangle: fuel, heat, **and oxygen**. Without oxygen, a fuel cannot burn.

**Mistake 2: "Pour water on a grease or cooking-oil fire"**
Never! Water sinks under burning oil and vaporises explosively, splattering flaming oil everywhere. Turn off the burner and smother the pan with a metal lid or fire blanket to cut off oxygen.

**Mistake 3: "You can smell carbon monoxide if it builds up in a room"**
No—carbon monoxide (CO) has **no smell, no taste, and no colour**. Good ventilation and CO alarms are the only protection.

**Mistake 4: "Rust is just dirty iron"**
Rust (hydrated Fe₂O₃) is a completely **new chemical compound** formed when iron oxidises; it has lost the strength and metallic properties of iron.

**Mistake 5: "Water alone causes iron to rust"**
Iron needs **both** water (moisture) **and** oxygen (air) to rust.

**Mistake 6: "Rancidity has nothing to do with chemical reactions"**
Rancidity is the chemical **oxidation** of fats and oils by oxygen in the air.

## Quick Recap

- **Combustion:** a fuel reacts with oxygen to release heat and light (CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O).
- **Fire triangle:** fuel, oxygen, and heat. Removing any one of the three puts out the fire.
- Limited oxygen causes **incomplete combustion**, producing **carbon monoxide (CO)**—a colourless, odourless, deadly gas. Never burn charcoal indoors, keep gas appliances vented, and make sure stove flames burn blue.
- **Rusting:** iron + oxygen + moisture → flaky reddish-brown rust (simplified: 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃). Prevented by painting, oiling/greasing, galvanising (zinc coating), plating, or alloying (stainless steel).
- Green patina on copper, black tarnish (Ag₂S) on silver, and the protective Al₂O₃ film on aluminium are all forms of **corrosion**.
- **Rancidity:** spoilage of fats and oils by slow oxidation in air. Prevented by airtight containers, refrigeration, cool/dark storage, antioxidants, and flushing snack bags with nitrogen gas (N₂).
- **Cellular respiration:** slow, enzyme-controlled combustion inside our cells: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy.

## Chapter Summary: Chemical Changes and Reactions

Here is our complete journey through this chapter at a glance—from what happens to chemical bonds during a reaction, through the four main reaction patterns, energy and rate, oxidation and reduction, and finally the chemistry happening around us every day:

| Topic | What we learned | In one sentence |
|---|---|---|
| Topic 1 | What a chemical reaction is | Old bonds in reactants break and new bonds form in products as atoms rearrange without any atom being created or destroyed, conserving mass. |
| Topic 2 | Combination and decomposition | In combination, two or more substances join into one product (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O); in decomposition, one reactant breaks down into two or more products by heat, electricity, or light (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂). |
| Topic 3 | Displacement and double displacement | A more reactive metal displaces a less reactive metal from its salt solution (Fe + CuSO₄ → FeSO₄ + Cu) according to the reactivity ladder; in double displacement, two compounds swap ionic partners to form a precipitate or water. |
| Topic 4 | Energy and rate of reaction | Exothermic reactions release heat while endothermic reactions absorb energy; reaction rate increases with higher temperature, higher concentration, larger surface area (smaller pieces), and catalysts (which are not used up). |
| Topic 5 | Oxidation and reduction | Oxidation is gaining oxygen (or losing hydrogen) and reduction is losing oxygen (or gaining hydrogen); both always happen together in a redox reaction driven by oxidising and reducing agents. |
| Topic 6 | Everyday chemistry | Combustion (the fire triangle and the hazard of CO), rusting and its prevention, corrosion of metals, rancidity of oils, and cellular respiration are all real-world oxidation processes. |
