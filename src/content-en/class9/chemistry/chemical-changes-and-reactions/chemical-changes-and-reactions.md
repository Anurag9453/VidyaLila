# Topic 1: What Is a Chemical Reaction: Breaking and Making Bonds

At the end of the previous chapter, we left a question open: **what happens when old bonds break and new bonds form?** In the chapter "Chemical Bonding," we saw how atoms stay joined by giving, taking, or sharing electrons. In "Language of Chemistry," we learned to write and balance chemical equations. Today we bring those two ideas together: behind the arrow of a chemical equation, there is really a game of **breaking old bonds and making new ones**.

---

## The story of a sparkler

It was New Year's Eve. Mr. Mitchell handed his daughter Clara a handheld sparkler and said, "Hold it at arm's length, and only light it while I'm right beside you." With her father watching, Clara lit the sparkler. Bright sparks flew out with a fizzing sound, a dazzling light glowed, and she could feel warmth even at her hand.

In two minutes the sparkler went out. Clara saw that the thick, coated rod had been reduced to a **thin, blackened wire**. She asked, "Dad, where did the rest of the sparkler go? Did it just vanish?"

Mr. Mitchell shook his head. "Nothing vanished, Clara. The metal powder and other ingredients in the sparkler coating **combined with oxygen in the air** and turned into **new substances**: wisps of smoke, powdery ash, and plenty of heat and light. The wire was just the support. What just happened was a **chemical reaction**."

Clara looked closely at the cooled wire. "So this wire, the ash, and those sparks are all made of the very same atoms that were here before?" "Exactly," Mr. Mitchell smiled. "The atoms are the exact same ones. They simply **changed partners**."

(A sparkler should only ever be lit outdoors with an adult, held well away from the body. We use it here purely as an **observation**—do not try to recreate its reaction yourself.)

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Chemical reaction** | A process in which starting substances (reactants) break apart and form new substances (products) as old bonds break and new bonds form | Burning of a sparkler |
| **Reactant** | A substance that starts a chemical reaction (written on the left of the arrow) | H₂ and Cl₂ |
| **Product** | A new substance formed in a chemical reaction (written on the right of the arrow) | HCl |
| **Bond breaking** | Overcoming the shared electron pair or ionic attraction holding atoms together; this requires energy input | Breaking the H–H bond |
| **Bond making** | Atoms joining with new partners; this releases energy back | Forming the H–Cl bond |
| **Precipitate** | An insoluble solid that forms when two solutions are mixed and either settles to the bottom or turns the liquid cloudy | Covered in Topic 3 |
| **Types of reactions** | Grouping reactions by their pattern so we can predict what will happen | Combination, decomposition, displacement, double displacement |

## Part A: A reaction means new partnerships between atoms

In the previous chapter, we saw that atoms in a molecule are held together by **bonds**. That raises a question: if chemical bonds hold atoms so firmly, how does anything new ever form in a reaction?

The answer is: **old bonds have to be broken, and then new bonds have to be made.** Picture a school dance. Couples are dancing on the floor. Halfway through a song, the dancers **let go of their current partner's hand** (an old bond breaks) and **take the hand of a different partner** (a new bond forms). The people in the hall are the exact same people as before—only the partnerships have changed.

In every chemical reaction, three things always happen:

1. **Some old bonds inside the reactants break.** This takes energy (just like pulling apart two stuck magnets takes a little effort).
2. **The atoms rearrange.** No new atom is created, and no atom is destroyed (conservation of mass from Chapter 4).
3. **New bonds form**, and this gives energy back (qualitatively: sometimes more energy is released than was put in, sometimes less; you will calculate exact numbers in higher grades).

> **Note:** Here we are talking about energy only qualitatively, without numbers. Which reactions release heat and which absorb heat is the focus of Topic 4.

That is why **elements never change into other elements** in a chemical reaction—only **substances** change. When we called something a "chemical change" in Chapter 2, we meant that a new substance was formed. Now we know **why** a new substance formed: the bonds between atoms changed.

## Part B: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl: counting the bonds broken and made

Let us trace one simple reaction from start to finish. Hydrogen gas and chlorine gas react to form hydrogen chloride (HCl) gas. (We examine this reaction on paper only; chlorine is a toxic gas, so even teachers demonstrate it only under strict fume-hood precautions, and students never perform it.)

**Balanced equation:** H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl

We read this as: "**One** molecule of hydrogen and **one** molecule of chlorine react to form **two** molecules of HCl." Now let us count how many bonds break and how many form.

```
   Before (reactants)            Middle (bonds broken)          After (products)

   H - H       Cl - Cl           H    H    Cl    Cl             H - Cl        H - Cl
   one bond    one bond          (4 separate atoms)             one bond      one bond

   Bonds broken: 2  --------------------------------------------->  Bonds formed: 2
   Atoms: H = 2, Cl = 2           H = 2, Cl = 2                   H = 2, Cl = 2
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Bond picture of H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl. Left: 2 bonds (H–H and Cl–Cl). Middle: after bond breaking, 4 separate atoms (2 H, 2 Cl). Right: 2 new bonds (H–Cl and H–Cl). On both sides, H = 2 and Cl = 2: no atom is gained or lost—only the partners changed. (The middle "separate atoms" stage is a mental stepping-stone to help us count; in a real reaction, breaking and making happen in one smooth rearrangement.)

### Checking the count (with electrons)

Just as in the previous chapter, let us also count the outer (valence) electrons: H has 1, and Cl has 7.

- **Before:** In H₂ there are 1 + 1 = 2 electrons (1 shared pair). In Cl₂ there are 7 + 7 = 14 electrons (1 shared pair and 6 lone pairs = 12 electrons). Total = 2 + 14 = **16**.
- **After:** In each HCl molecule, H and Cl bring 1 + 7 = 8 electrons, arranged as 1 shared pair and 3 lone pairs. Across 2 HCl molecules, 2 × 8 = **16**.

16 = 16 ✅. Shared pairs: 2 before (1 in H₂ + 1 in Cl₂), and 2 after (1 in each HCl). Lone pairs: 6 before (on Cl₂), and 2 × 3 = 6 after. **Only the bonding partners changed; the total number of electrons stayed exactly the same.**

One more check—mass: taking H = 1 and Cl = 35.5, before the reaction H₂ = 2 and Cl₂ = 71, giving a total of **73**. After the reaction, 2 × HCl = 2 × (1 + 35.5) = 2 × 36.5 = **73** ✅. That is the law of conservation of mass from Chapter 4: total mass before and after the reaction is identical.

## Part C: Another reaction: new teams of atoms

Now let us look at another reaction, the formation of water: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. Because O₂ has a double bond, we draw it as O=O in our diagram.

```
   Before: 2H₂ + O₂                     After: 2H₂O

   H - H     H - H     O = O            H - O - H        H - O - H

   Atoms: H = 4, O = 2                  Atoms: H = 4, O = 2
   Bonds: two H–H and one O=O           Bonds: four O–H (2 in each H₂O)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O as a rearrangement of atomic partnerships. Before: 4 H atoms and 2 O atoms in separate H₂ and O₂ molecules. After: the very same 4 H and 2 O atoms combined into 2 water molecules. Same atoms, new partners.

Atom and mass check (from Chapter 3): H: left 2 × 2 = 4, right 2 × 2 = 4 ✅. O: left 2, right 2 × 1 = 2 ✅. Mass: 2 × 2 + 32 = 36 u (with H = 1, O = 16) and 2 × 18 = 36 u ✅.

## Part D: Reading equations aloud and clues to a reaction

### Reading equations aloud

Practice reading chemical equations out loud in plain sentences:

| Equation | How to read it aloud |
|---|---|
| H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl | One molecule of hydrogen and one molecule of chlorine react to form two molecules of hydrogen chloride |
| 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O | Two molecules of hydrogen and one molecule of oxygen combine to form two molecules of water |
| CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ | Calcium carbonate, when heated, breaks down into calcium oxide and carbon dioxide |
| Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ | Zinc reacts with two formula units of hydrochloric acid to give zinc chloride and hydrogen gas |

We read the arrow as **"forms," "gives,"** or **"reacts to produce."** The coefficients tell us how many molecules, atoms, or formula units participate (Chapter 3).

### Clues to a chemical reaction (recalling Chapter 2)

In Chapter 2, we saw that chemical changes give observable **clues**. Now we understand why they appear: because brand-new substances with different properties are being formed.

| Clue | Everyday example | Keep in mind |
|---|---|---|
| Bubbles of gas | Dropping baking soda into lemon juice | Boiling water also makes bubbles, but those are just steam (a physical change). The real question is whether a new substance formed |
| Colour change | Reddish-brown rust forming on an iron nail | Colour can also change simply by dissolving a dye |
| Precipitate (solid settling out) | Two clear solutions turning milky when mixed | Covered in detail in Topic 3 |
| Warming or cooling (temperature change) | A hand-warmer packet heating up | Melting ice also feels cold, yet no new substance is formed |
| Light or sparks | A burning sparkler | An electric light bulb glows too, without any chemical reaction |
| New smell | Milk turning sour, or food burning | A smell can also come simply from evaporation |

So **a single clue by itself is not proof**; the most reliable test is always whether a **new substance** has been formed.

### Conditions and state symbols

In Language of Chemistry, we learned to write reaction **conditions** (such as heat or a catalyst) above the arrow and **state symbols**—(s), (l), (g), (aq)—after each formula. Here is a quick reminder:

CaCO₃(s) ──heat──► CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

This tells us at a glance that the reaction **requires heat**, that limestone (calcium carbonate) is a solid, and that CO₂ escapes as a gas. Some reactions happen on their own as soon as reactants touch, while others need a **push** (heat, light, or a catalyst) to get started. Think of lighting a gas stove with a match: first you supply a tiny spark (the push), and then the flame keeps going on its own.

## Part E: Not all reactions look the same: previewing reaction types

So far we have looked at three reactions: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, and CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. Notice a pattern? In the first two, **two substances join to make one**. In the third, **one substance breaks apart into two**. In other reactions, one atom **pushes another out of its place**.

Just as we sort groceries into fruits, vegetables, and grains so we know what to expect, chemists sort reactions into **four main types**:

```
   Joining together   A + B  ----->  AB          (combination)
   Breaking apart     AB     ----->  A + B       (decomposition)
   Taking a place     A + BC ----->  AC + B      (displacement)
   Swapping partners  AB + CD ---->  AD + CB     (double displacement)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Preview of the 4 main types of chemical reactions. In every case, old bonds break and new bonds form—only the pattern of rearrangement differs. We will explore each type thoroughly in Topic 2 and Topic 3.

Alongside these four patterns, we can also look at reactions through two other lenses:

| Lens | Key question | Where we study it |
|---|---|---|
| Energy and speed | Does heat come out (exothermic) or go in (endothermic)? How fast does the reaction go? | Topic 4 |
| Oxygen and hydrogen | Is oxygen being gained or lost (oxidation and reduction)? | Topic 5 |
| Everyday life | How do burning, rusting, and rancidity work? | Topic 6 |

You do not need to memorise all these names right now—just appreciate that **the very same reaction can be viewed from several helpful angles**.

## Part F: A quick home activity: **at home with an adult**

Pour a little fresh lemon juice into a clean glass or steel cup. With an adult present, add a pinch of baking soda and watch closely.

- Fizzing bubbles rise up: the gas clue.
- Touch the outside of the cup with your fingers: it feels slightly cool—the temperature clue.
- Never taste the mixture, and always use an open cup, never a sealed bottle or jar.

Did a new substance form? Yes—a gas (carbon dioxide) is bubbling out that was not present in the cup before. We will revisit this exact reaction in Topic 4 when we study energy changes. (Only mix lemon juice and baking soda; never mix household cleaners or other random chemicals.)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "New atoms are created during a chemical reaction"**

No. The atoms stay the exact same; in H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, there are 2 H and 2 Cl atoms before the arrow and 2 H and 2 Cl atoms after the arrow. Only the bonds between them changed.

**Mistake 2: "Whenever bubbles appear, a chemical reaction is happening"**

Boiling water produces plenty of bubbles, but those bubbles are just water vapour (a physical change). Always check whether a genuinely new substance has formed.

**Mistake 3: Treating bond breaking and bond making as the same thing**

Breaking a bond **requires** energy input; making a bond **releases** energy back. They are opposite processes.

**Mistake 4: "If an equation is balanced, the reaction will definitely happen"**

A balanced equation only means the atom count is correct on paper. Whether the reaction actually occurs—and how fast—is a separate question (covered in Topic 3 and Topic 4).

**Mistake 5: "When a sparkler burns down, its mass disappears"**

No mass disappears. If you add up the mass of the smoke and gases that escaped into the air plus the ash left on the wire, it equals the original mass of the sparkler coating plus the oxygen taken from the air.

## Quick Recap

- In a **chemical reaction**, old bonds in the reactants break, atoms rearrange, and new bonds form to make the products.
- Atoms are neither created nor destroyed; therefore, total mass and the count of each kind of atom stay constant.
- In H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, 2 bonds break (H–H and Cl–Cl) and 2 new bonds form (two H–Cl). Outer electrons: 16 before, 16 after; mass: 73 u on both sides.
- In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, there are 4 H and 2 O on both sides—only the bonding partners changed.
- Observable clues (gas bubbles, colour change, precipitate, warming/cooling, light, new smell) suggest a reaction, but the sure test is the formation of a new substance.
- Conditions above the arrow and state symbols give extra information; many reactions need an initial push to get started.
- Four main reaction patterns: combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement; we also classify reactions by energy change, oxidation–reduction, and everyday examples.

## Practice Questions

1. In your own words, explain what happens to the bonds of the reactants and the products during a chemical reaction. Use the dance-party idea or your own example.

2. For H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, count how many bonds are broken and how many are formed. Also count the atoms of H and Cl on both sides.

3. In the same reaction, count the outer electrons (H has 1, Cl has 7) before and after, and show that the number is the same.

4. The mass of H₂ is 2 u, of Cl₂ is 71 u and of HCl is 36.5 u. Show that the total mass is the same before and after for H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl.

5. Read aloud, in words, the equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. How many atoms of H and O are there on each side?

6. A student says: "Bubbles appeared when I boiled water, so a chemical reaction happened." Is she right? Give a reason.

7. Why is looking for only one clue not enough to say a chemical reaction has happened? Give one example of a clue that can also happen in a physical change.

8. Write CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ with state symbols and the heat condition on the arrow. What does the (g) tell you?

9. Look at the three reactions in this topic: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O and CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂. In which of them do two or more reactants make one product, and in which does one reactant break into two products?

10. State True or False and correct the false ones:
    (a) In a reaction, new atoms are created.
    (b) Breaking bonds takes energy.
    (c) Forming bonds gives energy back.
    (d) A balanced equation always means the reaction will happen.

### Answers

1. Some old bonds in the reactants **break**, the atoms rearrange into new partnerships, and **new bonds** form in the products. Just like dancers at a party letting go of one partner's hand and joining hands with a new partner, the people (atoms) stay the same—none are created or destroyed.

2. Bonds broken: **2** (one H–H and one Cl–Cl). Bonds formed: **2** (two H–Cl). Atoms: left H = 2, Cl = 2; right H = 2 × 1 = 2, Cl = 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

3. Before: 1 + 1 = 2 in H₂, and 7 + 7 = 14 in Cl₂, total = **16**. After: 1 + 7 = 8 in each HCl, and across two HCl molecules 2 × 8 = **16**. Both sides match ✅.

4. Before: 2 + 71 = **73** u. After: 2 × 36.5 = **73** u. Equal ✅.

5. "Two molecules of hydrogen and one molecule of oxygen combine to form two molecules of water." Left: H = 2 × 2 = 4, O = 2. Right: H = 2 × 2 = 4, O = 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

6. No. The bubbles in boiling water are simply steam (water vapour), which is still H₂O. Because no new substance is formed, boiling is a physical change.

7. Several clues can also appear during a physical change—for example, bubbles when water boils or cooling when ice melts. We can be sure a chemical reaction has occurred only when a **new substance** is formed.

8. **CaCO₃(s) ──heat──► CaO(s) + CO₂(g).** The (g) tells us that CO₂ is a gas and escapes into the air. Check: Ca 1 = 1, C 1 = 1, O 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ✅.

9. In the first two (H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl and 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O), two reactants combine to form a single product. In the third (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂), one reactant breaks down into two products. We will learn their names and details in Topic 2.

10. (a) **False** ❌. No new atoms are created; the existing atoms simply rearrange in new ways. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌. Balancing only ensures the atom count is conserved; whether the reaction actually takes place is a separate question.
