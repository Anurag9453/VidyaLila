# Topic 2: Boyle's Law: Pressure and Volume

In Topic 1, we learned that gas pressure originates from countless microscopic particles colliding with container walls. We also met the four defining variables: pressure ($P$), volume ($V$), temperature ($T$), and amount of gas ($n$). Importantly, we agreed on the scientific rule: **change only one variable at a time** while keeping the others fixed. Today, we conduct our first formal investigation: hold temperature and gas quantity constant, and discover **what happens to gas pressure when its volume is altered**. The answer is encapsulated in one of chemistry's foundational principles, discovered in 1662 by the Anglo-Irish scientist Robert Boyle.

---

## Thumb on the Syringe: An Invisible Spring

In the science laboratory, Mr. Harrison held up a clean plastic medical syringe. **It had no needle**—just a clear graduated barrel, a rubber-tipped plunger, and a narrow plastic nozzle tip. He pulled the plunger back to the $20\text{ mL}$ mark, trapping $20\text{ mL}$ of room air inside, and then pressed his thumb firmly against the nozzle to create an airtight seal.

"Maya, try pushing the plunger in," Mr. Harrison instructed.

Maya gripped the syringe and pressed the plunger. At first, it moved forward smoothly. But as the air compressed toward the $10\text{ mL}$ mark, resistance mounted rapidly. By the time the volume shrank to $5\text{ mL}$, she had to push with all her strength. The moment she relaxed her fingers, the plunger **sprang forcefully backward** on its own!

"Whoa! It felt like pushing against a coiled steel spring inside!" Maya exclaimed.

"Think of it as a pneumatic spring," smiled Mr. Harrison. "That springiness is **compressed air pressure** pushing back against your fingers. Today, we will quantify this relationship: exactly how much pressure climbs as volume shrinks, and why." *(Note: This investigation uses only needle-free plastic barrels under teacher supervision; needles must never be handled by students.)*

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Boyle's Law** | At constant temperature, the pressure of a fixed mass of gas is inversely proportional to its volume | Syringes, bicycle pumps |
| **Inversely proportional** | As one quantity multiplies by a factor, the other divides by the exact same factor | Halving volume doubles pressure |
| **Constant** | A mathematical value that remains unchanged during a transformation | The product $P \times V$ |
| **Hyperbola** | The smooth, curved graph produced when plotting two inversely related variables ($P$ vs $V$) | Figure 2.2 curve |
| **Straight-line graph** | A linear plot where doubling the independent variable doubles the dependent variable | Plotting $P$ against $1/V$ |
| **Isothermal** | Occurring at a constant, unvarying temperature | Compressing a gas very slowly |

## Part A: What Happens Inside the Syringe?

Let us zoom into the syringe barrel with our particle model. Recall from Topic 1: gas particles are in rapid, ceaseless motion. Every time a particle strikes the plastic barrel or plunger face, it delivers a minuscule impulse. Billions of simultaneous impacts per square centimetre add up to macroscopic **pressure**.

```
   Initial: 20 mL of air, pressure = 1 atm

                 +----------------------------------+
   plunger ======|  .  o    .     o   .      o      |==== nozzle tip
                 |    .   o     .      o   .    .   |     (sealed by thumb)
                 +----------------------------------+
                 <------------- 20 mL -------------->

   Compressed: plunger pushed in, 10 mL of air, pressure = 2 atm

                                   +----------------+
   plunger ========================|  .  o   .   o  |==== nozzle tip
                                   |  o   .   o  .  |     (sealed by thumb)
                                   +----------------+
                                   <---- 10 mL ----->

   o and . = gas particles (same number of particles, half the volume)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Microscopic representation of gas compression in a syringe. Initially, particles are widely dispersed across $20\text{ mL}$. When compressed to $10\text{ mL}$, the exact same number of particles is crowded into half the volume. (The diagram shows only a few representative particles for clarity; real air contains trillions.)

Notice three crucial facts:

1. **The number of particles remains constant ($n$ is fixed):** Because Mr. Harrison's thumb firmly seals the tip, no air molecules escape or enter.
2. **Particle speed remains unchanged ($T$ is fixed):** Provided the plunger is pushed slowly, the temperature remains steady, meaning the average kinetic speed of the particles does not change.
3. **The available space is cut in half ($V$ is halved):** The same number of particles now travels through a much smaller chamber. With less distance between opposing walls, each particle bounces back and forth much more frequently.

Because the interior surface area is reduced while collision frequency per unit area doubles, the total outward force per square centimetre **doubles**.

Therefore: **halving the volume doubles the pressure.** Reducing the volume to one-quarter quadruples the pressure. This mounting pressure is the "invisible spring" that shoved Maya's plunger backward.

> **Crucial Experimental Note:** If you ram the plunger inward very rapidly, mechanical work done on the gas warms it up (just like a bicycle pump gets hot during rapid pumping). That temperature spike momentarily violates the "constant temperature" condition. True Boyle's Law experiments require slow, gradual compression so that heat can dissipate and temperature remains strictly constant (**isothermal** conditions).

## Part B: Boyle's Law Statement and Equation

By recording careful pressure and volume measurements in a sealed J-tube apparatus, Robert Boyle discovered that the product of pressure and volume always yielded a constant value.

> **Boyle's Law:** At constant temperature, the volume of a given mass of dry gas is inversely proportional to its pressure.
> Mathematically:
> $$P \propto \frac{1}{V} \quad \text{or} \quad P \times V = \text{constant}$$
> Consequently, for any two states (initial State 1 and final State 2):
> $$P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$$

Where $P_1$ and $V_1$ represent the initial pressure and volume, and $P_2$ and $V_2$ represent the final pressure and volume.

For Maya's syringe: $P_1 = 1\text{ atm}$, $V_1 = 20\text{ mL}$, and $V_2 = 10\text{ mL}$.
$$P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \frac{1\text{ atm} \times 20\text{ mL}}{10\text{ mL}} = \mathbf{2\text{ atm}}$$

**Conditions for Boyle's Law:**
1. **Temperature must remain constant** ($T = \text{constant}$).
2. **The mass/amount of gas must remain constant** ($n = \text{constant}$; sealed container).

**Rule for Units:** $P_1$ and $P_2$ must share the **same unit** (both in $\text{atm}$, or both in $\text{mm Hg}$, or both in $\text{Pa}$). Likewise, $V_1$ and $V_2$ must share the **same unit** (both in $\text{mL}$ or both in $\text{L}$). Because Boyle's Law is a ratio, the conversion factors cancel out—provided the units match on both sides.

## Part C: Understanding the Graphs

Consider a controlled sample of gas held at constant temperature, yielding the following measurements:

| Volume $V$ (L) | Pressure $P$ (atm) | Product $P \times V$ ($\text{atm}\cdot\text{L}$) |
|---|---|---|
| 10 | 1.0 | 10 |
| 5.0 | 2.0 | 10 |
| 4.0 | 2.5 | 10 |
| 2.5 | 4.0 | 10 |
| 2.0 | 5.0 | 10 |

Observe how as volume is halved from $10\text{ L}$ to $5\text{ L}$, pressure doubles from $1.0\text{ atm}$ to $2.0\text{ atm}$. Across every measurement, **$P \times V = 10\text{ atm}\cdot\text{L}$**.

**Plotting $P$ against $1/V$: A Straight Line**

Both graphs represent the exact same physical law:
- **$P$ vs $V$:** Smooth hyperbolic curve.
- **$P$ vs $1/V$:** Straight line passing through the origin.

## Part D: Boyle's Law in Daily Life

- **Medical syringes:** Withdrawing the plunger increases the volume inside the barrel. The internal pressure drops below external atmospheric pressure, allowing higher atmospheric pressure outside to push medicine or fluid smoothly through the nozzle into the barrel.
- **Bicycle pumps:** Pushing down on the pump handle rapidly compresses the volume of air inside the barrel, driving its pressure above the pressure inside the bicycle tyre, forcing the valve open and injecting air into the tube.
- **Rising bubbles and scuba diving:** Water pressure increases by roughly $1\text{ atm}$ for every $10\text{ metres}$ of depth. An air bubble exhaled by a diver at $20\text{ m}$ experiences roughly $3\text{ atm}$ of total pressure. As the bubble ascends to the surface where pressure is only $1\text{ atm}$, its volume triples! For this reason, **scuba divers are strictly trained never to hold their breath while ascending**; expanding air inside trapped lungs could cause severe pulmonary tissue damage.
- **Human breathing (respiration):** When you inhale, your diaphragm contracts downward and your ribcage expands, enlarging your chest cavity. The increased lung volume causes internal air pressure to drop slightly below atmospheric pressure ($760\text{ mm Hg}$), causing fresh outside air to rush in. During exhalation, the chest cavity contracts, volume decreases, pressure rises, and air is pushed out.
- **Sealed plastic bottles on mountain descents:** If you seal an empty, flexible plastic water bottle on a high mountain summit where atmospheric pressure is low, and then drive down into a valley where atmospheric pressure is higher, the bottle will visibly crush inward as the greater external pressure compresses the trapped air to a smaller volume.

**Classroom Activity (with teacher guidance):**
Take a needle-free plastic syringe. Pull the plunger to the midpoint, seal the nozzle tip tightly with your thumb, and gently compress the trapped air. Feel the growing resistance. Then try gently pulling the plunger outward while keeping the nozzle sealed: notice how the plunger resists being pulled and snaps back inward when released! This demonstrates that expanding the volume creates a partial vacuum, allowing outside atmospheric pressure to push the plunger back in.

## Part E: Solved Examples

*(All calculations assume constant temperature and a fixed quantity of gas.)*

**Example 1: Syringe compression.** A needle-free syringe contains $20\text{ mL}$ of air at $1.0\text{ atm}$. The plunger is pushed to compress the gas first to $10\text{ mL}$ and then to $5\text{ mL}$. Determine the pressure at both stages.
- Using $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2 \implies P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{V_2}$:
- At $10\text{ mL}$: $P_2 = \frac{1.0 \times 20}{10} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ atm}}$
- At $5\text{ mL}$: $P_3 = \frac{1.0 \times 20}{5} = \mathbf{4.0\text{ atm}}$
- *Check:* $1.0 \times 20 = 2.0 \times 10 = 4.0 \times 5 = 20\text{ atm}\cdot\text{mL}$ ✅.

**Example 2: Finding final volume.** A gas sample occupies $600\text{ mL}$ at $1\text{ atm}$. What volume will it occupy if the pressure is increased to $3\text{ atm}$ at constant temperature?
$$V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{1 \times 600}{3} = \mathbf{200\text{ mL}}$$
- *Check:* $3 \times 200 = 600 = 1 \times 600$ ✅. Tripling the pressure reduces volume to one-third.

**Example 3: Gas expansion.** A cylinder fitted with a frictionless piston contains $2\text{ L}$ of gas at $4\text{ atm}$. The piston is pulled outward until the pressure drops to $1\text{ atm}$. What is the final volume?
$$V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{4 \times 2}{1} = \mathbf{8\text{ L}}$$
- *Check:* $1 \times 8 = 8 = 4 \times 2$ ✅. Quadrupling the volume reduces the pressure to one-fourth.

**Example 4: Pressure in mm Hg.** A gas sample occupies $500\text{ mL}$ at $760\text{ mm Hg}$. What will its volume be if the pressure is reduced to $380\text{ mm Hg}$?
$$V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{760 \times 500}{380} = \mathbf{1000\text{ mL}}\ (= 1\text{ L})$$
- *Check:* $380 \times 1000 = 380000 = 760 \times 500$ ✅. Halving the pressure doubles the volume.

**Example 5: Bicycle pump stroke.** The barrel of a hand pump contains $1000\text{ cm}^3$ of air at $1\text{ atm}$. If the discharge valve is momentarily blocked and the plunger is slowly pushed down until the volume inside the barrel is $250\text{ cm}^3$, what pressure is reached?
$$P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \frac{1 \times 1000}{250} = \mathbf{4\text{ atm}}$$
- *Check:* $4 \times 250 = 1000$ ✅.

**Example 6: Validating experimental data.** Two lab groups recorded the following data for a fixed gas sample at constant temperature:

| Group A: $P$ (atm) | $V$ (mL) | Group B: $P$ (atm) | $V$ (mL) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 | 48 | 1 | 30 |
| 1.0 | 24 | 2 | 15 |
| 2.0 | 12 | 3 | 10 |
| 2.5 | 9.6 | 4 | 8.0 |
| 4.0 | 6.0 | 5 | 6.0 |

- Group A: Products $P \times V = 24, 24, 24, 24, 24$. All products match perfectly, validating Boyle's Law.
- Group B: Products $P \times V = 30, 30, 30, 32, 30$. The fourth reading ($P = 4\text{ atm}, V = 8.0\text{ mL}$) is faulty. Since the constant is $30$, at $P = 4\text{ atm}$, $V = 30 / 4 = \mathbf{7.5\text{ mL}}$.

**Example 7: Underwater rising bubble.** A bubble of volume $1.0\text{ mL}$ forms at a depth of $20\text{ m}$ in a freshwater lake, where total pressure is $3.0\text{ atm}$ ($1\text{ atm}$ air $+ 2\text{ atm}$ water column). What will its volume be when it reaches the lake surface ($1.0\text{ atm}$)?
$$V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{3.0 \times 1.0}{1.0} = \mathbf{3.0\text{ mL}}$$
- *Check:* $1.0 \times 3.0 = 3.0 = 3.0 \times 1.0$ ✅. The bubble triples in volume as it reaches the surface.

**Example 8: Inhalation pressure differential.** Before inhalation, a person's lungs hold $2.00\text{ L}$ of air at $760\text{ mm Hg}$. As the diaphragm contracts, lung volume expands to $2.01\text{ L}$. What is the new pressure inside the lungs, and by how much did it drop?
$$P_2 = \frac{760 \times 2.00}{2.01} \approx \mathbf{756.2\text{ mm Hg}}$$
$$\Delta P = 760 - 756.2 = \mathbf{3.8\text{ mm Hg}}$$
- *Check:* $756.2 \times 2.01 \approx 1520 = 760 \times 2.00$ ✅. A tiny pressure difference of just under $4\text{ mm Hg}$ is all that is needed to draw fresh air into the lungs!

**Example 9: Mountain bottle crush.** A flexible $1.0\text{ L}$ water bottle is capped atop a mountain pass where pressure is $0.90\text{ atm}$. Down at sea level ($1.00\text{ atm}$), what is the new volume of the sealed air inside?
$$V_2 = \frac{0.90 \times 1.0}{1.00} = \mathbf{0.90\text{ L}}$$
- *Check:* $1.00 \times 0.90 = 0.90$ ✅. The bottle crushes inward by $10\%$.

**Example 10: Understanding "increased by".** A $3\text{ L}$ sample of gas is at $2\text{ atm}$. The pressure is **increased by $2\text{ atm}$**. What is the new volume?
- "Increased by $2\text{ atm}$" means final pressure $P_2 = 2 + 2 = \mathbf{4\text{ atm}}$.
$$V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{2 \times 3}{4} = \mathbf{1.5\text{ L}}$$
- *Check:* $4 \times 1.5 = 6 = 2 \times 3$ ✅. (Always use the *total final pressure*, not the increment, in formulas.)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Doubling pressure doubles the volume"**
   No. Pressure and volume are **inversely** proportional. Doubling pressure **halves** the volume ($P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$).

2. **Mistake 2: Mixing incompatible pressure units**
   If $P_1$ is in $\text{atm}$ and $P_2$ is in $\text{mm Hg}$, convert one to match the other before substituting into $P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$.

3. **Mistake 3: Applying Boyle's Law to an open or leaking system**
   Boyle's Law strictly requires a **fixed mass of gas** ($n = \text{constant}$) at **constant temperature** ($T = \text{constant}$). If gas escapes, the law cannot be used directly.

4. **Mistake 4: Confusing "increased to" with "increased by"**
   If pressure is "increased by $3\text{ atm}$", the final pressure is $P_2 = P_1 + 3$. If "increased to $3\text{ atm}$", $P_2 = 3$.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming $P + V$ is constant**
   It is the **product** $P \times V$ that is constant, never the sum $P + V$.

6. **Mistake 6: Forgetting that real gases deviate at extreme conditions**
   At very high pressures and very low temperatures, real gas molecules are forced close together and intermolecular attractions cause slight deviations from ideal Boyle's Law behaviour.

7. **Mistake 7: Calculating underwater pressure without including atmospheric pressure**
   Total pressure at depth equals atmospheric pressure at the surface **plus** the hydrostatic water column pressure ($P_{\text{total}} = P_{\text{atm}} + P_{\text{water}}$).

## Quick Recap

- At constant temperature and constant mass: **$P \propto 1/V$**, meaning **$P \times V = \text{constant}$** or **$P_1V_1 = P_2V_2$** (Robert Boyle, 1662).
- Microscopic explanation: Halving volume crowds the same particles into half the space, doubling the collision rate on container walls and thus doubling pressure.
- A plot of **$P$ vs $V$** produces a **rectangular hyperbola**; a plot of **$P$ vs $1/V$** produces a **straight line through the origin** with slope $= P \times V$.
- Units for $P_1$ and $P_2$ must match; units for $V_1$ and $V_2$ must match.
- Real-world applications include medical syringes, bicycle pumps, breathing mechanics, scuba diving ascent precautions, and bottles collapsing during mountain descents.

    (a) When the volume of a gas is halved at constant temperature, its pressure is halved.

    (c) The graph of $P$ against $1/V$ for an ideal gas at constant temperature is a straight line through the origin.

### Answers

1. Gas pressure is the cumulative force of gas particles colliding against the container walls. When volume decreases at constant temperature, the same number of particles move at the same average speed in a smaller space. Consequently, particles travel shorter distances between collisions and strike the walls **more frequently per unit area per second**, resulting in higher pressure.

2. $P_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{V_2} = \frac{1.0 \times 50}{20} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ atm}}$.
   *Check:* $2.5 \times 20 = 50 = 1.0 \times 50$ ✅.

3. $V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{3.0 \times 5.0}{1.5} = \mathbf{10\text{ L}}$.
   *Check:* $1.5 \times 10 = 15 = 3.0 \times 5.0$ ✅.

4. $V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{380 \times 250}{950} = \mathbf{100\text{ mL}}$.
   *Check:* $950 \times 100 = 95000 = 380 \times 250$ ✅.

5. Products: $1.0 \times 20 = 20$, $2.0 \times 10 = 20$, $4.0 \times 5.0 = 20$, but $5.0 \times 3.0 = 15$.
   **The reading $V = 3.0\text{ mL}$ is incorrect.** At $P = 5.0\text{ atm}$, the correct volume is $V = 20 / 5.0 = \mathbf{4.0\text{ mL}}$ ✅.

6. $V_2 = \frac{P_1V_1}{P_2} = \frac{2.0 \times 2.0}{1.0} = \mathbf{4.0\text{ mL}}$.
   *Check:* $1.0 \times 4.0 = 4.0 = 2.0 \times 2.0$ ✅. The bubble doubles in size.

7. The ball's volume is fixed at $2.0\text{ L}$. At $1.5\text{ atm}$, the total quantity of air contained, if expanded to $1.0\text{ atm}$, equals:
   $V_{\text{total}} = \frac{1.5 \times 2.0}{1.0} = 3.0\text{ L at } 1.0\text{ atm}$.
   The ball already holds $2.0\text{ L}$ of air at $1.0\text{ atm}$, so the pump must add:
   $3.0 - 2.0 = 1.0\text{ L of air at } 1.0\text{ atm}$.
   Number of strokes $= \frac{1.0\text{ L}}{0.25\text{ L/stroke}} = \mathbf{4\text{ strokes}}$ ✅.

8. (a) $P = 12 / 3 = \mathbf{4\text{ atm}}$.
   (b) $1/V = 0.5\text{ L}^{-1} \implies V = 2\text{ L}$; thus $P = 12 / 2 = \mathbf{6\text{ atm}}$.
   (c) The slope of $P$ vs $1/V$ equals $P \times V = \mathbf{12\text{ atm}\cdot\text{L}}$ ✅.

9. $V_2 = \frac{0.92 \times 1.5}{1.00} = \mathbf{1.38\text{ L}}$. The bottle volume compresses by roughly $8\%$.
   *Check:* $1.00 \times 1.38 = 1.38 = 0.92 \times 1.5$ ✅.

10. Rapid compression performs mechanical work on the gas without giving heat sufficient time to escape, causing the gas temperature to rise. Because **temperature was not held constant**, the condition for Boyle's Law was violated, leading to higher-than-expected pressure readings.

11. (a) **False** ❌. When volume is halved, pressure **doubles**.
    (b) **True** ✅.
    (c) **True** ✅.
    (d) **False** ❌. A leaking cylinder loses gas molecules, violating the requirement of a constant mass of gas ($n = \text{constant}$).
