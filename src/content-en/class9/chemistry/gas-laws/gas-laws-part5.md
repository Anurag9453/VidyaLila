# Topic 5: STP, Avogadro's Law, and Molar Volume (Extension & Class 10 Foundation)

Across the previous three topics, we investigated the interplay among **pressure ($P$), volume ($V$), and temperature ($T$)**, culminating in the Combined Gas Equation. Yet one fundamental variable remained in the shadows: **the amount of gas ($n$)**. In every calculation so far, we assumed a sealed container with a fixed quantity of particles. Today, we bring gas quantity directly into the spotlight. The core question is straightforward: **how much space does one mole of gas occupy?** In our stoichiometry studies, we promised that molar gas volume would be revealed here. Furthermore, our study of water electrolysis showed that hydrogen and oxygen gases evolve in a crisp **$2 : 1$ volume ratio**; today, we explain exactly why that happens.

*(Note: This topic serves as an **enrichment module** that lays the foundation for Class 10 stoichiometry. Master the core principles, and higher-grade chemistry will feel intuitive.)*

**Standard Atomic Masses Used Throughout:**
$\text{H} = 1$, $\text{He} = 4$, $\text{C} = 12$, $\text{N} = 14$, $\text{O} = 16$, $\text{Cl} = 35.5$. Standard molar volume $= 22.4\text{ L/mol}$ at STP. Avogadro's number $= 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ particles/mol}$.

## A Birthday Party and Two Floating Balloons

At a family birthday party, Liam and his uncle, Mr. Nelson, were decorating the living room. Liam held a bright foil balloon filled with **helium** from a party supply store. When he accidentally let go of the ribbon, the balloon shot straight upward and bumped gently against the ceiling. Next, Mr. Nelson blew a soap bubble filled with exhaled air and carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$). The bubble floated downward and landed softly on the carpet.

Liam looked puzzled. "Uncle, both are flexible spheres filled with gas. Why does the helium balloon race to the ceiling, while the carbon dioxide bubble sinks to the floor? And back in our mole unit, we learned that one mole of any substance contains the exact same number of particles. Does one mole of gas always occupy the same volume, regardless of which gas it is?"

Mr. Nelson smiled. "Those two questions share the exact same explanation! If equal volumes of different gases contain **equal numbers of particles** under identical conditions, the weight of one litre of gas depends entirely on the mass of its individual molecules. Helium molecules are extraordinarily light; carbon dioxide molecules are heavy. Let's quantify this relationship."

> **Safety Notice:** Compressed gas cylinders—whether helium, propane, or nitrogen—must never be handled by students. Commercial balloons are inflated by trained professionals using regulated valves. Never inhale gas directly from any cylinder or balloon, and never expose gas cylinders to heat or sunlight.

## Part A: Why Do We Need STP?

Our earlier investigations proved that the volume of any gas expands or contracts whenever pressure or temperature shifts. Stating that a reaction produced "5 litres of gas" is scientifically meaningless unless you specify the pressure and temperature at which that volume was measured.

To ensure laboratory measurements can be compared accurately worldwide, scientists established an agreed baseline: **Standard Temperature and Pressure (STP)**.

- **Standard Temperature:** $273\text{ K}$ (equivalent to $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, the freezing point of water).
- **Standard Pressure:** $1\text{ atm}$ (equivalent to $760\text{ mm Hg} = 101325\text{ Pa}$, normal sea-level air pressure).

Whenever a problem specifies "at STP", you immediately know that $T = 273\text{ K}$ and $P = 1\text{ atm}$. Note that STP is a chilly $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$, whereas normal room conditions hover around $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$ to $27\ ^\circ\text{C}$ ($298\text{ K}$ to $300\text{ K}$). Gas volumes at room temperature will differ from STP volumes according to Charles's Law.

## Part B: Avogadro's Law

Imagine three identical sealed $1\text{-litre}$ flasks sitting on a lab bench at STP:
- Flask A holds **hydrogen gas** ($\text{H}_2$, tiny and lightweight).
- Flask B holds **oxygen gas** ($\text{O}_2$, medium-sized).
- Flask C holds **carbon dioxide gas** ($\text{CO}_2$, large and heavy).

How do the particle counts in the three flasks compare? In 1811, Italian physicist **Amedeo Avogadro** proposed an extraordinary hypothesis:

> **Avogadro's Law:** Equal volumes of all gases, under identical conditions of temperature and pressure, contain equal numbers of molecules.
> Mathematically:
> $$V \propto n \quad (\text{at constant } T \text{ and } P)$$

```
   Three identical 1-litre flasks, all at STP (same T and P)

   +--------------+   +--------------+   +--------------+
   | o   o    o   |   | O   O    O   |   | @@  @@   @@  |
   |    o   o     |   |   O    O     |   |   @@   @@    |
   | o     o      |   | O     O      |   | @@    @@     |
   +--------------+   +--------------+   +--------------+
      Hydrogen            Oxygen          Carbon Dioxide
    Small, light         Medium          Large, heavy

   Each flask contains the exact same number of molecules (7 shown).
   The masses differ, but the molecule counts are identical!
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Equal volumes of different gases contain equal numbers of molecules at the same temperature and pressure. The physical size of individual molecules is negligible compared to the vast empty space between them; hence, particle count alone dictates volume.

**Why Does Particle Size Not Alter Gas Volume?**

In a solid crystal or a dense liquid, particles are packed tightly against one another, so their physical size directly dictates macroscopic volume. But in a gas, molecules are separated by distances **tens of times greater than their own molecular diameters**. Over $99.9\%$ of a gas container is empty space!

Because gas molecules spend their time zooming across this empty void, the physical dimensions of an individual molecule are virtually irrelevant. At a given temperature and pressure, **the quantity of space required depends solely on the number of moving particles**, not on their physical mass or size.

## Part C: Molar Volume: 1 Mole Equals 22.4 Litres

Combining Avogadro's Law with the mole concept yields a profound conclusion:
1. One mole of *any* substance contains $6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles (Avogadro's number).
2. By Avogadro's Law, equal numbers of gas particles occupy equal volumes at standard conditions.
3. Therefore, **one mole of any ideal gas must occupy the exact same volume at STP!**

Experimental measurements confirm this value:
$$\mathbf{1\text{ mole of any gas at STP occupies } 22.4\text{ L}} \quad (\text{or } 22.4\text{ dm}^3 = 22400\text{ cm}^3)$$

This quantity is called the **molar volume of a gas at STP** (more precisely $22.414\text{ L/mol}$, rounded to $22.4\text{ L/mol}$).

To visualise $22.4\text{ litres}$, imagine a cube approximately $28.2\text{ cm}$ on each edge—large enough to hold twenty-two $1\text{-litre}$ water bottles. In contrast, one mole of liquid water occupies a mere $18\text{ mL}$ (about one tablespoon). Gas particles spread out so extensively that one mole occupies over **1200 times more space** as a vapour than as a liquid!

```
   1 mole of any gas at STP = 22.4 L

        +--------------------------+
        |  o      o     o       o  |   Contains:
        |     o        o     o     |   6.022 × 10²³ molecules
        |  o      o         o      |
        |      o      o        o   |   Mass:
        |  o         o      o      |   2 g of H₂
        +--------------------------+   4 g of He
          Cube: ~28.2 cm on edge       32 g of O₂
          Volume = 22.4 L              44 g of CO₂
```

> **Figure 5.2** – One mole of any gas at STP occupies $22.4\text{ L}$. While the molecule count ($6.022 \times 10^{23}$) and volume ($22.4\text{ L}$) are identical for all gases, the total mass reflects the molar mass of the specific chemical.

**The Stoichiometric "Mole Bridge" for Gases:**

1. **Mass to Moles:** $n = \frac{m}{M}$
2. **Moles to Volume at STP:** $V = n \times 22.4\text{ L}$
3. **Moles to Molecule Count:** $N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$

```
                  MASS (g)
                   ^    |
           ÷ M     |    |   × M
                   |    v
                 MOLES (n)
              /     |     \
     × 22.4  /      |      \  × 6.022 × 10²³
            v       |       v
     VOLUME AT STP (L)     MOLECULE COUNT (N)
```

> **Crucial Rule:** The $22.4\text{ L/mol}$ factor applies **exclusively to substances in the gaseous state** and **strictly at STP**. Never apply $22.4\text{ L}$ to liquids (like liquid water) or solids (like salt or iron).

## Part D: Solved Problems (at STP)

### Problem 1. Volume to moles: 44.8 L nitrogen

How many moles are present in $44.8\text{ L}$ of nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) at STP?
$$n = \frac{V}{22.4} = \frac{44.8\text{ L}}{22.4\text{ L/mol}} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ mol}}$$
- *Check:* $2.0\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = 44.8\text{ L}$ ✅.

### Problem 2. Moles to volume: 0.25 mol oxygen

What volume does $0.25\text{ mol}$ of oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$) occupy at STP?
$$V = n \times 22.4 = 0.25\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = \mathbf{5.6\text{ L}}$$
- *Check:* $5.6 / 22.4 = 0.25\text{ mol}$ ✅.

### Problem 3. Volume to mass: 5.6 L oxygen

Find the mass of $5.6\text{ L}$ of oxygen gas ($\text{O}_2$) at STP.
- Molar mass of $\text{O}_2 = 2 \times 16 = 32\text{ g/mol}$.
- Step 1: Find moles:
  $$n = \frac{5.6\text{ L}}{22.4\text{ L/mol}} = 0.25\text{ mol}$$
- Step 2: Convert moles to mass:
  $$m = n \times M = 0.25\text{ mol} \times 32\text{ g/mol} = \mathbf{8.0\text{ g}}$$
- *Check:* $8.0\text{ g} / 32\text{ g/mol} = 0.25\text{ mol}$; $0.25 \times 22.4 = 5.6\text{ L}$ ✅.

### Problem 4. Mass to volume: 4.4 g carbon dioxide

What volume does $4.4\text{ g}$ of carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) occupy at STP?
- Molar mass of $\text{CO}_2 = 12 + 2(16) = 44\text{ g/mol}$.
- Step 1: Find moles:
  $$n = \frac{4.4\text{ g}}{44\text{ g/mol}} = 0.10\text{ mol}$$
- Step 2: Calculate volume:
  $$V = 0.10\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = \mathbf{2.24\text{ L}}$$
- *Check:* $2.24 / 22.4 = 0.10\text{ mol}$; $0.10 \times 44 = 4.4\text{ g}$ ✅.

### Problem 5. Volume to molecule count: 2.24 L of any gas

How many molecules are present in $2.24\text{ L}$ of *any* gas at STP?
$$n = \frac{2.24\text{ L}}{22.4\text{ L/mol}} = 0.10\text{ mol}$$
$$N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = 0.10 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{6.022 \times 10^{22}\text{ molecules}}$$
- Notice we did not need to know which gas it was: Avogadro's Law guarantees this molecule count for all ideal gases.

### Problem 6. Small mass, vast volume: 1 g hydrogen

What volume is occupied by $1.0\text{ g}$ of hydrogen gas ($\text{H}_2$) at STP?
- Molar mass of $\text{H}_2 = 2.0\text{ g/mol}$.
  $$n = \frac{1.0\text{ g}}{2.0\text{ g/mol}} = 0.50\text{ mol}$$
  $$V = 0.50\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = \mathbf{11.2\text{ L}}$$
- A single gram of hydrogen gas expands to fill eleven $1\text{-litre}$ bottles!

### Problem 7. Comparing equal masses: 28 g N₂ vs 28 g CO₂

Compare the volumes occupied at STP by $28\text{ g}$ of nitrogen gas ($\text{N}_2$) and $28\text{ g}$ of carbon dioxide gas ($\text{CO}_2$).
- For $\text{N}_2$ ($M = 28\text{ g/mol}$):
  $$n = \frac{28}{28} = 1.0\text{ mol} \implies V = 1.0 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{22.4\text{ L}}$$
- For $\text{CO}_2$ ($M = 44\text{ g/mol}$):
  $$n = \frac{28}{44} \approx 0.636\text{ mol} \implies V = 0.636 \times 22.4 \approx \mathbf{14.25\text{ L}}$$
- *Insight:* **Equal masses of different gases do not occupy equal volumes.** Volume tracks the number of *moles* (particle count), not grams!

### Problem 8. Non-STP conditions: Room temperature

What volume does $1.0\text{ mole}$ of gas occupy at room temperature ($27\ ^\circ\text{C} = 300\text{ K}$) and $1\text{ atm}$ pressure?
- Pressure is constant ($1\text{ atm}$), so we apply Charles's Law:
  $$V_2 = V_1 \times \frac{T_2}{T_1} = 22.4\text{ L} \times \frac{300\text{ K}}{273\text{ K}} \approx \mathbf{24.6\text{ L}}$$
- At room temperature, one mole of gas occupies $24.6\text{ L}$—not $22.4\text{ L}$. Always confirm whether conditions are STP!

## Part E: Gas Density: Why Balloons Rise or Sink

Density is defined as $\text{mass} / \text{volume}$. For one mole of gas at STP, the mass is its molar mass ($M$) and the volume is $22.4\text{ L}$.
$$\mathbf{\text{Density of a gas at STP (g/L)} = \frac{M}{22.4}}$$

Let us compute the densities of common gases at STP:

| Gas | Formula | Molar Mass $M$ ($\text{g/mol}$) | Density at STP $= M / 22.4$ ($\text{g/L}$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | $\text{H}_2$ | 2 | $0.089 \approx 0.09$ |
| Helium | $\text{He}$ | 4 | $0.179 \approx 0.18$ |
| Nitrogen | $\text{N}_2$ | 28 | $1.25$ |
| **Air (average mixture)** | — | **$\approx 29$** | **$1.29$** |
| Oxygen | $\text{O}_2$ | 32 | $1.43$ |
| Carbon dioxide | $\text{CO}_2$ | 44 | $1.96$ |

*(Note: Air is a mixture of roughly $78\%\ \text{N}_2$ and $21\%\ \text{O}_2$, yielding an average molar mass of approximately $29\text{ g/mol}$.)*

**The Buoyancy Rule for Gases:**
- If a gas has an effective molar mass **less than $29\text{ g/mol}$** (density $< 1.29\text{ g/L}$), it is **lighter than air** and floats upward.
- If a gas has an effective molar mass **greater than $29\text{ g/mol}$** (density $> 1.29\text{ g/L}$), it is **heavier than air** and sinks downward.

This solves Liam's birthday puzzle:
- **Helium ($M = 4$):** Density is $0.18\text{ g/L}$, far below air's $1.29\text{ g/L}$. The buoyant force easily lifts the balloon.
- **Carbon dioxide ($M = 44$):** Density is $1.96\text{ g/L}$, much denser than air. The bubble sinks.
- **Nitrogen ($M = 28$):** Density is $1.25\text{ g/L}$, nearly identical to ambient air.

### Problem 9. Buoyant lift of a helium balloon

Calculate the net lifting capacity of a $10\text{ L}$ helium party balloon at STP (ignoring the weight of the thin latex skin).
- Mass of $10\text{ L}$ of helium:
  $$m_{\text{He}} = 10\text{ L} \times \frac{4\text{ g}}{22.4\text{ L}} \approx 1.79\text{ g}$$
- Mass of $10\text{ L}$ of displaced air:
  $$m_{\text{air}} = 10\text{ L} \times \frac{29\text{ g}}{22.4\text{ L}} \approx 12.95\text{ g}$$
- Net upward lift:
  $$\Delta m = 12.95\text{ g} - 1.79\text{ g} \approx \mathbf{11.2\text{ g}}$$
- A $10\text{ L}$ helium balloon can lift approximately $11.2\text{ grams}$—plenty to lift the light latex skin and ribbon!

### Problem 10. Identifying an unknown gas from density

A laboratory gas sample has a density of $1.25\text{ g/L}$ at STP. Calculate its molar mass and suggest its identity.
$$M = \text{density} \times 22.4 = 1.25\text{ g/L} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = \mathbf{28\text{ g/mol}}$$
- A molar mass of $28\text{ g/mol}$ identifies the gas as **nitrogen ($\text{N}_2$)** or carbon monoxide ($\text{CO}$).

## Part F: Settling an Old Chemical Debt: Water Electrolysis

In our earlier chemistry topics, we discovered that passing an electrical current through acidified water decomposes it into hydrogen and oxygen:
$$2\text{H}_2\text{O}\text{ (l)} \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{ (g)} + \text{O}_2\text{ (g)}$$
We observed that the volume of hydrogen gas collected was **exactly double** the volume of oxygen gas ($2 : 1$).

Now we can finally explain why:
1. The balanced chemical equation tells us that every $2\text{ molecules}$ of water decompose to form **$2\text{ molecules of H}_2$** and **$1\text{ molecule of O}_2$**.
2. By Avogadro's Law, equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
3. Therefore, $2\text{ molecules of H}_2$ must occupy **twice the volume** of $1\text{ molecule of O}_2$!

```
   Electrolysis of Water: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂

      Hydrogen (−) Cathode      Oxygen (+) Anode
           ┌──────┐                 ┌──────┐
           │      │                 │      │
           │  H₂  │                 │      │
           │  H₂  │                 │  O₂  │
           │  H₂  │                 │  O₂  │
           │  H₂  │                 │      │
           └──────┘                 └──────┘
        Gas Volume: 2 units       Gas Volume: 1 unit

               Volume Ratio  H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Electrolysis tube volume comparison. Two volumes of hydrogen gas collect at the cathode for every one volume of oxygen gas at the anode, directly reflecting the molecular ratio in the chemical formula $\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

### Problem 11. Quantitative electrolysis: 36 g water

If $36\text{ g}$ of water is completely decomposed, find the volumes of $\text{H}_2$ and $\text{O}_2$ produced at STP.
- Moles of water:
  $$n(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = \frac{36\text{ g}}{18\text{ g/mol}} = 2.0\text{ mol}$$
- From $2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$:
  $$n(\text{H}_2) = 2.0\text{ mol}, \quad n(\text{O}_2) = 1.0\text{ mol}$$
- Calculate volumes at STP:
  $$V(\text{H}_2) = 2.0\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = \mathbf{44.8\text{ L}}$$
  $$V(\text{O}_2) = 1.0\text{ mol} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} = \mathbf{22.4\text{ L}}$$
- Volume ratio $= 44.8 : 22.4 = \mathbf{2 : 1}$ ✅.
- Check mass conservation: $m(\text{H}_2) = 2 \times 2 = 4\text{ g}$; $m(\text{O}_2) = 1 \times 32 = 32\text{ g}$; $4 + 32 = 36\text{ g}$ of original water ✅.

### Problem 12. Gas volumes in ammonia synthesis

In the industrial Haber process:
$$\text{N}_2\text{ (g)} + 3\text{H}_2\text{ (g)} \longrightarrow 2\text{NH}_3\text{ (g)}$$
At constant temperature and pressure, what is the volume relationship?
- $1\text{ molecule of N}_2 + 3\text{ molecules of H}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{ molecules of NH}_3$.
- Applying Avogadro's Law directly:
  $$\mathbf{1\text{ volume of N}_2 + 3\text{ volumes of H}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{ volumes of NH}_3}$$
- If $10\text{ L}$ of $\text{N}_2$ reacts with $30\text{ L}$ of $\text{H}_2$, exactly $\mathbf{20\text{ L of NH}_3}$ is formed! Notice that the total gas volume contracts from $40\text{ L}$ down to $20\text{ L}$ because the total number of gas molecules drops from 4 to 2.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Applying 22.4 L/mol at non-STP conditions**
   $22.4\text{ L/mol}$ is strictly valid at STP ($273\text{ K}, 1\text{ atm}$). At room temperature ($27\ ^\circ\text{C}$), one mole expands to $\approx 24.6\text{ L}$ (Problem 8).

2. **Mistake 2: Applying 22.4 L to liquids or solids**
   $22.4\text{ L/mol}$ applies **only to gases**. One mole of liquid water ($18\text{ g}$) occupies only $18\text{ mL}$, not $22.4\text{ L}$!

3. **Mistake 3: Assuming equal masses mean equal volumes**
   $28\text{ g}$ of $\text{N}_2$ ($22.4\text{ L}$) occupies far more space than $28\text{ g}$ of $\text{CO}_2$ ($14.25\text{ L}$) because $\text{N}_2$ contains more moles (Problem 7).

4. **Mistake 4: Mixing up millilitres and litres in molar volume calculations**
   $22.4\text{ L} = 22400\text{ mL}$. If given $224\text{ mL}$, convert to litres ($0.224\text{ L}$) before dividing by $22.4$, yielding $0.01\text{ mol}$ (not $10\text{ mol}$).

5. **Mistake 5: Misinterpreting Avogadro's Law as stating "equal masses have equal volumes"**
   Avogadro's Law states that equal volumes contain **equal numbers of molecules**, not equal mass.

6. **Mistake 6: Forgetting diatomic formulas when computing gas density**
   Oxygen gas is $\text{O}_2$ ($M = 32\text{ g/mol}$), nitrogen is $\text{N}_2$ ($M = 28\text{ g/mol}$), and chlorine is $\text{Cl}_2$ ($M = 71\text{ g/mol}$). Never use atomic weights ($16, 14, 35.5$) for elemental gases.

## Quick Recap

- **STP:** Standard Temperature and Pressure $= 273\text{ K}\ (0\ ^\circ\text{C})$ and $1\text{ atm}\ (760\text{ mm Hg})$.
- **Avogadro's Law:** At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of all gases contain equal numbers of molecules ($V \propto n$).
- **Molar volume:** $1\text{ mole of any gas at STP} = \mathbf{22.4\text{ L}}$ ($22.4\text{ dm}^3 = 22400\text{ cm}^3$).
- The mole bridge: $n = m / M$, $V = n \times 22.4\text{ L}$ (STP), $N = n \times 6.022 \times 10^{23}$.
- **Gas density at STP:** $\rho = M / 22.4\text{ g/L}$.
- Gases with $M < 29$ ($\text{He}, \text{H}_2$) float in air; gases with $M > 29$ ($\text{CO}_2, \text{O}_2, \text{Cl}_2$) sink.
- Electrolysis of water yields $\text{H}_2 : \text{O}_2 = 2 : 1$ by volume because the balanced equation produces molecules in a $2 : 1$ ratio.
- Industrial gas reactions: Coefficients in balanced gas equations represent direct volume ratios at constant $T$ and $P$ ($\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2 \to 2\text{NH}_3 \implies 1\text{ vol} + 3\text{ vol} \to 2\text{ vol}$).

3. Molar mass of $\text{He} = 4\text{ g/mol}$.
   $$n = \frac{10.0}{4} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ mol}}$$
   $$V = 2.5 \times 22.4 = \mathbf{56.0\text{ L}}$$
   *Check:* $56.0 / 22.4 = 2.5\text{ mol}$; $2.5 \times 4 = 10.0\text{ g}$ ✅.

4. Molar mass of $\text{Cl}_2 = 2 \times 35.5 = 71\text{ g/mol}$.
   $$n = \frac{67.2}{22.4} = 3.0\text{ mol}$$
   $$\text{Molecules } N = 3.0 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{1.807 \times 10^{24}\text{ molecules}}$$
   $$\text{Mass } m = 3.0 \times 71 = \mathbf{213\text{ g}}$$
   *Check:* $213 / 71 = 3.0\text{ mol}$ ✅.

5. Molar mass of $\text{Cl}_2 = 71\text{ g/mol}$.
   $$\text{Density} = \frac{71}{22.4} \approx \mathbf{3.17\text{ g/L}}$$
   Because $3.17\text{ g/L}$ is significantly greater than air ($1.29\text{ g/L}$), a chlorine-filled balloon will **sink rapidly to the ground**. *(Note: Chlorine is toxic; this is a theoretical calculation only.)*

6. From $2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$, the stoichiometric ratio of $\text{H}_2 : \text{O}_2$ is $2 : 1$. By Avogadro's Law, equal molecules occupy equal volumes, so hydrogen volume is double:
   $$V(\text{H}_2) = 2 \times 30\text{ mL} = \mathbf{60\text{ mL}}$$
   *Check:* $60 : 30 = 2 : 1$ ✅.

7. Volume ratio is $\text{N}_2 : \text{H}_2 : \text{NH}_3 = 1 : 3 : 2$.
   Given $45\text{ L}$ of $\text{H}_2$, one volume unit $= 45 / 3 = 15\text{ L}$.
   $$V(\text{N}_2) = 1 \times 15 = \mathbf{15\text{ L}}$$
   $$V(\text{NH}_3) = 2 \times 15 = \mathbf{30\text{ L}}$$
   *Check:* Ratio $15 : 45 : 30 = 1 : 3 : 2$ ✅.

8. Water at room conditions and at STP is a **liquid**, not a gas; the $22.4\text{ L/mol}$ rule applies strictly to gases.
   For $1\text{ mole}$ of liquid water ($18\text{ g}$ with density $\approx 1.0\text{ g/mL}$):
   $$V = \mathbf{18\text{ mL}} = \mathbf{0.018\text{ L}}$$
   Liquid water occupies over $1200\times$ less space than gaseous water vapour at STP ($22.4\text{ L} / 0.018\text{ L} \approx 1244$).

9. Molar mass $M = \text{density} \times 22.4 = 1.78\text{ g/L} \times 22.4\text{ L/mol} \approx \mathbf{39.9\text{ g/mol}} \approx \mathbf{40\text{ g/mol}}$.
   This gas is **argon (Ar)** ✅.

10. Moles of methane:
    $$n(\text{CH}_4) = \frac{8.0\text{ g}}{16\text{ g/mol}} = \mathbf{0.50\text{ mol}} \quad (3.011 \times 10^{23}\text{ molecules})$$
    Moles of nitrogen:
    $$n(\text{N}_2) = \frac{8.96\text{ L}}{22.4\text{ L/mol}} = \mathbf{0.40\text{ mol}} \quad (2.409 \times 10^{23}\text{ molecules})$$
    **Methane contains more molecules** ($0.50\text{ mol} > 0.40\text{ mol}$) ✅.

11. $T_1 = 273\text{ K}, \quad T_2 = 546\text{ K} = 2 \times 273\text{ K}$. Pressure is constant at $1.0\text{ atm}$.
    $$V_2 = 22.4\text{ L} \times \frac{546\text{ K}}{273\text{ K}} = \mathbf{44.8\text{ L}}$$
    Doubling the absolute Kelvin temperature doubles the molar volume.
