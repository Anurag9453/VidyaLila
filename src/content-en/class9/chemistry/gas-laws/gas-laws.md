# Topic 1: Behaviour of Gases: Pressure, Volume, Temperature, and Amount

Up to this point, you have visualised gases through a **particle model**: particles spaced far apart, zipping about randomly at high speeds, and constantly colliding with container walls. In this chapter, we take a major leap forward: learning how to **measure** a gas and asking what happens when we tweak one property while keeping others under control. In this opening topic, we will not jump straight into complex equations. First, we must master the **four fundamental measurements** of any gas (pressure, volume, temperature, and amount) alongside their **units and conversions**—because in chemistry, an incorrect unit ruins the entire calculation. We will wrap up with a preview of Boyle's Law.

## Volleyball Practice in PE Class

During an afternoon PE session, Coach Davies was setting up drills on the volleyball court. Leo grabbed a deflated, squishy volleyball from the equipment bin. "Coach, this ball is completely flat. We can't play with this!"

Coach Davies pulled a compact hand pump from his sports bag and attached the nozzle pin. "Here, Leo. Pump it up yourself, and give the ball a squeeze every few strokes."

Leo began pumping. After 5 strokes, the ball felt slightly firmer. After 15 strokes, his thumb could barely dent the surface. By 30 strokes, the ball was rock-hard and bounced energetically off the court. "Coach, it's completely rigid now!"

"Notice something interesting," said Coach Davies. "The **outer size** of the ball barely changed—the leather shell stretched only slightly. Yet the air trapped inside is now exerting an immense outward **push** against the inner walls. That push is what makes the ball feel firm. What exactly changed inside that trapped air?"

Leo paused to think. "Coach... did the pump push **more gas particles** into the same fixed space?"

"Precisely," Coach Davies nodded. "And the macroscopic effect of those extra particles bombarding the inner wall is the **pressure** you feel beneath your fingers. Let's explore what gas pressure really means, how scientists measure it, and how it connects to volume, temperature, and quantity."

Leo jotted a question in his notebook: *"What are the four measurable variables that define any gas sample?"*

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gas pressure ($P$)** | The force exerted per unit area by gas particles bombarding the container walls | The taut firmness of an inflated volleyball |
| **Volume ($V$)** | The amount of space occupied by a gas (equal to the container's interior volume) | The interior capacity of a sports ball |
| **Temperature ($T$)** | A measure of the average kinetic energy of gas particles; measured in **Kelvin** | $27\ ^\circ\text{C} = 300\text{ K}$ |
| **Amount of gas ($n$)** | The quantity of gas particles, expressed in grams or **moles** | $32\text{ g of }\text{O}_2 = 1\text{ mole}$ |

| **mm Hg** | Millimetres of mercury: pressure expressed as the height of a mercury column | $760\text{ mm Hg} = 1\text{ atm}$ |
| **Pascal (Pa)** | The SI unit of pressure: $1\text{ newton}$ of force applied uniformly over $1\text{ m}^2$ | $1\text{ atm} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ |
| **Constant** | A variable deliberately held unchanged throughout an experiment | Keeping temperature constant |

## Part A: Gas Particles and Container Walls (Recall)

Back in our study of the states of matter, we established that gas particles are separated by **vast empty spaces**, travel in **random straight lines** at incredible speeds, and disperse to fill every corner of their container. As they zoom around, they continually collide with one another and crash into the container's interior walls.

Now let us connect this microscopic picture to a measurable force. Every single particle collision imparts a tiny, momentary **outward nudge** on the wall. While an individual collision imparts an imperceptible force, a macroscopic container holds **trillions of trillions of gas particles**, colliding with every square centimetre billions of times every single second. These countless microscopic collisions blend into a smooth, steady, and continuous outward force. This distributed force per unit area is **gas pressure**.

```
   +-----------------------------------+
   | o->        <-o            o       |
   |        o                   |      |
   |  o         ->o        <-o  v      |
   |       o->           o             |
   |  <-o          o->           o->   |
   +-----------------------------------+
        ^   ^   ^   ^   ^   (bombardment on wall)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Gas particles in rapid, chaotic motion colliding relentlessly with the container walls. Each collision imparts a tiny outward force; billions of simultaneous collisions across the surface area create **gas pressure**. (Only a few particles are drawn here; real containers hold trillions upon trillions.)

In physics, you defined pressure as **force divided by area** ($P = F / A$), noting that air possesses weight and exerts atmospheric pressure (measured with barometers). Chemistry explains the microscopic *cause* behind that macroscopic fact: Physics tells us **what** pressure is, while Chemistry reveals **why** it arises.

This leads to a simple, intuitive conclusion: **more frequent or more energetic wall collisions mean higher gas pressure.** When Leo pumped extra air into the volleyball, he packed billions more particles into the same volume, causing collision frequency to soar and making the ball rock-hard.

> **Check:** *"Gas pressure exists because gas particles fall downward under gravity."* ❌ Incorrect. Gas particles move at hundreds of metres per second in all three dimensions; their collisions strike all interior surfaces equally, making gas pressure act **uniformly in every direction**, not just downward.

## Part B: The Four Measurable Properties of a Gas

To fully define the state of any gas sample, scientists monitor four key variables:

1. **Pressure ($P$):** The force per unit area exerted by particle collisions on the container walls.
2. **Volume ($V$):** The total three-dimensional space occupied by the gas (the interior capacity of its container).
3. **Temperature ($T$):** The thermal state of the gas, reflecting the average kinetic speed of its particles.
4. **Amount ($n$):** The quantity of gas particles present, measured in moles.

```
          +-------------------------------+
          |       GAS CONTAINER           |
          |                               |
          |   P : outward wall push       |
          |   V : interior volume         |
          |   T : thermal state (Kelvin)  |
          |   n : quantity of gas (mol)   |
          |                               |
          +-------------------------------+
```

> **Figure 1.2** – The four defining measurements of a gas: pressure ($P$), volume ($V$), temperature ($T$), and amount ($n$). In subsequent topics, we will isolate pairs of these variables while holding the other two constant.

These four properties are deeply interconnected. When Leo pumped the volleyball, he increased $n$ (amount of gas), which drove up $P$ (pressure), while $V$ (volume) and $T$ (temperature) remained roughly unchanged. Why gases respond this way forms the core of this chapter.

## Part C: Units of Measurement and Conversions

### Pressure Units

Pressure appears in several common units depending on the scientific context:

- **atm** (standard atmosphere): The average atmospheric pressure at sea level.
- **mm Hg** (millimetres of mercury): The vertical height of a column of mercury supported by air pressure in a standard barometer. **$1\text{ atm} = 760\text{ mm Hg} = 76\text{ cm Hg}$**. (Also called the *torr*, where $1\text{ torr} = 1\text{ mm Hg}$).
- **Pascal (Pa)**: The official SI unit ($1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ N/m}^2$). **$1\text{ atm} = 101325\text{ Pa} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$** (approximately 101.3 kPa).

To convert pressure values:
- From **atm to mm Hg**: **Multiply by 760.**
- From **mm Hg to atm**: **Divide by 760.**
- From **atm to Pa**: Multiply by $101325$ (or $\approx 1.013 \times 10^5$).

### Volume Units

- **$1\text{ L (litre)} = 1000\text{ mL} = 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ dm}^3$.**
- **$1\text{ m}^3 = 1000\text{ L}$.**
- Remember: **$1\text{ mL}$ and $1\text{ cm}^3$ represent the exact same volume.**

### Temperature: Why Kelvin Is Essential

In introductory science, you learned the Kelvin absolute temperature scale: **$T\ (\text{K}) = t\ (^\circ\text{C}) + 273$**. In all gas law calculations, **you must always express temperature in Kelvin**. Using degrees Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$) in proportionality ratios will produce completely incorrect results because zero on the Celsius scale is an arbitrary freezing point of water, not the point of zero kinetic energy.
- To convert **$^\circ\text{C}$ to K**: **Add 273** (or $273.15$ for ultra-precise work).
- To convert **K to $^\circ\text{C}$**: **Subtract 273**.

### Amount of Gas

We express the amount of gas by mass in **grams ($m$)** or particle count in **moles ($n$)**. Recall from stoichiometry:
$$n = \frac{m}{M}$$
where $m$ is mass in grams and $M$ is molar mass in $\text{g/mol}$. One mole of any gas contains **$6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles** (Avogadro's number).

```
   PRESSURE                      VOLUME                  TEMPERATURE
   -----------------------       --------------------    ------------------
   x 760    : atm  -> mm Hg      x 1000 : L  -> mL       + 273 : °C -> K
   / 760    : mm Hg -> atm       / 1000 : mL -> L        - 273 : K  -> °C
   x 101325 : atm -> Pa          x 1000 : m³ -> L
   / 101325 : Pa  -> atm         / 1000 : L  -> m³       (1 mL = 1 cm³)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Summary chart of standard conversion factors for pressure ($\text{atm}$, $\text{mm Hg}$, $\text{Pa}$), volume ($\text{L}$, $\text{mL}$, $\text{m}^3$), and temperature ($^\circ\text{C}$, $\text{K}$).

## Part D: Solved Examples on Unit Conversions

Let us walk through practical conversion exercises:

**Example 1: atm to mm Hg.** A high-pressure gas cylinder reads $3\text{ atm}$. Express this pressure in $\text{mm Hg}$.
$$3 \times 760 = \mathbf{2280\text{ mm Hg}}$$

**Example 2: Low pressure conversion.** A weather barometer registers $0.25\text{ atm}$ in a simulated vacuum chamber. What is this in $\text{mm Hg}$?
$$0.25 \times 760 = \mathbf{190\text{ mm Hg}}$$

**Example 3: atm to Pascals.** A scuba diving regulator is rated at $1.5\text{ atm}$. Express this in Pascals ($\text{Pa}$).
$$1.5 \times 101325 = 151987.5 \approx \mathbf{1.52 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}}$$

**Example 4: Volume conversions.** An inflatable beach ball holds $3.2\text{ L}$ of air. What is its volume in $\text{mL}$ and $\text{cm}^3$? A medical syringe contains $450\text{ mL}$ of air. What is its volume in litres?
$$3.2 \times 1000 = \mathbf{3200\text{ mL}} = \mathbf{3200\text{ cm}^3}$$
$$450 \div 1000 = \mathbf{0.45\text{ L}}$$

**Example 5: Celsius to Kelvin and vice versa.** A laboratory room is kept at $27\ ^\circ\text{C}$. A winter night in the Arctic plunges to $-73\ ^\circ\text{C}$. An engine cylinder reaches $400\text{ K}$. Convert all three:
$$27 + 273 = \mathbf{300\text{ K}}$$
$$-73 + 273 = \mathbf{200\text{ K}}$$
$$400 - 273 = \mathbf{127\ ^\circ\text{C}}$$

**Example 6: Combining all four parameters.** A weather balloon carries $1.2\text{ L}$ of gas at $37\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and $760\text{ mm Hg}$. Convert its volume to $\text{mL}$, temperature to $\text{K}$, and pressure to $\text{atm}$.
$$V = 1.2 \times 1000 = \mathbf{1200\text{ mL}}$$
$$T = 37 + 273 = \mathbf{310\text{ K}}$$
$$P = 760 \div 760 = \mathbf{1\text{ atm}}$$

**Example 7: Moles of gas.** How many moles are in $4\text{ g}$ of helium gas (molar mass of $\text{He} = 4\text{ g/mol}$)? How many helium atoms does this represent?
$$n = \frac{4\text{ g}}{4\text{ g/mol}} = \mathbf{1\text{ mol}}$$
$$\text{Atoms} = 1 \times 6.022 \times 10^{23} = \mathbf{6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ atoms}}$$

> **Check:** $3\text{ atm} \times 760 = 2280\text{ mm Hg}$, and inversely $2280 \div 760 = 3\text{ atm}$ ✅. Also $310 - 273 = 37\ ^\circ\text{C}$ ✅.

## Part E: Why Change Only One Variable at a Time?

Coach Davies asked Leo: "If I asked you why the volleyball became rock-hard, what single change would you name?"

"The amount of air pumped into it, Coach."

"Now imagine you pumped air into the ball while simultaneously leaving it under baking hot desert sun, while the rubber seam stretched outward. If the pressure changed, how could you possibly know which factor caused what? This highlights the cornerstone of the **scientific method**: **change only one variable at a time while keeping all other variables strictly constant.**"

This controlled approach allowed historical scientists to discover the fundamental gas laws:
- Hold **$n$ and $T$ constant**, change $V$, and observe $P$ $\rightarrow$ **Boyle's Law** (Topic 2).
- Hold **$n$ and $P$ constant**, change $T$, and observe $V$ $\rightarrow$ **Charles's Law** (Topic 3).
- Hold **$n$ and $V$ constant**, change $T$, and observe $P$ $\rightarrow$ **Gay-Lussac's Law** (Topic 4).
- Hold **$T$ and $P$ constant**, change $n$, and observe $V$ $\rightarrow$ **Avogadro's Law** (Topic 5).

Here is a quick qualitative overview of how these pairs behave:

| Held Constant | Variable Changed | Observed Response | Real-World Illustration |
|---|---|---|---|
| $T$ and $n$ | Decrease $V$ | $P$ increases | Compressing air inside a sealed syringe |
| $P$ and $n$ | Increase $T$ | $V$ increases | A hot-air balloon inflating when heated |
| $V$ and $n$ | Increase $T$ | $P$ increases | Pressure rising inside a sealed pressure cooker |
| $T$ and $V$ | Increase $n$ | $P$ increases | Pumping extra air into a rigid sports ball |

## Part F: Safe Everyday Experiments

> **Safety First:** Perform these investigations **only under adult supervision** or with your science teacher in the lab. **Never heat sealed aerosol cans, gas cylinders, or vehicle tyres**, and never leave them exposed to direct, scorching sunlight; internal pressure can surge past safety limits, risking an explosion. Never use an open flame around gas equipment.

**Investigation 1: Squeezing an inflated balloon (with an adult).**
Gently inflate a rubber party balloon to moderate size and knot the neck. Press the balloon between your palms. Notice how it **yields under your hands**, yet simultaneously **springs outward**, pushing back against your palms. That outward resistance is the cumulative force of internal particle collisions. Do not press too hard, or the rubber will rupture.

**Investigation 2: Compressing air in a needle-free syringe (with an adult).**
Take a clean plastic medical syringe (**strictly without any needle**). Pull the plunger halfway so it fills with room air, then press your thumb firmly over the front tip to create an airtight seal. Now press the plunger inward with your other hand. Notice that the plunger slides smoothly at first, but quickly becomes **stubbornly stiff**. Release your thumb, and the trapped air rushes out. As you shrunk the trapped volume, particle collisions surged, raising the internal pressure—the very phenomenon we will measure mathematically in Topic 2!

**Investigation 3: Comparing soft and firm sports balls.**
Take two identical sports balls (volleyballs or footballs)—one slightly deflated and one fully pumped. Press your thumbs into both. The deflated ball indents easily because it holds fewer gas particles ($n$), generating fewer wall collisions. The fully inflated ball resists deformation because dense particle packing produces high internal pressure ($P$).

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: "Gas pressure only acts downward due to gravity"**
   Gas pressure acts **equally in all directions** against every boundary wall because gas particles move chaotically in every direction at hundreds of metres per second.

2. **Mistake 2: "1 atm equals 100 mm Hg"**
   Standard atmospheric pressure is **$760\text{ mm Hg}$** (or $76\text{ cm Hg}$). Remember that $1\text{ cm} = 10\text{ mm}$.

3. **Mistake 3: "1 m³ equals 100 L"**
   One cubic metre is a large volume: **$1\text{ m}^3 = 1000\text{ L}$**.

4. **Mistake 4: "Millilitres and cubic centimetres are different units"**
   They are identical: **$1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3$**, and $1000\text{ mL} = 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ L}$.

5. **Mistake 5: "Temperatures can be plugged into gas equations in °C"**
   Gas calculations strictly require the absolute **Kelvin scale**: $T\ (\text{K}) = t\ (^\circ\text{C}) + 273$. Using Celsius ruins proportion ratios.

6. **Mistake 6: "The volume of a gas is the sum of the volumes of its particles"**
   The volume of a gas refers to the **capacity of the container** in which the particles are free to move. The physical volume of the individual molecules themselves is negligibly small compared to the vast empty space between them.

7. **Mistake 7: "A deflated ball contains zero air"**
   A soft ball still contains air; it simply contains fewer moles of gas at atmospheric pressure, so it does not exert the excess pressure needed to keep the skin rigid.

8. **Mistake 8: "Heating a sealed aerosol can is an interesting DIY experiment"**
   Never heat a sealed container. Rigid walls prevent expansion, causing internal pressure to spike dangerously, which can result in catastrophic failure.

## Quick Recap

- Gas particles move rapidly, chaotically, and far apart; their collective collisions against container walls generate **gas pressure**.
- Any gas sample is fully defined by four measurable variables: **$P$** (pressure), **$V$** (volume), **$T$** (temperature), and **$n$** (amount in moles).
- Standard pressure equivalence: $1\text{ atm} = 760\text{ mm Hg} = 76\text{ cm Hg} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ ($101325\text{ Pa}$).
- Standard volume equivalence: $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL} = 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 1\text{ dm}^3$; $1\text{ m}^3 = 1000\text{ L}$.
- Absolute temperature: $T\ (\text{K}) = t\ (^\circ\text{C}) + 273$. Gas laws require Kelvin.
- Quantity: $n = m / M$ (moles), where $1\text{ mole} = 6.022 \times 10^{23}$ particles.
- The scientific method isolates gas behaviour by changing one variable while holding others strictly constant.
- Never heat sealed containers, aerosol cans, or tyres.
