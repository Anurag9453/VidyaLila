# Topic 2: Laboratory Preparation of Hydrogen

In the previous topic, we learned that hydrogen is the lightest and most abundant element in the universe, yet **on Earth it is almost never found free**: it is locked up inside water, hydrocarbons, and living organisms. So if we want **hydrogen gas**, we have to **release** it from a compound. Today we will learn how this is done in a school laboratory: by reacting a metal with a **dilute acid** (an acid dissolved in plenty of water). Keep in mind: this entire topic is meant for **observing and understanding**, not doing on your own. Hydrogen is an extremely flammable gas, so every experiment involving it is performed **only by a teacher**.

---

## Prof. Kim's Memory and a Sharp "Pop"

After school, Maya was showing her science notebook to her uncle, Prof. Kim. On one page, her teacher had written: "Next week in the lab: **teacher demonstration of hydrogen**. Everyone will remain seated." Maya asked, "Uncle Kim, how does that demo work? You can't even see the gas, so how do we know hydrogen was actually made?"

Prof. Kim's eyes lit up with an old memory. "We watched the exact same demonstration when I was in school. Our teacher dropped a few **granules of zinc** into a small flask, poured in **dilute acid** from the top, and right away **bubbles** started fizzing up. Then she collected the gas in a small inverted test tube, held it upside down away from the setup, and brought a **lighted wooden splint** to its mouth. Immediately, there was a sharp, squeaky **'pop!'** The whole class jumped in surprise. Our teacher told us that this squeaky pop is the signature test for hydrogen."

Maya asked, "Wait—how did bubbles come out of just zinc and acid?" Prof. Kim smiled. "Because zinc **pushed hydrogen out** of the acid, just like iron pushed copper out of copper sulfate solution in Chapter 7. And which metal works—and which doesn't—is predicted by your **reactivity series**. Let's go through it step by step."

> **Safety first (applies to this entire topic):** Hydrogen is **highly flammable** and can burn with an **explosion** when mixed with air or oxygen. Therefore, **students never prepare, collect, burn, or test hydrogen.** Every step is performed **only by a teacher**, in **very small amounts**, with **no open flame or spark near the apparatus**, in a well-ventilated room, and behind a **safety screen**. Your job is to watch and understand. Wherever an experiment appears in this topic, it is labeled: **"For observation only (teacher demonstration)"**.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Dilute acid** | A mild acid solution mixed with plenty of water; handled only by the teacher | Dilute sulfuric acid (H₂SO₄), dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl) |
| **Thistle funnel** | A long-stemmed glass funnel used to pour liquid into a flask while its lower tip stays dipped in the liquid | Upper tube of the hydrogen generator |

| **Pop test** | The identification test for hydrogen: a lighted splint brought near a small sample produces a sharp, squeaky "pop" | Performed only by the teacher |

## Part A: Which Metals Give Hydrogen, and Why?

In Chapter 7, we studied the **reactivity series**—the list of metals arranged from most reactive to least reactive ([chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md](../chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part3.md)):

**K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, H, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au**

Notice that **hydrogen (H) is included in this list** as if it were a metal: this is a handy benchmark that lets us **compare the strength** of a metal against hydrogen. In a dilute acid, hydrogen is chemically combined. The rule is straightforward:

> **Any metal ABOVE hydrogen in the reactivity series can displace hydrogen from a dilute acid.** **Any metal BELOW hydrogen cannot.**

Just like the stronger player taking the spot of a weaker one in Chapter 7, a more reactive metal takes the place of hydrogen.

Read the reactivity series relative to the "hydrogen line":

- **Above the line, violently fast and dangerous:** K, Na, Ca (never used with acid in the lab).
- **Above the line, usable:** Mg (fast), Al (slow at first because of its surface oxide layer), Zn (moderate and steady—the best choice for the lab), Fe (slow), Pb (too slow to be practical in the lab).
- **Hydrogen (H): the reference line.**
- **Below the line, no hydrogen from dilute acid:** Cu, Hg, Ag, Au.

### Why Is Zinc Preferred in the Lab?

- **Mg** reacts very rapidly: bubbles surge out so fast that the gas is hard to control, and the flask gets hot.
- **Fe** reacts too slowly: collecting enough gas takes a long time.
- **Zn** sits right in the middle: bubbles form at a **steady, manageable pace** that the teacher can easily control. That is why **granulated zinc** is the favorite metal for school lab demonstrations.
- **Na and K** (and Ca) react violently even with cold water (as described in Chapter 8). They are **never mixed with acid**—doing so would cause a dangerous explosion.

**Maya's question: would copper turnings work instead of zinc?** No. Copper lies **below** hydrogen in the series, so it **cannot displace** hydrogen from dilute acid: no bubbles, no gas.

### Two Common Acids: Sulfuric and Hydrochloric

We use two dilute acids here (you will study acids in full detail in Class 10; for now, simply note that both contain replaceable hydrogen in solution):

- **Dilute sulfuric acid**, H₂SO₄: works smoothly with zinc, iron, or magnesium.
- **Dilute hydrochloric acid**, HCl: works smoothly with zinc or magnesium.

**Nitric acid (HNO₃) is different.** Although it is an acid, it behaves as a strong oxidizing agent with metals and generally **does not release hydrogen gas**. Therefore, nitric acid is **not** used to prepare hydrogen in the lab. You will learn the full reason in Class 10; for now, remember that **not every acid follows the simple "metal + acid = hydrogen" rule.**

## Part B: The Apparatus: Flask, Thistle Funnel, and Delivery Tube

**For observation only (teacher demonstration).** The teacher sets up a flat-bottomed or **conical flask** fitted with an **airtight two-holed cork**. One hole holds a **thistle funnel**, and the other holds a bent **delivery tube**. The stem of the thistle funnel is long enough that its lower tip stays **submerged beneath the surface of the acid** (this is crucial, as we will see in a moment). First, **zinc granules** are placed on the bottom of the flask, and then **dilute acid** is poured in through the thistle funnel.

```
                \         /
                 \       /      <- thistle funnel
                  \     /          (dilute acid is poured in here)
                   \   /
                    │ │       ┌──────────────> to the gas collection jar
                    │ │       │   <- delivery tube (gas exits through here)
         cork -> [==│ │=======│==]
                 │  │ │       ╵  │       <- lower end of delivery tube sits
                /   │ │           \         just below the cork, well above acid
               /    │ │            \
              /     │ │             \
             │      │ │              │
             │~~~~~~│~│~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│   <- level of dilute acid
             │      │ │              │   <- tip of thistle funnel dipped in acid
             │   Zn   Zn   Zn   Zn   │   <- zinc granules
             └───────────────────────┘
                   conical flask
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Apparatus for preparing hydrogen (for observation only, teacher demonstration): conical flask, airtight cork, thistle funnel, and delivery tube. The lower tip of the thistle funnel dips **below** the acid level so that the hydrogen gas cannot escape back up the funnel. The other end of the delivery tube leads to the collection jar. Schematic diagram, not to scale.

**Why must the tip of the thistle funnel dip below the acid?** As soon as hydrogen gas forms, it rises toward the top of the flask. If the tip of the funnel were left **above** the liquid level, the gas would simply **escape out through the open funnel** instead of traveling through the delivery tube (and escaping hydrogen in the room is a fire hazard). Submerging the tip in the acid creates a **liquid seal** that blocks the funnel path and forces all the gas through the delivery tube.

**Why must the cork be airtight?** If hydrogen leaks around the cork, the jar will not fill properly and the gas will spread into the room, where it can form an **explosive mixture** with air. That is why the teacher checks the setup for leaks beforehand and performs the demonstration in a well-ventilated space, far from any flame, behind a safety screen.

## Part C: How Hydrogen Is Collected

Hydrogen can be collected in two ways. Each method relies on one physical property of hydrogen: **first, it is almost insoluble in water; second, it is much lighter than air.**

### Method 1: Collection Over Water (Downward Displacement of Water)

In a wide trough of water, a **gas jar completely filled with water** is inverted so its mouth stays underwater. The tip of the delivery tube is positioned beneath the mouth of the jar. Bubbles of hydrogen rise into the jar, **push the water downward**, and fill the jar with hydrogen gas.

```
          inverted jar (initially completely filled with water)
              ┌───────────┐
              │           │
              │  H₂ gas   │   <- gas collects at the top
              │           │
              │- - - - - -│   <- water level inside jar pushed down
              │~~~~~~~~~~~│
   ~~~~~~~~~~~│~~~~~~~~~~~│~~~~~~~~~~~   <- water level in trough
   ~~~~~~~~~~~│~~~ o ~~~~~│~~~~~~~~~~~   <- mouth of jar underwater
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ o ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
        bubbles from delivery tube rise
        up through the mouth of the jar
   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Collecting hydrogen over water (for observation only, teacher demonstration). Gas from the delivery tube rises to the top of the inverted jar and pushes the water downward into the trough. How far the water level drops shows how much gas has collected. Schematic diagram.

**Why does this work?** Hydrogen is **sparingly soluble in water** (it barely dissolves). So as it bubbles through water, almost none of it is lost. This method has two extra advantages: you can clearly see the water level drop as the jar fills, and the water in the trough forms a natural **seal** that keeps air out. The only drawback is that gas collected over water contains **a little moisture (water vapor)**.

### Method 2: Downward Displacement of Air

Hydrogen is **much lighter than air** (from Chapter 10: density of hydrogen is about 0.09 g per L, while air is 1.29 g per L, [gas-laws-part5.md](../gas-laws/gas-laws-part5.md)). So if an empty gas jar is held **upside down (mouth pointing down)** and the delivery tube is led up near the top inside the jar, the light hydrogen rises to the **top of the inverted jar** and **pushes the heavier air downward and out through the open mouth**.

```
            ┌───────────┐   <- jar held upside down (closed end on top)
            │ H₂  ╷  H₂ │   <- light hydrogen collects at the top
            │  H₂ │ H₂  │
            │     │     │
            │  ↓  │  ↓  │   <- heavier air is pushed downward
            │ air │ air │
            │     │     │
            ↓     │     ↓   <- open mouth at bottom: air exits here
                  │
     ─────────────┘
     delivery tube (extending from flask up to the top of the jar)
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Collecting hydrogen by downward displacement of air (for observation only, teacher demonstration). The jar is held upside down with its mouth open at the bottom. Because hydrogen is lighter than air, it stays at the top and pushes the heavier air down and out. The jar must remain inverted, or the hydrogen will float right out. Schematic diagram.

**Note carefully:** this method works only for a gas that is **lighter than air**. For a heavier-than-air gas (like CO₂ in Chapter 10), the jar is kept upright, not inverted. For hydrogen, the jar must be inverted because hydrogen rises. Advantage of this method: the collected gas stays **dry** (no contact with liquid water). Drawback: because hydrogen is so light and diffuses rapidly, the jar must be **covered quickly**, and some mixing with air can occur—which can form an explosive mixture. That is why **only the teacher** handles and tests it.

### Comparing the Two Collection Methods

| Method | Why it works | Condition of gas | Key precaution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Over water | Hydrogen barely dissolves in water | Slightly moist | Watch the water level drop to see when the jar is full |
| Downward displacement of air | Hydrogen is much lighter than air | Dry | Keep the jar upside down and cover its mouth quickly |

## Part D: Drying the Gas

Hydrogen collected over water carries a small amount of **moisture (water vapor)**. If completely **dry** hydrogen is needed, the gas is passed through a **drying agent**. In Chapter 9 we studied drying agents ([water-part4.md](../water/water-part4.md)): substances that absorb moisture from air or gases.

Two common drying agents used for hydrogen are:

- **Concentrated sulfuric acid**, placed in a small wash bottle through which the gas bubbles. The acid absorbs the water vapor. **Concentrated sulfuric acid is severely corrosive** (causes chemical burns), so **only the teacher** handles it.
- **Anhydrous calcium chloride (CaCl₂)**, packed as solid granules inside a U-tube; as the gas passes between the granules, moisture is trapped by the CaCl₂.

**For observation only (teacher demonstration):** drying the gas is also performed only by the teacher, in small quantities, away from flames and behind a safety screen.

Remember: **once hydrogen has been dried, never collect it over water** (otherwise it will pick up moisture all over again!). Dry hydrogen must be collected by **downward displacement of air**.

## Part E: Identification: The "Pop" Test

Now let's understand the science behind the "pop" that Prof. Kim's teacher demonstrated.

> **For observation only (teacher demonstration).** The teacher collects a **very small sample** of the gas in a small test tube, holding the tube upside down (since light hydrogen stays at the top). Keeping the test tube **well away from the reaction flask and apparatus**, the teacher brings a **lighted wooden splint** to the mouth of the tube while **students stay seated at a safe distance**. If the gas is hydrogen, it burns with a **sharp, squeaky "pop" sound**.

**Why does it "pop"?** At the mouth of the tube, the hydrogen mixes with a little air. The heat of the burning splint ignites the hydrogen, causing it to **react with oxygen in the air in a fraction of a second** to form **water vapor**: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O** (the combustion reaction from Chapter 7). The hot water vapor expands suddenly, sending out a tiny pressure wave in the air that our ears hear as a "pop." In a tiny test tube, this is just a **harmless squeak**, but **in a larger container it can be a dangerous explosion.** That is why only the teacher performs the test, using a tiny test tube far from the generator.

After the pop, **tiny droplets of water** can often be seen misting the cool inner wall of the test tube—further proof that burning hydrogen forms water.

**Is the "pop" test enough to identify hydrogen?** Yes, the squeaky pop test is the **classic classroom test** for hydrogen: no other common laboratory gas burns with this characteristic high-pitched pop.

## Part F: Equations for the Reaction and Atom Checks

Now let's write the balanced chemical equations for this preparation (using the Chapter 3 method: counting every atom on both sides).

### Zinc + dilute sulfuric acid

> **Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂**

- Zn: 1 and 1
- H: 2 and 2
- S: 1 and 1
- O: 4 and 4

Balanced ✅. **ZnSO₄ (zinc sulfate)** is a salt that remains dissolved in the liquid; **H₂ gas** fizzes out as bubbles. Notice how one zinc atom displaced the 2 hydrogen atoms of **H₂SO₄** (a single-displacement reaction from Chapter 7).

### Zinc + dilute hydrochloric acid

> **Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂**

- Zn: 1 and 1
- H: 2 and 2
- Cl: 2 and 2

Balanced ✅. Here we need **2HCl** because one ZnCl₂ unit requires 2 Cl atoms and one H₂ molecule requires 2 H atoms.

### Magnesium + dilute hydrochloric acid

> **Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂**

- Mg: 1 and 1; H: 2 and 2; Cl: 2 and 2. Balanced ✅.

### Iron + dilute sulfuric acid

> **Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂**

- Fe: 1 and 1; H: 2 and 2; S: 1 and 1; O: 4 and 4. Balanced ✅.

All four equations follow one general pattern:

> **Metal (above hydrogen) + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen gas**

### Checking Conservation of Mass (Chapter 4 Rule)

Atomic masses: H = 1, O = 16, Mg = 24, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Zn = 65.

**Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂**

- H₂SO₄ = 2 × 1 + 32 + 4 × 16 = **98**
- ZnSO₄ = 65 + 32 + 64 = **161**
- Reactants: 65 + 98 = **163**. Products: 161 + 2 = **163** ✅

**Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂**

- 2HCl = 2 × (1 + 35.5) = **73**; ZnCl₂ = 65 + 2 × 35.5 = **136**
- Reactants: 65 + 73 = **138**. Products: 136 + 2 = **138** ✅

Notice something striking in both reactions: **65 g of zinc** yields only **2 g of hydrogen** (1 mole of Zn produces 1 mole of H₂, and 1 mole of H₂ weighs just 2 g). Even though a heavy piece of zinc reacts, hydrogen is so light that its mass in grams is tiny.

## Part G: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Which metals will release hydrogen?

Which of the following metals will release hydrogen gas from dilute hydrochloric acid: **Mg, Cu, Fe, Ag, Zn**?

Reactivity series: K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, **H**, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au.

- Mg: above H, so **yes**
- Zn: above H, so **yes**
- Fe: above H, so **yes** (slowly)
- Cu: below H, so **no**
- Ag: below H, so **no**

**Check:** The first three (Mg, Zn, Fe) sit above the hydrogen line, while Cu and Ag sit below it ✅

### Example 2. Comparing two unknown metals

An unknown metal **X** fizzes briskly in dilute sulfuric acid, while another unknown metal **Y** produces no bubbles at all. Which of the two sits above hydrogen in the reactivity series?

- **X** sits **above** hydrogen (it displaced hydrogen from the acid).
- **Y** sits **below** hydrogen (no hydrogen was displaced).

Therefore, X is more reactive than Y.

**Check:** Bubbles = hydrogen released = above H. No bubbles = below H ✅

### Example 3. Role of the thistle funnel

On a worksheet, a student writes: "Keeping the tip of the thistle funnel above the acid level makes pouring easier." What is wrong with this setup?

- If the tip of the thistle funnel sits **above** the acid surface, the hydrogen gas produced will escape straight up through the funnel.
- Very little gas will reach the delivery tube, and flammable hydrogen will leak into the room—which is **dangerous**.
- Therefore, the tip must stay **submerged beneath the acid level**.

**Check:** The liquid acid seals the bottom of the funnel so the gas can only exit through the delivery tube ✅

### Example 4. Choosing a collection method

A teacher needs a jar of **dry** hydrogen. Which collection method should she use: over water, or downward displacement of air? Why?

- Collecting over water introduces **moisture (water vapor)** into the gas.
- To get dry hydrogen, the gas is first passed through a **drying agent** (such as conc. H₂SO₄ or anhydrous CaCl₂) and then collected by **downward displacement of air** in an inverted jar (since hydrogen is lighter than air).

**Check:** Keeping the dried gas away from liquid water prevents moisture from re-entering ✅

### Example 5. Mass calculation

In the reaction **Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂**, if **130 g** of zinc reacts completely, how many grams of hydrogen gas will be produced?

- 65 g of Zn produces 2 g of H₂ (1 : 1 mole ratio in the balanced equation).
- 130 g of Zn = 130 ÷ 65 = **2 mol Zn**.
- 2 mol of Zn produces 2 mol of H₂, which is 2 × 2 = **4 g of H₂**.

**Check:** 130 ÷ 65 = 2 mol ✅; 2 mol H₂ × 2 g = 4 g ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Any metal and any acid will produce hydrogen."**

No. The metal must sit **above** hydrogen in the reactivity series (Cu, Ag, and Au will not react), and oxidizing acids like nitric acid behave differently with metals and generally do not give hydrogen.

**Mistake 2: "Sodium or potassium are more reactive, so they are better choices for the lab."**

More reactive here means violently dangerous. Sodium and potassium react explosively even with water and must never be put into acid. Zinc gives a **steady, controllable** reaction.

**Mistake 3: "Collect hydrogen in an upright jar filled with air."**

Hydrogen is **much lighter than air**. In an upright jar, it will float right out the top. Always use an **inverted jar** (downward displacement of air) or collect it over water.

**Mistake 4: "Collect dried hydrogen over water."**

Collecting over water puts moisture right back into the gas. Dried hydrogen must be collected by downward displacement of air.

**Mistake 5: "We can try the pop test at home."**

Absolutely not. Hydrogen is highly flammable and forms explosive mixtures with air. **The pop test—and the entire preparation of hydrogen—is strictly a teacher demonstration.**

## Quick Recap

- In the lab, hydrogen is prepared from **a metal + dilute acid**: **Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂** and **Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂**; also **Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂** and **Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂**.
- Metals **above hydrogen** in the reactivity series (Mg, Zn, Fe...) displace hydrogen from dilute acids; metals **below hydrogen** (Cu, Hg, Ag, Au) do not.
- Zinc is ideal for the school lab: **neither too fast nor too slow**. Na and K are dangerously explosive; **nitric acid behaves differently** and does not give hydrogen (studied in higher classes).
- Apparatus: **flask, airtight cork, thistle funnel (tip dipped below the acid surface), delivery tube**.
- Collection: **over water** (because hydrogen is sparingly soluble in water) or by **downward displacement of air** in an inverted jar (because hydrogen is lighter than air).
- Drying: **concentrated H₂SO₄** or **anhydrous CaCl₂** (drying agents from Chapter 9); never collect dried gas over water.
- Identification: a **lighted splint produces a squeaky "pop"**.
- Mass is conserved: 65 + 98 = 161 + 2; 65 g of Zn gives 2 g of H₂.
- **Safety:** hydrogen is highly flammable; **every step is strictly a teacher demonstration**—small quantities, no flames nearby, good ventilation, safety screen. Never performed by students.
