# Topic 3: Other Ways of Making Hydrogen

In the previous topic, we saw how hydrogen is made in a school lab from **zinc and dilute acid**, prepared cautiously in tiny amounts by a teacher as a demonstration. Yet the modern world needs hydrogen by the **millions of tonnes**: for making fertilizers, for oil refineries, and increasingly as a clean fuel for the future. You cannot supply factories with a conical flask of zinc granules and acid! So the question is: **what other ways exist** to make hydrogen, which methods belong to the lab, which belong to industry, and which ones are **truly clean**? Today we will explore how hydrogen is produced from water, natural gas, coke, salt water (brine), and solar power.

---

## The Solar Panel on the School Roof

A brand-new solar panel had just been installed on the school roof. Ms. Alvarez, the chemistry teacher, walked up to inspect it with Liam. Underneath the panel sat a small charge controller and a rechargeable battery box with a glowing green indicator light. "Ms. Alvarez, the panel only makes electricity while the sun is shining," Liam remarked. "Once the sun goes down, generation stops. The battery stores some electricity, sure, but our physics teacher told us yesterday that giant batteries are heavy, expensive, and eventually wear out..."

Ms. Alvarez smiled. "Spot on. Storing huge amounts of electricity is tough. So let's ask an interesting question: **can we use sunlight to make a substance that we can store in a tank and use tomorrow, or next month, or ship to another city as a fuel?**" Liam thought for a moment. "Well, the solar panel gives electricity. And in our chemistry class demo, electricity split water into two gases... hydrogen and oxygen!" Ms. Alvarez clapped her hands. "There you go—you just figured out the idea behind **green hydrogen**. Solar electricity plus water gives a gas that produces nothing but pure water when you use it."

Then Liam asked a follow-up question: "Wait—if that's the cleanest way, why doesn't the whole world make all its hydrogen like that already?" In this topic, we will look at **all the major methods** of making hydrogen, and see why the cleanest method is not yet the most common one.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electrolysis** | Splitting a compound using an electric current | Splitting water into H₂ and O₂ |
| **Steam** | Water in the gas phase at high temperature | Hot steam from a boiler |
| **Steam reforming** | Reacting natural gas (methane) with hot steam over a catalyst to produce hydrogen | CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂ |
| **Natural gas** | Underground fossil fuel gas consisting mostly of methane (CH₄) | Pipeline gas / CNG |
| **Coke** | A black, nearly pure carbon solid made by heating coal without air | Fuel used in steel blast furnaces |
| **Water gas** | A mixture of CO and H₂ made by passing steam over red-hot coke | C + H₂O → CO + H₂ |
| **Bosch step** | Reacting CO with more steam over an iron oxide catalyst to produce extra H₂ and CO₂ | CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ |
| **Brine** | A concentrated solution of common salt (NaCl) in water | Saltwater feed in chemical plants |
| **By-product** | A secondary product formed alongside the main product of a reaction | H₂ formed during the electrolysis of brine |
| **Green hydrogen** | Hydrogen made by electrolyzing water using renewable electricity (solar or wind) | Rooftop solar panel + electrolyzer |

## Part A: Hydrogen from Water

### 1. Electrolysis of Water (Recall from Chapter 7)

In Chapter 7, we saw that passing an electric current through water splits it into **two gases**:

2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂

**For observation only (teacher demonstration).** The teacher connects a low-voltage DC battery to two electrodes immersed in water (since pure water barely conducts electricity, a suitable electrolyte is added). Bubbles of **hydrogen** form at the negative electrode, and bubbles of **oxygen** form at the positive electrode. The volume of hydrogen collected is **twice** the volume of oxygen, matching the 2H₂ to 1 O₂ ratio in the equation (by Avogadro's law from Chapter 10: at the same temperature and pressure, gas volume is proportional to the number of molecules, [gas-laws-part5.md](../gas-laws/gas-laws-part5.md)). The teacher performs this demo in small quantities, away from flames, in a well-ventilated room behind a safety screen. **Students must never attempt this at home, and never use wall-socket electricity**: a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen can explode violently from a single spark.

```
        Hydrogen (−)         Oxygen (+)
            tube                tube
            ┌──┐                ┌──┐
            │H₂│                │O₂│
            │H₂│                │O₂│
            │H₂│                │~~│
            │H₂│                │~~│
        ┌───│~~│────────────────│~~│───┐
        │~~~│~~│~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│~~│~~~│  <- water + a little electrolyte
        │~~~│[]│~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│[]│~~~│  <- electrodes ([ ])
        │~~~~│~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│~~~~~│
        └────┼───────────────────┼─────┘
             │                   │
             └─(−)  battery  (+)─┘

        Volume of Hydrogen : Volume of Oxygen = 2 : 1
        (The gas column in the H₂ tube is twice as tall)
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Electrolysis of water (for observation only, teacher demonstration): hydrogen collects in the inverted tube over the negative (−) electrode, and oxygen collects over the positive (+) electrode, pushing the water downward. The volume of hydrogen is twice the volume of oxygen (2 : 1) because 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂.

This method yields **very pure hydrogen** (since the two gases collect in separate tubes), but it **consumes a large amount of electricity**, so it is economical only where electricity is cheap or renewable. Today it is used for high-purity needs and increasingly in large solar- and wind-powered plants.

### 2. Active Metals and Cold Water (Description Only)

Metals at the very top of the reactivity series (Chapter 7) are so reactive that they displace hydrogen even from **cold water**:

2Na + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + H₂

2K + 2H₂O → 2KOH + H₂

Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂

Sodium and potassium react **violently**: the metal skitters across the water surface, releasing so much heat that the escaping hydrogen often ignites on its own, sometimes with an explosion (recall the story of alkali metals in Chapter 8). Calcium reacts more gently: steady bubbles rise and the water turns milky (because the Ca(OH)₂ formed is only slightly soluble in water). **We study these reactions on paper only. Students never perform them**: handing sodium or potassium to students, even in tiny pieces, is strictly forbidden. Because these metals are expensive and hazardous, this is never used as an industrial method to make hydrogen.

### 3. Metals and Steam (In Brief)

Slightly less active metals, such as magnesium (and red-hot iron), barely react with cold water but react with **hot steam**:

Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂

Here the metal oxide is formed along with hydrogen gas. This too is **for reading only**: generating flammable hydrogen near red-hot metal is dangerous and handled only by trained specialists.

### Check (Part A)

Does copper release hydrogen from cold water? ❌ No: copper lies **below** hydrogen in the reactivity series, so it cannot displace hydrogen from water (Chapter 7 and Topic 2 of this chapter).

## Part B: Industrial Methods: From Natural Gas and Coke

Around the world today, **most industrial hydrogen** is made not by splitting pure water, but from **fossil fuels**. The reason is simple: these processes are currently **cheaper at massive scale**. Fossil fuels like natural gas already contain plenty of combined hydrogen (each methane molecule, CH₄, holds 4 hydrogen atoms), so industry uses **hot steam** to strip that hydrogen out and add even more from the steam itself.

### Step 1: Steam Reforming (From Natural Gas)

Natural gas consists mainly of **methane (CH₄)**. At high temperature and pressure, methane is reacted with steam over a nickel catalyst:

CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂

One methane molecule and one water molecule yield **3 hydrogen molecules** and **1 carbon monoxide molecule**. This mixture of CO + H₂ is often called **synthesis gas** (or syngas).

### Step 2: Coke and Steam (Water Gas)

Where natural gas is scarce, red-hot **coke** (nearly pure carbon made from coal) is blasted with superheated steam:

C + H₂O → CO + H₂

This produces **water gas**, a mixture of CO and H₂. This older industrial route is well known in coal-rich regions around the world.

### Step 3: The Bosch Step (Converting CO into Extra Hydrogen)

In both routes above, carbon monoxide (CO) is left mixed with the hydrogen. Rather than wasting the CO, factories react the gas mixture with **more steam** over an **iron oxide catalyst**:

CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂

This step (often called the water-gas shift or Bosch step) produces **additional hydrogen** and converts toxic CO into **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**.

### Step 4: Removing the CO₂

Now the hydrogen is mixed with CO₂. To separate them, the mixture is **scrubbed with water under high pressure**: CO₂ dissolves readily in pressurized water, whereas hydrogen barely dissolves at all (as we saw in Topic 2). The undissolved gas that emerges is **nearly pure hydrogen**.

```
    NATURAL GAS (methane)          COKE (solid carbon)
          │                               │
          v                               v
    +------------------+          +------------------+
    │ 1. Steam         │          │ 2. Water gas     │
    │    reforming     │          │    C + H₂O       │
    │  CH₄ + H₂O       │          │    -> CO + H₂    │
    │  -> CO + 3H₂     │          │                  │
    +------------------+          +------------------+
               \                       /
                v                     v
             +---------------------------+
             │  Mixture of CO + H₂       │
             │  (synthesis gas)          │
             +---------------------------+
                          │
                          v
             +---------------------------+
             │ 3. Bosch step             │
             │  CO + H₂O -> CO₂ + H₂     │
             │  (iron oxide catalyst)    │
             +---------------------------+
                          │
                          v
             +---------------------------+
             │ 4. Remove CO₂             │
             │  (dissolve in water       │
             │   under high pressure)    │
             +---------------------------+
                          │
                          v
                   PURE HYDROGEN
```

> **Figure 3.2** – The industrial route: methane or coke reacts with steam to form a mixture of CO and H₂, the Bosch step converts CO with more steam into extra H₂ and CO₂, and finally CO₂ is washed out in pressurized water.

**Important note:** these fossil-fuel routes are **not clean** if the resulting CO₂ is vented into the sky, because CO₂ is a greenhouse gas (which we will study in the next chapter, "Atmospheric Pollution"). That is why the energy industry uses **color labels** to distinguish how hydrogen is made (see Part D).

### Overall Accounting for Natural Gas Reforming

Let's add the two equations together:

- Step 1: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂
- Step 3: CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂

Adding them (CO cancels on both sides): **CH₄ + 2H₂O → CO₂ + 4H₂**

Thus, **1 methane molecule** ultimately yields **4 hydrogen molecules** (8 H atoms): 4 H atoms come from the methane, and 4 H atoms come from the 2 water molecules. Half the hydrogen comes from the methane, and half comes from the steam!

## Part C: Brine Electrolysis By-product and Green Hydrogen

### Hydrogen as a By-product of Brine Electrolysis

In the chlor-alkali industry, an electric current is passed through **brine (a concentrated solution of NaCl in water)** primarily to manufacture **chlorine gas** and **sodium hydroxide (NaOH)** (used in making soap, paper, and bleach). During this process, **hydrogen gas is automatically produced** at the negative electrode:

2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂

Here, hydrogen is **not the main target**; it is a valuable **by-product**. The plant runs to make chlorine and NaOH, and collects the hydrogen alongside them to sell or use as clean fuel on-site. (You will study the full chlor-alkali process in higher classes.)

### Green Hydrogen: Powered by Sun and Wind

Now let's return to Liam's rooftop question. Electrolyzing water is completely **clean** when the electricity driving it is also **clean**. When the electricity comes from **solar panels** or **wind turbines**:

2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂   (powered by solar or wind electricity)

Hydrogen made this way is called **green hydrogen**: zero CO₂ is emitted when making it (the only other product is oxygen), and when the hydrogen is later burned or used in a fuel cell, it turns right back into **pure water**.

```
       SUN
       \│/
     --- O ---
       /│\
        │
        v
   +-----------+   electricity  +------------------+
   │  SOLAR    │ -------------> │  ELECTROLYZER    │
   │  PANEL    │                │  (splits water)  │
   +-----------+                +------------------+
                                  │             │
                         water ---+             │
                         in                     │
                                                v
                            +----------+   +----------+
                            │ HYDROGEN │   │ OXYGEN   │
                            │ (H₂)     │   │ (O₂)     │
                            +----------+   +----------+
                                 │
                                 v
                    STORE IT: in tanks or pipelines,
                    and use later as a clean fuel
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Green hydrogen: electricity from a solar panel powers an electrolyzer that splits water into H₂ and O₂. The hydrogen can be stored and used later as a zero-emission fuel.

**Global initiatives:** Around the world—from the US and Europe to the UAE, Japan, and India (which launched its **National Green Hydrogen Mission** in 2023 with an approximate target of producing around 5 million tonnes per year by 2030)—countries with abundant sunshine and wind are investing heavily in **green hydrogen** to replace fossil fuels in refineries, fertilizer plants, and steelmaking.

**Why isn't green hydrogen cheap yet?** Because splitting water by electrolysis requires **a large amount of electricity**, and building large electrolyzers and renewable power plants takes major investment. See the solved examples below for the numbers.

## Part D: The "Color Names" of Hydrogen (A Quick Note)

Scientists and engineers often give hydrogen a **color label** based on **how it was produced**. Remember that these are just **industry code names**—hydrogen gas itself has no color at all (it is completely **colorless**):

- **Grey hydrogen:** made from natural gas (steam reforming), with the resulting CO₂ released into the atmosphere.
- **Blue hydrogen:** made from natural gas just like grey hydrogen, except the CO₂ is **captured and stored deep underground** so it does not enter the atmosphere.
- **Green hydrogen:** made by electrolyzing water using renewable electricity from solar or wind power.

(Hydrogen made from coal is sometimes called black or brown hydrogen; terminology can vary slightly by region, so simply remember the core idea: **the "color" describes the production pathway and its carbon footprint**.)

## Part E: Comparing the Methods

| Method | Raw materials | Main products | Clean or not? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lab: metal + dilute acid | Zinc or magnesium, dilute acid | Hydrogen (and a salt) | Tiny scale, strictly for teacher demonstration |
| Water electrolysis (grid electricity) | Water, electricity | Hydrogen and oxygen | Depends on how the grid electricity was generated |
| Green hydrogen | Water, solar or wind electricity | Hydrogen and oxygen | Clean: zero CO₂ emitted |
| Steam reforming (natural gas) | Methane, steam | Hydrogen and CO₂ | Not clean if CO₂ is vented (grey); cleaner if CO₂ is captured (blue) |
| Water gas (coke) | Coke, steam | Hydrogen, CO, then CO₂ | Not clean: releases large amounts of CO₂ |
| Brine electrolysis | Salt solution, electricity | Chlorine, NaOH, and hydrogen as by-product | Hydrogen is a by-product, not the primary goal |
| Sodium or potassium + water | Active metal, water | Hydrogen and a metal hydroxide | Description only: violent and expensive, never used in industry |

**The big takeaway:** no single method wins on every count today. The cheapest large-scale method (fossil fuel reforming) is not clean, while the cleanest method (green hydrogen) is still more expensive. Bridging that gap is one of today's biggest engineering challenges.

## Part F: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (electrolysis gas volumes):** In a teacher's demonstration of water electrolysis, **24 mL** of hydrogen is collected. How much oxygen is collected, and what is the total volume of gas?

- Hydrogen : oxygen = 2 : 1
- Oxygen = 24 ÷ 2 = **12 mL**
- Total gas = 24 + 12 = **36 mL**

**Check:** 24 ÷ 12 = 2 ✅ (2 : 1). And in the balanced equation 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, 2 molecules of H₂ form for every 1 molecule of O₂: H = 4 = 4, O = 2 = 2 ✅.

**Example 2 (counting molecules in natural gas reforming):** In a reactor, **100 methane molecules** undergo steam reforming (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂). How many hydrogen molecules form in Step 1? How many more form in the Bosch step (CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂)? What is the total?

- Step 1: 100 × 3 = **300** H₂ (plus 100 CO)
- Step 3: 100 CO → 100 × 1 = **100** H₂ (plus 100 CO₂)
- Total = 300 + 100 = **400** H₂

**Check:** From the combined equation CH₄ + 2H₂O → CO₂ + 4H₂, 100 methane molecules give 100 × 4 = 400 H₂ ✅. Also, 100 CO₂ molecules form—the exact CO₂ that must be scrubbed out in Step 4.

**Example 3 (mass balance in the water gas reaction):** For the reaction C + H₂O → CO + H₂, use relative atomic masses (C = 12, H = 1, O = 16) to check whether mass is conserved on both sides.

- Left side: C = 12, H₂O = 2 × 1 + 16 = 18, total = 12 + 18 = **30**
- Right side: CO = 12 + 16 = 28, H₂ = 2, total = 28 + 2 = **30**

**Check:** 30 = 30 ✅ (conservation of mass, Chapter 4). That means 12 g of coke and 18 g of steam yield 28 g of CO and 2 g of hydrogen.

**Example 4 (how much hydrogen from water?):** How many kilograms of hydrogen can be obtained by electrolyzing **9 kg of water**? (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂, H = 1, O = 16.)

- Mass of 2H₂O = 2 × 18 = 36 u; mass of 2H₂ = 2 × 2 = 4 u
- Mass ratio of water : hydrogen = 36 : 4 = 9 : 1
- Therefore, 9 kg of water yields **1 kg of hydrogen** (and 8 kg of oxygen)

**Check:** 1 + 8 = 9 ✅. Also, 9 kg = 9,000 g = 500 × 18 g, so 500 mol of water gives 500 mol of H₂ = 500 × 2 g = 1,000 g = 1 kg ✅.

**Example 5 (green hydrogen electricity calculation):** A small solar array generates **4 kW** for **5 hours**. Suppose producing 1 kg of hydrogen by electrolysis requires about **50 kWh** of electricity (an approximate rounded figure for practice). How much hydrogen is produced?

- Electricity generated = 4 kW × 5 h = **20 kWh**
- Hydrogen produced = 20 ÷ 50 = **0.4 kg = 400 g**

**Check:** 0.4 kg × 50 kWh/kg = 20 kWh ✅.

**Example 6 (comparing energy in vs. energy out):** Burning 1 kg of hydrogen releases about **120 MJ** of energy (approximate). Since 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ, how many kWh of energy is that? If it took 50 kWh of electricity to make that 1 kg of hydrogen, what percentage of the electrical energy is stored in the hydrogen?

- 120 ÷ 3.6 = **33.3 kWh** (approx.)
- Fraction recovered = 33.3 ÷ 50 × 100 ≈ **67%** (approx.)

**Check:** 33.3 × 3.6 = 119.88 ≈ 120 ✅. In other words, you do not get back 100% of the electrical energy you put in—about a third is lost as heat during electrolysis. That is one reason green hydrogen is comparatively expensive.

## Part G: Reasoning: "Which Method When?"

| Situation | Best method | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Demonstrating a tiny sample of hydrogen in a school lab (teacher demo) | Zinc + dilute acid | Small scale, simple glass apparatus |
| A large fertilizer plant that needs cheap hydrogen in bulk near a natural gas pipeline | Steam reforming | Lowest cost at massive industrial scale |
| A sunny or windy region aiming for zero-carbon hydrogen | Electrolysis using solar/wind power (green) | Emits no CO₂ |
| A chlor-alkali plant making chlorine and NaOH | Brine electrolysis | Hydrogen is obtained automatically as a by-product |

**Check (spotting a bad idea):** "Dropping sodium into water is a great industrial way to manufacture hydrogen." ❌ False: it is violently explosive, dangerous, and far too expensive for industry.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Green, grey, and blue hydrogen have different colors."**

False. Hydrogen is always a **colorless** gas. The color words are just industry labels for **how it was made**.

**Mistake 2: "Any water electrolysis gives green hydrogen."**

False. It is only "green" when the electricity comes from **renewable sources like solar or wind**. If the electricity comes from burning coal, the hydrogen is not green.

**Mistake 3: "Brine electrolysis is run mainly to make hydrogen."**

False. Its primary products are chlorine and sodium hydroxide (NaOH). Hydrogen is a **by-product**.

**Mistake 4: "Students can test the sodium + water reaction to collect hydrogen."**

Never. Alkali metal reactions with water are described for understanding only; they are far too dangerous for students to handle.

**Mistake 5: "Steam reforming is completely clean because it uses steam (water)."**

False. It uses methane (a fossil fuel) and produces **CO₂** gas, so unless that CO₂ is captured, it is not clean.

**Mistake 6: "Because hydrogen burns cleanly to water, all hydrogen is automatically 100% green."**

False. To judge how clean a fuel is, you must look at the **entire lifecycle**: raw materials, electricity source, and whether CO₂ was released during production.

## Quick Recap

- **From water:** electrolysis 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (volume ratio 2 : 1, teacher demo). Active metals + cold water (Na, K, Ca) and Mg + steam: **for reading/description only**.
- **Steam reforming:** CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂. **Water gas:** C + H₂O → CO + H₂. **Bosch step:** CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ (iron oxide catalyst). CO₂ is removed by dissolving it in water under high pressure. Overall methane equation: CH₄ + 2H₂O → CO₂ + 4H₂.
- **Brine electrolysis:** 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂ (hydrogen is a by-product).
- **Green hydrogen:** water electrolysis powered by solar or wind electricity. Major clean-energy programs worldwide (including India's 2023 National Green Hydrogen Mission) are scaling this up.
- **Color labels:** grey (from natural gas, CO₂ vented), blue (CO₂ captured and stored underground), green (from renewable electricity). Hydrogen gas itself is colorless.
- Trade-off: the cleanest method is currently more expensive; the cheapest method emits CO₂.
- Students **never** prepare hydrogen on their own or put sodium/potassium in water; those are strictly teacher demonstrations or paper topics.
