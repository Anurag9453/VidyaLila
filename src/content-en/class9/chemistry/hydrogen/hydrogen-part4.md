# Topic 4: Properties of Hydrogen: Physical and Chemical

In the previous topics, we learned what hydrogen is and **how it is prepared**: from a metal and dilute acid in the lab, from natural gas and steam in industry, and cleanly from water. Now comes a direct question: once we have hydrogen gas, **what is it like and what does it do?** Can you see it? Does it have a taste or smell? Why does it lift a balloon into the sky, and why does it go off with a sharp pop the moment a flame comes near? Today we will examine both the **physical properties** (what we can observe or measure without changing the substance) and the **chemical properties** (how it reacts with other substances) of hydrogen.

---

## The Rocket Flame That Turned Out to Be Hot Steam

On the living-room television, Elena and her uncle, Dr. Chen, were watching a live space rocket launch. Elena sat cross-legged on the sofa as the mission commentator announced: "The rocket's **cryogenic upper stage** has now ignited." On screen, a blindingly fast plume billowed behind the nozzle, yet the flame itself was almost transparent—just a pale, shimmering cone in the air.

Elena frowned. "Uncle Chen, what kind of fire is that? Normal flames are bright yellow or orange, and rockets usually leave thick dark smoke. Here the flame is barely visible, and the cloud looks pure white!"

Dr. Chen smiled. "That white cloud isn't smoke at all—it's **water vapor (steam)** condensing in the cold upper air. Inside this stage, **liquid hydrogen** is burning with **liquid oxygen**. Tell me: when hydrogen burns with oxygen, what is the only product?"

Elena thought for a second. "Water?"

"Exactly! 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O. What shoots out of that rocket nozzle is superheated steam. Because hydrogen is the lightest element of all, every kilogram of hydrogen fuel gives the rocket an enormous kick—though keeping it liquid inside the tank requires chilling it down to **−253 °C**."

"Minus 253 degrees?! Something that freezing cold burns like a blowtorch?"

"It sure does. Those are the two faces of hydrogen: an ultra-cold liquid in the tank, and a fierce, clean-burning fuel in the engine. Let's explore its **properties** one by one."

> **Safety:** Hydrogen is a highly flammable gas that forms **explosive mixtures** with air. In this chapter, no student prepares, fills, or burns hydrogen. Wherever burning or testing is mentioned, it is **strictly for observation (teacher demonstration)**: tiny quantities, no open flames nearby, good ventilation, and a safety screen. Party balloons are never filled with hydrogen; they are filled with non-flammable helium from a cylinder at a shop.

## Part A: Physical Properties: Observe, Measure, Compare

| Property | Hydrogen (H₂) |
|---|---|
| Color, odor, taste | Colorless, odorless, tasteless (no one ever tastes lab gases; this is a known physical fact) |
| Density (at STP) | About 0.09 g per L (compared with air at 1.29 g per L) |
| Boiling point | About −253 °C, which is 20 K |
| Melting point | About −259 °C, which is 14 K |
| Solubility in water | Very low / sparingly soluble (which is why it can be collected over water in the lab, Topic 2) |
| Effect on litmus | None: **neutral** |
| Combustion | Burns itself (it is a fuel), but does not support the burning of other things |

Recall from Chapter 1 how to convert Celsius to Kelvin: K = °C + 273. Check: −253 + 273 = 20 K and −259 + 273 = 14 K ✅. Notice that its melting point and boiling point are only 6 degrees apart: hydrogen stays a liquid only within a tiny 6 K window between 14 K and 20 K! That is why liquefying and storing liquid hydrogen is so challenging (as we will see in Topic 5).

**Why is its boiling point so extraordinarily low?** (In one line): H₂ molecules are tiny and very light, and the attractions between one H₂ molecule and the next are extremely weak, so you have to cool them almost to absolute zero before they cling together as a liquid. (You will study intermolecular forces in greater depth in higher grades.)

## Part B: The Lightest Gas: Comparing Densities

From our Gas Laws chapter ([gas-laws-part5.md](../gas-laws/gas-laws-part5.md)), recall the rule: **gas density at STP = M ÷ 22.4 g per L**. For hydrogen: 2 ÷ 22.4 = **0.0893**, or about **0.09 g per L**. For helium: 4 ÷ 22.4 = **0.1786**, or about **0.18 g per L**. For air: roughly 29 ÷ 22.4 = **1.29 g per L**. For large-scale balloon calculations, it is handy to use **kg per m³** (since 1 m³ = 1,000 L, 0.09 g per L is numerically equal to 0.09 kg per m³).

```
         Mass of 1 m³ of gas (at STP-like conditions)

                                  +---------------+
                                  │               │
                                  │      AIR      │
                                  │   N₂, O₂ ...  │
                   +-------+      │               │
                   │  He   │      │               │
    +-------+      │       │      │               │
    │  H₂   │      │       │      │               │
    +-------+      +-------+      +---------------+
    0.09 kg        0.18 kg        1.29 kg
    (lightest)     (twice H₂)     (over 14 times H₂)

    Air / H₂ = 1.29 / 0.09 = 14.3     Air / He = 1.29 / 0.18 = 7.2
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Mass of three gases occupying the same 1 m³ volume: hydrogen 0.09 kg, helium 0.18 kg, and air 1.29 kg. The heights of the bars illustrate their relative masses (helium is twice as heavy as hydrogen; air is the heaviest; not drawn to exact scale), while the volume of all three is identical (1 m³).

Thus, hydrogen is **the lightest gas of all**: more than **14 times lighter than air** (1.29 ÷ 0.09 = 14.3), and half the density of helium.

### Example 1. Lift of a hydrogen balloon

A large weather balloon has a volume of 100 m³ (this is a paper calculation only; no one is filling a balloon with hydrogen) and is filled with hydrogen. How much upward lift does the gas provide? (Ignore the mass of the balloon fabric.)

- Mass of 100 m³ of displaced air: 100 × 1.29 = **129 kg**.
- Mass of 100 m³ of hydrogen inside: 100 × 0.09 = **9 kg**.
- Lift = 129 − 9 = **120 kg**.

Or directly per cubic meter: each 1 m³ provides 1.29 − 0.09 = **1.20 kg** of lift, so 100 m³ provides 100 × 1.20 = 120 kg.

Check: 129 − 9 = 120 and 100 × 1.20 = 120 ✅

**How does lift work?** Subtract the mass of the light gas inside the balloon from the mass of the heavier surrounding air it displaces; the difference is the net upward buoyancy (just like a block of light wood pushed up by heavier water). As long as the balloon skin and payload weigh less than this lift, the balloon rises.

### Example 2. Hydrogen versus helium: how big is the difference?

Suppose the exact same 100 m³ balloon is filled with helium instead:

- Lift of helium per m³: 1.29 − 0.18 = **1.11 kg**.
- For 100 m³: 100 × 1.11 = **111 kg**.
- Compared with hydrogen's 120 kg: 111 ÷ 120 = **0.925**, meaning helium provides **92.5 percent** of the lift of hydrogen!

Helium gives only 7.5 percent less lift than hydrogen, yet helium **cannot burn or explode**. That is why modern balloons and airships are filled with helium, not hydrogen.

Check: 1.29 − 0.09 = 1.20 and 1.29 − 0.18 = 1.11 ✅; difference 120 − 111 = 9 kg ✅

(Note: these calculations assume STP-like conditions. As temperature and pressure change with altitude, densities shift slightly, so actual lift varies a little.)

## Part C: Chemical Property 1: Hydrogen Burns (With Oxygen)

Hydrogen is a combustible gas, but **it requires oxygen (or air) in order to burn**. The reaction is the exact one powering Dr. Chen's rocket:

**2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O** (releases heat: exothermic, Chapter 7 Topic 4)

- H: 4 = 4, O: 2 = 2 ✅
- Recall the mass ratio from Chapter 9: 4 g H₂ + 32 g O₂ → 36 g water, so H : O = 1 : 8 by mass.

**For observation only (teacher demonstration):** when a teacher ignites a tiny, pure jet of hydrogen, it burns with a **pale blue, nearly invisible flame** (performed only in tiny quantities, far from any other flame, behind a safety screen). In bright daylight, a hydrogen flame is so faint that it is almost impossible to see with the naked eye. **This makes hydrogen fires especially hazardous** (you might not realize a leak is burning), so industrial hydrogen facilities use specialized optical and thermal flame detectors.

Check (conservation of mass): 4 + 32 = 36 ✅

### Example 3. Mass calculation for burning hydrogen

If 4 g of hydrogen burns completely in oxygen, how many grams of oxygen are consumed and how many grams of water are formed?

- In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, the mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen is 1 : 8.
- Oxygen consumed = 4 × 8 = **32 g**.
- Water formed = 4 + 32 = **36 g**.

Check: 36 ÷ 9 = 4 g of hydrogen (since hydrogen makes up 1/9 of water's mass) ✅

## Part D: Chemical Property 2: Hydrogen Combines with Other Elements

Hydrogen reacts not only with oxygen, but also with several other non-metals (each reaction below belongs to a teacher demo or an industrial plant, never a student experiment):

| Reaction | Equation | Conditions | Product formed |
|---|---|---|---|
| With oxygen | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O | Spark or heat | Water (plus heat) |
| With chlorine | H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl | Bright sunlight or heat | Hydrogen chloride gas (dissolves in water to form hydrochloric acid) |
| With nitrogen | N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ | Haber process: iron catalyst, high pressure, heat; reversible reaction | Ammonia (raw material for fertilizers) |

Atom check for all three equations: 2H₂ + O₂: H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2 ✅. H₂ + Cl₂: H 2 = 2, Cl 2 = 2 ✅. N₂ + 3H₂: N 2 = 2, H 6 = 6 ✅.

One quick note: the ammonia reaction is **reversible** (it can go both forward and backward), and you will study its exact industrial conditions in Class 10. Here, simply remember its name (the Haber process) and why it matters: it makes the ammonia needed for agricultural fertilizers (more in Topic 5).

## Part E: Chemical Property 3: Hydrogen as a Reducing Agent

In Chapter 7 (**Oxidation and Reduction**, [chemical-changes-and-reactions-part5.md](../chemical-changes-and-reactions/chemical-changes-and-reactions-part5.md)), we met this classic reaction; now let's look at it from hydrogen's point of view:

**CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O**

**For observation only (teacher demonstration):** a slow stream of dry hydrogen gas is passed over heated black copper(II) oxide (CuO) powder in a hard-glass tube. The black powder turns into reddish-brown metallic copper, and droplets of water condense on the cooler end of the tube.

```
         hydrogen ----->  [ heated CuO (black) ]  -----> Cu (reddish-brown) + H₂O
                                   ^
                              gentle heat

    CuO + H₂  ---->  Cu  +  H₂O

    CuO : loses oxygen  -> is REDUCED   -> acts as OXIDIZING AGENT
    H₂  : gains oxygen  -> is OXIDIZED  -> acts as REDUCING AGENT
```

> **Figure 4.2** – CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O. Oxygen leaves CuO and joins H₂. In this reaction, **CuO** is reduced (it is the oxidizing agent) and **H₂** is oxidized (it is the reducing agent): hydrogen acts as a reducing agent by stripping oxygen from the metal oxide.

Another important example: **Fe₂O₃ + 3H₂ → 2Fe + 3H₂O**.

- Atom check: Fe 2 = 2, O 3 = 3, H 6 = 6 ✅
- Fe₂O₃ loses oxygen: **reduced**. H₂ gains oxygen: **oxidized**, acting as the reducing agent.

**Which metal oxides can hydrogen reduce?** It can reduce the oxides of metals that are moderately or less reactive in the reactivity series (Chapter 7, Topic 3), such as copper, lead, tungsten, and heated iron oxide. Very reactive metals (sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, aluminum) hold on to oxygen so tightly that hydrogen cannot pull their oxygen away. You will learn the full thermodynamic reason for this in Class 10.

### Example 4. Identifying roles: WO₃ + 3H₂ → W + 3H₂O

Tungsten (symbol W, the high-melting metal used in specialized filaments and alloys) is extracted in industry from tungsten trioxide (WO₃) using hydrogen gas.

- Atom check: W 1 = 1, O 3 = 3, H 6 = 6 ✅
- WO₃ loses oxygen (forming W): **reduced**, so WO₃ is the oxidizing agent.
- H₂ gains oxygen (forming H₂O): **oxidized**, so H₂ is the reducing agent.

Check: O = 3 on both sides ✅, and the oxygen atoms transferred from W to H₂ ✅

## Part F: Why Is Hydrogen Quiet at Room Temperature?

Here is a surprising puzzle: if you mix hydrogen and oxygen at room temperature in the dark, **nothing happens**—not even over many years! Yet the moment a tiny spark touches the mixture, it explodes in an instant. Why?

1. Each H₂ molecule is held together by a strong **H–H covalent bond** (Chapter 6: a single covalent bond), and O₂ has a strong double bond. Before H and O can join to form water, those existing bonds must **break**, and breaking bonds requires an **initial input of energy** (Chapter 7, Topic 4).
2. At room temperature, colliding molecules simply do not have enough energy to snap the H–H and O=O bonds.
3. A tiny spark or flame supplies enough energy to break the bonds of a few molecules right around the spark. Those freed atoms immediately snap together into strong H–O bonds in water, **releasing a burst of heat**. That heat breaks neighboring H–H and O=O bonds, triggering a runaway chain reaction through the whole mixture in a flash.

```
    At room temperature:         H–H   O=O      (bonds intact, no reaction)
                                     │
                               spark / heat
                                     │
                                     v
    A few bonds break ----> new H–O bonds form ----> heat is released
                                                             │
                                 this heat breaks neighboring bonds (chain reaction!)
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Why a hydrogen-oxygen mixture sits quietly at room temperature: a spark or heat is needed first to break the initial H–H and O=O bonds; forming new H–O bonds releases heat, which breaks more bonds and makes the reaction race through the mixture.

This is the same principle we see with paper or cooking gas: a fuel needs an initial **ignition source** (a spark or flame) to start burning, after which its own released heat keeps the fire going. Interestingly, in the presence of a **catalyst** such as finely divided platinum or palladium, hydrogen and oxygen can combine even at room temperature without a spark (recall catalysts from Chapter 7, Topic 4). That controlled catalytic reaction is the key idea behind the hydrogen fuel cell, which we will study in Topic 5.

## Part G: When Does an Explosion Happen: Hydrogen Mixtures

Pure hydrogen in a sealed pipe with no oxygen cannot burn by itself; what is dangerous is **hydrogen mixed with air**. If the percentage of hydrogen in air is too low, the mixture is too lean to catch fire. If the percentage of hydrogen is too high, there is not enough oxygen to sustain a flame. Between roughly **4% and 75% hydrogen in air** (by volume; approximate figures), any spark will ignite the mixture rapidly—this is called the **explosive range** (or flammable range) of hydrogen. Notice how unusually wide this range is: common fuels like natural gas or gasoline vapor have much narrower explosive ranges.

```
    Percentage of hydrogen in air (by volume, approximate)

     4%                                 75%
      ↓                                   ↓
    +-+===================================+-----------+
    0%                                             100%

    0% to 4%    : too little hydrogen (too lean), mixture does not ignite
    4% to 75%   : EXPLOSIVE RANGE (===), ignites rapidly from a single spark
    75% to 100% : too little oxygen (too rich), but becomes explosive as soon as air mixes in!
```

> **Figure 4.4** – The explosive range of hydrogen in air is roughly 4% to 75% (approximate). Below 4%, there is too little hydrogen to sustain combustion; above 75%, there is too little oxygen. Across the wide middle band, a single spark can trigger an explosion.

### Example 5. Calculating volumes in a 1 m³ air-hydrogen mixture

In a 1 m³ (1,000 L) volume of air-hydrogen mixture, what volume of hydrogen puts the mixture inside the explosive range?

- At the 4% lower limit: 1000 × 0.04 = **40 L** of hydrogen.
- At the 75% upper limit: 1000 × 0.75 = **750 L** of hydrogen.
- Thus, anywhere from 40 L to 750 L of hydrogen in a 1,000 L mixture is within the explosive range.

Just 40 L of hydrogen (the volume of forty 1-liter water bottles) in a 1 m³ space is enough to reach the lower explosive limit! That is why even small hydrogen leaks in enclosed spaces are taken very seriously.

Check: 40 ÷ 1000 = 4% ✅ and 750 ÷ 1000 = 75% ✅

**One more practical point:** because hydrogen is so light, any leaked hydrogen **rises rapidly toward the ceiling** and can collect in roof pockets if there are no top vents. That is why rooms and vehicles designed for hydrogen always place ventilation louvers and leak sensors at the highest points near the ceiling.

## Part H: What the "Pop" Test Really Means

In Topic 2's laboratory preparation, the test for hydrogen was bringing a **lighted splint** to the mouth of a small inverted test tube and hearing a squeaky **"pop"**. (**For observation only (teacher demonstration).** Performed only by the teacher with a tiny test tube.)

Now we can explain the exact chemistry of that pop:

1. At the mouth of the small test tube, the hydrogen mixes with a little surrounding air, bringing the mixture inside the **explosive range** (4% to 75%).
2. The flame of the splint supplies the activation energy to break the first bonds, and the **entire tiny sample of mixture reacts in a millisecond**.
3. The heat released by 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O causes the hot water vapor and air in the tube to expand suddenly, creating the miniature shockwave we hear as a squeaky **"pop"**.

If pure hydrogen is burned quietly at a tiny nozzle without pre-mixing with air, it burns smoothly without a pop. Conversely, **a mixture of hydrogen and air is never ignited in a large jar or closed vessel**, because instead of a harmless squeak in a tiny test tube, it would produce a dangerous explosion.

## Part I: Summary of Hydrogen's Properties

| Property | Value / Fact | Why it matters (in one line) |
|---|---|---|
| Color and odor | Colorless, odorless | Leaks cannot be seen or smelled without electronic sensors |

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Thinking hydrogen supports combustion like oxygen**

No. Hydrogen **burns itself** (it is a fuel). **Oxygen** is the gas that supports combustion. If you push a lighted splint deep into pure hydrogen with no oxygen around, the splint flame actually goes out while the hydrogen burns only at the mouth where it meets air.

**Mistake 2: Confusing hydrogen with helium**

Both are light gases, but **helium never burns**, whereas hydrogen is highly flammable. Helium's density (0.18 g per L) is twice hydrogen's (0.09 g per L), yet helium still gives 92.5% as much lift in air. That is why balloons use helium.

**Mistake 3: Thinking any percentage of hydrogen in air will explode on contact with a spark**

The explosive range is roughly 4% to 75% in air. At 1% (too lean) or 95% (too little oxygen), a mixture won't explode in place—though a 95% leak is still dangerous because as it dilutes into room air, it will pass right through the 75%–4% explosive range!

**Mistake 4: Thinking "reduced" means "decreased in amount"**

In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O, saying CuO is **reduced** means **oxygen was removed from it** (Chapter 7), turning black CuO into reddish-brown Cu metal.

**Mistake 5: Thinking hydrogen can never react with oxygen at room temperature**

Without a spark, heat, or a catalyst, they do not react at room temperature because the H–H and O=O bonds remain unbroken. Add a spark or a platinum catalyst, and the reaction proceeds immediately.

**Mistake 6: Adding densities instead of subtracting when calculating balloon lift**

Lift = mass of displaced air **minus** mass of gas inside: 1.29 − 0.09 = 1.20 kg per m³ (never 1.29 + 0.09).

## Quick Recap

- Hydrogen is **colorless, odorless, tasteless**, sparingly soluble in water, and **neutral** to litmus.
- Density at STP is about **0.09 g per L**: more than **14 times lighter than air** (1.29 g per L) and half as dense as helium (0.18 g per L).
- Boiling point is about **−253 °C (20 K)**; melting point is **−259 °C (14 K)**.
- **Lift** = mass of displaced air − mass of gas: hydrogen gives 1.20 kg per m³, helium gives 1.11 kg per m³ (helium provides 92.5% of hydrogen's lift and cannot burn).
- **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**: exothermic; H : O mass ratio in water is 1 : 8.
- H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl; N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (Haber process, iron catalyst, reversible).
- Hydrogen is a **reducing agent**: CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O (CuO is reduced, H₂ is oxidized).
- Quiet at room temperature because the strong H–H bond needs a spark, heat, or catalyst to break.
- **Explosive range** in air is roughly 4% to 75% (approx.); the squeaky pop test is a tiny controlled ignition in a small test tube (teacher demo only).

## Practice Questions

2. A balloon must carry a camera of mass 8 kg. Using helium (0.18 kg per m³) and air (1.29 kg per m³), find the minimum volume of helium needed to lift only this camera (ignore the balloon cover).

5. 27 g of water is formed when hydrogen burns in oxygen. How much hydrogen and how much oxygen (mass) were used?

6. A mixture contains 2% hydrogen in air, another 30%, and another 90%. Which one(s) can explode on meeting a spark? Give a reason using the explosive range.

9. Why is the pop test done only in a small test tube and only by a teacher?

11. A colourless gas is lighter than air, is neutral to litmus, and burns with a pop in a small test tube with a spark (teacher demo). It forms water on burning. Name the gas.

12. Will Al₂O₃ be reduced by hydrogen at the same conditions as CuO? Give a reason using the reactivity series.

### Answers

1. Lift per 1 m³ = 1.29 − 0.09 = 1.20 kg. For 250 m³ = 250 × 1.20 = **300 kg**. Check: 250 × 1.29 = 322.5 kg, 250 × 0.09 = 22.5 kg, 322.5 − 22.5 = 300 ✅

2. Lift of helium per m³ = 1.29 − 0.18 = 1.11 kg. Minimum volume = 8 ÷ 1.11 = **7.2 m³** (approx.). Check: 7.2 × 1.11 = 7.99 kg ≈ 8 kg ✅ (in practice, a larger volume is needed to lift the balloon envelope as well.)

3. Oxygen: −218 + 273 = **55 K** (melting) and −183 + 273 = **90 K** (boiling). Oxygen's liquid range: 90 − 55 = **35 K**; hydrogen's liquid range: 20 − 14 = only **6 K**. Thus **oxygen** stays liquid over a much wider temperature range. Check: −183 − (−218) = 35 ✅; 20 − 14 = 6 ✅

4. **PbO** loses oxygen (is reduced), so it is the **oxidizing agent**. **H₂** gains oxygen (is oxidized), so it is the **reducing agent**. Atom check: Pb 1 = 1, O 1 = 1, H 2 = 2 ✅

5. In water, the mass ratio H : O = 1 : 8, so hydrogen makes up 1/9 of the total mass. H₂ = 27 ÷ 9 = **3 g**; O₂ = 27 − 3 = **24 g**. Check: 24 ÷ 3 = 8 ✅ and 3 + 24 = 27 ✅

6. Only the **30%** mixture (which lies between 4% and 75%) can explode immediately on meeting a spark. The 2% mixture is below the lower limit (too little hydrogen), and the 90% mixture is above the upper limit (too little oxygen). Check: 4 < 30 < 75 ✅ (note that if the 90% mixture leaks out into room air, it will pass through the 4%–75% explosive range.)

7. 4% of 60 m³ = 60 × 0.04 = **2.4 m³** of hydrogen. Check: 2.4 ÷ 60 = 0.04 ✅

8. At room temperature, colliding molecules lack the energy needed to break the strong H–H and O=O covalent bonds, so no reaction starts. A spark supplies energy to break a few bonds; forming new H–O bonds in water releases heat, which breaks neighboring bonds and triggers a rapid chain reaction.

9. Inside the mouth of the test tube, hydrogen mixes with air to enter the explosive range and ignites with a tiny "pop." In a larger vessel, the same mixture would cause a dangerous explosion that could shatter glass. That is why only a teacher performs the test, using a tiny test tube behind a safety screen.

10. (a) **True** ✅. It is only sparingly soluble in water. (b) **False** ❌. Hydrogen has **no effect** on litmus (it is neutral). (c) **True** ✅. Helium is non-flammable. (d) **True** ✅. H₂ gains oxygen to form H₂O, so it is oxidized.

11. **Hydrogen**. Being lighter than air, neutral to litmus, burning with a squeaky pop in a teacher demo, and forming only water on combustion uniquely identify hydrogen.

12. **No.** Aluminum sits very high in the reactivity series and holds on to oxygen far too strongly for hydrogen to remove it. Copper is much less reactive, so hydrogen easily strips oxygen from heated CuO.
