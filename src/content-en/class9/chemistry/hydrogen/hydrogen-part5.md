# Topic 5: Uses, Safety, and the Future of Hydrogen

In the previous topic, we explored the properties of hydrogen: the lightest gas known, explosive in air between 4% and 75%, a clean-burning fuel, and an effective reducing agent. Now let's ask: **how do we put these properties to work?** In this topic, we will see how hydrogen is used to make ammonia, solidify vegetable oils, launch rockets, and run **fuel cells**; why its **energy per kilogram is nearly three times that of gasoline** yet **storing it in a small tank is so hard**; what the world learned from the **Hindenburg** disaster; why helium beats hydrogen for balloons; and what it will take to build a future **hydrogen economy**.

---

## The Silent Bus at the Auto Expo

Mr. Davis took his student Noah to the city's annual **Clean Mobility & Auto Expo**. Outside in the traffic, engines roared and a faint smell of gasoline and diesel hung in the air.

Inside the exhibition yard, a sleek city bus glided past them almost **in complete silence—no roaring engine, no dark exhaust smoke**. From a small tailpipe at the back of the bus, only **clear droplets of water** dripped onto the pavement, like condensation from an air conditioner.

"Mr. Davis, how is this bus even moving?" Noah asked in amazement. "There's zero smoke coming out!"

Mr. Davis pointed to the large lettering along the side: **"Hydrogen Fuel-Cell Transit Bus."** "Inside that roof tank is compressed hydrogen gas. The hydrogen and oxygen from the outside air meet inside a device called a **fuel cell**, which generates electricity directly to turn an electric motor. And when hydrogen and oxygen combine, what is the only chemical product left over?"

"Water!" Noah said. "So those drips are literally the exhaust!"

"Exactly. But look closely at the safety panel near the tank," Mr. Davis added, pointing to a bright **yellow warning label**: *Flammable gas. High pressure. No open flame.* "This is the very same gas that can form an explosive mixture with air. Engineers spend just as much effort designing the carbon-fiber tanks, pressure valves, and ceiling leak sensors on this bus as they do designing the fuel cell itself. Let's look at why hydrogen is so useful—and why it demands such strict respect."

Noah jotted in his notebook: **"Hydrogen: huge potential, serious safety."**

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel cell** | An electrochemical device that combines hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity directly without a flame | Hydrogen fuel-cell city bus |
| **Hydrogenation** | Adding hydrogen across double bonds in a substance (such as vegetable oil) using a nickel catalyst | Turning liquid vegetable oil into solid fat |

## Part B: Ammonia and Fertilizers

By far the **largest industrial use of hydrogen** worldwide is the manufacture of ammonia. As we saw in Topic 4, nitrogen and hydrogen react according to:

N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

This industrial method is called the **Haber process**: it uses an iron catalyst, high pressure, and high temperature. The reaction is **reversible** (it can go both forward and backward); you will study its exact equilibrium conditions in higher classes. The ammonia produced is converted into **nitrogen fertilizers** (such as urea and ammonium nitrate) that nourish food crops around the globe. Every loaf of bread or bowl of rice we eat relies in part on hydrogen!

**Example 1: Mass calculation for making ammonia.**
Approximate atomic masses: H = 1, N = 14.
- Mass of 3H₂ = 3 × 2 = **6 g**
- Mass of N₂ = 2 × 14 = **28 g**
- Mass of 2NH₃ = 2 × (14 + 3) = 2 × 17 = **34 g**

Thus, **6 g of hydrogen + 28 g of nitrogen → 34 g of ammonia**.
Total mass before: 6 + 28 = 34 g; total mass after: 34 g. Mass is conserved ✅ (Chapter 4 rule).

> **Check:** Atom count: left side has N = 2, H = 6; right side (2NH₃) has N = 2, H = 6 ✅. 6 + 28 = 34 ✅.

## Part C: Hydrogenation: Turning Vegetable Oil into Solid Fat

Liquid vegetable oils (like sunflower or soybean oil) consist of long carbon-chain molecules that contain one or more carbon-carbon **double bonds**. When warm vegetable oil is reacted with hydrogen gas in the presence of a finely divided **nickel catalyst**, hydrogen atoms add across those double bonds, converting them into single bonds so the chains pack together more tightly as a semi-solid fat—such as **margarine or vegetable shortening (vanaspati)**.

This chemical addition of hydrogen is called **hydrogenation**. We can see the exact idea using a simple two-carbon molecule, ethene (C₂H₄), as a miniature model (you will study carbon compounds in detail in Class 10):

C₂H₄ + H₂ → C₂H₆

Here, C₂H₄ (which has a double bond, just like the chains in liquid oil) reacts with H–H over a nickel catalyst; the double bond opens up and one H atom attaches to each carbon, forming saturated C₂H₆ (like the chains in solid fat).

> **Check:** Atom count: left side has C = 2, H = 4 + 2 = 6; right side has C = 2, H = 6 ✅.

**Note:** Industrial hydrogenation is carried out in sealed pressurized reactors with specialized catalysts. **It is never done at home or in a school lab**; we study it to understand how everyday food products are made.

## Part D: Rocket Fuel (Cryogenic Stage)

A space rocket needs an enormous amount of energy while keeping its fuel mass as light as possible. That makes hydrogen the ultimate rocket propellant: **liquid hydrogen (LH₂)** is burned with **liquid oxygen (LOX)** inside the upper-stage **cryogenic engines** of major space launch vehicles (such as NASA's Space Launch System, ESA's Ariane rockets, and ISRO's LVM3 / GSLV Mk III).

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

- Liquid hydrogen boils at about **−253 °C**. In Kelvin: −253 + 273 = **20 K** (the boiling point from Topic 4).
- Liquid oxygen boils at about **−183 °C** (approximate).
- The exhaust shooting out of the nozzle consists almost entirely of **superheated steam (water vapor)**—which is why the flame Elena watched in Topic 4 looked so clean and pale.

> **Check:** Atom count: left side has H = 4, O = 2; right side (2H₂O) has H = 4, O = 2 ✅. −253 + 273 = 20 ✅.

Cryogenic rocket tanks require **heavy-duty thermal insulation** so the −253 °C liquid hydrogen does not boil away before liftoff. Only **trained aerospace engineers** handle cryogenic propellants under strict safety protocols.

## Part E: The Fuel Cell: Direct Electricity with Water as a Bonus

Now let's look inside the silent bus Noah saw. A **fuel cell** is an electrochemical cell—similar in spirit to the battery cells you study in Physics, except that its reactants are **fed in continuously from outside**: hydrogen gas enters on one side, and oxygen (from the air) enters on the other. As they react to form water, **electrons are forced to flow through an external wire as an electric current** that powers a motor or light bulb.

The overall chemical equation is identical to burning hydrogen, but it happens **electrochemically, without any flame or explosion**:

2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O (+ electrical energy)

```
        H₂ (hydrogen) in                        O₂ (oxygen from air) in
              │                                       │
              v                                       v
     +-----------------+   electrolyte    +--------------------+
     │   H₂ electrode  │  (a specialized  │   O₂ electrode     │
     │   plate (−)     │   membrane)      │   plate (+)        │
     +-----------------+                  +--------------------+
              │                                       │
              +------ wire ------[ bulb / motor ]-----+
                       flow of electrons (current)

                       ... the two sides combine ...
                               │
                               v
                        WATER (H₂O) out
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Simplified diagram of a hydrogen fuel cell: H₂ enters at the negative (−) plate and O₂ enters at the positive (+) plate, separated by a specialized electrolyte membrane (studied in higher grades). Electrons flow through the external circuit to run a bulb or electric motor, and the only chemical product leaving the cell is pure water. (Schematic sketch; real fuel cells use catalyst-coated plates stacked together.)

How does a fuel cell differ from ordinary burning? When hydrogen **burns** in a flame, chemical energy is released all at once as heat. In a **fuel cell**, a catalyst lets the reaction proceed smoothly at low temperature, converting a much larger share of the chemical energy **directly into electricity**. That is why a fuel-cell bus runs quietly, efficiently, and with zero tailpipe smoke.

**Example 2: How much water does a fuel cell produce?**
In 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O:
- Mass of 2H₂ = 2 × 2 = **4 g**
- Mass of 2H₂O = 2 × 18 = **36 g**

Since 4 g of hydrogen produces 36 g of water, **1 kg of hydrogen produces 9 kg of water** (36 ÷ 4 = 9). No wonder pure water drips from the bus's tailpipe!

> **Check:** Atom count ✅ (H = 4, O = 2 on both sides). 36 ÷ 4 = 9 ✅. The remaining 8 kg of mass came from oxygen drawn in from the outside air: 36 − 4 = 32 g ✅ (mass of O₂ is 32).

**Paper activity (on paper only, no chemicals):** On a sheet of paper, sketch your own diagram of a fuel cell. Label the H₂ inlet, the O₂ inlet, the two electrodes, the electrolyte membrane, the external wire with a motor/bulb, and the water outlet.

## Part G: Energy per Kilogram: How Powerful Is Hydrogen?

One straightforward way to compare fuels is to ask: **how much heat energy is released when 1 kg of the fuel burns completely?**

- **Hydrogen:** about **120 MJ per kg** (approximate)
- **Gasoline (petrol):** about **45 MJ per kg** (approximate)

```
    Energy per kilogram (approximate, in MJ per kg)
    each  █  =  5 MJ

    Hydrogen  ████████████████████████  120 MJ
    Gasoline  █████████                  45 MJ

    Hydrogen delivers about 2.7 times as much energy per kg as gasoline
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Energy per kilogram: burning 1 kg of hydrogen releases about 120 MJ, compared with about 45 MJ from 1 kg of gasoline—roughly 2.7 times as much energy per kilogram. (Approximate values; exact figures vary slightly across sources.)

**Example 3: Comparing equal masses of fuel.**
If 2 kg of each fuel is burned completely:
- Hydrogen: 2 × 120 = **240 MJ**
- Gasoline: 2 × 45 = **90 MJ**
- Ratio: 120 ÷ 45 = **2.67** (about 2.7 times)

This explains why aerospace engineers love hydrogen for rockets, where **every single kilogram of liftoff mass** counts.

> **Check:** 2 × 120 = 240 ✅, 2 × 45 = 90 ✅, 120 ÷ 45 = 2.667 ✅.

**Here is the catch:** energy **per kilogram (mass)** is only half the story; for cars and trucks, energy **per liter (volume)** matters just as much. Because hydrogen is the lightest gas in the universe, 1 kg of uncompressed hydrogen takes up an enormous volume! Let's do the math.

## Part H: The Storage Challenge

Recall the density of hydrogen from Topic 4: at STP, hydrogen gas has a density of only about **0.09 kg per m³** (approximate). Liquid gasoline, by contrast, has a density of about **740 kg per m³** (approximate).

**Example 4: How much energy fits inside 1 cubic meter (1 m³)?**
- **Hydrogen gas (at STP):** 0.09 kg × 120 MJ per kg = **10.8 MJ**
- **Liquid hydrogen** (density about 71 kg per m³ at −253 °C, approx.): 71 × 120 = **8,520 MJ**
- **Gasoline (petrol):** 740 × 45 = **33,300 MJ**

Let's compare them side by side in a table:

| Fuel occupying 1 m³ | Energy in 1 m³ (approx.) | Compared with gasoline (by volume) |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen gas at STP | 10.8 MJ | About 3,000 times less energy per m³ |
| Liquid hydrogen (−253 °C) | 8,520 MJ | About 3.9 times less energy per m³ |
| Gasoline (petrol) | 33,300 MJ | 1 (reference) |

In short: **by mass (per kilogram), hydrogen wins easily; by volume (per liter), gasoline wins**. Because a car or bus has limited space for fuel tanks, hydrogen must either be compressed to hundreds of atmospheres or chilled to a cryogenic liquid.

> **Check:** 0.09 × 120 = 10.8 ✅. 71 × 120 = 8,520 ✅. 740 × 45 = 33,300 ✅. 33,300 ÷ 10.8 = 3,083 (about 3,000) ✅. 33,300 ÷ 8,520 = 3.91 ✅.

**Two main ways to store hydrogen in practice:**
1. **Compressed gas:** stored at very high pressure inside thick, reinforced carbon-fiber or steel cylinders (all the gas-cylinder safety rules from Chapter 10 apply: keep away from heat and direct sunlight, never drop or damage valves, inspect regularly).
2. **Liquid hydrogen:** chilled to −253 °C inside heavily insulated cryogenic tanks. Liquefying hydrogen takes a lot of energy, and if heat slowly seeps in, some hydrogen gradually "boils off."

**Additional safety challenges in storing hydrogen:**
- Hydrogen **molecules are tiny**, so they can sneak through microscopic **leaks** in seals or welds that larger gas molecules cannot pass.
- It is **colorless and odorless**, so a leak cannot be seen or smelled without electronic hydrogen sensors.
- Its **explosive range in air (4% to 75%)** is very wide (Topic 4), making any leak in an unventilated space dangerous.

## Part I: Lessons from the Hindenburg

In **1937**, the giant German passenger airship **Hindenburg**, which was filled with hydrogen gas for lift, caught fire while mooring in New Jersey, USA, and was destroyed in less than a minute. **Thirty-six people lost their lives.** The airship held roughly **2 × 10⁵ m³** (200,000 m³) of hydrogen (approximate); once that hydrogen mixed with air and ignited, the fire spread almost instantaneously.

**Example 5: Comparing the lift of hydrogen vs. helium.**
Just as in Topic 4, lift per m³ = density of air − density of gas. Using approximate densities (in kg per m³): air = 1.29, hydrogen = 0.09, helium = 0.18.
- Lift of hydrogen = 1.29 − 0.09 = **1.20 kg per m³**
- Lift of helium = 1.29 − 0.18 = **1.11 kg per m³**
- Difference = 1.20 − 1.11 = 0.09 kg per m³, meaning hydrogen gives only about **7.5% more lift** (helium provides 92.5% of hydrogen's lift).

In other words, helium sacrifices **only a tiny fraction** of lift while being **completely non-flammable**. For a giant 2 × 10⁵ m³ airship: hydrogen gave 1.20 × 2 × 10⁵ = **2.4 × 10⁵ kg** (240 tonnes) of lift, whereas safe helium would give 1.11 × 2 × 10⁵ = 2.22 × 10⁵ kg (222 tonnes).

> **Check:** 1.29 − 0.09 = 1.20 ✅, 1.29 − 0.18 = 1.11 ✅, 1.11 ÷ 1.20 = 0.925 ✅. 1.20 × 200,000 = 240,000 ✅.

**Key safety rules learned:**
- Modern **airships and party/event balloons use helium**, never hydrogen.
- **Never give children hydrogen-filled balloons.** Even a tiny static electric spark or nearby candle can turn a hydrogen balloon into a fireball.
- Always buy **helium-filled balloons** from a reputable shop; never attempt to fill balloons from industrial gas cylinders yourself.
- **Keep all flames, sparks, matches, incense, and unapproved electronics far away from any hydrogen storage area.**

## Part J: The Hydrogen Economy: Dream and Reality

The vision of a **hydrogen economy** works like a clean circle: use **solar and wind power** to split water by electrolysis into **green hydrogen** (Topic 3), store and transport that hydrogen, and use it in fuel-cell buses, heavy trucks, ships, fertilizer plants, and steel mills—releasing nothing but **clean water** back to nature.

Around the world—in the European Union, the United States, Japan, Australia, the UAE, and India (which launched its **National Green Hydrogen Mission** in 2023)—governments and industries are building pilot plants to make this cycle a reality.

However, major hurdles remain to be solved:
- **Most hydrogen today is still "grey" hydrogen** made from fossil natural gas (Topic 3), which releases CO₂ during production.
- **Green hydrogen is currently expensive** because renewable electrolysis requires huge amounts of electricity.
- **Storage and transport** require high-pressure tanks or −253 °C cryogenic systems (Part H).
- **New safety infrastructure** (pipelines, refueling stations, leak sensors, and trained technicians) must be built on a massive scale.

Thus, the hydrogen economy is **an exciting work in progress**, not an overnight switch. And it leaves us with a crucial question: **when hydrogen burns, it produces only pure water—so what harmful gases do ordinary fuels like coal, gasoline, and diesel release into the sky?** That question leads us directly into our final chapter: **Atmospheric Pollution**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "We can fill party balloons with hydrogen since it's lighter than helium."**
Never. Party balloons must only be filled with non-flammable helium. Hydrogen balloons can explode from a static spark or candle flame.

**Mistake 2: "A fuel cell burns hydrogen with a flame inside to make electricity."**
False. A fuel cell has no flame; it uses a controlled electrochemical reaction across a membrane to convert chemical energy directly into electric current.

**Mistake 3: "Hydrogen has more energy than gasoline in every way."**
Hydrogen has nearly 2.7 times more energy **per kilogram**, but **per liter (by volume)** gasoline holds far more energy than even liquid hydrogen.

**Mistake 4: "Hydrogen is a natural fuel that we drill out of the ground like oil."**
On Earth, hydrogen is almost always chemically combined (in water or hydrocarbons); we have to spend energy to extract it (Topic 3), so scientists call hydrogen an **energy carrier** rather than a primary fuel source.

**Mistake 5: "Fuel cells are only used in buses."**
Fuel cells are used in cars, trucks, trains, backup power generators for hospitals and data centers, and even spacecraft.

**Mistake 6: "You can smell a hydrogen leak just like a kitchen gas leak."**
Hydrogen is completely odorless (and its tiny molecules leak out fast), so electronic hydrogen sensors and ceiling ventilation are essential.

**Mistake 7: "It's fine to leave a compressed hydrogen cylinder out in the hot sun."**
Never. Heating a sealed high-pressure cylinder raises its internal pressure dangerously and can cause a rupture (Chapter 10).

## Quick Recap

- Major uses of hydrogen: **ammonia** (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, for fertilizers), **hydrogenating vegetable oils** (oil + H₂ with nickel catalyst to make solid fats/margarine), **cryogenic rocket fuel** (LH₂ + LOX), **fuel cells** (H₂ + O₂ → water + electricity), **oxy-hydrogen welding**, making HCl and methanol, and reducing metal oxides.
- A fuel cell takes in H₂ and O₂ and produces electricity directly with pure water as the only chemical product: 1 kg of H₂ yields 9 kg of water.
- Energy per kilogram: hydrogen gives about 120 MJ/kg vs. gasoline's 45 MJ/kg—roughly 2.7 times higher by mass (approximate).
- Energy per liter (volume): hydrogen gas at STP has ~3,000 times less energy per m³ than gasoline, and even liquid hydrogen (−253 °C) has ~3.9 times less. Storing hydrogen requires high-pressure cylinders or cryogenic tanks.
- The Hindenburg disaster (1937, 36 fatalities) showed the danger of hydrogen in airships. Modern airships and balloons use **helium**; **never use hydrogen in balloons**.
- Helium gives only about 7.5% less lift than hydrogen and is completely non-flammable.
- The hydrogen economy aims to pair green hydrogen with fuel cells and clean industry, but must overcome production cost, storage volume, safety infrastructure, and the fact that most hydrogen today is still grey.
- Burning hydrogen produces only water; what burning fossil fuels does to the air is the subject of the next chapter.

8. Atom check: left side has C = 3, H = 6 + 2 = 8; right side (C₃H₈) has C = 3, H = 8 ✅. The hydrogen **added across the carbon-carbon double bond**, turning the double bond into a single bond and "saturating" the carbon chain (hydrogenation).

9. Four essential safety features: (i) **Electronic hydrogen leak detectors and alarms near the ceiling**, because hydrogen is odorless, colorless, and rises rapidly. (ii) **Open-air canopy or strong roof ventilation**, so any leaked gas disperses immediately instead of reaching the 4%–75% explosive range. (iii) **Strict no-flame, no-smoking, anti-static grounding zones**, because even a tiny spark can ignite a hydrogen-air mixture. (iv) **Thick-walled, certified pressure cylinders and relief valves shaded from heat and sunlight**, because heating a sealed cylinder dangerously increases pressure. (Trained staff and flame detectors are also valid points.)

10. (a) Lightest gas → **weather balloon** (though helium is safer in practice). (b) Burns/reacts to give only water → **fuel cell** (and rocket propulsion). (c) Reducing agent → **extracting a metal from its oxide**. (d) Very hot flame with oxygen → **welding**. (e) Combines with nitrogen → **ammonia**.
