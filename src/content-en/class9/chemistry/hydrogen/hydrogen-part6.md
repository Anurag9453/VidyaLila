# Topic 6: Hydrogen: Practice and Chapter Summary

Over the past five topics, we have explored hydrogen from every angle: what its atom and isotopes look like, how it is prepared in the school lab, how it is manufactured in industry, what physical and chemical properties it has, and where its uses and hazards lie. Now comes the most rewarding part: **putting all those pieces together**. Starting with a rainy-day riddle game in the school science club, we will work through **14 solved problems** covering "which method?", "which metal?", "who am I?", balanced equations, balloon lift, fuel energy, and connections across Chapters 5 to 10—followed by a master summary of the entire chapter.

## A Rainy Afternoon and "Who Am I?"

Rain had been pouring down since morning, canceling both soccer and track practice. Inside the school science club room, students sat tapping their pencils, looking bored.

Ms. Ortiz pulled a small card box from her desk. "Alright, everyone—today we play **Who Am I?** Each card in this box lists a set of clues about a gas. Let's see who can prove the identity of the gas first!"

Chloe drew the first card and read it aloud: "I am the lightest gas of all. I have no color, odor, or taste. I burn in air, and when I burn, pure water is the only product formed. Who am I?"

"Hydrogen!" Chloe said immediately—then paused. "Wait, Ms. Ortiz... how can I be 100% certain no other gas fits those clues?"

Ms. Ortiz smiled. "Now you're thinking like a chemist. A good riddle has **one and only one valid answer**, and a good scientist checks every clue to rule out all other possibilities. Today, every topic from our Hydrogen chapter will show up as a clue—and you'll even design your own gas riddles."

Outside, the rain kept drumming against the windows, while inside the club room notebooks and pencils came out. Notice that throughout this game **no gas is prepared or burned**—everything is solved through chemical reasoning on paper.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Clue** | A piece of evidence that helps narrow down and identify a substance | "Forms only water when burned in oxygen" |
| **Decision flow** | A step-by-step sequence of questions used to choose the right method | Figure 6.1 |
| **Grey, blue, green hydrogen** | Industry "color labels" based on how the hydrogen was produced | Grey from natural gas, blue with CO₂ capture, green from solar/wind electrolysis |
| **By-product** | A secondary substance formed automatically alongside the main product | Hydrogen from the electrolysis of brine |
| **Reducing agent** | A substance that removes oxygen from another substance | Hydrogen in CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O |
| **Lift** | The net upward buoyancy of a balloon, equal to the mass of displaced air minus the mass of the gas inside | Helium provides about 1.11 kg of lift per m³ |
| **Flammable range** | The concentration range in air within which a gas mixture can ignite or explode | Roughly 4% to 75% for hydrogen in air |

## Part A: Which Method to Choose? (Decision Flow)

We have learned several ways to make hydrogen (Topics 2 and 3). The practical question is: **which method fits which situation?** To decide, we look at three things: **how much** hydrogen is needed, **what raw materials** are available, and **whether zero-carbon production** is required.

```
    +---------------------------------------------------+
    QUESTION A: Do you need only a tiny amount for a
                classroom observation (teacher demo)?
    +---------------------------------------------------+
         yes --> metal + dilute acid (Zn, Mg, or Fe)
                 [Topic 2, tiny scale, teacher demo only]
         no
          v
    +---------------------------------------------------+
    QUESTION B: Do you need huge industrial quantities at
                low cost, with natural gas or coke on hand?
    +---------------------------------------------------+
         yes --> steam reforming or water gas, + Bosch step
                 [Topic 3, "grey" (releases CO₂)]
         no
          v
    +---------------------------------------------------+
    QUESTION C: Do you need zero-carbon hydrogen where
                abundant solar or wind electricity is available?
    +---------------------------------------------------+
         yes --> electrolysis of water
                 [Topic 3, "green" hydrogen]
         no
          v
    +---------------------------------------------------+
    QUESTION D: Is a chlor-alkali plant already electrolyzing
                brine (NaCl solution) to make Cl₂ and NaOH?
    +---------------------------------------------------+
         yes --> collect the hydrogen produced as a by-product
         no  --> re-check from Question A: which requirement changed?
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Decision flow for choosing a hydrogen production method: first check scale and available feedstocks. Tiny classroom demo (by a teacher only): metal + dilute acid. Massive industrial scale at lowest cost: natural gas or coke with steam. Clean and zero-carbon with renewable electricity: water electrolysis (green hydrogen). Chlor-alkali brine plant: hydrogen is collected automatically as a by-product.

**Example 1: Choosing the right method.**
Three scenarios: (a) A chemistry teacher wants to show her class a small sample of hydrogen in a test tube. (b) A large fertilizer plant needs thousands of kilograms of hydrogen every day and sits next to a natural gas pipeline. (c) A sunny desert region with abundant solar power wants to produce carbon-free hydrogen under a national clean-energy initiative.
- (a) Question A: yes → **zinc granules and dilute acid** (Topic 2: zinc reacts at a steady, controllable rate), performed strictly as a teacher demonstration in tiny amounts, away from flames, in a well-ventilated room behind a safety screen.
- (b) Question A: no (massive scale). Question B: yes → **steam reforming**: CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂, followed by the Bosch step: CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂. This produces "grey" hydrogen because CO₂ is released.
- (c) Question C: yes → **water electrolysis powered by solar electricity**: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. This produces **green hydrogen**, the focus of clean-energy initiatives worldwide (including India's 2023 National Green Hydrogen Mission).

> **Check:** Each scenario stops at a different branch of the decision tree. Note that if the electricity in (c) came from burning coal instead of solar panels, the hydrogen would not be "green," because true cleanliness depends on the electricity source too. ✅

**Example 2: How much hydrogen comes from natural gas?**
Add the equations for steam reforming and the Bosch step:
CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂
CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂
Combined equation: **CH₄ + 2H₂O → CO₂ + 4H₂** (the intermediate CO cancels on both sides).
Thus, 1 CH₄ molecule yields **4 H₂** molecules, so 10 CH₄ molecules yield **10 × 4 = 40 H₂** molecules. Those 4 H₂ molecules contain 8 H atoms in total: 4 come from the CH₄ molecule, and 4 come from the two H₂O molecules (2 × 2).

> **Check:** Atom count. Left: C = 1, H = 4 + 4 = 8, O = 2. Right: C = 1, O = 2, H = 8. ✅

Remember: releasing that CO₂ into the sky makes the hydrogen "grey." If the CO₂ is captured and stored deep underground, it is called "blue" hydrogen (Topic 3).

**Example 3: Water electrolysis—mass and volume.**
Suppose 90 g of water undergoes electrolysis: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. The molar mass of water is 2 × 1 + 16 = 18 g/mol, so 90 g = 90 ÷ 18 = 5 mol of water. Since 2 mol of water gives 2 mol of H₂, 5 mol of water gives **5 mol of H₂**, which is 5 × 2 = **10 g of H₂**. For oxygen: 2 mol of water gives 1 mol of O₂, so 5 mol gives 2.5 mol of O₂ = 2.5 × 32 = **80 g of O₂**. Total mass of products = 10 + 80 = 90 g, matching the starting mass of water (conservation of mass).
For gas volumes (Chapter 10): 2 volumes of hydrogen form for every 1 volume of oxygen. If 40 mL of hydrogen collects at the negative electrode, then **20 mL of O₂** collects at the positive electrode.

> **Check:** 10 + 80 = 90 ✅. The mass ratio of hydrogen to oxygen is 10 : 80 = 1 : 8, the fixed composition of water (Chapter 9). Volume ratio 40 : 20 = 2 : 1 ✅.

## Part B: Which Metal Will Work?

In Topic 2, we learned that only metals sitting **above hydrogen** in the reactivity series displace hydrogen from dilute acids. Reactivity series (from top to bottom): K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al, Zn, Fe, Pb, **(H)**, Cu, Hg, Ag, Au.

**Example 4: Which metals release hydrogen from dilute acid?**
Consider adding dilute hydrochloric acid to each of these metals (for observation only, teacher demonstration): Mg, Zn, Fe, Cu, Ag, Na.
- **Mg, Zn, Fe**: sit above hydrogen, so they fizz and release H₂ gas. Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂ (fast), Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ (moderate and steady), Fe + 2HCl → FeCl₂ + H₂ (slow).
- **Cu, Ag**: sit below hydrogen, so **no reaction occurs**.
- **Na**: sits near the very top of the series and reacts so violently and explosively with acid or water that it is never used in the lab to prepare hydrogen.

> **Check:** Atom counts: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂: Mg 1, H 2, Cl 2 on both sides ✅. Zn + 2HCl: Zn 1, H 2, Cl 2 ✅. Fe + 2HCl: Fe 1, H 2, Cl 2 ✅.

**Example 5: Identifying an unknown metal X.**
An unknown metal X produces bubbles of gas when placed in dilute HCl. Metal X **cannot displace** zinc from zinc sulfate solution, but it **does displace** copper from copper sulfate solution. Options: Mg, Fe, Cu, Ag. What is metal X?
- Gives bubbles with dilute HCl → X sits above hydrogen, ruling out **Cu and Ag**.
- Cannot displace zinc → X is **less reactive than zinc**, ruling out **Mg** (since Mg is more reactive than zinc and would displace it).
- Displaces copper → X is more reactive than copper.
The only metal on the list that sits below Zn and above both H and Cu is **Fe** (iron). Answer: **Fe**, uniquely determined by the three clues.

> **Check:** Fe is below Zn ✅ (does not displace Zn), Fe is above H ✅ (fizzes in dilute acid), and Fe is above Cu ✅ (displaces Cu). Three clues, one unique answer.

## Part C: The Riddle Card: "Who Am I?"

Now let's test Chloe's riddle card clue by clue. Each clue eliminates one or more gases until **only one** remains.

```
    *====================================================*
    *            RIDDLE CARD No. 1  (Who Am I?)          *
    *----------------------------------------------------*
    *  1. I am the lightest gas of all.                  *
    *  2. I have no color, odor, or taste.               *
    *  3. I am almost insoluble in water.                *
    *  4. When I burn in oxygen, pure water is the       *
    *     only product formed.                           *
    *  5. A small sample of me gives a squeaky "pop"     *
    *     with a lighted splint (teacher demo only).     *
    *                                                    *
    *  WHO AM I?                                         *
    *====================================================*
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Riddle Card No. 1: five clues about an unknown gas. Each clue narrows down the possibilities until only one gas fits every clue. (This is a paper-and-pencil reasoning game; no gas is prepared or burned.)

**Example 6: Solving Riddle Card No. 1.**
- Clue 1 ("lightest gas of all"): helium is the second lightest (M = 4), while hydrogen is the lightest (M = 2). Only **hydrogen** fits!
- Clue 2: hydrogen is colorless, odorless, and tasteless ✅ (Topic 4).
- Clue 3: hydrogen barely dissolves in water ✅ (which is why it can be collected over water, Topic 2).
- Clue 4: 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, forming only water ✅ (unlike methane, which also forms CO₂, or CO₂/N₂, which do not burn).
- Clue 5: burns with a squeaky **pop** when tested with a lighted splint (teacher demo only, Topics 2 and 4) ✅.
Every single clue matches hydrogen, and Clue 1 alone rules out all other gases (He, N₂, O₂, CO₂). Answer: **hydrogen**.

**Example 7: Riddle Card No. 2—a different gas.**
"I am a gas formed alongside hydrogen in the **Bosch step** (CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂). I dissolve readily in water under high pressure, allowing pure hydrogen to be separated from me. I turn clear limewater milky. I am heavier than air." Who am I?
- The Bosch step produces two gases: H₂ and **CO₂**.
- The one that dissolves in pressurized water is CO₂ (hydrogen barely dissolves).
- Turning limewater milky is the classic test for CO₂.
- "Heavier than air": the molar mass of CO₂ is 12 + 32 = 44 g/mol, compared with about 29 g/mol for air ✅ (Chapter 10). Answer: **carbon dioxide (CO₂)**.

> **Check:** Washing the Bosch-step mixture with pressurized water dissolves CO₂ while leaving insoluble H₂ behind ✅. 44 > 29 ✅.

## Part D: Equations and Reducing Agents

**Example 8: Hydrogen as a reducing agent.**
Fe₂O₃ + 3H₂ → 2Fe + 3H₂O
Atom count: left side has Fe = 2, O = 3, H = 6; right side has Fe = 2, H = 6, O = 3 ✅.
Using Chapter 7 definitions: **Fe₂O₃ loses oxygen**, so it is **reduced** (forming metallic iron, Fe). **H₂ gains oxygen** (forming H₂O), so it is **oxidized**. Therefore, hydrogen acts as the **reducing agent**: it removes oxygen from the metal oxide.
Remember that this works for oxides of moderately or less reactive metals (such as Cu, Pb, W, and Fe), whereas oxides of very reactive metals (like Mg or Al) cannot be reduced by hydrogen.

> **Check:** In CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O: Cu = 1, O = 1, H = 2 on both sides ✅. Water is formed because hydrogen took the oxygen from CuO ✅.

**Example 9: Counting molecules in ammonia synthesis.**
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ (Haber process: iron catalyst, high pressure, reversible reaction—studied further in Class 10).
If 5 molecules of N₂ react completely, they require **5 × 3 = 15 H₂** molecules and produce **5 × 2 = 10 NH₃** molecules (in the ideal case). Atom count: left side has N = 5 × 2 = 10, H = 15 × 2 = 30; right side has N = 10, H = 10 × 3 = 30 ✅.
The molar mass of NH₃ is 14 + 3 × 1 = **17 g/mol**, and its chief use is manufacturing agricultural fertilizers (Topic 5).

> **Check:** Each NH₃ molecule has 1 N atom bonded to 3 H atoms, matching nitrogen's valency of 3 and hydrogen's valency of 1 ✅.

**Example 10: Comparing percentage by mass of hydrogen across compounds.**
Calculate the mass percentage of hydrogen in CH₄, NH₃, H₂O, and HCl (using H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, Cl = 35.5):
- CH₄: 4 ÷ 16 × 100 = **25.0%**
- NH₃: 3 ÷ 17 × 100 = **17.6%**
- H₂O: 2 ÷ 18 × 100 = **11.1%**
- HCl: 1 ÷ 36.5 × 100 = **2.7%**
Of the four, **methane (CH₄)** has the highest mass percentage of hydrogen—one reason natural gas is such a rich feedstock for industrial hydrogen production.

> **Check:** Mass percentage = (mass of H in 1 mol ÷ molar mass of compound) × 100; in CH₄, 4 g of H out of 16 g total gives 4 ÷ 16 = 0.25 (25%) ✅.

## Part E: Balloons, Energy, and Volume Calculations

**Example 11: Balloon lift comparison.**
From Chapter 10: density at STP = molar mass ÷ 22.4 L. For hydrogen: 2 ÷ 22.4 = 0.0893 g/L ≈ **0.09 kg per m³**. For air: 1.29 kg per m³, making air 1.29 ÷ 0.09 ≈ **14.3 times** heavier than hydrogen. For helium: 4 ÷ 22.4 = 0.1786 ≈ **0.18 kg per m³**.
Lift per m³: hydrogen = 1.29 − 0.09 = **1.20 kg**, helium = 1.29 − 0.18 = **1.11 kg**.
For a 10 m³ balloon: hydrogen gives 10 × 1.20 = 12.0 kg of lift, while helium gives 10 × 1.11 = **11.1 kg** of lift. Helium provides 11.1 ÷ 12.0 = **92.5%** of hydrogen's lift—only 7.5% less—while being completely non-flammable. That is why balloons use **helium** (never fill a balloon with hydrogen; buy only helium balloons from a shop, Topic 5).

> **Check:** 1.20 − 1.11 = 0.09 ✅. The 0.9 kg difference over 12.0 kg is 0.9 ÷ 12.0 = 7.5% ✅.

**Example 12: Fuel energy vs. storage volume.**
Hydrogen releases about **120 MJ per kg**, whereas gasoline releases about **45 MJ per kg** (approximate). Per kilogram, hydrogen delivers 120 ÷ 45 = **2.67 times** (about 2.7 times) as much energy.
Thus, 2.5 kg of hydrogen releases 2.5 × 120 = **300 MJ** of energy. To get that same 300 MJ from gasoline requires 300 ÷ 45 = **6.67 kg** of gasoline—so by mass, hydrogen is far lighter.
What about volume? 2.5 kg of H₂ = 2,500 g ÷ 2 g/mol = 1,250 mol. At STP, 1,250 × 22.4 L = 28,000 L = **28 m³** (the size of a small bedroom!). That is why hydrogen must be stored under high compression or chilled to a cryogenic liquid—the **storage challenge** we studied in Topic 5.

> **Check:** 1,250 × 22.4 = 28,000 L = 28 m³ ✅. 6.67 × 45 = 300 MJ ✅.

**Example 13: Checking the explosive range.**
Hydrogen in air can ignite or explode between roughly **4% and 75%** by volume (approximate). Classify three mixtures: (a) 3% hydrogen in air, (b) 30% hydrogen in air, (c) 80% hydrogen in air.
- (a) 3% < 4%: below the lower limit (too lean to sustain a flame).
- (b) 30%: **inside the explosive range—extremely hazardous**.
- (c) 80% > 75%: above the upper limit (too rich in place), **but still dangerous** because as soon as it mixes with surrounding room air, it dilutes right through the 75%–4% explosive window!
Therefore, the rule for any hydrogen leak is strict: **keep all sparks and flames away, ventilate from the ceiling, and never let hydrogen accumulate in an enclosed space.**

> **Check:** 3 < 4 ✅, 4 ≤ 30 ≤ 75 ✅, 80 > 75 ✅.

## Part F: Connecting Across Chapters

**Example 14: Isotopes and heavy water.**
From Chapter 5 and Topic 1: protium (1 proton, 0 neutrons), deuterium (1 proton, 1 neutron), tritium (1 proton, 2 neutrons, radioactive, trace). Protium makes up about 99.98% of natural hydrogen: out of 50,000 hydrogen atoms, **50,000 × 0.9998 = 49,990** are protium, and the remaining 10 are mostly deuterium.
Heavy water (D₂O) has a molar mass of 2 × 2 + 16 = **20 g/mol**, compared with 18 g/mol for ordinary water (H₂O). Ratio: 20 ÷ 18 = **1.11** (about 11% heavier). Counting neutrons in one molecule of D₂O: 2 deuterium atoms contribute 1 + 1 = 2 neutrons, and oxygen-16 contributes 8 neutrons, giving **10 neutrons in total** (compared with 8 neutrons in H₂O). Heavy water is used as a **moderator** in pressurized heavy-water nuclear reactors.

> **Check:** In D₂O, total protons = 1 + 1 + 8 = 10 and total neutrons = 1 + 1 + 8 = 10, giving a mass number sum of 20 ✅.

## Safety Rules (Hydrogen)

- Hydrogen is **extremely flammable**, and mixtures of hydrogen with air or oxygen can **explode**. Preparing, collecting, burning, or testing hydrogen is performed **only by a teacher in the school lab (for observation only, teacher demonstration)**, in tiny amounts, far from flames, with good ventilation and a safety screen.
- **Never attempt to make or burn hydrogen at home.** Student activities at home are strictly paper-based (riddles, sorting tables, diagrams, calculations).
- **Never fill balloons with hydrogen:** always use store-bought helium balloons. Remember the lesson of the 1937 Hindenburg airship disaster (36 fatalities).
- **Never heat a sealed container or gas cylinder.** Keep compressed gas cylinders cool, ventilated, and out of direct sunlight.
- Never mix reactive metals like sodium, potassium, or calcium with water or acid: those reactions are studied on paper only.
- Never **taste** or directly **inhale** any laboratory gas.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **"Hydrogen can burn even without air or oxygen."** False. Hydrogen is a **fuel**, so it requires **oxygen** to burn. Without oxygen, pure hydrogen cannot burn.
2. **"Every metal reacts with dilute acid to give hydrogen."** False. Only metals **above** hydrogen in the reactivity series displace hydrogen from dilute acids (Cu, Ag, and Au do not). And alkali metals like Na and K are far too explosive to use.
3. **"Dilute nitric acid also gives hydrogen gas with zinc."** False. Nitric acid acts as an oxidizing agent and behaves differently; you will study its reactions in Class 10.
4. **"Students can try the squeaky pop test at home."** False. Preparing, igniting, or testing hydrogen is strictly a **teacher demonstration**.
5. **"Because hydrogen burns to water, all hydrogen fuel is automatically carbon-free."** Burning hydrogen produces only water at the tailpipe, but if the hydrogen was made from natural gas ("grey" hydrogen), **CO₂ was emitted at the factory.** Always check how the hydrogen was produced.
6. **"Hydrogen is a weak fuel because it is so light."** Per **kilogram**, hydrogen has nearly 2.7 times *more* energy than gasoline (120 MJ vs. 45 MJ); it is only per **cubic meter (volume)** that uncompressed hydrogen is low, creating a storage challenge.
7. **"Hydrogen is a metal because it sits at the top of Group 1."** False. Hydrogen is a **non-metal gas** placed above Group 1 purely for convenience because it has 1 outer electron (Chapter 8).
8. **"A 90% hydrogen mixture in a pipe is completely safe because it's above 75%."** False. The moment it leaks into the room, mixing with air brings it right into the 4%–75% explosive range (Example 13).

## Quick Recap

- Hydrogen: lightest element, most abundant in the universe, symbol H, valency 1, diatomic gas H₂ (one single covalent bond). Isotopes: protium, deuterium, tritium. Heavy water D₂O (20 g/mol).
- Lab preparation (teacher demo only): Zn, Mg, or Fe + dilute acid (HCl or H₂SO₄); copper does not react. Collected over water or by downward displacement of air in an inverted jar; identified by the squeaky pop test.
- Other methods: electrolysis of water (2 : 1 volume ratio of H₂ : O₂), active metals + water (description only), steam reforming of natural gas and water-gas reaction from coke + Bosch step in industry, by-product of brine electrolysis, and green hydrogen from solar/wind electrolysis.
- Properties: colorless, odorless, density ~0.09 g/L at STP, boiling point −253 °C (20 K), neutral to litmus. Burns in oxygen (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O), combines with Cl₂ and N₂, and reduces heated oxides of moderately/less reactive metals.
- Uses: ammonia (fertilizers), hydrogenating vegetable oils, cryogenic rocket fuel, fuel cells, welding. Delivers ~120 MJ/kg by mass, but takes up a huge volume as a gas.
- Safety: highly flammable, explosive in air from ~4% to 75%, use helium (never hydrogen) in balloons, never heat sealed cylinders.
- Burning hydrogen produces only pure water; what burning fossil fuels releases into the air is the subject of our next chapter.

## Practice Questions

1. Choose the best method of making hydrogen for each situation and state one reason: (a) a steel plant that already has coke and steam; (b) a chlor-alkali plant that splits brine to make chlorine and sodium hydroxide; (c) a school lab that has only copper turnings and dilute hydrochloric acid.

2. A teacher plans a demonstration with dilute sulphuric acid. Which of these metals will give hydrogen: magnesium, gold, potassium, iron? For each one that does not, give the reason.

3. Balance and check atoms: Fe + H₂O (steam) → Fe₃O₄ + H₂. Is iron oxidised or reduced here? Why?

4. Combine C + H₂O → CO + H₂ and CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂ into one equation. How many H₂ molecules come from 7 carbon atoms by this two-step route?

5. Riddle: "I am a colourless, odourless gas. I am made at the positive electrode when water is electrolysed. The volume I make is half the volume of the gas at the other electrode. I relight a glowing splinter." Who am I? Explain why only one gas fits.

6. Riddle: "I am a greenish-yellow gas. I am made together with hydrogen and sodium hydroxide when brine is electrolysed. Hydrogen joins with me in sunlight or on heating to give hydrogen chloride. I am heavier than air." Who am I? Write the two equations and give the molar mass check for "heavier than air" (Cl = 35.5).

7. A rocket stage carries 4 kg of hydrogen. (a) How much energy is that (120 MJ per kg)? (b) What mass of petrol would give the same energy (45 MJ per kg)? (c) What volume would 4 kg of hydrogen occupy at STP, in m³?

8. A weather balloon of 25 m³ is filled with helium (density 0.18 kg per m³, air 1.29 kg per m³). Find the lift. What lift would hydrogen give, and why is helium still chosen?

9. In the Haber process N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, how many H₂ and how many NH₃ particles correspond to 12 N₂ particles (ideal case)? Name the catalyst and state whether the reaction can go both ways.

10. "Semi-heavy" water is HDO (one protium, one deuterium, one oxygen-16). Find its molar mass and the number of neutrons in one molecule. In 20000 hydrogen atoms of ordinary hydrogen, how many are protium (99.98%)?

11. Find the mass percent of hydrogen in methanol, CH₃OH (C = 12, O = 16, H = 1). Is it more or less than in water?

12. Classify each mixture as below, inside or above the flammable range (about 4% to 75% hydrogen in air) and say which is safe to leave near a spark: (a) 6%; (b) 50%; (c) 90%; (d) 0.5%.

13. Liquid oxygen boils at −183 °C and hydrogen at −253 °C. Give both in kelvin, the difference, and say which gas needs more cooling to liquefy.

14. True or false, with a reason: (a) Copper gives hydrogen with dilute hydrochloric acid. (b) In Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂ → 3Fe + 4H₂O hydrogen is oxidised. (c) Ordinary hydrogen (protium) has no neutron. (d) Students may test hydrogen with a lighted splinter at home.

### Answers

1. (a) **Water-gas reaction + Bosch step**: C + H₂O → CO + H₂ followed by CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂, since coke and steam are already on hand. (b) **By-product collection**: electrolysis of brine automatically produces hydrogen alongside chlorine and NaOH, so no separate process is needed. (c) Copper lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series and **will not release hydrogen** from dilute HCl—the lab needs a metal above hydrogen such as Zn, Mg, or Fe. Check: each case answers a different branch (A, B, D) of the decision flow. ✅

2. **Magnesium** (Mg + H₂SO₄ → MgSO₄ + H₂) and **iron** (Fe + H₂SO₄ → FeSO₄ + H₂) will release hydrogen. **Gold** sits far below hydrogen in the reactivity series, so no reaction occurs. **Potassium** sits at the very top of the series and reacts violently and explosively with acid or water, so it is never used in a lab demonstration. Check: Mg 1, S 1, O 4, H 2 on both sides ✅.

3. **3Fe + 4H₂O → Fe₃O₄ + 4H₂**. Atom count: left side has Fe = 3, H = 8, O = 4; right side has Fe = 3, O = 4, H = 8 ✅. Iron gained oxygen, so **iron is oxidized** (while the steam lost oxygen and was reduced to H₂). Check: gaining oxygen is oxidation (Chapter 7) ✅.

4. Add C + H₂O → CO + H₂ and CO + H₂O → CO₂ + H₂: **C + 2H₂O → CO₂ + 2H₂** (CO cancels on both sides). Since 1 C yields 2 H₂ molecules, 7 C atoms yield 7 × 2 = **14 H₂** molecules. Check: left has C = 1, H = 4, O = 2; right has C = 1, O = 2, H = 4 ✅.

5. **Oxygen (O₂)**. During water electrolysis, oxygen forms at the positive electrode and hydrogen at the negative electrode in a 1 : 2 volume ratio (so oxygen's volume is half that of hydrogen). Relighting a glowing splint is the signature test for oxygen. Only O₂ fits both the electrolysis ratio and the glowing-splint test. Check: 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ gives 2 volumes of H₂ per 1 volume of O₂ ✅.

6. **Chlorine (Cl₂)**. Brine electrolysis: 2NaCl + 2H₂O → 2NaOH + Cl₂ + H₂ (Topic 3). Reaction with hydrogen: H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl (Topic 4). The molar mass of Cl₂ is 2 × 35.5 = 71 g/mol, compared with ~29 g/mol for air; since 71 > 29, chlorine is heavier than air ✅. Its greenish-yellow color and formation from brine uniquely identify chlorine. Check: in 2NaCl + 2H₂O, Na = 2, Cl = 2, H = 4, O = 2 on both sides ✅; in H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, H = 2, Cl = 2 on both sides ✅. (Note: chlorine is a toxic gas; this is strictly a paper riddle.)

7. (a) 4 × 120 = **480 MJ**. (b) 480 ÷ 45 = **10.67 kg** of gasoline (about 10.7 kg). (c) 4 kg = 4,000 g ÷ 2 g/mol = 2,000 mol; at STP, 2,000 × 22.4 L = 44,800 L = **44.8 m³**. That huge volume is why rockets chill hydrogen into a dense cryogenic liquid. Check: 10.67 × 45 = 480 ✅.

8. Lift with helium = 1.29 − 0.18 = 1.11 kg/m³, so 25 × 1.11 = **27.75 kg**. Lift with hydrogen = 1.29 − 0.09 = 1.20 kg/m³, so 25 × 1.20 = **30.0 kg**. Difference = 30.0 − 27.75 = 2.25 kg (about 7.5%). Helium is chosen because it **cannot burn or explode**, whereas hydrogen is highly flammable. Check: 27.75 ÷ 30.0 = 0.925 ✅.

9. For 12 N₂ molecules, 12 × 3 = **36 H₂** molecules are needed and 12 × 2 = **24 NH₃** molecules form. Catalyst: **iron**. The reaction is **reversible** (can go both ways, studied in higher classes). Check: left has N = 24, H = 72; right has N = 24, H = 24 × 3 = 72 ✅.

10. Molar mass of HDO = 1 + 2 + 16 = **19 g/mol**. Neutrons in one HDO molecule: protium (0) + deuterium (1) + oxygen-16 (8) = **9 neutrons**. Out of 20,000 hydrogen atoms: 20,000 × 0.9998 = **19,996** are protium. Check: 19,996 + 4 = 20,000 ✅.

11. For CH₃OH: molar mass = 12 + (4 × 1) + 16 = **32 g/mol**, with 4 g of hydrogen. Mass percentage of H = 4 ÷ 32 × 100 = **12.5%**. In water it is 11.1%, so methanol has a **higher** mass percentage of hydrogen. Check: 12 + 4 + 16 = 32 ✅.

12. (a) 6%: **inside** the explosive range (hazardous). (b) 50%: **inside** (hazardous). (c) 90%: **above** the upper limit in place, but dangerous because mixing with air dilutes it right through the explosive range. (d) 0.5%: **below** the lower limit (too lean to burn). None of them should ever be left near a spark—all hydrogen leaks require immediate ventilation and zero ignition sources. Check: 4 ≤ 6 ≤ 75, 4 ≤ 50 ≤ 75, 90 > 75, 0.5 < 4 ✅.

13. Oxygen: −183 + 273 = **90 K**. Hydrogen: −253 + 273 = **20 K**. Difference: 90 − 20 = **70 K** (or 70 °C, since step sizes are equal on both scales). **Hydrogen** requires much deeper cooling (down to 20 K) to liquefy. Check: −183 − (−253) = 70 ✅.

14. (a) **False** ❌. Copper lies below hydrogen in the reactivity series and does not displace hydrogen from dilute HCl. (b) **True** ✅. Hydrogen gains oxygen to form H₂O, so it is oxidized (while Fe₃O₄ is reduced). Atom count: Fe = 3, O = 4, H = 8 on both sides. (c) **True** ✅. Protium has 1 proton and 0 neutrons. (d) **False** ❌. Testing hydrogen is strictly a teacher demonstration in a school lab, never done by students at home.

| Topic 4 | Physical and chemical properties | Colorless, odorless, lightest gas (0.09 g/L), boils at −253 °C (20 K), burns in oxygen (2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O), combines with Cl₂ and N₂, reduces heated metal oxides, and has a wide 4%–75% explosive range in air. |
