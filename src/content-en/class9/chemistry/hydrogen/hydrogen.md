# Topic 1: Hydrogen: Position, Introduction, and Isotopes

In the previous chapter, "Gas Laws," we learned from the story of floating balloons that how light a gas is depends on its **molar mass**: any gas with an M lower than the average molar mass of air (about 29) rises upward. At the very top of that density table sat the gas with the lowest M of all, just **2 g per mol**: **hydrogen**. Back then, we met it only as a name. Now, this entire chapter is dedicated to this remarkable gas. Today, we first ask what hydrogen **actually is**: its atom, its molecule, its place in the universe and in the periodic table, and its three "siblings" (isotopes). In the upcoming topics, we will see how it is prepared, what properties it has, and where it is used.

## The Biggest Secret at the Planetarium

During the school holidays, Uncle Marcus took his nephew Leo to the city planetarium. Above their heads arched a giant dome screen; the hall lights dimmed, and a breathtaking view of the stars appeared. Then a deep narrator's voice echoed across the dome: "Our Sun looks like a giant ball of fire. Yet in reality, it is made almost entirely of **hydrogen**. Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the entire universe."

Leo leaned over and whispered to Uncle Marcus, "Wait—isn't hydrogen that super-light gas that lifts weather balloons? How can there be so much of it in the Sun? And I've heard that hydrogen catches fire easily, so why doesn't the Sun just blow up like a giant bomb?"

Uncle Marcus smiled. "Two questions, and both are great ones. What happens inside the Sun is completely different from ordinary burning here on Earth; for now, just know that deep inside the Sun, hydrogen atoms **fuse together** and release enormous energy. You'll study that in higher grades. Today, let's simply get to know **who hydrogen is**: what's inside its atom, where it is found, and how much of it exists. And here's a bonus secret: hydrogen comes in more than one form, like three brothers in the same family."

Walking out of the planetarium, Leo kept wondering: how could the biggest things in the cosmos be built from the smallest atom of all? Let's examine it step by step.

## Part A: Introduction to Hydrogen: Atom and Molecule

### The Atom: The Simplest of All

Recall from Chapter 5 that an atom's identity is fixed by its **atomic number Z**, the number of protons in its nucleus. For hydrogen, **Z = 1**: its atom has **1 proton** and **1 electron**. Most remarkably, ordinary hydrogen has **no neutron at all** (0 neutrons) in its nucleus. That is why it is the smallest and lightest atom: mass number A = 1 + 0 = 1, and atomic mass 1 u (Chapter 4).

- **Symbol:** H
- **Electronic configuration:** 1 (just 1 electron in the K shell)
- **Valency:** 1. Hydrogen can either **lose** its single electron, or **gain / share** one more electron to fill its K shell as a stable duplet (2 electrons). Either way, its valency is 1.

### The Molecule: H₂

In nature, a single isolated hydrogen atom rarely stays alone. Two hydrogen atoms **share** one electron each (the covalent bond from Chapter 6) to form **H₂**. Each atom then sees **2 electrons** in its K shell (a complete duplet), making both stable. Between the two atoms lies **one shared pair of electrons**, which is **one single covalent bond** (H–H). That is why hydrogen gas is written as "H₂" and not "H": it is **diatomic**.

```
    One atom of hydrogen (H)          One molecule of hydrogen (H₂)

          1 electron                       shared pair
             o                                (e e)
         .   .   .                        .--------------.
       .           .                    (  H     :    H  )
      .     (+)     .                    '--------------'
       .   1 proton .
         .   .   .                      H : H   or   H–H
                                        (one single bond)

    Only 1 proton in the nucleus,       Two nuclei sharing 2 electrons
    0 neutrons. 1 electron in           between them. Each atom's K shell
    the half-filled K shell.            is completed as a "duplet".
```

> **Figure 1.1** – A hydrogen atom (1 proton, 1 electron, 0 neutrons) and an H₂ molecule (one shared pair of electrons between two atoms, forming a single covalent bond). The drawing is purely illustrative; in reality, the electron orbits far from a tiny central nucleus.

Check: Molar mass of H₂ = 2 × 1 = 2 g per mol, which matches the Gas Laws table (M = 2 for hydrogen) ✅

### How Light Is Hydrogen?

One mole of hydrogen gas (H₂) has a mass of only **2 g**. That is the lowest of any gas: helium is 4 g, nitrogen is 28 g, and air averages about 29 g (Gas Laws, Topic 5). For this reason, **hydrogen is the lightest gas known**, and it rises faster than any other gas in air.

> **Check:** An atom with 1 proton and 0 neutrons has a mass number of 1: 1 + 0 = 1 ✅

## Part B: Hydrogen in the Universe and on Earth

Now let's return to Leo's question: "How much hydrogen is in the Sun?" Scientists split sunlight into a rainbow of colors (a "spectrum") to measure how much of each element is present. By **mass**, the Sun is roughly **73 percent hydrogen**, about **25 percent helium**, and only about **2 percent** all other elements combined (oxygen, carbon, iron, and so on). (These figures are **approximate**; different sources may quote slightly different numbers.)

```
    100 "parts" of the Sun (by mass, approximate)

    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H H H H H H H H
    H H H E E E E E E E
    E E E E E E E E E E
    E E E E E E E E x x

    H = hydrogen (73 parts)    E = helium (25 parts)
    x = all other elements (2 parts)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Approximate breakdown of the Sun's mass across 100 small squares: 73 hydrogen, 25 helium, and 2 everything else (approximate figures). Hydrogen makes up the bulk of the Sun and most other stars.

Across the entire **universe**, the picture is very similar: by mass, roughly 75 percent is hydrogen, about 24 percent is helium, and all other elements together make up about 1 percent. That is why hydrogen is called **the most abundant element in the universe**, serving as the primary "fuel" and raw building block of stars.

### On Earth, the Story Is Reversed

On Earth, **free (uncombined)** hydrogen is almost non-existent. In the atmosphere, H₂ makes up only about 0.00005 percent (approximate). Why? Because H₂ is so light that it rises to the top of the atmosphere and escapes into outer space (just as we saw for light gases in Gas Laws). Consequently, almost all hydrogen on Earth is found **combined with other elements in compounds**:

- **Water (H₂O):** every water molecule contains 2 hydrogen atoms—in oceans, rivers, rain, and ice.
- **Living things:** in plants, animals, and our own bodies (across countless organic compounds).
- **Hydrocarbons (fuels):** gasoline, diesel, LPG, natural gas, and candle wax (compounds of hydrogen and carbon).
- **Acids:** such as hydrochloric acid (HCl) in our stomach. You will study the full story of acids in Class 10.

Let's do a quick calculation of how much hydrogen is locked inside water:

### Problem 1. Mass of hydrogen in water

How many grams of hydrogen are present in 180 g of water? (H = 1, O = 16.)

- M(H₂O) = 2 × 1 + 16 = 18 g per mol.
- Fraction of hydrogen = 2 ÷ 18 = 0.1111, or about **11.1 percent**.
- Hydrogen in 180 g of water = 180 × 2 ÷ 18 = **20 g**.

Check: 20 g hydrogen + 160 g oxygen = 180 g ✅ (oxygen: 180 × 16 ÷ 18 = 160 g)

This is the exact 1 : 8 mass ratio we met in Chapter 9: 20 : 160 = 1 : 8 ✅

## Part C: Hydrogen's Unusual Place in the Periodic Table

In Chapter 8, we saw that hydrogen sits **at the top of Group 1, in Period 1**, right above Li, Na, and K. But is it truly one of their family? Let's look at both sides of the story.

| Feature | Like Group 1 metals (Li, Na, K) | What hydrogen is in reality |
|---|---|---|
| Outer electron | 1 | 1 (which is why it is placed above Group 1) |
| Valency | 1 | 1 |
| Appearance | Shiny, solid metal | Colorless, odorless gas |
| Type | Metal | **Non-metal** |
| Molecule | Metallic lattice | H₂, a two-atom molecule (like Cl₂) |

Having 1 outer electron makes hydrogen resemble Group 1 on paper, but its actual properties (being a gas, a non-metal, and forming covalent H₂ molecules) resemble the halogens (F, Cl), which need **one more electron** to complete a duplet or octet. Thus, hydrogen stands "between two groups." It is placed at the top of Group 1 purely for **convenience**, and you must remember that it is **not a metal** (as noted in periodic-table-part3.md).

> **Check:** Seeing hydrogen in Group 1 and assuming it is a metal is wrong. ❌ Hydrogen is a non-metal gas; its placement in Group 1 is only because it has 1 outer electron. ✅

## Part D: The Three Isotopes of Hydrogen

In Chapter 5, Topic 6, we learned that atoms of the same element can have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons—these are called **isotopes**. We saw a brief table of hydrogen's three isotopes there. Now let's examine them from the perspective of this chapter:

| Name | Symbol | Protons | Neutrons | Electrons | Mass number A |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Protium | ¹₁H | 1 | 0 | 1 | 1 |
| Deuterium | ²₁H | 1 | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| Tritium | ³₁H | 1 | 2 | 1 | 3 |

Neutrons = A − Z: 1 − 1 = 0, 2 − 1 = 1, 3 − 1 = 2 ✅

```
    Protium          Deuterium        Tritium
    (A = 1)          (A = 2)          (A = 3)

      (+)            (+)(n)           (+)(n)(n)
    nucleus          nucleus          nucleus
       o                o                o
    1 electron       1 electron       1 electron

    (+) = proton   (n) = neutron   o = electron

    Ordinary         Heavy            Radioactive,
    hydrogen         hydrogen         extremely rare
    (about
    99.98 percent)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – The three isotopes of hydrogen: all three have 1 proton and 1 electron, with 0, 1, and 2 neutrons respectively. Their mass numbers are therefore 1, 2, and 3. (Sketch is only for counting particles.)

- **Protium** is our "ordinary" hydrogen: about **99.98 percent** of all hydrogen atoms are protium. Out of 10,000 hydrogen atoms, roughly 9,998 are protium. The tiny remainder (about 0.02 percent) is mostly deuterium.
- **Deuterium** is often called "heavy hydrogen" (also written with the symbol D).
- **Tritium** is **radioactive** (it emits radiation) and occurs in nature only in trace amounts. Its uses were mentioned in Chapter 5; here we simply recognize it.

All three have **almost identical chemical behavior**, because chemical reactions depend on electrons, and all three have just 1 electron (Chapter 5, Part C). Extra neutrons only change the **mass**.

### Problem 2. Inside a deuterium atom

How many protons, neutrons, and electrons are in one neutral atom of deuterium (²₁H)?

- Z = 1, so protons = **1**.
- In a neutral atom, electrons = protons = **1**.
- Neutrons = A − Z = 2 − 1 = **1**.

Check: protons + neutrons = 1 + 1 = 2 = A ✅

### Problem 3. Three forms of hydrogen gas (molar mass)

Ordinary hydrogen gas (H₂) has a molar mass of 2 g per mol. If both atoms are deuterium (D, mass 2), what is the molar mass of D₂? And what about a mixed molecule HD (one protium + one deuterium)?

- D₂: 2 × 2 = **4 g per mol**. (Equal to helium's molar mass! Yet D₂ is still hydrogen, not helium; having the same mass is just a coincidence.)
- HD: 1 + 2 = **3 g per mol**.

Check: H₂ 2 < HD 3 < D₂ 4 ✅ (each deuterium atom adds one extra neutron, or 1 u of mass.)

## Part E: Heavy Water

If the ordinary hydrogen (protium) in water (H₂O) is replaced by **deuterium**, the resulting compound is called **heavy water**, written **D₂O**. It looks just like ordinary water, but it is slightly heavier because each D atom brings an extra neutron. (Same chemistry, different mass—just like isotopes.)

### Problem 4. Molar mass of H₂O and D₂O

Calculate and compare the molar masses of both. (H = 1, D = 2, O = 16.)

- H₂O: 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 g per mol**.
- D₂O: 2 × 2 + 16 = **20 g per mol**.
- Difference = 20 − 18 = **2 g per mol**.
- D₂O is heavier: 20 ÷ 18 = 1.111, or about **11 percent** heavier.

Check: 18 × 1.111 = 20.0 ✅

This slight extra mass is useful in nuclear engineering: **heavy water** is used in certain types of nuclear reactors (such as pressurized heavy-water reactors in Canada and India) as a **moderator**, meaning it helps slow down fast-moving neutrons inside the reactor core. For Class 9, this is just a brief glimpse; you will study nuclear reactors in higher classes.

> **Safety:** Heavy water is part of specialized nuclear technology and is handled only by trained professionals. It is never used for home or school experiments. The water we drink every day is ordinary water (H₂O).

## Part F: Quick Calculations: Counting Hydrogen Atoms

### Problem 5. Atoms in 1 g of hydrogen

How many atoms are in 1 g of hydrogen gas (H₂)? (Avogadro number = 6.022 × 10²³.)

- M(H₂) = 2 g per mol, so 1 g = 1 ÷ 2 = 0.5 mol of **molecules**.
- Each molecule has 2 atoms: moles of atoms = 0.5 × 2 = **1 mol**.
- Atoms = 1 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **6.022 × 10²³ atoms**.

Check: Directly, 1 mol of H atoms weighs 1 g (atomic mass 1), so 1 g contains 1 mol of atoms ✅

### Problem 6. In 1,000 kg of solar matter

If 1,000 kg of the Sun's matter is divided in the mass ratio 73 : 25 : 2, how many kilograms of hydrogen, helium, and other elements does it contain?

- Hydrogen = 1000 × 0.73 = **730 kg**.
- Helium = 1000 × 0.25 = **250 kg**.
- Others = 1000 × 0.02 = **20 kg**.

Check: 730 + 250 + 20 = 1000 kg ✅

### Problem 7. Isotopes in 10,000 hydrogen atoms

Out of 10,000 hydrogen atoms, roughly how many are protium and how many are "others" (deuterium + tritium)?

- Protium = 10000 × 0.9998 = **9998**.
- Others = 10000 − 9998 = **2**.

Check: 2 ÷ 10000 = 0.0002 = 0.02 percent ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Writing hydrogen gas as "H" instead of H₂**

Hydrogen gas is **H₂** (a diatomic molecule). A single H represents an isolated atom. Always write H₂ in chemical equations for hydrogen gas.

**Mistake 2: Assuming hydrogen is a metal because it sits above Group 1**

Hydrogen is a **non-metal gas**. It is placed above Group 1 only for convenience because it has 1 outer electron.

**Mistake 3: Thinking free hydrogen is abundant on Earth**

Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe, but on Earth **free (uncombined)** H₂ is extremely rare; almost all terrestrial hydrogen is **combined** in water, living organisms, and fuels.

**Mistake 4: Thinking isotopes are different elements**

Protium, deuterium, and tritium are **all hydrogen**, because every one of them has 1 proton. They differ only in their number of neutrons.

**Mistake 5: Thinking heavy water is a "super-dense, deadly" liquid**

Heavy water (D₂O) is simply water made with deuterium atoms, and it is only about 11 percent heavier than ordinary water (molar mass 20 vs. 18). It is used in specialized nuclear technology and handled by trained professionals.

## Quick Recap

- **Hydrogen:** symbol H, Z = 1, 1 proton + 1 electron, 0 neutrons in ordinary hydrogen; valency 1; the lightest element.
- Gaseous form **H₂**: **one single covalent bond** (H–H) between two atoms; molar mass 2 g per mol, the lightest of all gases.
- **Most abundant element in the universe:** by mass, the Sun is about 73 percent H, 25 percent He, and 2 percent other elements.
- On Earth, hydrogen occurs mostly in **compounds**: water (about 11.1 percent H by mass in H₂O), living matter, hydrocarbon fuels, and acids. Free H₂ in air is tiny (about 0.00005 percent).
- In the periodic table, hydrogen is placed above Group 1 for convenience; it is a **non-metal gas**, not a metal.
- **Isotopes:** protium (1p, 0n, about 99.98 percent), deuterium (1p, 1n), tritium (1p, 2n, radioactive, trace); all three share nearly identical chemistry.
- **Heavy water D₂O:** molar mass 20 compared to 18 for H₂O; used as a moderator in certain nuclear reactors.
- Safety: hydrogen is a highly flammable gas; all demonstrations involving it are performed only by a teacher, never by students on their own.

## Practice Questions

1. How many protons, neutrons and electrons are there in a neutral tritium atom (³₁H)? What is its mass number?

2. Compare the percentage by mass of hydrogen in ammonia (NH₃) and in methane (CH₄). Which has the larger share? (N = 14, C = 12, H = 1.)

3. Find the molar mass of tritium gas T₂ (each T has mass 3) and of the mixed molecule HT.

4. How many moles of molecules and how many hydrogen atoms are there in 4 g of hydrogen gas (H₂)? (Avogadro number = 6.022 × 10²³.)

5. In 5,000,000 hydrogen atoms, about how many are protium and how many are not (use 99.98 percent for protium)?

6. A sample of 400 kg of cosmic gas is about 75 percent hydrogen, 24 percent helium and 1 percent other elements by mass. Find the mass of each.

7. Compare the mass of 5 mol of D₂O with the mass of 5 mol of H₂O. Which is heavier, and by how much?

8. Hydrogen is placed above group 1 in the periodic table. Give one reason it fits there and two reasons it is not a group 1 metal.

9. Why is hydrogen mostly found in compounds on Earth, although it is the most abundant element in the universe? Name three places where you will find combined hydrogen.

10. Protium, deuterium and tritium behave almost the same in chemical reactions but have different masses. Explain why.

### Answers

1. Tritium: Z = 1, so **1 proton**; a neutral atom has **1 electron**; neutrons = A − Z = 3 − 1 = **2 neutrons**; mass number **A = 3**. Check: 1 + 2 = 3 ✅

2. NH₃: M = 14 + 3 = 17; hydrogen = 3 ÷ 17 = 0.1765, or **about 17.6 percent**. CH₄: M = 12 + 4 = 16; hydrogen = 4 ÷ 16 = 0.25, or **25 percent**. **Methane** has the larger share of hydrogen by mass. Check: in NH₃, nitrogen is 14 ÷ 17 = 82.4 percent, 17.6 + 82.4 = 100 ✅; in CH₄, carbon is 12 ÷ 16 = 75 percent, 25 + 75 = 100 ✅

3. T₂ = 2 × 3 = **6 g per mol**; HT = 1 + 3 = **4 g per mol**. Check: each tritium atom is 2 u heavier than ordinary hydrogen, so from H₂ (2) to T₂ is 2 + 2 × 2 = 6 ✅

4. M(H₂) = 2, so moles of molecules = 4 ÷ 2 = **2 mol**. Moles of atoms = 2 × 2 = 4 mol. Number of atoms = 4 × 6.022 × 10²³ = **2.409 × 10²⁴ atoms** (approx.). Check: 4 g of hydrogen atoms = 4 mol (atomic mass 1), 4 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 2.409 × 10²⁴ ✅

5. Protium = 5,000,000 × 0.9998 = **4,999,000**. Others = 5,000,000 − 4,999,000 = **1,000**. Check: 1000 ÷ 5,000,000 = 0.0002 = 0.02 percent ✅

6. Hydrogen = 400 × 0.75 = **300 kg**; helium = 400 × 0.24 = **96 kg**; other elements = 400 × 0.01 = **4 kg**. Check: 300 + 96 + 4 = 400 kg ✅

7. M(H₂O) = 18, M(D₂O) = 20. 5 mol H₂O = 5 × 18 = **90 g**; 5 mol D₂O = 5 × 20 = **100 g**. **D₂O is heavier by 10 g** (about 11 percent heavier). Check: 100 ÷ 90 = 1.111 ✅

8. **Reason it fits:** it has **1 outer electron** and a **valency of 1**, just like the Group 1 metals. **Two reasons it is not a metal:** (i) it is a **colorless gas**, not a shiny solid metal; (ii) it is a **non-metal** that forms a diatomic **covalent H₂ molecule** like Cl₂ (similar to halogens). Its placement above Group 1 is purely for **convenience**.

9. In the universe, stars are made mostly of hydrogen, but on Earth free H₂ is so **light** that it rises through the atmosphere and escapes into space, leaving almost none in the air. On Earth it is found mostly **combined**: in **water** (H₂O), **fuels** (gasoline, LPG: hydrocarbons), and **living things** (bodies, plants), as well as in acids.

10. Chemical reactions depend on **electrons**, and all three isotopes have **1 proton and 1 electron**, so their chemical behavior is nearly identical. They differ only in the **number of neutrons** (0, 1, 2), which changes their **mass** (mass numbers 1, 2, 3).
