# Topic 2: Solutions: Dissolving and Concentration

In Topic 1, we learned that most matter around us consists of **mixtures**, which fall into two broad types: **heterogeneous** (where different components remain visibly distinct, like tossed salad or soil) and **homogeneous** (where the composition is uniform throughout and individual components are indistinguishable, like clean air or brass). Now we explore the most widespread and practically important family of homogeneous mixtures: **solutions**. We will not only investigate how substances dissolve at the particle level, but also learn how to calculate precisely how much solute is packed into a given solution.

---

## Oral Rehydration Salts in the School Clinic

After a vigorous lunchtime soccer match on a warm afternoon, Liam began to feel lightheaded and exhausted. His classmate Elena noticed how pale he looked and helped him walk over to the school health clinic, where Dr. Vance was on duty.

Dr. Vance quickly diagnosed mild dehydration. He opened a foil sachet of Oral Rehydration Salts (ORS), poured the fine white powder into a glass of clean water, and stirred it briskly with a spoon. Elena watched with keen interest: within seconds, the white powder seemed to **vanish completely**. No crystals floated in the water, no sediment settled to the bottom, and the liquid remained as clear and transparent as fresh tap water.

Elena asked, "Dr. Vance, where did the powder go? Did it simply disappear out of existence?"

Dr. Vance smiled. "It did not disappear at all. The powder broke apart into billions of individual, sub-microscopic particles that spread themselves evenly among the water molecules. You do not need to taste it to know it is there—and if we were to gently evaporate all the water away, every single grain of that salt and glucose powder would be recovered right at the bottom of the dish."

He then pointed to the printed label on the sachet. "Notice that the packet specifies an exact ratio: **dissolve in exactly 1 liter of clean water**. If you add too little powder to the water, the solution will be too weak to restore Liam's electrolytes quickly. If you add far too much, water cannot dissolve it all and excess powder remains at the bottom. In chemistry, knowing **how much** of a substance is dissolved is just as important as knowing what is dissolved. Today, we will learn how to measure that exact 'strength'—what chemists call **concentration**."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Solution** | A homogeneous mixture of two or more substances uniform throughout | Salt water, ORS electrolyte drink |
| **Solute** | The component that gets dissolved (typically present in smaller quantity) | ORS powder, table salt, sucrose |
| **Solvent** | The dissolving medium in which the solute dissolves (typically present in larger quantity) | Water |
| **Dissolve** | The process where solute particles separate and disperse uniformly between solvent particles | Sugar dissolving into hot tea |
| **Concentration** | The amount of solute present in a given quantity of solution (or solvent) | A 5% salt solution |
| **Mass percent** | The mass of solute present in 100 g of solution ($\% \text{ m/m}$) | 20 g sugar in 100 g solution = 20% |
| **Volume percent** | The volume of solute present in 100 mL of solution ($\% \text{ v/v}$) | 15 mL ethanol in 100 mL solution = 15% |
| **Mass-by-volume percent** | The mass of solute (in g) present in 100 mL of solution ($\% \text{ m/v}$) | 2 g salt in 100 mL solution = 2% |
| **Unsaturated solution** | A solution in which more solute can still be dissolved at that given temperature | A single spoonful of sugar in a large cup of tea |
| **Saturated solution** | A solution in which no more solute can dissolve at that specific temperature | Adding so much salt that crystals accumulate at the bottom |
| **Solubility** | The maximum mass of solute (in grams) that dissolves in 100 g of solvent at a specific temperature | Table salt: ~36 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C |

## Part A: What Is a Solution?

Take a glass of water, drop in a teaspoon of table sugar, and stir. At first, the solid sugar crystals swirl and settle. After a few seconds of stirring, they disappear completely. The liquid remains perfectly clear and transparent, yet every sip tastes pleasantly sweet. This is a **true solution**.

A solution always consists of two parts:

- **Solute:** The substance that dissolves (here, sugar).
- **Solvent:** The substance that dissolves the solute (here, water).

As a general guideline, the component present in the **larger amount** is regarded as the solvent, while the component in the **smaller amount** is the solute.

Recall from Topic 1 that a homogeneous mixture is completely uniform throughout. That is why a solution is homogeneous: whether you take a sip from the surface, the middle, or the bottom of the glass, it tastes **identically sweet**.

### What Does Dissolving Look Like at the Particle Level?

In Chapter 1, we learned that all matter consists of particles that are in continuous motion. A sugar crystal is an orderly packed cluster of sugar molecules. Water molecules are also constantly moving, colliding with the faces of the solid crystal, and pulling individual sugar molecules away from the cluster. These separated sugar molecules then slip into the empty spaces (**interparticle spaces**) between the water molecules, dispersing evenly throughout the container.

```
   Before stirring:             After thorough stirring:

   water particles: o           o   o  S   o   o
   sugar crystal:   SSS         S  o   o   S   o
                                o   S   o  o  S
        o  o  SSS  o            o  o   S  o   o
        o   SSS  o  o           S   o  o   S  o
        o  o  o  o              o  o   S   o  o
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Dissolution at the particle level: Initially, sugar particles (S) are tightly bound together in a crystal surrounded by water particles (o). After stirring, individual sugar particles disperse uniformly into the spaces between the water particles.

Because individual molecules are smaller than 1 nanometer ($10^{-9}\text{ m}$), our eyes cannot see them. This explains why the solution appears completely clear and transparent.

## Part B: Water Is Not the Only Solvent

Because water dissolves a vast variety of substances, it is universally hailed as the **universal solvent** (or the common solvent). However, not all solutions use water as the solvent, and solutes are not always solids!

| Solution | Solute | Solvent | Important Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Salt water** | Table salt (solid) | Water (liquid) | The most common aqueous solution |
| **Tincture of iodine** (antiseptic) | Iodine (solid) | Alcohol / Ethanol (liquid) | Iodine is only sparingly soluble in water, but dissolves readily in ethanol |
| **Nail polish remover** | Dissolved resins/polymers | Acetone or ethyl acetate (liquid) | Water cannot dissolve nail lacquer |
| **Carbonated soda** | Carbon dioxide (gas) | Water (liquid) | A gas dissolved in a liquid under pressure |
| **Clean air** | Oxygen, argon, $CO_2$ (gases) | Nitrogen (gas) | A gas dissolved in another gas |
| **Brass (alloy)** | Zinc (solid) | Copper (solid) | A solid dissolved in another solid (solid solution) |

Two crucial lessons emerge from this table:
1. **Solutes and solvents can exist in any physical state**—solid, liquid, or gas.
2. **No single solvent dissolves everything.** Sugar dissolves easily in water, but nail polish does not. Iodine dissolves poorly in pure water, but easily in alcohol. Choosing the right solvent is essential in science and industry.

> [!CAUTION]
> Tincture of iodine, acetone, and laboratory chemical solvents should never be tasted or inhaled. At home, limit your dissolving activities to kitchen-safe ingredients like sugar and salt.

## Part C: Properties of a True Solution

A **true solution** possesses five defining characteristics:

1. **Homogeneous mixture:** It has uniform composition and appearance throughout.
2. **Extremely tiny particles:** Solute particles are smaller than $1\text{ nm}$ ($10^{-9}\text{ m}$) in diameter. They cannot be seen with the naked eye or even under a standard light microscope.
3. **Remarkably stable:** If left undisturbed on a shelf for days or weeks, solute particles never settle to the bottom.
4. **Passes through filter paper:** Because the particles are sub-microscopic, they pass freely through the pores of ordinary filter paper along with the solvent. You cannot separate a solute from a true solution by filtration.
5. **Clear and transparent:** A solution does not scatter a beam of light passing through it (it does not show the Tyndall effect, which we will study in Topic 3). Even colored solutions (like blue copper sulfate) remain completely transparent rather than cloudy.

**Try This Safe Activity:** Dissolve a spoonful of table salt in a glass of water. Pour it through a coffee filter or paper towel. The liquid passing through remains salty—salt cannot be filtered out. Now stir a spoonful of garden soil in water and filter it: the muddy soil particles are trapped on the paper, while clear water drips through. This fundamental difference sets true solutions apart from other mixtures.

## Part D: Expressing Concentration of a Solution

Just as a sachet of ORS requires a precise volume of water, chemical solutions require precise recipes. Saying a cup of tea is "a little sweet" or "very sweet" is qualitative and imprecise. In science, we define the exact proportion of solute relative to solution as its **concentration**.

Here are the three standard methods used to calculate concentration:

### 1. Mass Percent (Mass-by-Mass Percentage)

This represents the number of grams of solute contained in 100 g of the final **solution**:

$$\text{Mass percent} = \left(\frac{\text{Mass of solute}}{\text{Mass of solution}}\right) \times 100$$

$$\text{Mass of solution} = \text{Mass of solute} + \text{Mass of solvent}$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The denominator must always be the **total mass of the solution**, never just the mass of the solvent!

**Example 1:** A solution is prepared by dissolving 20 g of sugar into 80 g of water. Calculate the mass percent of sugar.

- $\text{Mass of solution} = 20\text{ g} + 80\text{ g} = \mathbf{100\text{ g}}$
- $\text{Mass percent} = \left(\frac{20}{100}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{20\%}$

**Check:** In 100 g of solution there are 20 g of sugar and 80 g of water: $20 + 80 = 100$ ✅. And $20\%$ means 20 g per 100 g ✅.

**Example 2:** A solution is made by dissolving 5 g of table salt into 95 g of water. What is the mass percent?

- $\text{Mass of solution} = 5 + 95 = \mathbf{100\text{ g}}$
- $\text{Mass percent} = \left(\frac{5}{100}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{5\%}$

**Check:** $5\%$ of $100\text{ g} = 5\text{ g}$, leaving $100 - 5 = 95\text{ g}$ water ✅.

**Example 3 (Working Backward):** How many grams of salt and how many grams of water are needed to prepare 250 g of an 8% (by mass) salt solution?

- $\text{Mass of salt} = \left(\frac{8}{100}\right) \times 250 = 0.08 \times 250 = \mathbf{20\text{ g}}$
- $\text{Mass of water} = 250 - 20 = \mathbf{230\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $\left(\frac{20}{250}\right) \times 100 = 0.08 \times 100 = 8\%$ ✅; $20 + 230 = 250\text{ g}$ ✅.

**Example 4 (Common Trap: Misidentifying the Denominator):** A student dissolves 15 g of sucrose into 135 g of water. What is the mass percent?

- $\text{Total mass of solution} = 15 + 135 = \mathbf{150\text{ g}}$
- $\text{Mass percent} = \left(\frac{15}{150}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{10\%}$

**Check:** $10\% \text{ of } 150 = 0.1 \times 150 = 15\text{ g}$ ✅. *(Note: Dividing 15 by 135 gives $11.1\%$, which is incorrect because 135 g is only the solvent!)*

---

### 2. Volume Percent (Volume-by-Volume Percentage)

When both the solute and solvent are **liquids** (such as ethanol mixed into water), concentration is typically expressed in milliliters of solute per 100 mL of solution:

$$\text{Volume percent} = \left(\frac{\text{Volume of solute}}{\text{Volume of solution}}\right) \times 100$$

**Example 5:** If 40 mL of alcohol is mixed with sufficient water to make a total volume of 200 mL of solution, calculate the volume percent.

- $\text{Volume percent} = \left(\frac{40}{200}\right) \times 100 = 0.2 \times 100 = \mathbf{20\%}$

**Check:** $20\% \text{ of } 200\text{ mL} = 0.2 \times 200 = 40\text{ mL}$ ✅.

**Example 6:** A 500 mL bottle of antiseptic mouthwash contains "15% alcohol by volume." How many milliliters of pure alcohol are present in the bottle?

- $\text{Volume of alcohol} = \left(\frac{15}{100}\right) \times 500 = 0.15 \times 500 = \mathbf{75\text{ mL}}$

**Check:** $\left(\frac{75}{500}\right) \times 100 = 0.15 \times 100 = 15\%$ ✅.

---

### 3. Mass-by-Volume Percent

This unit is widely used in medicine, biology, and pharmacology when a **solid solute** is dissolved into a **liquid solution**, because weighing solids in grams and measuring liquid volumes in milliliters are the easiest laboratory operations:

$$\text{Mass-by-volume percent} = \left(\frac{\text{Mass of solute in grams}}{\text{Volume of solution in mL}}\right) \times 100$$

This represents the number of grams of solute dissolved in 100 mL of solution.

**Example 7 (The ORS Sachet):** A standard sachet containing 20 g of ORS powder is dissolved in water to make exactly 1 liter ($1000\text{ mL}$) of oral rehydration solution. Calculate the mass-by-volume percent.

- $\text{Mass-by-volume percent} = \left(\frac{20}{1000}\right) \times 100 = 0.02 \times 100 = \mathbf{2\%}$

**Check:** $2\%$ means 2 g solute per 100 mL solution. For 1000 mL, $2 \times 10 = 20\text{ g}$ ✅.

**Example 8:** Elena pours a 200 mL glass of this prepared 2% ORS solution for Liam. How much powder is contained in this single glass?

- $\text{Mass of powder} = \left(\frac{2}{100}\right) \times 200 = 0.02 \times 200 = \mathbf{4\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $\left(\frac{4}{200}\right) \times 100 = 0.02 \times 100 = 2\%$ ✅.

**Example 9:** An intravenous saline drip bag contains a 0.9% mass-by-volume solution of sodium chloride. How many grams of salt are present in a 500 mL IV bag?

- $\text{Mass of salt} = \left(\frac{0.9}{100}\right) \times 500 = 0.009 \times 500 = \mathbf{4.5\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $\left(\frac{4.5}{500}\right) \times 100 = 0.009 \times 100 = 0.9\%$ ✅.

---

### Three Methods at a Glance

| Method | Formula | Common Application |
|---|---|---|
| **Mass percent ($\% \text{ m/m}$)** | $\frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100$ | Solid solute dissolved in solid/liquid measured by mass |
| **Volume percent ($\% \text{ v/v}$)** | $\frac{\text{volume of solute}}{\text{volume of solution}} \times 100$ | Liquid solute dissolved in liquid solvent (e.g., alcohol in water) |
| **Mass-by-volume percent ($\% \text{ m/v}$)** | $\frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100$ | Solid solute dissolved in liquid medicine, pharmacy, or IV drips |

Notice the common rule across all three methods: **the denominator is always the total quantity of the solution**, never just the solvent alone.

## Part E: Saturated and Unsaturated Solutions

Imagine you have a glass of water at room temperature. You add a single teaspoon of sugar and stir; it dissolves effortlessly. You add a second spoon; it dissolves too. But if you keep adding spoonful after spoonful, you will eventually reach a point where no matter how vigorously or long you stir, additional sugar crystals simply **refuse to dissolve** and sit at the bottom of the glass.

- As long as more solute can dissolve at that temperature, the solution is **unsaturated**.
- When no more solute can dissolve at that specific temperature, the solution has become **saturated**.

```
   UNSATURATED                      SATURATED
   (room for more solute)           (maximum solute dissolved)

   o  S   o   o  S                  o  S   o  S   o
   S   o  o  S   o                  S   o  S  o   S
   o   o  S   o  o                  o  S   o   S  o
   S  o   o  o  S                   S   o  S   o  S
                                    o  S   o  S   o
   more solute can dissolve         ------------------
                                     SSS  SS  SSS  (undissolved sediment)
                                     no more solute can dissolve
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Unsaturated vs. Saturated Solution: In an unsaturated solution, ample space remains between solvent particles to accommodate more solute particles. In a saturated solution, all available spaces are filled at that temperature; excess solute (SSS) accumulates at the bottom.

Remember: "saturated" does not mean a solution is thick or syrupy; it simply means that **at that specific temperature, the solvent is holding the absolute maximum amount of solute it can hold**.

## Part F: Solubility: How Much Solute Can Dissolve?

The maximum amount of solute (in grams) that can dissolve in **100 g of a given solvent** at a specified temperature to form a saturated solution is called the **solubility** of that solute at that temperature.

Different chemical substances have widely different solubilities in water. Look at approximate solubilities at 20 °C:

| Solute | Approximate Solubility (g per 100 g of water at 20 °C) |
|---|---|
| **Sodium chloride (table salt)** | $\approx 36\text{ g}$ |
| **Potassium nitrate ($KNO_3$)** | $\approx 32\text{ g}$ |
| **Sucrose (table sugar)** | $\approx 200\text{ g}$ |

Notice how dramatic the difference is: at room temperature, 100 g of water can hold nearly 200 g of sugar, but only 36 g of table salt!

### Effect of Temperature

Solubility depends strongly on temperature, and the effect is completely opposite for solids versus gases:

1. **Solid Solutes in Liquid Solvents:**  
   For the vast majority of solid substances, **solubility increases as temperature rises**. When water is heated, its molecules gain kinetic energy, move faster, and create greater space to accommodate solute particles. Consider the solubility of potassium nitrate ($KNO_3$) in 100 g of water:

   | Temperature | Approximate Solubility of $KNO_3$ per 100 g water |
   |---|---|
   | 20 °C | 32 g |
   | 40 °C | 64 g |
   | 60 °C | 110 g |

   This is why hot tea or coffee dissolves sugar much faster and in far greater quantities than iced tea.

2. **Gas Solutes in Liquid Solvents:**  
   For gases, the rule reverses: **the solubility of gases in liquids decreases as temperature rises.**  
   When a liquid is heated, dissolved gas molecules gain kinetic energy and escape into the air above. 
   - A cold soda opened straight from the refrigerator fizzes gently because the $CO_2$ gas remains dissolved. A warm soda opened on a hot day fizzes violently, bubbles over, and quickly goes "flat."
   - In hot summer months, warm river and lake waters hold significantly less dissolved oxygen gas, creating severe respiratory stress for fish and aquatic organisms (thermal pollution).

---

**Example 10 (Converting Solubility to Mass Percent):**  
At 20 °C, the solubility of table salt is 36 g per 100 g of water. Calculate the mass percent of salt in this saturated solution.

- $\text{Mass of solute} = 36\text{ g}$
- $\text{Mass of solvent} = 100\text{ g}$
- $\text{Mass of solution} = 36 + 100 = \mathbf{136\text{ g}}$
- $\text{Mass percent} = \left(\frac{36}{136}\right) \times 100 = 0.2647 \times 100 \approx \mathbf{26.5\%}$

**Check:** $26.5\% \text{ of } 136\text{ g} = 0.265 \times 136 = 36.04 \approx 36\text{ g}$ ✅. *(Note: The mass percent is 26.5%, not 36%, because the solution mass is 136 g, not 100 g!)*

---

**Example 11 (Solubility in Half the Volume of Solvent):**  
At 40 °C, 100 g of water can dissolve 64 g of potassium nitrate. How much potassium nitrate will dissolve in 50 g of water at 40 °C?

- Since 50 g of water is half of 100 g, the maximum mass of solute dissolved is also halved:
  $$\text{Solute dissolved} = \frac{64}{2} = \mathbf{32\text{ g}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{32}{50} = 0.64\text{ g/g water}$, and $\frac{64}{100} = 0.64\text{ g/g water}$ ✅.

---

**Example 12 (Crystallization Upon Cooling):**  
A saturated solution of potassium nitrate is prepared in 100 g of water at 60 °C and then allowed to cool slowly to 20 °C. What mass of solid potassium nitrate will crystallize out of solution?

- Mass of solute dissolved at 60 °C $= 110\text{ g}$
- Maximum mass soluble at 20 °C $= 32\text{ g}$
- Mass of solid that precipitates out $= 110 - 32 = \mathbf{78\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $32 + 78 = 110\text{ g}$ ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Dividing by the mass of the solvent instead of the solution.**  
   In mass percent calculations, the denominator is always $\text{mass of solute} + \text{mass of solvent}$. In Example 4, dividing 15 g by 135 g yields an incorrect 11.1% instead of the correct 10.0%.

2. **Mistake 2: Equating "saturated" with "very concentrated."**  
   A saturated solution of table sugar is indeed very concentrated and syrupy, but a saturated solution of a sparingly soluble substance (like calcium hydroxide) is very dilute! "Saturated" only means the solvent cannot dissolve any more of that specific solute at that specific temperature.

3. **Mistake 3: Assuming heating always increases solubility for all substances.**  
   Heating increases solubility for most solid solutes, but it **decreases** solubility for gases. Warm soda loses its fizz much faster than chilled soda.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing that dissolved solute has disappeared or lost mass.**  
   Dissolved solute molecules simply disperse into the gaps between solvent molecules. The total mass is perfectly conserved: $20\text{ g sugar} + 80\text{ g water} = 100\text{ g solution}$.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming all solutions must be liquid and made with water.**  
   Air is a gaseous solution; brass is a solid solution (alloy); and tincture of iodine uses ethanol, not water, as the solvent.

6. **Mistake 6: Confusing mass percent ($\% \text{ m/m}$) with mass-by-volume percent ($\% \text{ m/v}$).**  
   Dissolving 20 g of sugar into 80 g of water produces a 20% by mass solution ($100\text{ g}$ total mass). Dissolving 20 g into enough water to make 100 mL of solution gives a 20% mass-by-volume solution. Always read the problem carefully to identify whether you are given grams or milliliters.

## Quick Recap

- A **solution** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more substances. The dissolved substance is the **solute**; the dissolving medium is the **solvent**.
- Dissolving involves solute particles separating and dispersing uniformly between solvent particles; no mass is destroyed.
- Water is the most common solvent, but non-aqueous solutions exist (e.g., tincture of iodine in ethanol), as do gaseous solutions (air) and solid solutions (brass).
- **Properties of a true solution:** homogeneous, particles $< 1\text{ nm}$, highly stable (does not settle), passes completely through filter paper, and perfectly clear.
- **Concentration formulas:**
  - $\text{Mass \%} = \frac{\text{mass of solute}}{\text{mass of solution}} \times 100$
  - $\text{Volume \%} = \frac{\text{volume of solute}}{\text{volume of solution}} \times 100$
  - $\text{Mass-by-volume \%} = \frac{\text{mass of solute (g)}}{\text{volume of solution (mL)}} \times 100$
  - In all formulas, the denominator represents the **total solution**.
- An **unsaturated solution** can dissolve more solute at that temperature; a **saturated solution** cannot.
- **Solubility** is the maximum grams of solute that dissolve in 100 g of solvent at a given temperature.
- Heating **increases** solubility for most solid solutes, but **decreases** solubility for gases.
- Liam's ORS drink: 20 g in 1000 mL = 2% mass-by-volume, which provides 4 g of electrolytes in a 200 mL glass.

At room temperature, the higher temperature drives the gas out of solution rapidly, causing the drink to go flat quickly.

    (b) **False** ❌. Solute particles are smaller than 1 nm and pass freely through the pores of filter paper.  
    (c) **False** ❌. Solute particles simply disperse uniformly among solvent molecules; mass is completely conserved.  
    (d) **True** ✅. For most solid solutes, solubility increases as temperature rises.
