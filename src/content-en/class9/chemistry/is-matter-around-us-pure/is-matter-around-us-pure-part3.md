# Topic 3: Suspensions and Colloids: The Middle Ground

In Topic 2, we examined **true solutions**: salt or sugar dissolves so thoroughly that its particles become invisible. These sub-microscopic particles pass freely through filter paper, never settle to the bottom, and leave the liquid crystal-clear.

However, not every mixture behaves so neatly. Stir garden soil into a glass of water, and within minutes heavy mud settles to the bottom while murky water floats above. Leave a glass of whole milk on the counter, and it never settles into clear layers, nor can its components be separated by paper filters. Does a "middle ground" exist between matter that dissolves completely and matter that refuses to dissolve at all? Let us find out on a foggy morning highway.

---

## Headlights Piercing the Morning Fog

Dr. Vance and his student Maya were driving along a coastal highway early one autumn morning to attend a regional science exhibition. A dense blanket of **fog** rolled across the road. When Dr. Vance switched on the vehicle's high-beam headlights, Maya observed a striking sight: the light did not merely illuminate distant signs—it formed a **sharp, brilliant, glowing cone of light** through the misty air, almost like a solid luminous beam.

"Dr. Vance, why is the light beam so vividly visible in the air?" Maya asked. "On a clear night, the air looks completely empty, and you can only see where the light strikes a tree or a wall."

Dr. Vance smiled. "Because on a foggy morning, air is not traveling alone, Maya. Millions of tiny, microscopic **water droplets** are suspended in the air. These droplets are light enough to float without falling, but large enough to scatter the headlight rays in every direction. That scattered light enters your eyes, revealing the exact path of the light beam."

He added with a chuckle, "When we get to the lab, you can recreate this exact phenomenon on your tabletop using just a flashlight, a glass of water, and a few drops of milk."

Maya realized that fog, whole milk, and muddy water are all variations of non-solution mixtures. Today, we will explore this fascinating family.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Suspension** | A heterogeneous mixture containing large, visible particles that remain suspended in a liquid and settle upon standing | Muddy river water, chalk powder in water |
| **Colloid** | A heterogeneous mixture where particles are intermediate in size—too small to settle or be seen, but large enough to scatter light | Whole milk, fog, butter, fruit jelly |
| **Dispersed phase** | The finely divided solute-like particles scattered throughout a colloid | Tiny fat droplets in whole milk |
| **Dispersing medium** | The continuous medium (solvent-like) in which the dispersed phase is distributed | Water in whole milk, air in atmospheric fog |
| **Tyndall effect** | The scattering of a visible light beam by colloidal particles, rendering the light path visible | Headlight beams cutting through fog or mist |
| **Brownian motion** | The continuous, rapid, erratic zigzag motion of colloidal particles driven by collisions with medium molecules | Dust motes dancing under a beam of sunlight |
| **Nanometer (nm)** | A metric unit of length equal to one-billionth of a meter ($1\text{ nm} = 10^{-9}\text{ m}$) | Used to quantify atomic and particle dimensions |

## Part A: Suspensions: When Particles Are Large

Take a glass of clean water, add a spoonful of **crushed chalk powder** (or fine garden soil), and stir briskly. The water turns cloudy and murky. Now place the glass on a table and let it stand undisturbed. Within a few minutes, heavy chalk particles settle to form a distinct **sediment layer** at the bottom, leaving the upper water noticeably clearer.

This mixture is a **suspension**. In a suspension, the solid particles are relatively massive:

- **Visible to the eye:** The particles can be seen floating or swirling without any microscope.
- **Unstable:** When left undisturbed, gravity pulls the heavy particles downward. A suspension settles over time.
- **Separated by filtration:** When poured through ordinary filter paper, the large suspended particles are trapped as residue, while clear liquid passes through as filtrate.
- **Opaque or murky:** Suspensions block or heavily diffuse light passing through them.
- **Heterogeneous:** The composition differs between the top and bottom of the container.

Everyday examples include: muddy floodwater, river silt, calamine lotion, and medical syrups labelled **"Shake well before use"** (because the active medicine settles to the bottom during storage).

> [!NOTE]
> The word *suspension* comes from the idea that particles are temporarily "hung up" or suspended against gravity by stirring, but eventually settle out.

## Part B: Colloids: Particles That Neither Settle Nor Are Seen

Now pour a glass of fresh **whole milk**. Milk is not clear like water; it is white and opaque. Yet if you leave a sealed glass of milk in the refrigerator all day, the white particles never settle to the bottom. If you pour milk through an ordinary coffee filter, the entire liquid passes straight through—no white solid is trapped on the paper. Under a standard magnifying glass, milk appears completely uniform. Why does it look cloudy if nothing settles?

Milk consists of microscopic **droplets of liquid fat and protein clusters** dispersed in water. These particles are in a sweet spot:

- **Invisible to the naked eye:** Individual particles cannot be distinguished without powerful magnification (which makes the mixture appear homogeneous, though it is technically **heterogeneous**).
- **Remarkably stable:** Particles remain suspended indefinitely because gravity is countered by constant particle collisions.
- **Passes through filter paper:** The particles are small enough to pass through standard filter paper pores.
- **Scatters light:** Unlike true solutions, the particles are large enough to collide with and scatter light waves, creating a milky or translucent appearance.

This unique class of mixture is called a **colloid** (or colloidal solution). Colloids represent the **middle ground** of chemistry: cleaner and more stable than suspensions, but with particles distinctly larger than those in true solutions.

Everyday examples include: **fog, smoke, clouds, butter, mayonnaise, gelatin, shaving cream, fountain-pen ink, and blood**.

### Particle Sizes Across the Three Mixtures

To compare these mixtures scientifically, we use the **nanometer ($1\text{ nm} = 10^{-9}\text{ m}$)**. To appreciate how tiny a nanometer is: a single millimeter contains **$1{,}000{,}000\text{ nm}$ ($10^6\text{ nm}$)**!

| Mixture Type | Particle Size Range | Visible to Naked Eye? | Settles on Standing? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **True Solution** | Less than $1\text{ nm}$ ($< 10^{-9}\text{ m}$) | No | No (stable) |
| **Colloid** | Between $1\text{ nm}$ and $1000\text{ nm}$ | No | No (stable) |
| **Suspension** | Greater than $1000\text{ nm}$ ($> 10^{-6}\text{ m}$) | Yes | Yes (unstable) |

*(Note: The $1000\text{ nm}$ boundary is an approximate convention; particles near this threshold show borderline behavior, but the general principle holds: larger particles settle, intermediate particles scatter light and stay suspended, and sub-nanometer particles dissolve completely.)*


## Part C: The Tyndall Effect: A Safe Hands-On Activity

Here is a safe, fascinating experiment you can perform at home or in class to verify whether a mixture is a true solution or a colloid.

> [!CAUTION]
> Use only a small standard **LED flashlight**, never a high-power laser pointer. Never shine any light beam directly into anyone's eyes. Do not drink the liquids used in the experiment.

**Materials Needed:**
- Three clear, identical drinking glasses, each filled with about 250 mL of clean water
- A small handheld LED flashlight
- A few drops of whole milk
- A pinch of fine soil or chalk dust
- A dimly lit room (draw the blinds or curtains)

**Procedure:**
1. **Glass 1:** Plain clean water (or dissolve half a teaspoon of sugar: a **true solution**).
2. **Glass 2:** Add just **3 to 5 drops of milk** to the water and stir gently. The water will remain mostly transparent with a faint opalescence (a dilute **colloid**).
3. **Glass 3:** Add a pinch of soil, stir briskly (a **suspension**).
4. Darken the room. Shine the flashlight horizontally through each glass, and view the glass from the **side** (at a 90° angle to the light path).

**Observations:**
- **Glass 1 (True Solution):** The beam passes through the liquid **invisibly**. The water remains dark from the side view; you only see a bright patch on the opposite wall where the beam exits.
- **Glass 2 (Colloid):** A **bright, glowing, clearly defined beam path** lights up inside the liquid! This is the **Tyndall effect**: colloidal particles scatter light toward your eyes.
- **Glass 3 (Suspension):** The beam path is visible, but the liquid is cloudy and muddy. Within a few minutes, particles settle to the bottom and the scattering changes.

```

  Clear water /         Water + 3-5 drops      Muddy water
  sugar solution        of milk (colloid)      (suspension)

  +---------+           +---------+            +---------+
  |         |           |         |            | * *  *  |
  | torch   |           | torch   |            | torch * |
  | ==>     |           | ==>=====|            | ==>==*==|
  |  (beam  |           |  (beam  |            |  (beam  |
  |  not    |           |  clearly|            | cloudy  |
  |  seen)  |           |  seen)  |            |  seen)  |
  +---------+           +---------+            +---------+

                        (Tyndall effect)       particles settle
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Testing the Tyndall effect with a flashlight: The light beam is invisible in a true solution (Glass 1), shines as a glowing illuminated track in a colloid (Glass 2), and illuminates a muddy suspension where particles settle over time (Glass 3).

### Why Does Light Scatter?

When light waves encounter particles that are comparable in size to the wavelength of light (roughly 400 nm to 700 nm), the light bounces off the particle surfaces in all directions (**scattering**). 

- In a **true solution**, particles are smaller than 1 nm—much smaller than light wavelengths—so light passes straight through without scattering.
- In a **colloid**, particle sizes (1 nm to 1000 nm) are perfectly matched to scatter light, illuminating the beam path.

Everyday examples of the Tyndall effect:
- Car headlights cutting through **fog or mist** on an early morning drive.
- Sunlight streaming through a skylight or a gap in the curtains of a dusty room, creating a **visible ray of dancing dust motes**.
- Sunlight filtering through the **dense canopy of a misty forest**.
- The projector beam visible in a **movie theater** due to tiny airborne smoke and dust particles.

## Part D: Brownian Motion: Why Do Colloid Particles Never Settle?

If colloidal particles are denser than water, why does gravity not pull them down over time?

The answer lies in the dynamic world of molecules. Under a high-power microscope, colloidal particles do not sit still. Instead, they dart about continuously in a chaotic, rapid, **erratic zigzag path**.

This phenomenon was discovered by botanist Robert Brown and is named **Brownian motion**. It occurs because surrounding solvent molecules (which are in perpetual, frantic thermal motion) constantly slam into the colloidal particle from all directions. At any given instant, more molecules hit one side than the other, shoving the particle in a random direction. These endless collisions act like a natural microscopic blender, keeping the particles permanently suspended against the downward pull of gravity!

## Part E: Common Types of Colloids

In a colloid, both the **dispersed phase** (solute-like particles) and the **dispersing medium** (solvent-like environment) can be a solid, liquid, or gas. Depending on these states, colloids are grouped into several classes:

| Dispersed Phase | Dispersing Medium | Colloid Type | Everyday Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Liquid | Gas | **Aerosol** | Fog, clouds, aerosol sprays |
| Solid | Gas | **Aerosol** | Smoke, automobile exhaust |
| Gas | Liquid | **Foam** | Shaving cream, whipped cream |
| Liquid | Liquid | **Emulsion** | Whole milk, mayonnaise, face cream |
| Solid | Liquid | **Sol** | Fountain-pen ink, paint, milk of magnesia |
| Gas | Solid | **Solid Foam** | Sponge, pumice stone, foam rubber |
| Liquid | Solid | **Gel** | Fruit jelly, butter, cheese, hair gel |
| Solid | Solid | **Solid Sol** | Ruby glass, colored gemstones |

*(Note: A gas dispersed in another gas is always a true homogeneous solution—like air—never a colloid, because gas molecules mix completely at the molecular level.)*

## Part F: Comparing All Three Mixtures Side-by-Side

| Property | True Solution | Colloid | Suspension |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Particle Size** | $< 1\text{ nm}$ | $1\text{ nm} \text{ to } 1000\text{ nm}$ | $> 1000\text{ nm}$ |
| **Visibility** | Invisible to naked eye and microscope | Invisible to eye; seen with electron microscope | Visible to naked eye or standard magnifier |
| **Classification** | Homogeneous | Heterogeneous (appears homogeneous) | Heterogeneous |
| **Stability** | Highly stable (never settles) | Stable (never settles under gravity) | Unstable (settles upon standing) |
| **Filtration** | Passes through filter paper | Passes through filter paper | Trapped by ordinary filter paper |
| **Tyndall Effect** | Does not scatter light (beam invisible) | Scatters light (beam visible) | Scatters light (until particles settle) |
| **Appearance** | Clear and transparent | Translucent, cloudy, or opalescent | Murky and opaque |
| **Everyday Examples** | Salt water, sugar solution, vinegar | Milk, fog, mayonnaise, ink, jelly | Muddy water, chalk dust in water |

> **The Quick Three-Question Identification Key:**  
> 1. *Do particles settle when left to stand?* If **yes** $\rightarrow$ **Suspension**.  
> 2. *Does a flashlight beam shine as a glowing path through the liquid without settling?* If **yes** $\rightarrow$ **Colloid**.  
> 3. *Is the liquid completely clear, never settles, and passes light invisibly?* If **yes** $\rightarrow$ **True Solution**.

## Part G: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Unit Conversion to Nanometers):**  
A fine grain of river silt has a diameter of 0.2 mm. Convert this dimension into nanometers. Based on size, which class of mixture would it form in water?

- $1\text{ mm} = 10^6\text{ nm}$
- $\text{Diameter} = 0.2 \times 10^6\text{ nm} = \mathbf{200{,}000\text{ nm}}$ ($2 \times 10^5\text{ nm}$)

**Check:** $2 \times 10^5\text{ nm} \times 10^{-9}\text{ m/nm} = 2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m} = 0.2\text{ mm}$ ✅. Because $200{,}000\text{ nm} \gg 1000\text{ nm}$, this particle will settle under gravity and form a **suspension**.

---

**Example 2 (Classifying by Particle Size):**  
Classify the mixture formed by particles of each diameter into true solution, colloid, or suspension: (a) 0.5 nm, (b) 200 nm, (c) 20,000 nm, (d) 1 mm.

- (a) **0.5 nm:** Below 1 nm $\rightarrow$ **True solution**.
- (b) **200 nm:** Lies between 1 nm and 1000 nm $\rightarrow$ **Colloid**.
- (c) **20,000 nm:** Far exceeds 1000 nm $\rightarrow$ **Suspension**.
- (d) **1 mm** ($10^6\text{ nm}$): Far exceeds 1000 nm $\rightarrow$ **Suspension**.

**Check:** Verified against the standard ranges: $< 1\text{ nm}$, $1\text{--}1000\text{ nm}$, $> 1000\text{ nm}$ ✅.

---

**Example 3 (Particle Size Ratio):**  
A typical colloidal protein particle has a diameter of 100 nm, while a dissolved sucrose molecule has a diameter of 0.5 nm. How many times larger is the colloidal particle? How does it compare to a 20,000 nm suspension silt grain?

- Ratio to solution particle $= \frac{100\text{ nm}}{0.5\text{ nm}} = \mathbf{200\text{ times larger}}$.
- Ratio of suspension particle to colloid $= \frac{20{,}000\text{ nm}}{100\text{ nm}} = \mathbf{200\text{ times larger}}$.

**Check:** $0.5 \times 200 = 100\text{ nm}$ ✅; $100 \times 200 = 20{,}000\text{ nm}$ ✅. Colloidal particles sit squarely in the middle ground!

---

**Example 4 (Concentration of Milk in the Tyndall Experiment):**  
Maya added 5 drops of milk to 250 mL of water. Assuming each drop is approximately 0.05 mL, calculate the approximate volume-by-volume percentage of milk in the glass.

- Total volume of milk $= 5 \times 0.05\text{ mL} = \mathbf{0.25\text{ mL}}$
- $\text{Volume percent} = \left(\frac{0.25}{250}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{0.1\% \text{ v/v}}$

**Check:** $0.1\% \text{ of } 250\text{ mL} = 0.001 \times 250 = 0.25\text{ mL}$ ✅. Notice how a mere **0.1%** concentration of colloidal droplets is sufficient to produce a brilliant Tyndall light beam!

---

**Example 5 (Mystery Mixture Identification):**  
A chemist is given a bottle of an unknown white, cloudy liquid. When tested with a flashlight, a sharp luminous beam is visible through the liquid. The bottle sits undisturbed on a shelf for three days, and no sediment settles. When poured through standard filter paper, no residue is trapped. Identify the mixture type.

- The light beam is visible $\rightarrow$ Demonstrates Tyndall effect (rules out true solution).
- Does not settle over 3 days $\rightarrow$ Stable (rules out suspension).
- Passes through filter paper $\rightarrow$ Particle size is under $1000\text{ nm}$.
- **Conclusion:** The liquid is a **colloid**.

---

**Example 6 (Colloidal Anatomy of Fog):**  
Identify the dispersed phase and the dispersing medium in atmospheric fog, and name the resulting colloid class.

- Dispersed phase: Liquid water droplets.
- Dispersing medium: Air (gas).
- Colloid class: **Aerosol** (liquid in gas).

**Check:** Matches Part E summary table for aerosol ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that because milk looks smooth and uniform, it must be a true solution.**  
   Milk appears uniform to the naked eye, but under high magnification, tiny fat globules are clearly visible. Furthermore, it scatters light strongly. Scientifically, milk is a heterogeneous colloid (specifically an emulsion).

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking that colloidal particles can be separated with regular filter paper.**  
   Colloidal particles range from 1 nm to 1000 nm, easily slipping through standard filter paper pores. Specialized semi-permeable membranes or ultra-centrifugation are required to separate them.

3. **Mistake 3: Assuming the Tyndall effect occurs only in colloids.**  
   Suspensions also scatter light while particles remain suspended, but suspensions settle out over time and are visibly murky. True solutions never exhibit the Tyndall effect.

4. **Mistake 4: Equating suspensions with colloids.**  
   Suspension particles are massive ($> 1000\text{ nm}$) and settle under gravity; colloidal particles ($1\text{--}1000\text{ nm}$) are kept afloat indefinitely by Brownian motion.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating fog as pure gas.**  
   Fog is not simply cool air; it is liquid water droplets suspended in air (an aerosol). That is why car headlights light up in fog.

## Quick Recap

- **Suspensions:** Large particles ($> 1000\text{ nm}$), visible to the naked eye, settle down upon standing, trapped by filter paper, murky and heterogeneous. Examples: muddy water, chalk in water.
- **Colloids:** Intermediate particles ($1\text{ nm} \text{ to } 1000\text{ nm}$), invisible to the naked eye, do not settle upon standing, pass through filter paper, scatter light. Examples: milk, fog, butter, ink, jelly.
- **True Solutions:** Smallest particles ($< 1\text{ nm}$), completely clear, highly stable, pass through filter paper, do not scatter light.
- **Tyndall Effect:** The scattering of light by colloidal or suspended particles, illuminating the path of the beam.
- **Brownian Motion:** Rapid, chaotic, zigzag motion of colloidal particles caused by continuous collisions with solvent molecules, preventing them from settling.
- **Colloid Types:** Aerosol (fog, smoke), emulsion (milk, face cream), foam (shaving cream), sol (ink, paint), gel (fruit jelly).
- Metric scale: $1\text{ mm} = 10^6\text{ nm}$; $1\text{ nm} = 10^{-9}\text{ m}$.
