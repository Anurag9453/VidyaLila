# Topic 4: Separating Mixtures: Part 1 — Fundamental Methods

In the preceding topics, we discovered that the majority of materials around us are **mixtures**: true solutions, suspensions, and colloids. At the end of Topic 3, we noticed a critical clue: particles in a suspension are large enough that they **settle under gravity** and **are trapped by filter paper**, whereas solute particles in a true solution pass straight through. 

This reveals that the components of a mixture always differ in some physical characteristic. Now comes the practical question: when we need to isolate the pure components of a mixture, how can we exploit these differences? Today, we will master the essential, fundamental methods of physical separation.

---

## Cleaning Harvested Wheat on the Farm

During the autumn break, Elena visited her uncle, Mr. Davis, at his family farm. The wheat harvest had just concluded. In the barn, Elena looked into a large burlap sack containing freshly harvested wheat mingled with light papery husks (chaff), small clumps of dry soil, and tiny pebbles.

"Uncle Davis," Elena asked, "how will you ever turn this into clean flour? Will you have to pick out every pebble and piece of husk by hand?"

Mr. Davis chuckled warmly. He called over his farmhand, who poured a scoop of grain into a wide, shallow basket, held it at shoulder height, and tipped it gently so the grain fell in a steady stream. A steady autumn breeze was blowing across the barnyard. 

Elena watched in amazement: the lightweight **chaff drifted away on the breeze**, forming a separate pile a few meters away. The dense **wheat grains fell straight down** into a clean mound. The heaviest pebbles and dirt clumps dropped almost vertically into an adjacent bin.

"Look at that, Elena!" Mr. Davis pointed out. "We did not touch a single pebble or grain of chaff individually, yet all three components sorted themselves cleanly. The secret lies in a single physical property: **relative weight (density)**. That is the golden rule of separation in chemistry: first find **what physical property differs** between the substances, and then choose a technique that exploits that difference."

Elena wondered, "Does that mean every mixture requires a different strategy?" 

"Precisely," replied Mr. Davis. "Let us explore the full toolkit of separation methods, from simple kitchen techniques to clever laboratory apparatus."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Separation** | Isolating the individual components of a mixture without altering their chemical identity | Removing stones from lentils or grain |
| **Handpicking** | Manually separating visible, distinct solids using one's fingers or forceps | Removing small pebbles from dried beans |
| **Winnowing** | Using wind or air currents to separate lighter particles from heavier ones | Separating light husk (chaff) from wheat grains |
| **Sieving** | Using a perforated mesh screen to separate particles of different sizes | Sifting coarse bran out of fine wheat flour |
| **Sedimentation** | The settling of heavier insoluble solid particles to the bottom of a liquid | Mud settling to the bottom of standing water |
| **Decantation** | Carefully pouring off the upper clear liquid without disturbing the settled sediment | Pouring clear water away from settled soil |
| **Filtration** | Passing a mixture through a porous barrier (like filter paper) to separate solid residue from liquid filtrate | Brewing coffee through a paper filter |
| **Filtrate** | The clear liquid that passes through the filter | Clear water collected beneath a coffee filter |
| **Residue** | The solid matter retained on the filter | Mud or coffee grounds left behind on filter paper |
| **Centrifugation** | Rapidly spinning a mixture so that denser components are driven outwards and downwards | Separating cream from milk in a dairy |
| **Separating funnel** | A pear-shaped glassware apparatus with a stopcock used to separate immiscible liquids | Separating cooking oil from water |
| **Sublimation** | The process where a solid converts directly into gas upon heating, bypassing the liquid state | Separating ammonium chloride or camphor from salt |

## Part A: First Principle of Separation: Exploiting Differences in Properties

Before choosing how to separate any mixture, always ask: **which physical property is different among the components?**

Possible differences include:
- **Particle size:** large vs. tiny
- **Mass / density:** heavy vs. lightweight
- **Magnetic susceptibility:** attracted by a magnet vs. non-magnetic
- **Solubility:** dissolves in water vs. insoluble
- **Boiling point / volatility:** turns into vapor easily vs. non-volatile
- **Sublimation:** vaporizes directly from solid vs. non-sublimable

For example, in a bag of potato chips, you might find whole chips mixed with tiny crushed crumbs. The difference is **particle size**. By gently shaking the contents over a coarse wire basket, the small crumbs drop through while whole chips stay behind: separation by size.

Let us now examine each method systematically.

## Part B: Manual and Wind Separation: Handpicking and Winnowing

### 1. Handpicking

When the unwanted impurity is present in **small quantities**, differs noticeably in **color, shape, or size**, and the total sample is relatively small, **handpicking** is the simplest method. 

Before cooking dried lentils, beans, or rice, cooks spread the grains on a wide plate and manually pick out occasional dark pebbles, damaged seeds, or foreign debris.

However, if you have a 50 kg commercial sack of grain, handpicking would take days. In such cases, mechanized or wind-assisted methods are necessary.

### 2. Winnowing

As Elena observed on Uncle Davis's farm, **winnowing** exploits the **difference in weight (density)** between components when air currents are applied:

- The mixture of grain and husk is dropped from a height in a breeze (or in front of a mechanical fan).
- The lighter **husk and chaff** are carried away by the wind to form a distant pile.
- The heavier **wheat grains** fall almost straight down to form a clean heap.

**Try This Safe Activity at Home:** Mix a cup of uncooked rice with a handful of small torn scraps of dry paper. Stand over a wide tray outdoors (or near an open window with a gentle breeze) and let the mixture fall slowly from a height of half a meter. The paper scraps drift away, while the heavier rice grains land directly into the tray.

## Part C: Separation by Size: Sieving

**Sieving** separates a mixture of solid particles of **different sizes** using a mesh screen or sieve. Particles smaller than the mesh openings fall through, while larger particles are retained on top.

- **In the kitchen:** Sifting flour through a fine wire sieve removes coarse bran husks or flour clumps, yielding smooth, fine flour for baking.
- **At construction sites:** Builders throw coarse sand against a large, tilted wire screen to separate fine building sand from large gravel and pebbles.
- **Tea strainer:** While brewing tea, a metal mesh traps tea leaves while the brewed liquid flows into the cup (a simple form of filtration/sieving).

> [!NOTE]
> The size of the sieve's openings determines what passes through. Coarse sieves are used for gravel, whereas ultrafine sieves are used for powdered chemicals and flour.

---

## Part D: Magnetic Separation

If one constituent of a mixture is **magnetic** (such as iron, nickel, or cobalt) while the others are non-magnetic, a magnet provides an instant, effortless separation.

**Try This Safe Activity:** Mix a spoonful of dry sand with several steel paperclips or small iron pins. Wrap a small bar magnet in plastic wrap (to keep it clean) and sweep it across the surface of the sand. The iron clips instantly leap up and cling to the magnet, leaving pure sand behind.

**Industrial Applications:**
- In scrapyards, colossal **electromagnets mounted on cranes** hoist tons of scrap iron and steel away from piles of aluminum cans, glass, plastic, and rubber.
- In mining, magnetic drums extract iron ore from crushed, non-magnetic mineral rock.

## Part E: Gravity and Filtration: Sedimentation, Decantation, and Filtration

When an insoluble solid is mixed with a liquid (a suspension, as studied in Topic 3), gravity and porous barriers provide two complementary separation techniques.

### 3. Sedimentation and Decantation

**Sedimentation:** When an insoluble suspension (such as muddy water) is left undisturbed, gravity slowly pulls the denser solid particles down to the bottom of the container, forming a layer of **sediment**.

**Decantation:** Once sedimentation is complete, the clear liquid above the sediment is carefully poured into another container without disturbing the settled mud at the bottom.

In traditional rural communities, muddy river water is left standing in clay pots overnight; by morning, the silt has settled, and the clearer water is decanted off. When cooking rice or lentils, washing them with water followed by gently tilting the pot to pour off the rinse water is an everyday example of decantation.

However, decantation is never 100% efficient: light particles remain suspended, and pouring too fast stirs up the sediment. To obtain truly clean liquid, we turn to filtration.

### 4. Filtration

**Filtration** passes the mixture through a porous medium—most commonly **laboratory filter paper** folded into a cone inside a funnel. 

- The liquid and any dissolved particles pass freely through the microscopic pores of the paper and collect below as the **filtrate**.
- The insoluble solid particles, being larger than the pores, are trapped on the paper as the **residue**.

```
          Suspension mixture
              (muddy water)
             \           /
              \  ~~~~~  /      <- Glass funnel
               \ ~~~~~ /
          ──────\─────/──────
                 \ v /         <- Filter paper cone
                  \ /          <- Residue (mud trapped on paper)
                   V
                   │
                   │
             ┌─────┴─────┐
             │           │
             │  . . . .  │     <- Filtrate (clear liquid)
             └───────────┘
              Beaker / Flask
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Laboratory filtration assembly: Muddy water is poured through a folded filter paper cone supported inside a funnel. Insoluble mud particles are trapped as residue, while clear water drips into the beaker as filtrate.

> [!WARNING]
> While filtrate from muddy water looks clear, it is **not safe for drinking** without boiling or chemical treatment, because dissolved salts and microscopic bacteria pass freely through ordinary paper pores!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Filtration cannot separate a dissolved solute from a true solution!** Solute particles in salt water or sugar water are smaller than $1\text{ nm}$, slipping effortlessly through filter paper pores along with water molecules. To separate dissolved salt, we must use evaporation.

## Part F: High-Speed Spinning: Centrifugation

When particles in a liquid are so small that they do not settle under gravity (as in colloids, like milk) or settle too slowly, **centrifugation** provides the solution.

Centrifugation involves placing the mixture in sealed tubes within a machine called a **centrifuge** and spinning them at thousands of revolutions per minute:

- Rapid rotation generates a powerful outward centrifugal force (many times stronger than gravity).
- The **denser particles are forced outward and downward** to the bottom of the centrifuge tube.
- The **less dense liquid remains at the top** as a separate supernatant layer.

```
       Before spinning               After rapid spinning

        ┌───────────┐                 ┌───────────┐
        │ o . o .   │                 │ Lighter   │
        │ . o  . o  │                 │ liquid    │
        │ o . o .   │     ───▶        │ (top)     │
        │ . o  . o  │                 │───────────│
        │ (mixed)   │                 │ o o o o   │
        │           │                 │ Dense     │
        └───────────┘                 │ sediment  │
         Centrifuge tube              └───────────┘
                                      Particles packed at bottom
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Principle of centrifugation: High-speed rotation drives dense particles to the bottom of the tube, separating them cleanly from the lighter liquid above.

**Practical Applications:**
- **In Dairies:** Separating lighter milk fat (cream) from heavier skimmed milk.
- **In Washing Machines:** The spin cycle whirls wet laundry at high speeds, forcing water droplets through the perforated drum walls to dry clothes.
- **In Medical Pathology Labs:** Centrifuging whole blood samples drives heavy red and white blood cells to the bottom of the tube, leaving clear, straw-colored blood plasma at the top for diagnostic testing.

## Part G: Immiscible Liquids: The Separating Funnel

When two liquids **do not mix (immiscible)**—such as vegetable oil and water, or kerosene and water—they naturally separate into two distinct layers based on density: the lighter liquid floats on top, and the denser liquid sinks to the bottom.

A **separating funnel** is a pear-shaped glass vessel fitted with a ground-glass stopper at the top and a precise **stopcock (valve)** at the bottom stem:

1. Pour the oil-water mixture into the funnel, insert the stopper, and let it stand undisturbed on a ring stand.
2. Two sharp, distinct layers form: water (denser) sits at the bottom, and oil (less dense) floats above.
3. Place a clean beaker beneath the stem and open the stopcock slowly.
4. The lower water layer drains into the beaker.
5. As soon as the boundary interface between the two liquids reaches the stopcock, close the tap firmly!
6. Swap in a second beaker and open the tap to collect the upper oil layer.

```
           Glass stopper
             ┌──┐
          ┌──┘  └──┐
         ╱          ╲
        │ Light oil  │   <- Upper layer (less dense)
        │ ~~~~~~~~~~ │   <- Boundary interface
        │   Water    │   <- Lower layer (denser)
        │  (denser)  │
         ╲          ╱
          ╲        ╱
           ╲      ╱
            ╲    ╱
             │▒▒│        <- Precision stopcock
             │  │
              ▼
           Receiving beaker
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Separating funnel assembly: The denser liquid (water) drains first through the stopcock. The stopcock is closed the instant the interface reaches the opening, cleanly separating the immiscible liquids.

> [!CAUTION]
> A separating funnel works **only for immiscible liquids**. It cannot separate liquids that dissolve into each other (miscible liquids, such as ethanol and water). For miscible liquids, distillation is required (Topic 5).

## Part H: Recovering Dissolved Solute: Evaporation

How can we recover salt from a clear salt-water solution when filtration fails?

We rely on **evaporation**: the solvent (water) is volatile and evaporates away into vapor, whereas the solid solute (salt) has an extraordinarily high boiling point ($1465\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and remains behind.

**Safe Tabletop Activity:** Pour a shallow layer of salt water into an open glass saucer. Leave it on a warm windowsill for a day or two. As the water molecules evaporate into the air, a white crust of crystalline sodium chloride forms on the plate.

In warm coastal regions, seawater is channelled into vast shallow pools called **salt evaporation pans**. The sun and wind evaporate the water over several weeks, leaving behind massive mounds of raw sea salt.

**Limitations of Evaporation:**
- The **solvent is lost to the atmosphere** as vapor. If you need to collect pure drinking water from seawater, simple evaporation is useless.
- Some heat-sensitive substances (like sugar) may scorch or decompose if heated directly to dryness.
- The recovered crystals are often impure or poorly formed.

## Part I: Separating Sublimable Solids: Sublimation

Some substances undergo **sublimation**: they transition directly from a solid to a gas upon heating without ever melting into a liquid, and deposit back into a solid when cooled. Examples include **ammonium chloride ($NH_4Cl$), camphor, iodine, and naphthalene**.

If a mixture contains one sublimable solid and one non-sublimable solid (such as $NH_4Cl$ mixed with common salt or sand), sublimation provides a clean separation.

> [!CAUTION]
> This is a teacher-supervised laboratory demonstration only. Do not heat ammonium chloride at home, as heating chemical vapors requires proper laboratory ventilation (fume hood).

**Laboratory Procedure:**
1. Place the mixture of ammonium chloride and common salt in a porcelain evaporating dish.
2. Invert a clean glass funnel over the dish, plugging the upper stem with a small wad of cotton wool to prevent vapors from escaping.
3. Heat the dish gently over a Bunsen burner.
4. The ammonium chloride sublimes into white vapors, rises, and crystallizes onto the cool inner glass walls of the inverted funnel as a pure solid deposit (**sublimate**).
5. Non-sublimable common salt remains untouched in the porcelain dish.

## Part K: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Grain Cleaning on the Farm):**  
Uncle Davis's farm sack contains 40 kg of harvested material. After winnowing and sieving, 2 kg of stones and 1 kg of chaff are removed. Calculate the mass of clean wheat recovered and the percentage of impurities in the raw harvest.

- Total impurities $= 2\text{ kg} + 1\text{ kg} = \mathbf{3\text{ kg}}$
- Clean wheat $= 40\text{ kg} - 3\text{ kg} = \mathbf{37\text{ kg}}$
- Percentage of impurities $= \left(\frac{3}{40}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{7.5\%}$

**Check:** $37 + 3 = 40\text{ kg}$ ✅; $\frac{3}{40} = 0.075 = 7.5\%$ ✅.

---

**Example 2 (Evaporation of Salt Solution):**  
A student has 500 g of a 20% by mass sodium chloride solution. If the mixture is heated until all liquid evaporates, what mass of dry salt will be recovered, and what mass of water vapor was lost to the atmosphere?

- Mass of salt $= 20\% \text{ of } 500\text{ g} = \left(\frac{20}{100}\right) \times 500 = \mathbf{100\text{ g}}$
- Mass of water lost $= 500\text{ g} - 100\text{ g} = \mathbf{400\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $100 + 400 = 500\text{ g}$ ✅; $\frac{100}{500} \times 100 = 20\%$ ✅ (Topic 2 concentration formula).

---

**Example 3 (Centrifugation in a Dairy):**  
A local dairy processes 5 liters of whole milk containing approximately 6% cream by volume. Using a mechanical centrifugal cream separator, how many liters (and milliliters) of cream can be extracted?

- Volume of cream $= 6\% \text{ of } 5\text{ L} = \left(\frac{6}{100}\right) \times 5 = \mathbf{0.3\text{ L}} = \mathbf{300\text{ mL}}$

**Check:** $\frac{0.3\text{ L}}{5\text{ L}} = 0.06 = 6\%$ ✅.

---

**Example 4 (Separating Funnel Operation):**  
A 300 mL mixture of kerosene oil and water contains 20% kerosene by volume. When poured into a separating funnel: (a) Which liquid is collected first? (b) Calculate the volume of each liquid recovered.

- Volume of kerosene $= 20\% \text{ of } 300\text{ mL} = \mathbf{60\text{ mL}}$ (floats on top)
- Volume of water $= 300\text{ mL} - 60\text{ mL} = \mathbf{240\text{ mL}}$ (sinks to bottom)
- (a) **Water drains first** through the bottom stopcock because it is denser.
- (b) **240 mL of water** is collected first, followed by **60 mL of kerosene**.

**Check:** $60 + 240 = 300\text{ mL}$ ✅.

---

**Example 5 (Sublimation Recovery):**  
A 50 g dry mixture of ammonium chloride and common salt is heated under an inverted glass funnel. After sublimation is complete, 18 g of pure common salt remains in the dish. Calculate the mass and percentage of ammonium chloride originally present.

- Mass of ammonium chloride $= 50\text{ g} - 18\text{ g} = \mathbf{32\text{ g}}$
- Percentage of ammonium chloride $= \left(\frac{32}{50}\right) \times 100 = \mathbf{64\%}$

**Check:** $32 + 18 = 50\text{ g}$ ✅; $\frac{32}{50} = 0.64 = 64\%$ ✅.

---

**Example 6 (Multi-Step Separation Strategy):**  
Design a step-by-step procedure to completely separate a dry mixture containing **sand, iron nails, and table salt**. State the property difference utilized in each step.

1. **Step 1: Magnetic Separation:** Pass a magnet over the dry mixture. The iron nails are attracted to the magnet, leaving sand and salt behind. (*Property: magnetism of iron vs. non-magnetic sand/salt.*)
2. **Step 2: Dissolution in Water:** Add water to the remaining mixture and stir thoroughly. The salt dissolves completely into water, while the sand remains insoluble. (*Property: water solubility of salt vs. insolubility of sand.*)
3. **Step 3: Filtration:** Pour the mixture through a filter paper cone. Insoluble sand is trapped as **residue** on the filter, while the clear salt solution passes through as **filtrate**. Rinse and dry the sand. (*Property: particle size of sand vs. filter pores.*)
4. **Step 4: Evaporation:** Heat the clear filtrate in an evaporating dish until all water evaporates away into vapor, leaving dry table salt crystals in the dish. (*Property: volatility of water vs. high boiling point of salt.*)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing filtration can remove dissolved salt from water.**  
   Dissolved salt exists as individual ions surrounded by water molecules ($< 1\text{ nm}$). They pass straight through filter paper pores. Filtration only removes insoluble particles. Evaporation must be used for dissolved salt.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming winnowing works for any two solids.**  
   Winnowing requires a significant difference in mass/density in the presence of air currents. It cannot separate fine sand from table salt, as both grains have similar weights.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing sedimentation with decantation.**  
   Sedimentation is the settling of heavy particles to the bottom. Decantation is the physical pouring off of the clear liquid above the sediment. They are separate sequential steps.

4. **Mistake 4: Trying to use a separating funnel for miscible liquids.**  
   A separating funnel only works for immiscible liquids that form separate layers (oil and water). It cannot separate liquids that dissolve into each other, like alcohol and water.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming magnets attract all metals.**  
   Magnets attract ferromagnetic metals like iron, nickel, and cobalt. Common metals like aluminum, copper, brass, and gold are not attracted by standard magnets.

6. **Mistake 6: Believing evaporation recovers both water and salt.**  
   Simple evaporation releases water vapor into the open air; only salt is collected. If you need to recover the water as well, distillation is required (Topic 5).

## Quick Recap

- Always identify the **differing physical property** (size, density, magnetism, solubility, volatility, sublimation) before selecting a separation technique.
- **Handpicking:** Visual differences for small batches (stones from rice).
- **Winnowing:** Density difference using wind currents (chaff from grain).
- **Sieving:** Size difference using porous wire mesh (bran from flour).
- **Magnetic separation:** Exploits ferromagnetism (iron nails from sand).
- **Sedimentation & Decantation:** Gravity settling of insoluble solids followed by careful pouring of clear supernatant liquid.
- **Filtration:** Traps insoluble residue on filter paper while allowing clear filtrate to pass through.
- **Centrifugation:** High-speed spinning drives denser components outward/downward (cream from milk, blood plasma separation).
- **Separating Funnel:** Separates immiscible liquids by draining the denser bottom layer first (oil and water).
- **Evaporation:** Recovers non-volatile dissolved solute while boiling off volatile solvent to the atmosphere (salt from seawater).
- **Sublimation:** Separates solids that vaporize directly without melting ($NH_4Cl$, camphor) from non-sublimable solids.
