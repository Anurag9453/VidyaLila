# Topic 5: Separating Mixtures: Part 2 — Distillation, Crystallization, and Chromatography

In Topic 4, we learned how to separate mixtures by exploiting straightforward differences in **particle size, density, magnetic properties, and simple solubility**. 

However, some mixtures present tougher challenges. **Salt dissolved in water** slips right through filter paper because its solute particles are atomic in scale. A magnet has no effect on it, and simple evaporation leaves behind solid salt while **permanently losing all water to the open sky**. Moreover, what if two liquids dissolve completely into each other, or what if a single drop of ink is secretly a blend of three different dyes?

Today, we explore three powerful, refined separation techniques that solve these exact challenges: **crystallization**, **distillation (simple and fractional)**, and **chromatography**.

## Droplets on the Pot Lid and the Secret of Black Ink

On a Sunday afternoon, Maya was in the kitchen helping her mentor Dr. Vance prepare a savory vegetable broth. A heavy pot of soup with seasoned salt and herbs was simmering on the stove. When Dr. Vance lifted the stainless-steel lid, Maya noticed a cluster of **clear, glistening water droplets** clinging to the inner underside of the lid. A few drops trickled down and fell onto a clean side plate.

"Dr. Vance, where did that clean water come from?" Maya asked. "Inside the pot, the broth is dark brown and salty, yet these droplets look as clear as spring water."

"That is pure physics and chemistry at work," Dr. Vance explained. "The boiling water turned into **steam (water vapor)**, rose upward, struck the cooler steel lid, and **condensed back into liquid water**. Not a single grain of dissolved salt or heavy herb particle could vaporize with the steam. That is the exact principle of **distillation**."

Later that evening, Maya was drawing diagrams for her chemistry project with a black felt-tip pen when a stray drop of clean water accidentally splashed onto her sketch paper. Within seconds, the water soaked outward through the paper fibers, and the solid black ink dot began to spread and unfurl into vibrant concentric rings of **bright turquoise blue, vivid magenta, and pale yellow**!

Maya stared in disbelief. "I thought this pen was made of pure black ink! Where did all those vibrant colors come from?"

Dr. Vance smiled. "You have just witnessed two of chemistry's finest separation marvels in a single day: first, purifying a liquid by boiling and condensation (**distillation**), and second, unmasking the hidden pigments blended inside an ink drop (**chromatography**). Let us explore how both techniques work."

> [!CAUTION]
> Any laboratory procedures involving **burners, boiling liquids, or flammable organic solvents** must be conducted under direct adult or teacher supervision. Never taste any distilled liquid or chemical extract, regardless of how clear it appears.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Crystal** | A pure solid substance possessing a regular, repeating geometric shape with sharp faces and edges | Table sugar crystals, alum crystals |
| **Crystallization** | Purifying a solid by dissolving it into hot solvent to saturation, then cooling slowly to form pure crystals | Growing large copper sulfate crystals |
| **Mother liquor** | The residual liquid remaining after crystals have precipitated and been filtered out | The syrup left behind after cane sugar crystallizes |
| **Distillation** | A separation method where a liquid is vaporized by boiling and its vapors are condensed back into liquid | Purifying seawater into fresh water |
| **Distillate** | The condensed pure liquid collected in the receiving flask during distillation | Pure distilled water collected in the flask |
| **Condenser** | A specialized double-walled glass tube used to cool vapors back into liquid using cold circulating water | A Liebig condenser |
| **Fractional distillation** | Separating miscible liquids with close boiling points using a fractionating column | Separating ethanol and water; refining crude oil |
| **Fraction** | A distinct portion or component collected over a specific boiling range during fractional distillation | The gasoline or diesel fraction of crude oil |
| **Chromatography** | A technique that separates mixture components based on their different migration rates across a stationary phase | Separating dyes in black ink or food coloring |
| **Solvent front** | The furthest distance reached by the advancing solvent on a chromatography strip | The wet boundary line on chromatography paper |
| **Residue** | The non-volatile solid matter left behind in the distillation flask | Dry salt crust remaining in the distilling flask |

## Part A: Crystallization: Why It Is Superior to Simple Evaporation

In Topic 4, we saw that dissolved salt can be recovered from water by simple evaporation: heat the solution until all water boils away into the atmosphere. While functional, simple evaporation suffers from three major drawbacks:

1. **Thermal decomposition:** Many heat-sensitive solids char, decompose, or scorch when heated directly to complete dryness. Heating sugar solution to dryness produces charred black carbon (caramelization) rather than pure white sugar.
2. **Impurities precipitate together:** If soluble dirt, salts, or impurities are present in the solution, evaporation to dryness forces all impurities to precipitate directly into the product, yielding a dirty, contaminated crust.
3. **Powdery, irregular product:** Rapid boiling creates an amorphous, brittle crust rather than well-formed, pure crystals.

**Crystallization** overcomes all three limitations. Instead of boiling off every drop of solvent:

- The impure solid is dissolved in a minimum amount of hot solvent to form a hot, **concentrated saturated solution**.
- The hot solution is filtered to remove any insoluble impurities.
- Rather than heating to dryness, the solution is allowed to **cool slowly and undisturbed**.
- As the liquid cools, the solvent's solubility capacity decreases dramatically (recall Topic 2: solubility decreases as temperature drops). The excess solute molecules lock into an orderly, repeating lattice, precipitating out as **exceptionally pure, geometric crystals**.
- Soluble impurities remain dissolved in the cold liquid (**mother liquor**), which is simply filtered away!

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In evaporation, **all solvent is driven off**, trapping impurities in the solid. In crystallization, the solution is cooled slowly so that **pure crystals form while impurities remain dissolved in the mother liquor**.

**Industrial and Laboratory Applications:**
- **Purification of common salt:** Sea salt harvested from coastal pans contains magnesium chloride and silt; it is dissolved, filtered, and recrystallized to produce pure table salt.
- **Sugar refining:** Raw brown sugar syrup is concentrated and cooled under vacuum to crystallize pure sucrose crystals.
- **Purification of alum (potash alum) and copper sulfate:** Growing brilliant blue, diamond-shaped copper sulfate crystals in chemistry labs.

> [!TIP]
> **Safe Crystal-Growing Activity at Home (with adult supervision):** Dissolve alum powder (potash alum, available at grocery stores) into a cup of hot water, stirring spoonful by spoonful until no more dissolves (a saturated solution). Filter the hot solution through a coffee filter into a clean glass jar. Suspend a clean cotton string tied to a pencil across the top of the jar and leave it undisturbed in a quiet corner for a week. As the liquid slowly cools and evaporates, stunning, sharp-edged geometric crystals of pure alum will grow along the string! Never taste the crystals.

### Solved Example 1: Calculating Crystal Yield

A solution is saturated with an ionic compound at 80 °C, containing 50 g of solid per 100 g of water. When the solution is cooled slowly to 20 °C, its solubility drops to 20 g per 100 g of water. What mass of pure crystals will precipitate from a solution prepared with 100 g of water? How much would precipitate from 300 g of water?

- Solute dissolved at 80 °C (in 100 g water) $= \mathbf{50\text{ g}}$
- Solute remaining dissolved at 20 °C (in 100 g water) $= \mathbf{20\text{ g}}$
- Mass of crystals precipitated $= 50 - 20 = \mathbf{30\text{ g}}$
- For a solution prepared with 300 g of water ($3 \times \text{larger}$):
  $$\text{Crystal yield} = 30\text{ g} \times 3 = \mathbf{90\text{ g}}$$

**Check:** $20\text{ g} + 30\text{ g} = 50\text{ g}$ ✅. For 300 g of water: $(50 \times 3) - (20 \times 3) = 150 - 60 = 90\text{ g}$ ✅.

## Part B: Simple Distillation: Recovering Pure Water from Salt Water

When Maya observed water condensing on the pot lid, she witnessed the foundation of **simple distillation**:

$$\text{Liquid} \xrightarrow{\text{Boiling / Vaporization}} \text{Vapor} \xrightarrow{\text{Cooling / Condensation}} \text{Pure Liquid (Distillate)}$$

Simple distillation is used to separate a **liquid solvent from a non-volatile dissolved solid**, or to separate two miscible liquids that have a **large difference in boiling points (greater than 25 °C)**.

```
              Thermometer
                   │
              ┌────┴────┐     Liebig Condenser (cold water jacket)
              │  Vapors ├════════════════════════════════════════╗
              │    ▲    │   Hot vapor ──▶ Cools into liquid      ║
         ┌────┘    │    └────┐                                   ║
         │  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  │                                   ▼
         │   Salt Solution   │                                ┌─────┐
         │ (Distilling Flask)│                                │ ~~~ │ <- Pure water
         └───────────────────┘                                └─────┘    (Distillate)
                   ▲                                           Receiving Flask
                   │ Gentle heat (Teacher / Adult only)
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Simple distillation assembly: Salt water is boiled in the round-bottom distilling flask. Water vapor rises, passes into the water-cooled Liebig condenser, and condenses into pure liquid distillate in the receiving flask. Non-volatile salt stays behind as flask residue.

**Step-by-Step Operation:**
1. Salt water is placed in the distilling flask along with a few boiling chips (to ensure smooth boiling). A thermometer is fitted so its bulb sits level with the side-arm opening.
2. The flask is heated. At 100 °C, water boils vigorously and vaporizes into steam. Non-volatile salt molecules remain behind in the flask.
3. Steam enters the central tube of the **Liebig condenser**. Cold tap water continuously circulates through the outer glass jacket (entering from the lower inlet and exiting from the upper outlet), chilling the inner tube walls.
4. The chilled steam condenses into pure liquid droplets that trickle down into the receiving flask as the **distillate**.
5. Once most water has distilled over, heating is stopped before the flask boils completely dry. The solid salt crust left in the flask is the **residue**.

**Practical Applications:**
- Producing **distilled water** for scientific research, pharmaceutical injections, car battery maintenance, and steam irons.
- Desalination plants producing potable drinking water from seawater in arid coastal regions.

### Solved Example 2: Distillate and Residue Yield

A coastal survival test distils 500 g of seawater containing 3.5% dissolved salts by mass. Assuming complete recovery with negligible vapor loss, calculate the mass of pure distilled water collected and the mass of dry salt residue remaining in the distilling flask.

- Mass of salt residue $= 3.5\% \text{ of } 500\text{ g} = 0.035 \times 500 = \mathbf{17.5\text{ g}}$
- Mass of distilled water $= 500\text{ g} - 17.5\text{ g} = \mathbf{482.5\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $482.5\text{ g} + 17.5\text{ g} = 500\text{ g}$ ✅ (law of conservation of mass).

## Part C: Fractional Distillation: Separating Miscible Liquids

Consider a mixture of **ethanol (grain alcohol)** and **water**. They are completely **miscible** (they dissolve into each other in all proportions), so a separating funnel is completely useless. Furthermore, both components are volatile liquids:

| Liquid | Normal Boiling Point |
|---|---|
| **Ethanol** | **78 °C** |
| **Water** | **100 °C** |

The boiling point difference is only $100 - 78 = \mathbf{22\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$. When heated to 78 °C, ethanol boils rapidly, but water also vaporizes significantly. In a simple distillation apparatus, the resulting distillate would still be a mixture of both liquids!

To separate miscible liquids whose boiling points differ by **less than 25 °C**, we must use **fractional distillation**.

A fractional distillation assembly inserts a tall **fractionating column** between the distilling flask and the condenser:

- A fractionating column is packed with **glass beads** or internal glass trays, providing an enormous surface area for repeated, continuous condensation and vaporization.
- As mixed vapors rise through the column, the higher-boiling component (water, 100 °C) condenses on the cool glass beads and trickles back down into the flask.
- The lower-boiling component (ethanol, 78 °C) remains in vapor form, ascends to the top of the column, passes into the condenser, and is collected first as a pure fraction.
- The thermometer remains steady at **78 °C** while ethanol distils over. Once ethanol is depleted, the temperature climbs toward 100 °C, signalling the start of the water fraction.

```
          Thermometer (reads 78 °C)
               │
               ▼
           ┌───┴───┐
           │ ○ ○ ○ ├────────╲      Liebig Condenser
           │ ○ ○ ○ │         ╲
   Top:    │ ○ ○ ○ │          ╲
   Cooler  │ ○ ○ ○ │         ╭─┴─╮
           │ ○ ○ ○ │         │   │ <- First fraction: pure ethanol (78 °C)
           │ ○ ○ ○ │         ╰───╯
           │ ○ ○ ○ │   Fractionating column packed
   Bottom: │ ○ ○ ○ │   with glass beads
   Hotter  └───┬───┘
               │
           ┌───┴───┐
           │ ~~~~~ │
           │Ethanol│
           │+ Water│
           └───────┘
               ▲
               │ Heat source (Adult / Teacher only)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Fractional distillation assembly: The fractionating column provides repeated cycles of vaporization and condensation. The lower-boiling liquid (ethanol) reaches the top and distils first, while the higher-boiling liquid (water) condenses back into the flask.

> [!NOTE]
> **Rule of Thumb:**
> - Boiling point difference $> 25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ $\rightarrow$ **Simple distillation** is sufficient.
> - Boiling point difference $< 25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ $\rightarrow$ **Fractional distillation** is required.

### Separating Gases from Liquid Air

Recall from Topic 1 that clean air is a homogeneous gaseous mixture (~78% nitrogen, ~21% oxygen, ~1% argon). How do industrial chemical plants isolate pure oxygen for hospitals and nitrogen for fertilizers?

By **fractional distillation of liquid air**:
1. Air is compressed under high pressure and cooled dramatically to convert it into a pale blue liquid (**liquefaction**).
2. The liquid air is allowed to warm up slowly inside a towering industrial fractionating column:

| Component Gas | Boiling Point | Order of Distillation |
|---|---|---|
| **Nitrogen ($N_2$)** | **$-196\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$** | Distils first (lowest boiling point) |
| **Argon ($Ar$)** | **$-186\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$** | Distils second |
| **Oxygen ($O_2$)** | **$-183\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$** | Remains liquid at bottom; distils last |

Nitrogen boils off first at $-196\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and is piped into storage tanks, followed by argon, leaving pure liquid oxygen at the base of the column.

### Fractional Distillation of Crude Oil

Crude petroleum pumped from underground wells is a thick, dark, smelly mixture of hundreds of hydrocarbons. In an oil refinery, crude oil is vaporized and fed into a massive industrial fractionating column:

- The tower is hottest at the base (~400 °C) and coolest at the top (~20 °C).
- Small, light molecules with low boiling points rise to the very top: **refinery petroleum gas (LPG)** and **gasoline (petrol)** for cars.
- Intermediate molecules condense midway: **kerosene** (aviation fuel) and **diesel oil**.
- Heavy, high-boiling molecules condense near the base: **lubricating oils**, **fuel oils**, and thick **bitumen (asphalt)** used to pave roads.

### Solved Example 3: Which Liquid Distils First?

A miscible liquid mixture contains liquid A (boiling point 65 °C) and liquid B (boiling point 110 °C).  
(a) Which liquid distils over first?  
(b) What temperature will the thermometer display while the first liquid is being collected?  
(c) Can this mixture be separated effectively using simple distillation? Explain.

- (a) **Liquid A** distils over first because it has the lower boiling point (65 °C vs. 110 °C).
- (b) The thermometer will read approximately **65 °C** throughout the collection of liquid A.
- (c) The boiling point difference is $110 - 65 = \mathbf{45\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$. Because $45\text{ }^\circ\text{C} > 25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, this mixture can be separated effectively using **simple distillation**, although using a fractionating column yields even higher purity.

## Part D: Paper Chromatography: The Secret Colors in Black Ink

Why did Maya's black ink dot blossom into rings of blue, red, and yellow when wetted?

Dyes and pigments cannot be separated by distillation because they decompose before boiling. Instead, chemists use **chromatography** (from the Greek *chroma*, meaning color).

Paper chromatography relies on two distinct phases:
1. **Stationary phase:** The porous cellulose paper strip, which holds water in its fibers and provides an adsorption surface.
2. **Mobile phase:** The liquid solvent (water or alcohol), which moves upward through the paper by capillary action.

As the mobile solvent climbs upward through the paper strip, it dissolves the dried ink spot and carries the dye molecules along with it:
- A dye that is **highly soluble in the solvent** and weakly attracted to the paper fibers moves **rapidly and travels far**.
- A dye that is **less soluble in the solvent** or strongly adsorbed onto the cellulose fibers moves **sluggishly and travels a short distance**.
- Because each pigment moves at a unique speed, the mixed dyes separate cleanly into distinct colored bands along the strip!

```
        ┌───────────┐
        │- - - - - -│ <- Solvent front (furthest reach of water)
        │           │
        │     ●     │ <- Yellow spot (highest solubility, moves furthest)
        │           │
        │     ●     │ <- Red / Magenta spot (intermediate speed)
        │           │
        │     ●     │ <- Blue spot (lowest speed, strongly adsorbed)
        │           │
        │─────○─────│ <- Pencil baseline (where ink spot was applied)
        │           │
     ~~~│~~~~~~~~~~~│~~~ <- Solvent reservoir (water level below baseline)
        └───────────┘
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Paper chromatography strip: Capillary action draws water upward from the solvent reservoir. The different colored dye molecules in the original black spot migrate at different speeds, resolving into separate spots.

**Try This Safe Hands-On Activity:**
1. Cut a strip of white coffee filter paper (about 2 cm wide by 12 cm long).
2. Use a graphite **pencil** to draw a light baseline about 2 cm from the bottom edge. *(Why pencil? Pencil graphite is pure carbon and completely insoluble in water, so it will not dissolve or interfere with the colors!)*
3. Use a black washable, water-based marker to place a tiny, concentrated ink spot right on the pencil baseline. Allow it to dry completely.
4. Pour about 1 cm of clean water into a drinking glass.
5. Suspend the paper strip vertically inside the glass so that its bottom tip dips into the water, ensuring that the **ink spot stays at least 1 cm above the water surface**.
6. Leave the glass completely still. Watch the water climb up the paper strip, pulling beautiful bands of blue, pink, and yellow out of the single black dot!
7. When the water front reaches near the top, remove the strip, mark the solvent front with a pencil, and let it dry.

**Practical Applications of Chromatography:**
- **Forensic Science:** Comparing ink from ransom notes or disputed checks with suspect pens.
- **Biochemistry:** Separating amino acids, vitamins, and plant pigments (chlorophyll a, chlorophyll b, and carotenoids from crushed green leaves).
- **Drug Testing:** Detecting trace drugs or metabolites in blood and urine samples.

### Solved Example 4: Comparing Travel Distances and Rf Values

In a paper chromatography test of a black marker, the solvent front migrated a total distance of 8.0 cm from the baseline. Three separated pigment spots were recorded: Spot A moved 6.0 cm, Spot B moved 4.0 cm, and Spot C moved 2.0 cm.  
(a) Which pigment had the highest solubility in the mobile solvent?  
(b) Calculate the retention factor ($R_f$) for each pigment ($R_f = \frac{\text{distance moved by solute}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$).

- (a) **Pigment A** moved the furthest (6.0 cm), indicating that it had the highest solubility in water and lowest affinity for the paper.
- (b) $R_f$ values:
  - $R_f(\text{A}) = \frac{6.0\text{ cm}}{8.0\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{0.75}$
  - $R_f(\text{B}) = \frac{4.0\text{ cm}}{8.0\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{0.50}$
  - $R_f(\text{C}) = \frac{2.0\text{ cm}}{8.0\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{0.25}$

**Check:** All $R_f$ values lie strictly between 0 and 1, with $R_f(\text{A}) > R_f(\text{B}) > R_f(\text{C})$ ✅. Under standardized laboratory conditions, $R_f$ values are constant physical properties used to identify unknown chemical compounds.

## Part E: Designing a Complete Multi-Component Separation Plan

Dr. Vance presented Maya with a classic chemical challenge:  
**"Imagine you have a beaker containing a mixture of fine sand, table salt, iron filings, and water. How would you isolate all four components in their pure states? Outline your step-by-step strategy."**

Maya tackled the challenge systematically by analyzing the differing physical properties of each constituent:

| Component | Key Distinct Physical Property | Exploitation Technique |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron filings** | Ferromagnetic (attracted to a magnet) | **Magnetic separation** |
| **Sand** | Coarse, dense, insoluble in water ($> 1000\text{ nm}$) | **Filtration** (retained as residue) |
| **Table salt** | Highly soluble in water, non-volatile solid | **Crystallization** or **Distillation** |
| **Water** | Volatile liquid (boils at 100 °C), condensable | **Simple distillation** |

**The Step-by-Step Procedure:**

1. **Step 1: Magnetic Separation (Isolating Iron Filings):**  
   Wrap a strong bar magnet in a clean plastic bag and move it thoroughly through the slurry (or across the surface). The iron filings are magnetically attracted and lift out cleanly. Stripping the plastic bag releases clean, dry iron filings into a dish.
2. **Step 2: Filtration (Isolating Sand):**  
   Pour the remaining mixture of sand, salt, and water through a conical filter paper supported in a funnel. The insoluble sand is trapped on the filter paper as the **residue**. Rinse the sand with a small squirt of clean water and dry it on an evaporating dish. The clear liquid that passes through is the **filtrate** (salt water).
3. **Step 3: Distillation or Crystallization (Isolating Salt and Water):**  
   - If our goal is to recover **both pure water and pure salt**, pour the filtrate into a distilling flask and assemble a simple distillation apparatus. Heat the flask: pure water boils, condenses through the Liebig condenser, and is collected in the receiving flask as the **distillate**. Solid table salt remains in the distilling flask as the **residue**.
   - If only the salt is required, the filtrate can be gently concentrated by heating and allowed to cool slowly to harvest pure table salt **crystals**.

**Summary Plan:**

| Stage | Applied Method | Target Component Separated |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Magnetic separation | Iron filings |
| 2 | Filtration | Sand (residue) |
| 3 | Simple distillation | Pure water (distillate) and dry salt (residue) |

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing evaporation with crystallization.**  
   Evaporation boils the liquid to total dryness, scorching sensitive compounds and locking impurities into the solid crust. Crystallization cools a saturated solution slowly, yielding pure geometric crystals while leaving impurities dissolved in the mother liquor.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking dissolved salt vaporizes with steam during distillation.**  
   Salt has an extraordinarily high boiling point (1465 °C). At 100 °C, only water vaporizes. Distillate collected from salt water contains zero salt.

3. **Mistake 3: Attempting simple distillation for ethanol and water.**  
   Ethanol (78 °C) and water (100 °C) have boiling points differing by only 22 °C. Both liquids vaporize simultaneously at 78 °C. A fractionating column is essential to perform repeated condensation cycles.

4. **Mistake 4: Submerging the ink spot directly into the water during chromatography.**  
   The baseline ink spot must always sit above the solvent water level. If submerged, the ink dissolves directly into the water reservoir and muddies the glass rather than climbing the paper. Furthermore, the baseline must be drawn with a **pencil**, never an ink pen.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming chromatography creates new colors.**  
   Chromatography does not synthesize colors; it merely separates pre-existing pigments that were blended together during the ink's manufacture.

6. **Mistake 6: Believing distilled water is ideal for daily drinking.**  
   Distilled water is chemically pure $H_2O$, but lacks essential dietary minerals (calcium, magnesium) and tastes flat. It is intended for laboratory experiments, automotive batteries, and medical uses.

## Quick Recap

- **Crystallization** purifies solids by cooling a hot saturated solution slowly. Pure crystals precipitate while soluble impurities remain in the mother liquor. Superior to simple evaporation for producing high-purity solids without thermal damage.
- **Simple Distillation:** Separates a non-volatile dissolved solid from a volatile liquid, or miscible liquids with a large boiling point difference ($> 25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$). Steam condenses in a water-cooled Liebig condenser into pure liquid distillate.
- **Fractional Distillation:** Separates miscible liquids with close boiling points ($< 25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) using a fractionating column packed with glass beads. Used to separate ethanol from water, isolate gases from liquid air (nitrogen at $-196\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ distils first), and refine crude oil into gasoline, diesel, and bitumen.
- **Paper Chromatography:** Separates soluble pigments based on differential migration rates between a stationary paper phase and a moving mobile solvent phase. The baseline is drawn in pencil and kept above the solvent level.
- Multi-component separation strategy: **Target the most distinct physical property first** (e.g., magnetism before filtration, filtration before distillation).
