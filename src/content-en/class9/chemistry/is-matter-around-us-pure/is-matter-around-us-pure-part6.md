# Topic 6: Elements and Compounds: What Are They?

In Topics 4 and 5, we mastered an array of techniques to take mixtures apart: filtering, evaporating, spinning in centrifuges, distilling, and running paper chromatography. Each method relied on a difference in **physical properties** (particle size, magnetism, boiling point, or solubility).

Now consider a fundamental question: what happens if you apply these physical separation techniques to a **pure substance**? 

Boil pure distilled water, and it turns into steam—yet that steam is still water, recondensing into water with identical properties. Filter pure table salt, or dissolve and recrystallize it, and you get identical white salt crystals back. Does this mean pure substances cannot be broken down any further? Or is there something deeper hidden inside? 

Today, we discover that pure substances fall into two distinct families: **elements** and **compounds**. And along the way, we will uncover an astonishing truth about everyday table salt.

## The Salt Shaker on the Dining Table

During Sunday lunch, Dr. Vance was dining with his young student Leo. On the table sat bowls of vegetable soup, pasta, fresh bread, and in the center, a stainless-steel salt shaker. Leo picked up the shaker, dusted a pinch of salt over his soup, and paused thoughtfully.

"Dr. Vance, back in Topic 1 we learned that a pure substance consists of only one single type of particle. Table salt is a pure substance. But **what is it actually made of**? No matter how finely you crush it or how carefully you filter its solution, it remains white salt. What lies beneath?"

Dr. Vance leaned forward with a smile. "Leo, would you believe that inside that modest little salt shaker sits an **explosively violent metal** locked in chemical union with a **suffocating, poisonous green gas**?"

Leo set his spoon down immediately, eyes wide with alarm. "A poisonous gas and an explosive metal?! And I just sprinkled it into my soup?!"

"You certainly did, and you are completely safe," chuckled Dr. Vance. "Why? Because when those two dangerous elements combine chemically, they forfeit their original identities completely and form an entirely new substance with totally new properties. That is the magic of a **compound**. Let us explore the building blocks of the universe: **elements** and **compounds**."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Element** | A pure substance made of **only one type of atom** that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by physical or chemical means | Iron, copper, oxygen, sulfur |
| **Compound** | A pure substance formed when **two or more elements** chemically combine in a **fixed proportion by mass** | Water, table salt, carbon dioxide |
| **Metal** | A lustrous, malleable, ductile element that conducts heat and electricity efficiently | Iron, copper, aluminum, gold |
| **Non-metal** | An element lacking metallic luster, typically brittle when solid, and a poor conductor of heat and electricity | Sulfur, carbon, oxygen, chlorine |
| **Metalloid** | An element exhibiting intermediate properties between metals and non-metals | Silicon, germanium, arsenic |
| **Chemical method** | A process involving a chemical reaction that breaks or forms chemical bonds, producing new substances | Electrolysis of water into hydrogen and oxygen |
| **Fixed proportion by mass** | The unchanging, definite mass ratio in which elements combine to form a specific chemical compound | Water always has hydrogen to oxygen in a 1 : 8 mass ratio |

## Part A: Elements: Matter Made of a Single Type of Atom

In Chapter 1, we learned that all matter is composed of particles. Now we take the next conceptual leap: in some pure substances, every single constituent particle is **strictly of one kind**. Such a substance is called an **element**.

> **An element is the simplest form of matter that cannot be broken down into simpler substances by any physical or chemical means.**

Take an iron nail. You can cut it with shears, grind it into dust, melt it in a blast furnace, or react it with acids—yet you can never extract anything simpler than iron from it. Iron is an element. Similarly, copper, aluminum, silver, gold, oxygen, hydrogen, and sulfur are all elements.

Scientists have discovered **118 elements** to date. Think about that: all the millions of different substances in our universe—rocks, oceans, plastic chairs, cotton shirts, food, and the cells of our own bodies—are built from various combinations of just 118 fundamental elements, much like millions of English words are assembled from just 26 letters of the alphabet!

**Elements in Everyday Life:**
- **Aluminum foil:** Pure metallic aluminum ($Al$).
- **Copper plumbing pipes & electrical wires:** Pure metallic copper ($Cu$).
- **Pencil "lead":** Pure graphite, which is an allotropic form of the element carbon ($C$).
- **24-karat gold:** Pure elemental gold ($Au$) (unalloyed).
- **Oxygen gas:** The element that sustains aerobic life ($O_2$).
- **Sulfur:** The bright yellow elemental powder ($S_8$) used in medicines and vulcanized rubber.

## Part B: The Three Families of Elements: Metals, Non-Metals, and Metalloids

Because 118 elements are a lot to study individually, chemists classify them into three distinct groups based on their characteristic physical and chemical properties:

| Property | Metals | Non-Metals |
|---|---|---|
| **Luster** | Shiny and lustrous; can be polished | Dull; non-reflective |
| **Hardness & Rigidity** | Hard, strong, and dense (most) | Soft or brittle solids; crumble under pressure |
| **Malleability** | **Malleable:** can be hammered into thin sheets without breaking | **Non-malleable:** shatter into powder when struck |
| **Ductility** | **Ductile:** can be drawn or stretched into thin wires | **Non-ductile:** snap if stretched |
| **Conductivity** | Excellent conductors of both heat and electricity | Poor conductors (insulators) of heat and electricity |
| **Sonority** | **Sonorous:** produce a clear ringing sound when struck | Non-sonorous; make a dull thud |
| **Everyday Examples** | Iron, copper, aluminum, gold, silver | Sulfur, carbon, oxygen, nitrogen, chlorine |

*(A notable exception: **graphite**, a form of the non-metal carbon, is an excellent conductor of electricity, which is why it is used for dry-cell battery electrodes.)*

**Metalloids:**  
A small group of elements possess properties intermediate between metals and non-metals. They are called **metalloids** or semi-metals. The classic examples are **silicon ($Si$), germanium ($Ge$), and arsenic ($As$)**. Silicon looks metallic and lustrous, but is brittle like a non-metal. It conducts electricity better than an insulator but not as freely as a metal; this property makes it a **semiconductor**, the essential foundation of all computer microprocessors and smartphone chips!

### Two Notable Exceptions: Liquid Elements at Room Temperature

Most metals are hard solids at room temperature (25 °C), and non-metals exist mostly as solids or gases. However, two unique elements exist as **liquids** at room temperature:

- **Mercury ($Hg$):** The only **metal** that is a shiny, silvery liquid at room temperature. Formerly used in clinical thermometers. *(Caution: Mercury vapor is toxic; handle broken thermometers with extreme care).*
- **Bromine ($Br$):** The only **non-metal** that is a reddish-brown liquid at room temperature, releasing dense, choking fumes.

## Part C: Compounds: Chemical Bonding Creates Brand New Substances

Now let us return to Dr. Vance's dramatic claim about table salt. Table salt (chemical name: **sodium chloride**) is a **pure substance**. However, it is not an element, because chemical decomposition breaks it down into two completely different elements:

- **Sodium ($Na$):** A soft, silvery **metal** so reactive that it ignites violently upon touching cold water. It must be stored submerged under kerosene oil to prevent contact with moisture in the air.
- **Chlorine ($Cl_2$):** A suffocating, greenish-yellow **poisonous gas** with a pungent, choking odor, historically used as a toxic chemical weapon.

When violently reactive sodium metal reacts with toxic chlorine gas, a vigorous chemical reaction takes place. The atoms rearrange, forming strong chemical bonds to produce white, sparkling crystals of **sodium chloride ($NaCl$)**: ordinary table salt! 

Table salt exhibits **neither** the explosive metallic reactivity of sodium **nor** the toxic gaseous properties of chlorine. It is completely safe to eat and essential for human life.

This leads to the formal scientific definition of a **compound**:

> **A compound is a pure substance composed of two or more elements chemically combined in a fixed proportion by mass. The properties of a compound are entirely different from those of its constituent elements.**

**Three Familiar Compounds and Their Constituent Elements:**

| Compound | Constituent Elements | Dramatic Contrast in Properties |
|---|---|---|
| **Water ($H_2O$)** | Hydrogen ($H$) and Oxygen ($O$) | Hydrogen is an explosive, highly combustible gas. Oxygen is a gas that vigorously supports combustion. Yet when chemically bonded, they form water—a liquid that **extinguishes fire**! |
| **Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$)** | Carbon ($C$) and Oxygen ($O$) | Carbon is a black combustible solid (coal). Oxygen supports burning. Carbon dioxide is a dense, non-flammable gas used inside **fire extinguishers** to smother flames! |
| **Table salt ($NaCl$)** | Sodium ($Na$) and Chlorine ($Cl$) | Sodium is a reactive metal; chlorine is a lethal toxic gas. Their compound, sodium chloride, is a harmless, essential dietary nutrient. |

### How Can Compounds Be Broken Down?

A compound **cannot be broken down by physical methods** such as filtration, boiling, evaporation, or magnets. Boiling water simply turns it into water vapor ($H_2O$), not hydrogen and oxygen gas.

To break a compound apart, we must employ **chemical methods**, such as passing a direct electrical current through it (**electrolysis**):
$$\text{Water} \xrightarrow{\text{Electric current (Electrolysis)}} \text{Hydrogen gas} + \text{Oxygen gas}$$

### The Principle of Fixed Proportions by Mass

In any given chemical compound, the constituent elements are **always locked in an unvarying, fixed ratio by mass**, regardless of the sample's origin or preparation method:

- **Water ($H_2O$):** Hydrogen and oxygen always combine in a fixed mass ratio of **1 : 8**. In every 9 g of pure water, there is exactly 1 g of hydrogen and 8 g of oxygen.
- **Carbon dioxide ($CO_2$):** Carbon and oxygen always combine in a fixed mass ratio of **3 : 8**. In every 11 g of $CO_2$, there is exactly 3 g of carbon and 8 g of oxygen.
- **Table salt ($NaCl$):** Contains approximately **39.3% sodium** and **60.7% chlorine** by mass.

*(You will explore this fundamental law in detail in the next chapter as the Law of Definite Proportions).*

## Part D: Calculations Involving Constant Mass Ratios

**Example 1 (Constituent Masses in Water):**  
In pure water, hydrogen and oxygen combine in a mass ratio of 1 : 8. Calculate the mass of hydrogen and oxygen present in 27 g of pure water.

- Total ratio parts $= 1 + 8 = \mathbf{9\text{ parts}}$
- Mass of hydrogen $= 27 \times \left(\frac{1}{9}\right) = \mathbf{3\text{ g}}$
- Mass of oxygen $= 27 \times \left(\frac{8}{9}\right) = \mathbf{24\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $3\text{ g} + 24\text{ g} = 27\text{ g}$ ✅; $\frac{3}{24} = \frac{1}{8}$ ✅.

---

**Example 2 (Reactant Masses for Water Synthesis):**  
If 4 g of hydrogen gas reacts completely with oxygen to form water:  
(a) What mass of oxygen gas is required?  
(b) What mass of water is produced?

- (a) Since 1 g of hydrogen requires 8 g of oxygen:
  $$\text{Oxygen required} = 4 \times 8 = \mathbf{32\text{ g}}$$
- (b) Total mass of water formed $= 4\text{ g} + 32\text{ g} = \mathbf{36\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $\frac{4}{32} = \frac{1}{8}$ ✅. In 36 g of water, mass of hydrogen $= 36 \times \frac{1}{9} = 4\text{ g}$ ✅.

---

**Example 3 (Composition of Carbon Dioxide):**  
Carbon and oxygen combine in a mass ratio of 3 : 8 to form carbon dioxide. Calculate the mass of carbon and oxygen present in 22 g of carbon dioxide gas.

- Total ratio parts $= 3 + 8 = \mathbf{11\text{ parts}}$
- Mass of carbon $= 22 \times \left(\frac{3}{11}\right) = \mathbf{6\text{ g}}$
- Mass of oxygen $= 22 \times \left(\frac{8}{11}\right) = \mathbf{16\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $6\text{ g} + 16\text{ g} = 22\text{ g}$ ✅; $\frac{6}{16} = \frac{3}{8}$ ✅.

---

**Example 4 (Deconstructing the Dining Table Salt Shaker):**  
Dr. Vance's soup recipe called for 5.85 g of table salt ($NaCl$). How many grams of sodium and chlorine atoms were combined inside this quantity of salt? *(Recall: Salt is 39.3% sodium and 60.7% chlorine by mass).*

- Mass of sodium $= 5.85 \times \frac{39.3}{100} \approx \mathbf{2.30\text{ g}}$
- Mass of chlorine $= 5.85 \times \frac{60.7}{100} \approx \mathbf{3.55\text{ g}}$

**Check:** $2.30 + 3.55 = 5.85\text{ g}$ ✅; $39.3\% + 60.7\% = 100\%$ ✅.

---

**Example 5 (Invariance of Ratio Across Different Sample Sizes):**  
A student electrolyzes 9 g of water in Test Tube A, while another student electrolyzes 90 g of water in Test Tube B. Compare the mass ratios of hydrogen to oxygen obtained from each experiment.

- In Test Tube A: Mass of hydrogen $= 9 \times \frac{1}{9} = 1\text{ g}$; Oxygen $= 8\text{ g}$. Ratio $= 1 : 8$.
- In Test Tube B: Mass of hydrogen $= 90 \times \frac{1}{9} = 10\text{ g}$; Oxygen $= 80\text{ g}$. Ratio $= 10 : 80 = 1 : 8$.
- **Conclusion:** The ratio of elements in a compound is independent of sample size.

## Part E: Mixtures vs. Compounds: The Critical Differences

Both mixtures and compounds contain more than one element, which frequently confuses beginners. However, their nature is fundamentally different:

```
   ELEMENT                  MIXTURE                 COMPOUND
  (Only one kind           (Different particles    (Different atoms
   of particle)             physically adjacent     chemically bonded into
                            in any ratio)           identical units)

   o  o  o  o              o  x  o  o  x            ox   ox   ox
   o  o  o  o              x  o  x  o  o            ox   ox   ox
   o  o  o  o              o  o  x  x  o            ox   ox   ox
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Microscopic representation of an element, a mixture, and a compound: In an element, all particles are identical. In a mixture, "o" and "x" mingle loosely in variable proportions. In a compound, "o" and "x" are chemically locked in a precise 1 : 1 bond, repeating uniformly throughout.

| Feature | Mixture | Compound |
|---|---|---|
| **Formation** | Substances physically blended; no chemical bonds formed | Elements chemically react and bond together |
| **New Substance?** | No new substance created | A brand new substance with new properties is born |
| **Properties** | Components retain their individual properties | Properties are completely different from constituent elements |
| **Composition** | Variable composition; components can mix in any ratio | Fixed, definite composition by mass |
| **Separation** | Separable by physical methods (filtering, magnets, distillation) | Separable only by chemical or electrochemical methods |
| **Energy Change** | Little or no heat or light absorbed or released during mixing | Significant energy (heat, light) usually absorbed or evolved |
| **Classification** | Impure matter | **Pure substance** |
| **Everyday Examples** | Air, sea water, tea, brass, soil | Water, table salt, carbon dioxide, sucrose |

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A compound is a pure substance, NOT a mixture!** Even though pure water contains two elements (hydrogen and oxygen), every single water molecule ($H_2O$) is identical. Because it contains only one species of particle, water is scientifically a pure substance.

## Part F: The Classic Iron and Sulfur Demonstration

A famous school laboratory demonstration brilliantly illustrates the boundary between a mixture and a compound.

> [!CAUTION]
> This is a teacher demonstration only. Heating iron and sulfur produces toxic, irritating sulfur dioxide fumes and glowing exothermic reactions; it must be performed inside a properly ventilated fume hood.

**Step 1: Preparing the Mixture:**  
A chemistry teacher mixes 7 g of gray iron filings with 4 g of yellow sulfur powder on a watch glass.
- Under a magnifying glass, separate gray metallic flakes and yellow sulfur grains are clearly visible.
- Passing a magnet over the powder causes the **iron filings to cling to the magnet**, leaving yellow sulfur behind.
- **Conclusion:** Before heating, this is simply a **mixture**. Iron and sulfur retain their individual colors, magnetism, and properties.

**Step 2: Heating into a Compound:**  
The teacher places the 7 : 4 blend into a hard-glass test tube and heats it strongly over a Bunsen flame.
- The mixture begins to glow with a fierce red heat, and a vigorous chemical reaction spreads throughout the tube even after removing the flame.
- The tube cools to reveal a uniform, hard, brittle **black solid**.

**Step 3: Testing the Product:**  
When the teacher tests this black solid with a magnet, **the magnet has no effect whatsoever!** The iron has lost its magnetic properties. Under a microscope, neither gray flakes nor yellow grains remain. A brand new compound—**iron(II) sulfide ($FeS$)**—has been synthesized!

| Test / Feature | Iron + Sulfur (Unheated Mixture) | Iron(II) Sulfide (Heated Compound) |
|---|---|---|
| **Appearance** | Gray and yellow particles visible | Homogeneous black solid |
| **Magnetic effect** | Iron is readily pulled out by a magnet | Magnet has no effect |
| **Properties** | Iron and sulfur retain their original properties | Original properties completely vanished |
| **Separation** | Separable by simple physical means (magnet) | Cannot be separated by physical means |

### Solved Example 6: Excess Reactant in Iron Sulfide Synthesis

Iron and sulfur combine in a fixed mass ratio of 7 : 4 to form iron(II) sulfide. A teacher heats 7 g of iron filings with 6 g of sulfur powder.  
(a) What mass of iron(II) sulfide is produced?  
(b) Which element is in excess, and what mass of it remains unreacted?

- According to the fixed 7 : 4 ratio, 7 g of iron requires exactly 4 g of sulfur.
- (a) Mass of iron(II) sulfide formed $= 7\text{ g Fe} + 4\text{ g S} = \mathbf{11\text{ g}}$
- (b) The sulfur was supplied in excess:
  $$\text{Unreacted sulfur} = 6\text{ g} - 4\text{ g} = \mathbf{2\text{ g of sulfur remains}}$$

**Check:** Total mass before reaction $= 7 + 6 = 13\text{ g}$. Total mass after reaction $= 11\text{ g (compound)} + 2\text{ g (unreacted S)} = 13\text{ g}$ ✅.

## Part G: What About Alloys? Alloys Are Mixtures, Not Compounds

In Topic 1, we introduced alloys such as brass (copper + zinc) and steel (iron + carbon). Since metals are melted together to create alloys, are alloys compounds?

**No, alloys are homogeneous mixtures, not compounds!**

1. **Variable composition:** Brass can be prepared with 60% copper and 40% zinc, or with 70% copper and 30% zinc. A chemical compound can never have a variable recipe.
2. **Retained properties:** In brass, copper and zinc retain their basic metallic characteristics (conduction, malleability).
3. **No chemical bonds:** No fixed chemical formula exists for brass.

Similarly, **22-karat gold** is an alloy (mixture) consisting of 22 parts gold blended with 2 parts copper or silver to impart hardness for jewelry, whereas pure **24-karat gold** is an elemental pure substance.

## Part H: The Complete Classification of Matter

Having explored matter from every angle across Topics 1 through 6, we can now assemble the complete **family tree of matter**:

```
                          MATTER
                             │
             ┌───────────────┴───────────────┐
             │                               │
       PURE SUBSTANCES                   MIXTURES
  (Only one chemical species)      (Physically blended)
             │                               │
       ┌─────┴─────┐                   ┌─────┴─────┐
       │           │                   │           │
   ELEMENTS    COMPOUNDS          HOMOGENEOUS  HETEROGENEOUS
  (One type    (Fixed ratio;      (Uniform     (Non-uniform;
   of atom)     bonded atoms)     throughout)   boundaries)
       │           │                   │           │
   Iron, gold, Water, salt,        Solutions,   Suspensions,
   oxygen,     carbon dioxide,     clean air,   soil, salad,
   sulfur      sucrose             brass        muddy water
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Complete classification tree of matter: All matter is either a pure substance (element or compound) or a mixture (homogeneous or heterogeneous). Mixtures are separated by physical methods; compounds are broken down only by chemical methods; elements cannot be broken down any further.

**The Quick Categorization Key:**
1. *Can it be separated into simpler components by physical methods (filtering, boiling, magnet)?*  
   - **Yes** $\rightarrow$ **Mixture**.
2. **No** $\rightarrow$ It is a **Pure Substance**.  
   - *Can it be broken down into simpler substances by chemical reactions (electrolysis, heating)?*  
     - **Yes** $\rightarrow$ **Compound**.  
     - **No** $\rightarrow$ **Element**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing compounds with mixtures because both contain multiple elements.**  
   In a mixture, substances mingle loosely without chemical bonding and retain their original properties. In a compound, atoms bond chemically in an unvarying mass ratio to forge an entirely new substance with new properties.

2. **Mistake 2: Expecting compounds to exhibit the properties of their parent elements.**  
   Table salt contains explosive sodium and toxic chlorine, but salt is neither explosive nor toxic. Compound properties are distinct from their constituents.

3. **Mistake 3: Calling water a mixture because it contains two gases.**  
   Hydrogen and oxygen in water are not free gases; they are chemically bonded into $H_2O$ molecules in an unvarying 1 : 8 mass ratio. Water is a pure compound.

4. **Mistake 4: Assuming all metals are solid and all non-metals are gases.**  
   Mercury is a liquid metal at room temperature; bromine is a liquid non-metal; and carbon, sulfur, and phosphorus are solid non-metals.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating alloys as chemical compounds.**  
   Alloys have variable compositions and retain their constituent metallic properties without forming chemical bonds. They are homogeneous solid mixtures.

6. **Mistake 6: Believing a substance is an element simply because it cannot be separated by filtration or boiling.**  
   Pure water cannot be separated by filtration or distillation either, but it is a compound because electrolysis breaks it into hydrogen and oxygen. An element cannot be broken down even by chemical reactions.

## Quick Recap

- An **element** is a pure substance made of a single type of atom that cannot be chemically broken down into simpler matter. 118 elements are known.
- Elements divide into **metals** (shiny, malleable, ductile, good conductors), **non-metals** (dull, brittle, poor conductors), and **metalloids** (intermediate semiconductors like silicon and germanium).
- **Mercury** is a liquid metal; **bromine** is a liquid non-metal at room temperature.
- A **compound** forms when two or more elements chemically combine in a **fixed proportion by mass**. Its properties are entirely distinct from its constituents (e.g., table salt, water, carbon dioxide).
- A compound is a **pure substance**. It cannot be separated by physical methods, only by chemical reactions (like electrolysis).
- Iron + sulfur: unheated = **mixture** (iron separated by magnet); heated = **iron(II) sulfide compound** (black, non-magnetic, fixed 7 : 4 mass ratio).
- **Alloys** (brass, steel, 22-karat gold) are **mixtures**, not compounds, because their composition is variable and no chemical bonding occurs.

   (5) **Separation:** Mixtures are separated by physical methods; compounds require chemical methods.

8. 7 g iron combines with 4 g sulfur. For 14 g iron ($2 \times 7$), exactly $2 \times 4 = 8\text{ g}$ of sulfur is consumed.  
   (a) Mass of iron(II) sulfide $= 14 + 8 = \mathbf{22\text{ g}}$.  
   (b) Sulfur is in excess: $12 - 8 = \mathbf{4\text{ g of unreacted sulfur remains}}$ (iron is completely consumed).  
   *Check:* Initial mass $= 14 + 12 = 26\text{ g}$; Final mass $= 22 + 4 = 26\text{ g}$ ✅

9. (a) Carbon steel: **Mixture (alloy)**; variable carbon in iron.  
   (b) Pure copper pipe: **Element**; composed exclusively of copper atoms.  
   (c) Sucrose (sugar): **Compound**; carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen chemically bonded in fixed ratio.  
   (d) Sweet tea: **Mixture**; variable amounts of sugar and tea compounds dissolved in water.  
   (e) Atmospheric air: **Mixture**; variable blend of gases ($N_2, O_2, Ar, CO_2$).  
   (f) Carbon dioxide: **Compound**; carbon and oxygen bonded in fixed 3 : 8 mass ratio.

10. (a) **False** ❌. Brass is an alloy (homogeneous mixture) with variable copper-to-zinc proportions.  
    (b) **False** ❌. Heating synthesizes iron(II) sulfide, a non-magnetic compound.  
    (c) **False** ❌. Mercury is a liquid metal at room temperature.  
    (d) **False** ❌. Compounds possess completely new properties distinct from their parent elements.  
    (e) **True** ✅. Pure substances comprise both elements and compounds.
