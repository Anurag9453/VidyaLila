# Topic 2: Atoms, Molecules, and Ions: Three Forms of Particles

In Topic 1, we learned to write elements using compact symbols: H, O, C, Na, Fe. And remember our key rule: **a symbol represents one atom of that element — its smallest fundamental particle.** That raises a natural question. We write water as "H₂O", the oxygen gas in the air we breathe as "O₂", and carbon dioxide as "CO₂". Why are those small numbers tucked at the bottom right of the symbols? And do H, H₂, and 2H all mean the same thing? Today we will understand the three forms of chemical particles — **atoms, molecules, and ions** — and learn to read chemical notation like the grammar of a language.

---

## The Bead and Necklace Stall

On Saturday afternoon, Ms. Davis took her niece Chloe to a weekend craft market. In one corner stood a jewelry-making stall. Half the table was covered with trays of **loose beads** sorted by color: one tray held only red beads, another held only blue beads, and another held only white beads. The other half of the table displayed **finished necklaces and bracelets**: some strung from beads of a single color, and others made from repeating patterns of red and white beads.

Chloe asked the craftswoman, "How much is a single loose bead, and how much is a finished bracelet?"

"A single bead is \$1," the craftswoman replied, "and this bracelet made of 2 red beads and 1 white bead is \$10. In a bracelet, the beads are joined together into a complete piece you can wear."

Chloe turned to her aunt. "Aunt Davis, the bracelet has the exact same beads that are sitting loose in the tray. Why is it treated as something so different?"

Ms. Davis smiled. "That's the exact heart of today's chemistry lesson, Chloe. Think of it this way:

- **One loose bead** = one **atom**, the smallest particle of an element.
- **Two or more beads strung together as a unit** = one **molecule**, where atoms are bonded together and travel as a single particle.
- If identical beads are joined together, you get a **molecule of an element** (like H₂). If beads of different colors are joined together, you get a **molecule of a compound** (like H₂O).

And just as a finished bracelet looks and behaves differently from a handful of loose beads, a molecule often has properties completely different from the isolated atoms that make it up."

Chloe picked up two red beads in her hand. "So I just need to pay attention to which beads are present, how many there are, and whether they are loose or joined together?" Ms. Davis nodded. "Exactly. And in chemistry, all of that information is packed into a tiny piece of notation."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atom** | The smallest particle of an element, represented by its chemical symbol | H, O, Na, Fe |
| **Molecule** | A group of two or more atoms bonded together that stays together and acts as a single particle | H₂, O₂, H₂O, CO₂ |
| **Molecule of an element** | A molecule made of atoms of the **same element** bonded together | H₂, O₂, N₂, O₃, P₄, S₈ |
| **Molecule of a compound** | A molecule made of atoms of **different elements** bonded together | H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, HCl |
| **Atomicity** | The total number of atoms present in one molecule | O₂ has atomicity 2; O₃ has atomicity 3 |
| **Subscript** | A small number written at the lower right of a symbol | The ₂ in H₂ |
| **Coefficient** | A normal-sized number written **in front of** a symbol or formula, showing how many particles (atoms or molecules) there are | The 2 in 2H₂O |
| **Ion** | An atom or group of atoms that carries an electrical **charge** (positive or negative) | Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺, SO₄²⁻ |
| **Cation** | A positively charged ion | Na⁺, Ca²⁺ |
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion | Cl⁻, O²⁻ |

## Part A: The Atom: Smallest Particle of an Element

In Topic 1, we saw that an element's symbol stands for "one atom". Let's sharpen that idea.

> **An atom is the smallest particle of an element that retains the chemical identity of that element.**

Imagine taking a piece of pure iron and dividing it into smaller and smaller pieces. Eventually, you would reach a single **atom of iron** — the smallest unit that is still iron. If you did the same with pure gold, you would reach a single atom of gold. Every element has its own kind of atoms: all atoms of a given element are alike, and they are different from the atoms of every other element.

One important fact: atoms are unimaginably tiny. You cannot see an individual atom with your naked eyes or even with a standard school microscope — millions upon millions of atoms could fit across the period (.) at the end of this sentence. What is inside an atom will be covered in the "Structure of the Atom" chapter, and how much an atom weighs will be covered in the next chapter, "Atoms and Molecules". For now, remember: **atom = the smallest particle of an element**, represented by its symbol (H, O, Na, Fe...).

In nature, most atoms do not stay alone. They usually **bond with other atoms of the same kind** or **with atoms of different elements**. When they bond together into discrete units, they form molecules.

## Part B: The Molecule: Atoms Joined Together

> **A molecule is a group of two or more atoms chemically bonded together that stays together and behaves as a single unit.**

Just like Chloe's beaded bracelet, the atoms in a molecule do not wander off separately — they move as one unit.

### Molecules of Elements: Beads of the Same Color

When atoms of the **same element** bond together, they form a molecule of that element.

- **Hydrogen:** 2 hydrogen atoms join to form one hydrogen molecule, written **H₂**.
- **Oxygen:** 2 oxygen atoms join to form **O₂** — the gas we breathe.
- **Nitrogen:** **N₂**, which makes up about 78% of Earth's atmosphere.
- **Chlorine:** **Cl₂**.
- **Ozone:** 3 oxygen atoms join to form **O₃**, a gas with properties very different from ordinary oxygen (O₂).
- **Phosphorus:** 4 phosphorus atoms join to form **P₄**.
- **Sulphur:** 8 sulphur atoms join in a crown-shaped ring to form **S₈**.

Notice something remarkable: **both O₂ and O₃ are made purely of oxygen atoms**, yet they are two completely different gases because the number of atoms bonded in each molecule is different. That proves that in a molecule, not only the type of atom matters, but also **how many atoms are bonded together**.

Some elements do not form multi-atom molecules:

- **Helium (He), Neon (Ne), and Argon (Ar)** are noble gases that exist as single, unbonded atoms.
- **Metals** (iron, copper, gold, sodium) and **carbon** (C) are written simply using their symbols: Fe, Cu, Au, Na, C. In solids, their atoms are packed together in vast three-dimensional networks rather than small discrete molecules like H₂.

### Molecules of Compounds: Beads of Different Colors

When atoms of **different elements** bond together in a definite ratio, they form a **molecule of a compound**.

- **Water (H₂O):** 2 hydrogen atoms + 1 oxygen atom.
- **Carbon dioxide (CO₂):** 1 carbon atom + 2 oxygen atoms.
- **Ammonia (NH₃):** 1 nitrogen atom + 3 hydrogen atoms (a pungent gas used to make fertilizers and cleaning solutions).
- **Hydrogen chloride (HCl):** 1 hydrogen atom + 1 chlorine atom.

Every molecule of a given compound always contains its atoms in a **fixed ratio** (just as we learned in Chapter 2). Water is always 2 : 1 (H₂O), never 3 : 1.

Compare atoms and molecules in the three diagrams below. First, loose atoms versus bonded molecules:

```
   ATOMS (loose beads)           MOLECULES (bonded beads)

       (H)    (H)    (H)             (H)-(H)     (H)-(H)

   3 separate hydrogen atoms      2 hydrogen molecules (H₂)
   (each standing alone)          (each molecule has 2 bonded atoms)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Individual atoms (H) stand alone. In a molecule (H₂), two atoms are bonded together and travel as a single particle. Both contain only hydrogen, but how the atoms are grouped is completely different.

Next, molecules of elements — monoatomic vs diatomic:

```
   HELIUM (He)                    HYDROGEN (H₂)             OXYGEN (O₂)

   (He)   (He)   (He)             (H)-(H)  (H)-(H)          (O)=(O)  (O)=(O)

   each particle is 1 atom        each particle is 2 atoms   each particle is 2 atoms
   (monoatomic)                   (diatomic)                 (diatomic)
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Helium particles are single unbonded atoms (monoatomic). Hydrogen and oxygen particles are two-atom molecules (diatomic). The double line in oxygen simply indicates a double bond, which you will study in higher classes.

Finally, molecules of compounds, where atoms of different elements bond in a fixed pattern:

```
   WATER (H₂O)         CARBON DIOXIDE (CO₂)       AMMONIA (NH₃)

      (O)                                              (N)
     /   \             (O)=(C)=(O)                   /  |  \
   (H)   (H)                                       (H) (H) (H)

   1 O + 2 H           1 C + 2 O                   1 N + 3 H
   (3 atoms total)     (3 atoms total)             (4 atoms total)
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Molecules of compounds contain atoms of different elements bonded in fixed numbers: water has 2 H and 1 O, carbon dioxide has 1 C and 2 O, and ammonia has 1 N and 3 H. (These sketches show which atoms are connected and how many there are, rather than exact 3D bond angles.)

## Part C: Atomicity: How Many Atoms in a Molecule?

The total number of atoms present in a single molecule is called its **atomicity**. You can read it directly by adding the **subscripts** written at the lower right of each symbol. If a symbol has no subscript written next to it, the number is understood to be **1**.

| Substance | Formula | Number of atoms (Atomicity) | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Helium | He | 1 | Monoatomic |
| Neon | Ne | 1 | Monoatomic |
| Argon | Ar | 1 | Monoatomic |
| Hydrogen | H₂ | 2 | Diatomic |
| Oxygen | O₂ | 2 | Diatomic |
| Nitrogen | N₂ | 2 | Diatomic |
| Chlorine | Cl₂ | 2 | Diatomic |
| Hydrogen chloride | HCl | 2 | Diatomic |
| Ozone | O₃ | 3 | Triatomic |
| Water | H₂O | 3 | Triatomic |
| Carbon dioxide | CO₂ | 3 | Triatomic |
| Phosphorus | P₄ | 4 | Tetraatomic |
| Ammonia | NH₃ | 4 | Tetraatomic |
| Methane | CH₄ | 5 | Pentaatomic |
| Sulphur | S₈ | 8 | Polyatomic (many atoms) |

Easy Greek prefixes to remember: **mono = 1, di = 2, tri = 3, tetra = 4, penta = 5**. Molecules with many atoms are often grouped under the general term **polyatomic** (*poly* = many).

**Note carefully:** HCl is diatomic because it has 1 + 1 = 2 atoms in total, even though it is a **compound** (two different elements). H₂ is also diatomic, and it is an **element**. Atomicity only counts the **total number of atoms in one molecule** — it does not tell you whether the substance is an element or a compound.

### Worked Examples: Atomicity

**Example 1:** Sulphuric acid has the formula H₂SO₄. How many atoms of each element are in one molecule, and what is the total number of atoms?

- Subscript after H is 2 → **2** hydrogen atoms.
- No subscript after S → **1** sulphur atom.
- Subscript after O is 4 → **4** oxygen atoms.
- Total atoms = 2 + 1 + 4 = **7** atoms.

**Check:** 2 + 1 + 4 = 7 ✅. Since it has 7 atoms, we classify it as polyatomic.

**Example 2:** Glucose (a simple sugar our cells use for energy) has the formula C₆H₁₂O₆. How many atoms are in one molecule of glucose?

- C = 6, H = 12, O = 6
- Total = 6 + 12 + 6 = **24** atoms.

**Check:** 6 + 12 = 18, and 18 + 6 = 24 ✅.

**Example 3:** Both ozone (O₃) and oxygen gas (O₂) are made only of oxygen atoms. How do they differ?

- O₂: **2** oxygen atoms per molecule (diatomic).
- O₃: **3** oxygen atoms per molecule (triatomic).
- Because their molecules contain different numbers of bonded oxygen atoms, they are distinct substances with different physical and chemical properties.

**Check:** 2 ≠ 3, so the molecules are distinct ✅.

## Part D: Reading Chemical Notation: H, H₂, 2H, 2H₂, 3H₂O

Now let's master the grammar of chemical notation. A small number written **below and to the right** of a symbol means something completely different from a normal-sized number written **in front of** a formula.

> **A subscript (small lower-right number)** tells you how many atoms of that element are bonded **inside one molecule**.
> **A coefficient (large number in front)** tells you **how many separate particles (atoms or molecules)** you have.

In terms of Chloe's craft stall: the **subscript** tells you how many beads are strung **inside one bracelet**, while the **coefficient** tells you **how many bracelets** are on the table.

Let's compare these expressions for hydrogen and water:

| Notation | Meaning | Total number of atoms |
|---|---|---|
| H | **1 atom** of hydrogen | 1 |
| H₂ | **1 molecule** of hydrogen (containing 2 bonded H atoms) | 2 |
| 2H | **2 separate atoms** of hydrogen (not bonded to each other) | 2 |
| 2H₂ | **2 molecules** of hydrogen (each containing 2 bonded H atoms) | 4 |
| 3H₂O | **3 molecules** of water (each containing 2 H atoms and 1 O atom) | 9 (6 H + 3 O) |

```
   H          H₂          2H            2H₂

  (H)       (H)-(H)     (H)   (H)    (H)-(H)   (H)-(H)

  1 atom    1 molecule  2 separate    2 molecules
            (2 atoms)   atoms (2)     (4 atoms total)
```

> **Figure 2.4** – H is 1 single atom. H₂ is 1 molecule made of 2 bonded atoms. 2H means 2 separate, unbonded atoms. 2H₂ means 2 molecules, giving 4 atoms in total. The coefficient in front counts how many molecules (or atoms) there are; the subscript counts how many atoms are bonded inside each molecule.

**Golden rule:** You must **never alter a subscript** just to change the number of particles. Changing H₂ to H₃ or H₂O to H₂O₂ changes the identity of the substance itself! If you want more molecules of the same substance, change the **coefficient in front** (2H₂, 3H₂).

### Worked Examples: Reading Notation

**Example 4:** In 3H₂O, how many water molecules, hydrogen atoms, oxygen atoms, and total atoms are present?

- Coefficient 3 in front = **3 molecules** of water.
- Each molecule has 2 H atoms and 1 O atom.
- Hydrogen atoms = 3 × 2 = **6**
- Oxygen atoms = 3 × 1 = **3**
- Total atoms = 6 + 3 = **9**

**Check:** 3 molecules × 3 atoms per molecule (H₂O is triatomic) = 9 ✅.

**Example 5:** What is the difference between 2H₂ and 2H? How many total atoms does each have?

- 2H₂ = 2 molecules × 2 atoms per molecule = **4 atoms** (arranged as 2 bonded pairs).
- 2H = **2 separate atoms** (unbonded).
- They differ both in how the atoms are connected and in total atom count (4 vs 2).

**Check:** 2 × 2 = 4 ✅; 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

**Example 6:** State in words what 5O₂ represents and find the total number of oxygen atoms.

- **5 molecules** of oxygen gas, where each molecule is diatomic (O₂).
- Total oxygen atoms = 5 × 2 = **10**.

**Check:** 5 molecules × 2 atoms = 10 ✅.

**Example 7:** In 4NH₃, how many nitrogen atoms, hydrogen atoms, and total atoms are present?

- 4 molecules of ammonia. Each molecule contains 1 N and 3 H.
- Nitrogen atoms = 4 × 1 = **4**
- Hydrogen atoms = 4 × 3 = **12**
- Total atoms = 4 + 12 = **16**

**Check:** NH₃ is tetraatomic (4 atoms per molecule), and 4 × 4 = 16 ✅.

**Example 8:** Count the total number of atoms across the expression 2CO₂ + 3H₂. (This is purely a counting exercise, not a reaction.)

- 2CO₂ = 2 molecules × 3 atoms = **6 atoms** (2 C and 4 O).
- 3H₂ = 3 molecules × 2 atoms = **6 atoms** (6 H).
- Total = 6 + 6 = **12** atoms.

**Check:** 2 C + 4 O + 6 H = 12 ✅.

**Example 9 (Writing notation):** Write "five molecules of chlorine" in chemical notation and state the total number of chlorine atoms.

- One molecule of chlorine is Cl₂. Five molecules = **5Cl₂**.
- Total atoms = 5 × 2 = **10 Cl atoms**.

**Check:** 5 × 2 = 10 ✅.

## Part E: The Ion: When a Particle Carries a Charge

Both atoms and molecules are electrically **neutral** — they have no net electrical charge. However, an atom (or a bonded group of atoms) can sometimes **lose or gain** tiny negatively charged particles called **electrons**. (We will learn all about electrons and why atoms lose or gain them in the "Structure of the Atom" chapter. For now, simply remember that atoms can **lose or gain electrons**.)

When an atom or group of atoms loses or gains electrons, it acquires an electrical **charge**. A charged particle is called an **ion**.

- **A positively charged ion = a cation.** It forms when an atom **loses** one or more electrons. Examples: **Na⁺** (sodium ion), **Ca²⁺** (calcium ion).
- **A negatively charged ion = an anion.** It forms when an atom **gains** one or more electrons. Examples: **Cl⁻** (chloride ion), **O²⁻** (oxide ion).

The charge is written as a **superscript at the upper right** of the symbol. Remember: subscripts go at the bottom right (counting atoms), while charges go at the top right:

| Notation | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Na | A neutral **atom** of sodium |
| Na⁺ | A sodium **ion** with 1 positive charge (lost 1 electron) |
| Cl | A neutral **atom** of chlorine |
| Cl⁻ | A chloride **ion** with 1 negative charge (gained 1 electron) |
| Ca²⁺ | A calcium ion with **2** positive charges |
| O²⁻ | An oxide ion with **2** negative charges |

Furthermore, an ion can be made of **a single atom** or **a group of bonded atoms**:

- **Simple ion (monoatomic ion):** a single charged atom, such as Na⁺, Cl⁻, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺.
- **Polyatomic ion:** a group of bonded atoms that carries an overall charge and stays together as a single unit, such as **NH₄⁺** (ammonium), **OH⁻** (hydroxide), **SO₄²⁻** (sulphate), and **CO₃²⁻** (carbonate).

We will study polyatomic ions in full detail in **Topic 4**. For now, just learn to recognize them by their group formula and upper-right charge.

Ordinary table salt (NaCl) is actually built from **Na⁺ and Cl⁻** ions. Because opposite electrical charges attract each other, positive Na⁺ ions and negative Cl⁻ ions hold together tightly.

```
   NEUTRAL ATOMS                   IONS

   (Na)                        (Na⁺)  = lost an electron, positive
   (Cl)                        (Cl⁻)  = gained an electron, negative

   Na  +  Cl   --->   Na⁺  ..  Cl⁻
   (both neutral)    (opposite charges attract each other)
```

> **Figure 2.5** – Neutral atoms vs ions: sodium loses an electron to become positively charged Na⁺, while chlorine gains an electron to become negatively charged Cl⁻. Opposite charges attract each other strongly.

### Worked Example: Classifying Particles

**Example 10:** Classify each particle below as an atom, a molecule, or an ion: **Na, Cl₂, Mg²⁺, H₂O, F⁻, He, NH₄⁺**.

| Particle | Classification | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Na | Atom | Single element symbol, no charge |
| Cl₂ | Molecule (of an element) | 2 identical atoms bonded together, no charge |
| Mg²⁺ | Ion (cation) | Carries a 2+ positive charge (lost electrons) |
| H₂O | Molecule (of a compound) | Atoms of different elements bonded together, no charge |
| F⁻ | Ion (anion) | Carries a 1− negative charge (gained an electron) |
| He | Atom | Noble gas existing as single unbonded atoms, no charge |
| NH₄⁺ | Polyatomic ion (cation) | Group of 5 bonded atoms carrying an overall 1+ charge |

**Check:** Out of 7 particles: 2 atoms (Na, He), 2 molecules (Cl₂, H₂O), and 3 ions (Mg²⁺, F⁻, NH₄⁺). Total: 2 + 2 + 3 = 7 ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking H₂ and 2H mean the exact same thing because both have 2 hydrogen atoms.**
   No. H₂ is a single molecule in which 2 hydrogen atoms are **bonded together**. 2H represents 2 **separate, unbonded** hydrogen atoms. Just like a finished bracelet versus two loose beads, their properties are different.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking 2H₂O has only 2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom.**
   No. The coefficient 2 in front multiplies the entire formula. 2H₂O means **2 water molecules**, which contain 2 × 2 = 4 hydrogen atoms and 2 × 1 = 2 oxygen atoms.

3. **Mistake 3: Writing atmospheric oxygen gas simply as O.**
   In the air we breathe, oxygen exists as diatomic molecules, **O₂** (2 bonded atoms). Writing O alone represents a single isolated oxygen atom.

4. **Mistake 4: Thinking O₂ and O₃ are the same gas because both contain only oxygen.**
   No. Even though both contain only oxygen atoms, each molecule has a different number of atoms (2 vs 3), making oxygen (O₂) and ozone (O₃) two distinct gases with very different properties.

5. **Mistake 5: Confusing Co with CO.**
   As we saw in Topic 1, **Co** is the element cobalt, whereas **CO** is carbon monoxide (a compound of carbon and oxygen). Always check uppercase vs lowercase letters.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking water can also be written as H₃O or HO₂.**
   No. The subscripts in a compound's formula are **fixed**. Water is always **H₂O**; changing the subscripts changes the substance completely.

7. **Mistake 7: Thinking a molecule of a compound contains only one element.**
   No. A molecule of a compound always contains atoms of **at least two different elements**. A molecule made of only one kind of atom is a molecule of an **element**.

8. **Mistake 8: Thinking an ion is a brand-new element.**
   No. Na and Na⁺ are the same element (sodium); Na⁺ has simply lost one electron and acquired a positive charge, which gives it very different chemical behavior from neutral sodium metal.

## Quick Recap

- **Atom:** The smallest particle of an element, written using its symbol (H, O, Na).
- **Molecule:** Two or more atoms bonded together as a single unit. **Molecules of elements** (same kind of atom): H₂, O₂, N₂, Cl₂, O₃, P₄, S₈. **Molecules of compounds** (different kinds of atoms): H₂O, CO₂, NH₃, HCl.
- **Atomicity:** Total number of atoms in one molecule — monoatomic (1), diatomic (2), triatomic (3), tetraatomic (4), pentaatomic (5), polyatomic (many). Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) are monoatomic.
- A **subscript** (lower-right small number) counts atoms **inside one molecule**. A **coefficient** (large number in front) counts **how many molecules (or atoms)** are present.
- H = 1 atom; H₂ = 1 molecule (2 bonded atoms); 2H = 2 separate atoms; 2H₂ = 2 molecules (4 atoms total); 3H₂O = 3 water molecules (9 atoms total: 6 H + 3 O).
- Never alter subscripts to change quantity — change the coefficient in front instead.
- **Ion:** A charged particle formed by losing or gaining electrons. **Cations** (positive, lost electrons): Na⁺, Ca²⁺. **Anions** (negative, gained electrons): Cl⁻, O²⁻. **Polyatomic ions** (NH₄⁺, SO₄²⁻) are bonded groups of atoms carrying an overall charge.
- Charges are written as **upper-right superscripts**, while atom counts are written as **lower-right subscripts**.

### Answers

1. **Atom:** The smallest particle of an element that retains its identity. **Molecule:** A group of two or more atoms bonded together that behaves as a single particle. Molecules of an element: H₂, O₂ (or N₂, Cl₂). Molecules of a compound: H₂O, CO₂ (or NH₃, HCl).

2. **Atomicity** is the total number of atoms in one molecule. He = 1 (monoatomic), N₂ = 2 (diatomic), O₃ = 3 (triatomic), P₄ = 4 (tetraatomic), NH₃ = 1 + 3 = 4 (tetraatomic), CH₄ = 1 + 4 = 5 (pentaatomic).

3. H₂ is **one molecule** in which 2 hydrogen atoms are **bonded together** (like a two-bead bracelet). 2H represents **2 separate hydrogen atoms** that are not bonded (two loose beads). Sketch: H₂ = (H)-(H), whereas 2H = (H)   (H). Both have 2 atoms in total, but their bonding and properties are different.

4. (a) 3O₂ = 3 molecules of oxygen, 3 × 2 = **6** atoms. (b) 2H₂O = 2 molecules of water, 2 × 3 = **6** atoms (4 H + 2 O). (c) 4HCl = 4 molecules of hydrogen chloride, 4 × 2 = **8** atoms (4 H + 4 Cl). (d) 2P₄ = 2 molecules of phosphorus, 2 × 4 = **8** atoms.
   Check: (b) 4 + 2 = 6 ✅; (c) 4 + 4 = 8 ✅.

5. 5H₂O = **5 molecules** of water. Hydrogen atoms = 5 × 2 = **10**. Oxygen atoms = 5 × 1 = **5**. Total atoms = 10 + 5 = **15** atoms.
   Check: 5 molecules × 3 atoms per molecule = 15 ✅.

6. In 1 molecule of glucose: C = 6, H = 12, O = 6. In 2 molecules: C = **12**, H = **24**, O = **12**. Total atoms = 12 + 24 + 12 = **48** atoms.
   Check: 24 atoms per molecule × 2 = 48 ✅.

7. (a) **4Cl₂**. (b) **3CO₂**. (c) **5Na**. (d) **2NH₃**.

8. Each molecule of O₂ has 2 bonded oxygen atoms, whereas each molecule of O₃ has 3 bonded oxygen atoms. Because the number of atoms per molecule is different, they are different substances with different properties (O₂ is the oxygen we breathe; O₃ is ozone).

9. K = **atom**. N₂ = **molecule** (of an element). K⁺ = **ion, cation** (positive). CO₂ = **molecule** (of a compound). S²⁻ = **ion, anion** (negative). Ar = **atom** (monoatomic noble gas). SO₄²⁻ = **polyatomic ion, anion**. Cations: K⁺. Anions: S²⁻, SO₄²⁻.

10. (a) **False** ❌. H₂ is one molecule of two bonded atoms; 2H is two separate atoms. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌. Na⁺ has **lost** one electron, giving it a positive charge. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌. The coefficient 2 in front means there are **2 molecules** of water; the subscript ₂ after H shows that each molecule has 2 hydrogen atoms.

"Aunt Davis, now when anyone sees H₂O, they know right away that 2 hydrogens and 1 oxygen are joined together as a single unit!" Ms. Davis nodded. "Exactly. And that leads to the next big mystery: why does one oxygen atom join with *two* hydrogen atoms rather than one or three? What decides how many atoms can bond together?"

**What's next?** Every element's atom has a definite **combining capacity** — some bond with one partner, some with two, some with three or four. That combining capacity is what makes water H₂O and ammonia NH₃. Chemists call it **valency**, and we will explore it in **Topic 3: Valency: Combining Capacity of Elements**.
