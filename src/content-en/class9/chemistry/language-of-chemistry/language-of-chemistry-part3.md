# Topic 3: Valency: Combining Capacity of Elements

In Topic 1, we learned the shorthand symbols for elements, and in Topic 2, we saw how atoms bond together to form molecules such as H₂O, NH₃, and CO₂. Now comes a central question: **how many bonds can one atom form with other atoms?** Why does an oxygen atom in water always hold on to 2 hydrogen atoms, rather than 1 or 3? The answer lies in a simple whole number called **valency**. Mastering valency will allow us to predict and write the formulas of chemical compounds with complete confidence.

---

## The Shuttle Bus and Its Bench Seats

An evening shuttle bus was rolling along a winding country road. Mr. Carson was sitting by the window with his niece Zara. Looking down the aisle, Zara noticed that the bus had different types of bench seats: some **single seats** near the front, several **2-seater benches** in the middle, and a few **3-seater benches** toward the back.

At the next stop, the driver welcomed passengers aboard: "Family of three? Take that 3-seater bench in the middle. Two friends traveling together? Take the 2-seater on the left." A solo commuter stepped on and took a **single seat** up front.

Zara chuckled. "Uncle Carson, every bench has its own fixed capacity — 1 seat, 2 seats, or 3 seats."

Mr. Carson nodded. "Exactly. And notice the rule when groups match up with benches: **every seat on a chosen bench gets filled, and nobody is left standing in the aisle**. Chemistry works the exact same way. Every element's atom has a **fixed combining capacity** — a specific number of 'hands' or 'seats' it uses to connect with other atoms. Chemists call this combining capacity **valency**."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Valency** | The **combining capacity** of an atom — how many single connections (bonds) it can form with other atoms | Hydrogen has valency 1; oxygen has valency 2 |
| **Combining capacity** | Another phrase for valency | Like the number of seats on a bench |
| **Variable valency** | When the same element can exhibit more than one valency in different compounds | Iron shows valencies 2 and 3 |
| **Noble gases** | Unreactive gases like helium, neon, and argon that do not ordinarily combine with other atoms, so their valency is **0** | He, Ne, Ar |
| **Valency line** | A short dash (-) drawn between symbols in a diagram to represent one unit of combining capacity | The single line in H-Cl |

---

## Part A: What Is Valency?

Hydrogen is the simplest element of all. A hydrogen atom can form **only one** connection with another atom. Therefore, the valency of hydrogen is defined as **1**. In our shuttle-bus analogy, hydrogen is a **single seat**.

> **Valency is the number that tells us how many hydrogen atoms (or equivalent single connections) one atom of an element can combine with.**

Look at four familiar compounds of hydrogen:

- **HCl** (hydrogen chloride): 1 hydrogen atom combines with 1 chlorine atom. Both form one connection. Hydrogen has valency 1, so chlorine also has valency **1**.
- **H₂O** (water): 1 oxygen atom holds **2 hydrogen atoms**. Because oxygen forms 2 connections, oxygen has valency **2**.
- **NH₃** (ammonia): 1 nitrogen atom holds **3 hydrogen atoms**. Nitrogen has valency **3**.
- **CH₄** (methane): 1 carbon atom holds **4 hydrogen atoms**. Carbon has valency **4**.

See how clean that is? By treating hydrogen (valency 1) as our yardstick, we can immediately tell the combining capacity of other elements by counting how many hydrogen atoms bond to one atom of that element.

> **An important note on "Why?":** Why does oxygen have a valency of 2 while aluminium has a valency of 3? We will **not** answer that question in this chapter. The secret lies inside the atom's electron shells, which we will uncover in **Chapter 5 (Structure of the Atom)**. For now, we treat valency as a **reference table** to learn and apply — just as passengers check how many seats a bench has without needing to ask the bus engineer why it was built that way. Likewise, we will wait until Chapter 5 to learn how to deduce valency from an element's atomic number.

```
   BUS BENCH SEATS                COMBINING CAPACITY OF ELEMENTS

   [ 1 ]  single seat        ->    H, Na, Cl      (valency 1)
   [ 2 ][ 2 ]  2-seater      ->    O, Mg, Ca      (valency 2)
   [ 3 ][ 3 ][ 3 ]  3-seater ->    N, Al          (valency 3)
   [ 4 ][ 4 ][ 4 ][ 4 ]      ->    C, Si          (valency 4)
   [ 0 ]  closed seat        ->    He, Ne, Ar     (valency 0)
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The bench-seat analogy: just as each bench holds a fixed number of passengers, each element's atom has a fixed combining capacity (valency). Noble gases do not combine at all (valency 0).

## Part C: Other Important Elements and Variable Valency

Beyond the first 20 elements, several everyday metals have valencies you will use all the time:

| Element | Symbol | Valency |
|---|---|---|
| Zinc | Zn | 2 |
| Silver | Ag | 1 |
| Lead | Pb | 2 |
| Iron | Fe | 2 and 3 |
| Copper | Cu | 1 and 2 |
| Mercury | Hg | 1 and 2 |
| Tin | Sn | 2 and 4 |

Notice that several elements can have **more than one valency**. This property is called **variable valency**.

**What does variable valency mean in practice?** It means the same element can combine in different ratios in **different compounds**, forming a distinct compound each time. Here are two classic examples:

- **Iron (Fe):** In **FeCl₂**, iron has a valency of 2; in **FeCl₃**, iron has a valency of 3. These are two different compounds called **iron(II) chloride** and **iron(III) chloride**, where the Roman numeral in parentheses tells you the valency of iron.
- **Copper (Cu):** In **Cu₂O**, copper has a valency of 1 (copper(I) oxide), whereas in **CuO**, copper has a valency of 2 (copper(II) oxide).

Phosphorus behaves similarly: it shows valency 3 in PH₃ and valency 5 in PCl₅.

In this chapter, you simply need to remember **which elements can show variable valency**, and read the Roman numeral in a compound's name to know which valency is active. Why certain elements have variable valency will also become clear after Chapter 5.

## Part D: Matching Valencies to Build Compounds

Now let's put valency to work. **When two elements combine to form a compound, their total combining capacities must balance completely — no 'seat' can be left empty, and no connection can be left dangling.**

> **Total valency of an element = (number of atoms) × (valency of one atom)**
> A two-element formula is balanced when the **total valency of the first element equals the total valency of the second element**.

Check water (**H₂O**): there are 2 hydrogen atoms, each with valency 1, giving a total valency of 2 × 1 = **2**. There is 1 oxygen atom with valency 2, giving a total valency of 1 × 2 = **2**. Both sides equal 2 — all connections are matched!

Look at the valency-line diagrams below, where each line represents one matched connection:

```
       H₂O              NH₃                 CH₄

     H     H          H     H                H
      \   /            \   /                 :
        O                N               H - C - H
                         :                   :
                         H                   H

   O has 2 lines    N has 3 lines         C has 4 lines
   (valency 2)      (valency 3)           (valency 4)
   each H has       each H has            each H has
   1 line           1 line                1 line
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Atoms connected by valency lines. Oxygen has 2 lines (H₂O), nitrogen has 3 lines (NH₃), and carbon has 4 lines (CH₄), while every hydrogen atom has 1 line (valency 1). (These diagrams illustrate combining capacity rather than 3D molecular geometry.)

Here are three more examples showing HCl, MgO, and AlCl₃:

```
   HCl:   H - Cl                1 line between H (1) and Cl (1): matched

   MgO:   Mg = O                2 lines between Mg (2) and O (2): matched

   AlCl₃:      Cl
               :
         Cl - Al                Al has 3 lines; each of the 3 Cl atoms has 1 line
               :
               Cl
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Accounting for valency lines: every line connects one capacity unit on each atom, so the total valency of both elements is always equal.

## Part E: Worked Examples

**Example 1 (HCl):** In what ratio do hydrogen and chlorine atoms combine?

- Valency of H = 1; valency of Cl = 1.
- 1 H atom (total valency 1) matches 1 Cl atom (total valency 1).
- Formula: **HCl** (1 H and 1 Cl).

**Check:** 1 × 1 = 1 and 1 × 1 = 1 ✅.

**Example 2 (H₂O):** In what ratio do hydrogen and oxygen atoms combine?

- Valency of H = 1; valency of O = 2.
- One oxygen atom forms 2 connections, so it requires **2 hydrogen atoms** (1 connection each).
- Formula: **H₂O** (2 H and 1 O).

**Check:** Total valency of H = 2 × 1 = 2; total valency of O = 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

**Example 3 (NH₃):** How many hydrogen atoms combine with 1 nitrogen atom?

- Valency of N = 3; valency of H = 1.
- Nitrogen's 3 connections require **3 hydrogen atoms**.
- Formula: **NH₃**.

**Check:** 3 × 1 = 3 and 1 × 3 = 3 ✅.

**Example 4 (CH₄):** How many hydrogen atoms combine with 1 carbon atom?

- Valency of C = 4; valency of H = 1.
- Carbon's 4 connections require **4 hydrogen atoms**.
- Formula: **CH₄**.

**Check:** 4 × 1 = 4 and 1 × 4 = 4 ✅.

**Example 5 (MgO):** In what ratio do magnesium and oxygen atoms combine?

- Valency of Mg = 2; valency of O = 2.
- 1 Mg atom (total valency 2) matches 1 O atom (total valency 2) directly.
- Formula: **MgO** (1 Mg and 1 O).

**Check:** 1 × 2 = 2 and 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

**Example 6 (CaCl₂):** In what ratio do calcium and chlorine atoms combine?

- Valency of Ca = 2; valency of Cl = 1.
- Calcium's 2 connections require **2 chlorine atoms** (1 connection each).
- Formula: **CaCl₂**.

**Check:** Ca: 1 × 2 = 2; Cl: 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

**Example 7 (Al₂O₃ — finding a common multiple):** In what ratio do aluminium (valency 3) and oxygen (valency 2) combine?

- One Al has valency 3 and one O has valency 2, so a 1 : 1 ratio does not balance.
- Find the smallest common multiple of 3 and 2, which is **6**.
- To reach a total valency of 6: we need 6 ÷ 3 = **2 Al atoms** (2 × 3 = 6) and 6 ÷ 2 = **3 O atoms** (3 × 2 = 6).
- Formula: **Al₂O₃**.

**Check:** Al: 2 × 3 = 6; O: 3 × 2 = 6 ✅.

**Example 8 (Variable valency — FeCl₂ and FeCl₃):** How many chlorine atoms combine with 1 iron atom when iron has valency 2? What about when iron has valency 3?

- When Fe has valency 2, it matches **2 Cl atoms** (valency 1 each): **FeCl₂**.
- When Fe has valency 3, it matches **3 Cl atoms**: **FeCl₃**.

**Check:** FeCl₂: 1 × 2 = 2 and 2 × 1 = 2 ✅. FeCl₃: 1 × 3 = 3 and 3 × 1 = 3 ✅.

> This method of balancing total combining capacity is the foundation for the **criss-cross shortcut**, which we will master in Topic 5.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing valency with the number of atoms (subscript).**
   No. Valency is the **combining capacity** of a single atom, whereas the subscript in a formula shows **how many atoms** are present in that molecule (such as the 2 in H₂O).

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming every element has only one fixed valency.**
   No. Elements such as Fe, Cu, Hg, Sn, and P exhibit **variable valency** (two different valencies in different compounds).

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking noble gases are highly reactive.**
   The opposite is true. Helium, neon, and argon have a valency of **0** because their atoms do not ordinarily combine with other atoms.

4. **Mistake 4: Expecting to derive the electronic reason for valency right now.**
   Why an atom has a specific valency is explained by its electron configuration in **Chapter 5**. In this chapter, focus on learning the valency table and applying it to build formulas.

5. **Mistake 5: Trying to guess valency from an element's position before studying atomic structure.**
   Wait until Chapter 5 to use electron-shell rules; for now, rely on the grouped valency table in Part B.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking oxygen has valency 1 because its symbol is a single O.**
   No. Oxygen's valency is **2** (one oxygen atom bonds with 2 hydrogen atoms in H₂O). A symbol never tells you the valency by itself.

## Quick Recap

- **Valency** is the combining capacity of an atom — like the number of seats on a bench.
- Hydrogen has a valency of **1** and serves as the standard scale for measuring the valency of other elements.
- Valencies of the first 20 elements: H, Li, Na, K, F, Cl = **1**; Be, Mg, Ca, O, S = **2**; B, Al, N = **3**; C, Si = **4**; P = **3 and 5**; He, Ne, Ar = **0**.
- Everyday metals: Zn = 2, Ag = 1, Pb = 2, Fe = 2 and 3, Cu = 1 and 2, Hg = 1 and 2, Sn = 2 and 4.
- **Variable valency:** Certain elements exhibit more than one valency, forming different compounds each time (e.g., FeCl₂ and FeCl₃).
- In a binary compound, the **total valency of both elements must be equal**: (number of atoms) × (valency).
- Key examples: HCl, H₂O, NH₃, CH₄, MgO, CaCl₂, Al₂O₃.
- The underlying atomic reason for valency will be covered in **Chapter 5**.

## Practice Questions

1. What is valency? Why do we use hydrogen as the standard to measure it?

2. Write the valency of each: Na, Mg, Al, C, Cl, O, N, Ca, F, Ne.

3. How many hydrogen atoms combine with one atom of (a) chlorine, (b) oxygen, (c) nitrogen, (d) carbon?

4. How many atoms of chlorine combine with one atom of (a) sodium, (b) magnesium, (c) aluminium?

5. How many atoms of oxygen combine with one atom of (a) magnesium, (b) calcium, (c) sodium? (Na has valency 1, so think about how many Na atoms fit.)

6. How many atoms of aluminium join with how many atoms of oxygen? Write the formula.

7. Iron shows valency 2 and 3. Write how many chlorine atoms join with one atom of iron in each case.

8. How many hydrogen atoms combine with one atom of sulphur (valency 2)? Write the formula.

9. State True or False and correct the false ones: (a) Helium has valency 2. (b) Carbon has valency 4. (c) A compound can have different total valency on its two sides. (d) The reason for valency is explained in this chapter. (e) Copper has only one valency.

### Answers

1. **Valency** is the combining capacity of an atom — how many single bonds it can form with other atoms. Hydrogen is used as the standard because it is the simplest atom and always has a valency of 1, making it easy to measure other elements by how many hydrogen atoms they combine with.

2. Na = **1**, Mg = **2**, Al = **3**, C = **4**, Cl = **1**, O = **2**, N = **3**, Ca = **2**, F = **1**, Ne = **0**.

3. (a) Cl has valency 1 → **1** hydrogen atom (HCl). (b) O has valency 2 → **2** hydrogen atoms (H₂O). (c) N has valency 3 → **3** hydrogen atoms (NH₃). (d) C has valency 4 → **4** hydrogen atoms (CH₄).

4. (a) Na (valency 1) → **1** chlorine atom (NaCl). (b) Mg (valency 2) → **2** chlorine atoms (MgCl₂). (c) Al (valency 3) → **3** chlorine atoms (AlCl₃).
   Check: 1 × 1 = 1; 2 × 1 = 2; 3 × 1 = 3 ✅.

5. (a) Mg (valency 2) and O (valency 2) → **1** oxygen atom (MgO). (b) Ca (valency 2) and O (valency 2) → **1** oxygen atom (CaO). (c) Na has valency 1 and O has valency 2: one oxygen atom needs 2 connections, so **2 sodium atoms** combine with **1 oxygen atom** (Na₂O), a 2 Na : 1 O ratio.
   Check: In Na₂O, 2 × 1 = 2 and 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

6. Al has valency 3 and O has valency 2. Their least common multiple is 6: Al needs 6 ÷ 3 = **2 atoms**, and O needs 6 ÷ 2 = **3 atoms**. Formula: **Al₂O₃**.
   Check: 2 × 3 = 6 and 3 × 2 = 6 ✅.

7. For valency 2: **2** chlorine atoms (FeCl₂). For valency 3: **3** chlorine atoms (FeCl₃).

8. Sulphur has valency 2, so **2 hydrogen atoms** combine with 1 sulphur atom: **H₂S**.
   Check: 2 × 1 = 2 and 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

9. (a) **False** ❌. Helium has a valency of **0**. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌. The total valency of both sides in a binary compound must be equal. (d) **False** ❌. The electronic reason for valency is explained in Chapter 5. (e) **False** ❌. Copper shows variable valency of 1 and 2.
