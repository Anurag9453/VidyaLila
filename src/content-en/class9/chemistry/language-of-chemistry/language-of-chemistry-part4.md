# Topic 4: Radicals: Groups of Atoms That Travel Together

In Topic 3, we saw that every element has a **combining capacity (valency)** that determines how many atoms bond together. Now we add an exciting twist: in many compounds, atoms do not act alone — instead, they **band together as a team (a bonded group)** that acts as a **single unit** and carries an overall **electrical charge**. In chemistry, such a charged unit is called a **radical** (or ion). Today we will learn the key positive and negative radicals, the simple rules for naming them, and memory tricks to master them quickly.

---

## One Group Ticket for the Shuttle Van

It was Saturday morning at the mountain trailhead station. Mr. Parker and his nephew Noah were standing near the ticket booth. Right ahead of them, five friends — Maya, Liam, Ethan, Sofia, and Leo — were booking a shuttle van up to the scenic overlook.

When the clerk asked, "How many tickets?", the five friends replied, "One group pass for the five of us — we're traveling together as a single team."

Noah whispered, "Uncle Parker, there are five people in that group, yet they have just one group pass? Look, the dispatcher wrote 'Maya Party (5)' as a single unit on the manifest."

Mr. Parker smiled. "That's the key, Noah. For the shuttle dispatcher, all five friends travel as **one inseparable unit**. When they board, all five board together; when they transfer to another van, all five transfer together. Now here's the chemistry connection: certain atoms also **bond together into a tight group and travel on a single 'group ticket'**. They stay together through chemical reactions and combine with other particles as one unit."

"What do chemists call a group like that?" Noah asked.

"A **radical**," Mr. Parker replied. "Come on, let's grab a hot chocolate and meet the most important radicals in chemistry."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Radical** | A charged atom or a bonded group of atoms carrying an overall charge that behaves as a single unit in chemical reactions | Na⁺, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻, NH₄⁺ |
| **Simple radical** | A radical consisting of just a single charged atom | Na⁺, Mg²⁺, Cl⁻, O²⁻ |
| **Compound (polyatomic) radical** | A radical made of two or more bonded atoms that acts as one charged unit | OH⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, NH₄⁺ |
| **Positive radical (basic radical)** | A radical carrying a **positive (+)** charge; also called a cation | Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺ |
| **Negative radical (acid radical)** | A radical carrying a **negative (−)** charge; also called an anion | Cl⁻, OH⁻, CO₃²⁻ |
| **Charge** | The plus or minus sign and number written at the upper right of a radical | 2+ in Ca²⁺, 3− in PO₄³⁻ |

## Part A: What Is a Radical?

In Topic 2, we met **ions**: particles that carry an electrical charge. The terms *ion* and *radical* refer to the same charged particles; chemists often use the word **radical** when treating them as the building blocks of chemical formulas. There are two types of radicals:

1. **Simple radicals:** a single atom carrying a charge, such as Na⁺, Cl⁻, Mg²⁺, O²⁻.
2. **Compound radicals (polyatomic ions):** two or more atoms bonded together that carry **one overall charge for the entire group**, such as SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻, NH₄⁺, OH⁻.

Where does the charge come from? In one sentence: when an atom or group of atoms **loses or gains electrons**, it becomes electrically charged. (We will explore the exact electron transfers in **Chapter 5: "Structure of the Atom"**; for now, treat each radical's charge as its identifying tag.)

- **Losing** electrons leaves a **positive (+)** charge, such as Na⁺.
- **Gaining** electrons gives a **negative (−)** charge, such as Cl⁻.

### One Unit, One Overall Charge

The most important thing to understand about a compound radical is that **the charge belongs to the entire group as a whole, not to any single atom inside it.** In the sulphate radical (SO₄²⁻), 1 sulphur atom and 4 oxygen atoms are bonded into a single package, and the 2− charge belongs to that whole package. Just like the five friends on one group pass, the SO₄ group stays together as a unit.

```
        SO₄ radical (sulphate)

        +-----------------+
        |   O      O      |
        |     \  /        |
        |      S          |   ===>  SO₄²⁻
        |     /  \        |
        |   O      O      |
        +-----------------+

   1 S + 4 O = 5 atoms, acting as 1 single unit
   Overall charge on the whole group: 2 minus
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The sulphate radical (SO₄²⁻): one sulphur atom and four oxygen atoms form a single unit, and the 2− charge belongs to the entire group. In notation, the charge is written as a superscript at the upper right of the group: SO₄²⁻.

Pay close attention to the two numbers in **SO₄²⁻**:
- The **subscript** at the lower right (₄) counts the atoms of that element (4 oxygen atoms).
- The **superscript** at the upper right (²⁻) shows the electrical charge of the whole radical (2 minus).
- When the charge magnitude is 1, we write only the sign (+ or −) without the number 1: Na⁺, Cl⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻.

## Part B: Positive Radicals (Basic Radicals)

Positive radicals carry a **plus (+)** charge. Most positive radicals are formed by **metal** atoms. Two notable non-metal exceptions are **H⁺** (hydrogen) and **NH₄⁺** (ammonium, a compound radical made of nitrogen and hydrogen that carries a 1+ charge).

| Name | Formula | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | H⁺ | 1+ |
| Sodium | Na⁺ | 1+ |
| Potassium | K⁺ | 1+ |
| Silver | Ag⁺ | 1+ |
| Ammonium | NH₄⁺ | 1+ |
| Magnesium | Mg²⁺ | 2+ |
| Calcium | Ca²⁺ | 2+ |
| Zinc | Zn²⁺ | 2+ |
| Copper(I) (cuprous) | Cu⁺ | 1+ |
| Copper(II) (cupric) | Cu²⁺ | 2+ |
| Iron(II) (ferrous) | Fe²⁺ | 2+ |
| Lead | Pb²⁺ | 2+ |
| Aluminium | Al³⁺ | 3+ |
| Iron(III) (ferric) | Fe³⁺ | 3+ |

*(As we saw under variable valency in Topic 3, copper and iron can each form two different ions. The Roman numeral in parentheses tells you the positive charge: Fe²⁺ is iron(II), Fe³⁺ is iron(III), Cu⁺ is copper(I), and Cu²⁺ is copper(II). Older names like ferrous, ferric, cuprous, and cupric still appear on some reagent bottles, but modern chemistry uses the Roman numeral names.)*

Sorting positive radicals by charge makes them much easier to memorize:

| Charge | Radicals |
|---|---|
| 1+ | H⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ag⁺, NH₄⁺, Cu⁺ |
| 2+ | Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺ |
| 3+ | Al³⁺, Fe³⁺ |

## Part C: Negative Radicals (Acid Radicals)

Negative radicals carry a **minus (−)** charge. They consist of non-metal atoms or bonded groups of non-metal atoms (most often containing oxygen).

| Name | Formula | Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Chloride | Cl⁻ | 1− |
| Bromide | Br⁻ | 1− |
| Iodide | I⁻ | 1− |
| Hydroxide | OH⁻ | 1− |
| Nitrate | NO₃⁻ | 1− |
| Hydrogencarbonate (bicarbonate) | HCO₃⁻ | 1− |
| Oxide | O²⁻ | 2− |
| Sulphide | S²⁻ | 2− |
| Sulphate | SO₄²⁻ | 2− |
| Sulphite | SO₃²⁻ | 2− |
| Carbonate | CO₃²⁻ | 2− |
| Phosphate | PO₄³⁻ | 3− |

Here are the negative radicals grouped by charge:

| Charge | Radicals |
|---|---|
| 1− | Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, HCO₃⁻ |
| 2− | O²⁻, S²⁻, SO₄²⁻, SO₃²⁻, CO₃²⁻ |
| 3− | PO₄³⁻ |

In this list, **Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, O²⁻, and S²⁻** are **simple radicals** (only 1 atom each). All the rest — **OH⁻, NO₃⁻, HCO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, SO₃²⁻, CO₃²⁻, and PO₄³⁻** — are **compound radicals** (polyatomic ions).

## Part D: Charge and Valency: The Same Number

In Topic 3, we learned valency as a plain whole number: Na is 1, Mg is 2, Al is 3, Cl is 1, O is 2. Now compare those numbers with the charges on their radicals:

| Element or Radical | Valency (Topic 3) | Radical and Charge |
|---|---|---|
| Na | 1 | Na⁺ (1+) |
| Mg | 2 | Mg²⁺ (2+) |
| Al | 3 | Al³⁺ (3+) |
| Cl | 1 | Cl⁻ (1−) |
| O | 2 | O²⁻ (2−) |

**The number is identical!** Valency tells you the *magnitude* of the combining capacity, while the charge simply adds the **sign** (+ or −) showing whether the radical is positive or negative:

> **Magnitude of charge = valency**, and the sign (+ or −) tells you whether the radical is positive or negative.

Compound radicals work the exact same way: the valency of sulphate (SO₄²⁻) is **2**, the valency of phosphate (PO₄³⁻) is **3**, and the valency of nitrate (NO₃⁻) is **1**. We will use these exact numbers in Topic 5 to build chemical formulas.

## Part E: Rules for Naming Radicals

Chemical nomenclature follows clear, predictable patterns for naming radicals:

**Rule 1: Positive radicals keep the name of the element.** Na⁺ is sodium, Ca²⁺ is calcium, Al³⁺ is aluminium (with Roman numerals added for metals with variable valency, such as iron(II) and iron(III)). The only compound positive radical here is **NH₄⁺**, which is called **ammonium**.

**Rule 2: Simple (single-atom) negative radicals end in "-ide".** Chlorine becomes **chloride** (Cl⁻), bromine becomes **bromide** (Br⁻), iodine becomes **iodide** (I⁻), sulphur becomes **sulphide** (S²⁻), and oxygen becomes **oxide** (O²⁻).

**Rule 3: Oxygen-containing negative radicals end in "-ate" or "-ite".**
- The radical with **more oxygen atoms** ends in **-ate**: **SO₄²⁻** is sulphate, **NO₃⁻** is nitrate, **CO₃²⁻** is carbonate, and **PO₄³⁻** is phosphate.
- When a second radical of the same element has **one fewer oxygen atom**, its name ends in **-ite**: **SO₃²⁻** is sulphite.

**Rule 4: Two important special cases to remember.**
- **OH⁻** contains both oxygen and hydrogen, and its name is **hydroxide** (ending in *-ide*).
- **HCO₃⁻** is formed by attaching one H⁺ to carbonate (CO₃²⁻), so it is called **hydrogencarbonate** (or **bicarbonate**), and its charge is 1− instead of 2−.

```
   Single-atom negative radical -> "-ide"
   Cl⁻   chloride
   S²⁻   sulphide
   O²⁻   oxide

   Oxygen-containing group
   SO₄²⁻   sulphate    (more O atoms: 4)  -> "-ate"
   SO₃²⁻   sulphite    (one fewer O: 3)   -> "-ite"
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Naming pattern for negative radicals: single-atom anions end in "-ide", while oxygen-containing polyatomic anions end in "-ate" (more oxygen) or "-ite" (one fewer oxygen).

## Part F: Worked Examples

**Example 1 (Naming radicals):** Write the name of each radical: (a) SO₄²⁻ (b) NO₃⁻ (c) Ca²⁺ (d) OH⁻ (e) Br⁻.

- (a) SO₄²⁻: oxygen-containing sulphur group with 4 oxygens (more oxygen) → **sulphate**
- (b) NO₃⁻: **nitrate**
- (c) Ca²⁺: positive radical, keeps element name → **calcium**
- (d) OH⁻: oxygen + hydrogen special group → **hydroxide**
- (e) Br⁻: single-atom negative radical → **bromide**

**Check:** All 5 names verified against the reference tables in Parts B and C ✅.

**Example 2 (Counting atoms inside a radical):** How many atoms of each element, and how many atoms in total, are present in one unit of each radical? (a) SO₄²⁻ (b) PO₄³⁻ (c) NH₄⁺ (d) HCO₃⁻ (e) CO₃²⁻

- (a) SO₄²⁻: 1 S + 4 O = **5 atoms**
- (b) PO₄³⁻: 1 P + 4 O = **5 atoms**
- (c) NH₄⁺: 1 N + 4 H = **5 atoms**
- (d) HCO₃⁻: 1 H + 1 C + 3 O = **5 atoms** (three different elements)
- (e) CO₃²⁻: 1 C + 3 O = **4 atoms**

**Check:** Subscripts added carefully (no subscript = 1) ✅. Notice that the upper-right charge (such as ²⁻ or ³⁻) is the electrical charge, *not* an atom count!

**Example 3 (Sorting positive and negative radicals):** Sort this mixed list into positive and negative radicals: **Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺, OH⁻, NH₄⁺, CO₃²⁻, Al³⁺, NO₃⁻, S²⁻, Fe³⁺**.

| Positive radicals | Negative radicals |
|---|---|
| Na⁺, Ca²⁺, NH₄⁺, Al³⁺, Fe³⁺ | Cl⁻, OH⁻, CO₃²⁻, NO₃⁻, S²⁻ |

**Check:** 5 positive radicals + 5 negative radicals = 10 radicals in total ✅.

**Example 4 (Finding valency from charge):** State the valency of each radical: (a) Mg²⁺ (b) Cl⁻ (c) PO₄³⁻ (d) Fe³⁺ (e) OH⁻.

- (a) Mg²⁺: charge magnitude is 2 → valency **2**
- (b) Cl⁻: charge magnitude is 1 → valency **1**
- (c) PO₄³⁻: charge magnitude is 3 → valency **3**
- (d) Fe³⁺: charge magnitude is 3 → valency **3**
- (e) OH⁻: charge magnitude is 1 → valency **1**

**Check:** Mg (2), Cl (1), and Fe(III) (3) match their Topic 3 valencies ✅.

**Example 5 (Sulphate vs sulphite):** Compare SO₃²⁻ and SO₄²⁻. Which is sulphate and which is sulphite? How many total atoms does each have?

- **SO₄²⁻** has more oxygen (4 O atoms) → **sulphate (-ate)**; total atoms = 1 + 4 = 5.
- **SO₃²⁻** has one fewer oxygen (3 O atoms) → **sulphite (-ite)**; total atoms = 1 + 3 = 4.

**Check:** Both carry a 2− charge (valency 2), and their atom count differs by 1 oxygen atom (5 − 4 = 1) ✅.

**Example 6 (Counting multiple compound radicals as units):** Suppose we have **2 nitrate radicals (NO₃⁻)** together. How many N atoms, how many O atoms, and what total charge do they have together?

- One NO₃⁻ unit has 1 N atom, 3 O atoms, and a 1− charge.
- In 2 units: N = 2 × 1 = **2**, O = 2 × 3 = **6**.
- Total charge = 2 × (1−) = **2−**.

**Check:** 2 + 6 = 8 atoms in total, which is 2 × 4 atoms per unit ✅. The entire radical scales as a single unbroken package.

## Part G: Memory Tips and Patterns

1. **The group-ticket rule:** A compound radical is like five friends traveling on one shuttle ticket — it never splits apart during normal formula building. If you need more than one, you multiply the whole group.
2. **Metals are positive:** Na⁺, K⁺, Ag⁺, Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu⁺/Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺, Pb²⁺, and Al³⁺ are all metals and all carry positive charges. The two non-metal positive radicals to remember are **H⁺ and NH₄⁺**.
3. **Charge-3 radicals are rare:** In our tables, only **Al³⁺ and Fe³⁺** are 3+, and only **PO₄³⁻** (phosphate) is 3−. Everything else is 1 or 2!
4. **"-ide" vs "-ate" vs "-ite":** Single-atom anions end in **-ide** (chloride, oxide, sulphide). Oxygen-rich groups end in **-ate** (Think: *"the -ate ion **ate** more oxygen"*), and the version with one less oxygen ends in **-ite** (sulphate SO₄²⁻ vs sulphite SO₃²⁻).
5. **The three 1− compound radicals:** **OH⁻** (hydroxide), **NO₃⁻** (nitrate), and **HCO₃⁻** (hydrogencarbonate) all have a charge of **1−**.
6. **The three 2− compound radicals:** **SO₄²⁻** (sulphate), **SO₃²⁻** (sulphite), and **CO₃²⁻** (carbonate) all have a charge of **2−**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking the 2− charge in SO₄²⁻ belongs only to the oxygen atom.**
   No. In SO₄²⁻, the 2− charge belongs to the **entire SO₄ group** as a unit, not to a single atom inside it.

2. **Mistake 2: Confusing the subscript with the charge superscript.**
   No. The subscript (bottom right, such as ₄ in SO₄²⁻) counts atoms of that element, whereas the superscript (top right, ²⁻) gives the electrical charge of the whole radical.

3. **Mistake 3: Calling OH⁻ "oxide".**
   No. **O²⁻** is oxide (single oxygen atom, 2− charge). **OH⁻** is hydroxide (oxygen + hydrogen group, 1− charge).

4. **Mistake 4: Mixing up sulphate, sulphite, and sulphide.**
   Look at the endings carefully: **sulphide** is S²⁻ (no oxygen), **sulphate** is SO₄²⁻ (4 oxygens), and **sulphite** is SO₃²⁻ (3 oxygens).

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming NH₄⁺ must be negative because nitrogen and hydrogen are non-metals.**
   No. Ammonium (**NH₄⁺**) is a positively charged compound radical (1+).

6. **Mistake 6: Dropping the + or − sign when writing a radical.**
   Always include the sign when writing a standalone radical or ion. Both Na⁺ and Cl⁻ have a valency of 1, but the + and − signs tell you which is the cation and which is the anion.

## Quick Recap

- **Radical:** A charged atom (simple radical) or a bonded group of atoms carrying an overall charge (compound radical) that acts as a single unit in chemical reactions.
- **Positive radicals (basic radicals):**
  - **1+:** H⁺, Na⁺, K⁺, Ag⁺, NH₄⁺, Cu⁺
  - **2+:** Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe²⁺, Pb²⁺
  - **3+:** Al³⁺, Fe³⁺
- **Negative radicals (acid radicals):**
  - **1−:** Cl⁻, Br⁻, I⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, HCO₃⁻
  - **2−:** O²⁻, S²⁻, SO₄²⁻, SO₃²⁻, CO₃²⁻
  - **3−:** PO₄³⁻
- **The magnitude of a radical's charge equals its valency**, while the sign (+ or −) shows whether it is positive or negative.
- **Naming patterns:** Positive radicals keep the element's name (plus NH₄⁺ = ammonium); single-atom negative radicals end in **-ide**; oxygen-containing groups end in **-ate** (more oxygen) or **-ite** (one fewer oxygen); OH⁻ is **hydroxide**.
- Subscripts count atoms inside the radical; superscripts indicate the overall electrical charge.

## Practice Questions

1. Define a radical. Give two examples of a simple radical and two of a compound radical.

2. Write the name and the charge of each: Mg²⁺, K⁺, Zn²⁺, Br⁻, S²⁻, HCO₃⁻.

3. Write the formula (with charge) of each radical: (a) calcium, (b) ammonium, (c) carbonate, (d) phosphate, (e) hydroxide, (f) iron(III).

8. If you take 3 sulphate radicals (SO₄²⁻) together, how many S atoms and how many O atoms are there in all, and what is the total charge?

9. Hydroxide (OH⁻) contains oxygen, yet its name ends in "-ide". Explain this exception. Also say how HCO₃⁻ differs from CO₃²⁻ in name, formula and charge.

10. State True or False, and correct the false ones:
    (a) Cl⁻ is a positive radical.
    (b) NH₄⁺ is a positive radical.
    (c) The charge on SO₄²⁻ is on the sulphur atom only.
    (d) The number in the charge of a radical is equal to its valency.
    (e) OH⁻ is called oxide.

### Answers

1. **Radical:** A charged atom or bonded group of atoms carrying an overall electrical charge that behaves as a single unit in reactions. Simple radicals: Na⁺, Cl⁻ (or Mg²⁺, O²⁻). Compound radicals: SO₄²⁻, NO₃⁻ (or NH₄⁺, OH⁻).

2. Mg²⁺: magnesium, 2+. K⁺: potassium, 1+. Zn²⁺: zinc, 2+. Br⁻: bromide, 1−. S²⁻: sulphide, 2−. HCO₃⁻: hydrogencarbonate (bicarbonate), 1−.

3. (a) **Ca²⁺** (b) **NH₄⁺** (c) **CO₃²⁻** (d) **PO₄³⁻** (e) **OH⁻** (f) **Fe³⁺**.

4. (a) NO₃⁻: 1 N + 3 O = **4 atoms**. (b) HCO₃⁻: 1 H + 1 C + 3 O = **5 atoms**. (c) NH₄⁺: 1 N + 4 H = **5 atoms**. (d) OH⁻: 1 O + 1 H = **2 atoms**.

5. Positive: Ag⁺, Pb²⁺, Fe²⁺, K⁺. Negative: I⁻, SO₄²⁻, O²⁻, PO₄³⁻. Check: 4 + 4 = 8 ✅.

6. Na⁺: **1**. Ca²⁺: **2**. Al³⁺: **3**. Cl⁻: **1**. SO₄²⁻: **2**. PO₄³⁻: **3**.

7. **Sulphate** is **SO₄²⁻** (4 oxygen atoms, ends in *-ate*), whereas **sulphite** is **SO₃²⁻** (3 oxygen atoms — one fewer oxygen, ends in *-ite*). Both carry a 2− charge.

8. In 3 sulphate radicals: S atoms = 3 × 1 = **3**, O atoms = 3 × 4 = **12**, and total charge = 3 × (2−) = **6−**. Check: 1 radical has 5 atoms, so 3 radicals have 15 atoms, and 3 + 12 = 15 ✅.

9. **OH⁻** (hydroxide) contains oxygen and hydrogen but is named with the *-ide* ending as a standard historical exception to memorize. **HCO₃⁻** is hydrogencarbonate (contains 1 H atom, charge **1−**), whereas **CO₃²⁻** is carbonate (no H atom, charge **2−**).

10. (a) **False** ❌. Cl⁻ is a negative radical (chloride). (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌. The 2− charge belongs to the entire SO₄ group. (d) **True** ✅. (e) **False** ❌. OH⁻ is hydroxide; oxide is O²⁻.
