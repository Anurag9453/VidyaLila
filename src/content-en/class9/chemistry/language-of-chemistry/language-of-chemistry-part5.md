# Topic 5: Chemical Formulae: Writing and Naming Using the Criss-Cross Method

In Topic 4, we mastered two essential tables: positive radicals (Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Al³⁺, NH₄⁺...) and negative radicals (Cl⁻, O²⁻, SO₄²⁻, PO₄³⁻...). We also saw that **the magnitude of a radical's charge is its valency**. Now comes the practical question: how do we put these building blocks together to write the **complete chemical formula** of any compound? How many Na⁺ ions pair with one SO₄²⁻ ion? Today we will learn a fast, reliable technique called the **criss-cross method** that lets you write any formula in under 30 seconds — and then work in reverse to name compounds from their formulas.

---

## The Electronics Accessories Shop

On Saturday morning, Ms. Owens brought her nephew Lucas to her electronics shop, *Circuit Hub*. The counter was lined with bins of modular power connectors, pins, and sockets. Lucas picked up a **3-pin connector plug** and tried to snap it into a **2-slot socket**. One pin stuck out in the air.

"Aunt Owens, these two don't match cleanly!" he said.

Ms. Owens smiled. "Every plug has a specific number of **pins**, and every socket has a specific number of **slots**. A connection is only complete when **every single pin has a slot and no slot is left empty**. A 3-pin plug matches a 3-slot socket one-to-one. But what if you are building a modular power strip using 3-pin plugs and 2-slot sockets?"

Lucas thought for a moment. "I can use **more than one of each**! If I take **2** of the 3-pin plugs, that gives 6 pins. And if I take **3** of the 2-slot sockets, that gives 6 slots. Now 6 pins match 6 slots with nothing left over!"

"Spot on," Ms. Owens said. "And that is the exact secret behind writing chemical formulas. Think of the positive radical as a **plug** with pins equal to its positive charge, and the negative radical as a **socket** with slots equal to its negative charge. A valid chemical formula is one where total positive charge equals total negative charge. Let's see how fast you can match them."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Chemical formula** | A symbolic representation of a compound showing which elements or radicals are present and in what ratio | NaCl, CaCO₃ |
| **Subscript** | A small number written at the **lower right** of a symbol or bracket, telling how many atoms or radicals are present | 2 in H₂O; 2 and 3 in Al₂O₃ |
| **Coefficient** | A normal-sized number written **in front of** an entire formula, telling how many formula units or molecules are present | 2NaCl (two units of NaCl) |
| **Criss-cross method** | Writing the charge number of each radical diagonally down as the subscript of the other radical | Al³⁺ and O²⁻ give Al₂O₃ |
| **Brackets (parentheses)** | Used around a compound radical when more than one unit of that radical appears in a formula | Ca(OH)₂ |
| **Lowest whole-number ratio** | Dividing subscripts by their greatest common factor so they are in the simplest whole-number ratio | Writing MgO instead of Mg₂O₂ |

## Part A: What Does a Chemical Formula Tell Us?

A chemical formula is a compact **recipe card** for a compound. At a glance, it tells us:

- **Which elements or radicals** make up the compound.
- **How many** of each atom or radical are present in one formula unit (shown by the subscripts).
- Wherever no subscript is written, the number is understood to be **1**.

Let's read **Ca(OH)₂** carefully:

- **Ca:** 1 calcium ion (no subscript written means 1).
- **(OH)₂:** the subscript 2 outside the bracket means the entire OH⁻ radical appears **2** times.
- Inside one OH group there is 1 O atom and 1 H atom, so in 2 groups there are **2 O atoms and 2 H atoms**.
- Total atoms in one unit of Ca(OH)₂ = 1 + 2 + 2 = **5 atoms**.

**Check:** Ca²⁺ has a charge of 2+, and two OH⁻ ions have a total charge of 2 × (1−) = 2−. Total positive (2) = total negative (2) ✅.

One crucial rule: **never write charges (+ or −) in the final formula of a neutral compound.** Charges are used while working out the ratio, but the finished formula contains only element symbols, brackets (when needed), and subscripts. The compound as a whole is electrically neutral (net charge = 0).

## Part B: The Criss-Cross Method, Step by Step

The criss-cross method turns our "total positive charge = total negative charge" rule into a quick visual shortcut.

**Step 1: Write the symbols of both radicals side by side with their charges.** Always write the **positive radical first** (on the left) and the **negative radical second** (on the right).

**Step 2: Look only at the charge numbers (valencies).** Ignore the + and − signs for a moment.

**Step 3: Criss-cross the numbers.** Write the charge number of the positive radical as the **subscript** of the negative radical, and write the charge number of the negative radical as the **subscript** of the positive radical.

**Step 4: Reduce to the lowest whole-number ratio.** If both subscripts can be divided by a common factor (such as 2 and 2, or 2 and 4), divide them to get the simplest ratio.

**Step 5: Use brackets for multiple compound radicals.** If a **compound radical** (a group of two or more atoms, such as OH, NO₃, SO₄, CO₃, PO₄, or NH₄) gets a subscript of **2 or more**, enclose the radical in **brackets `( )`** and place the subscript outside. Never write a subscript of 1, and never put brackets around a radical that appears only once.

**Step 6: Final charge check.** Verify that (number of positive ions × positive charge) = (number of negative ions × negative charge).

```
      Al  3+              2-  O
            \            /
             \          /
              \        /
               \      /
                \    /
                 \  /
                  \/
                  /\
                 /  \
                /    \
               /      \
              v        v
          Al   2      3   O

           ==>   Al₂O₃
```

> **Figure 5.1** – The criss-cross method: the 3 from Al³⁺ crosses down to become the subscript of O, and the 2 from O²⁻ crosses down to become the subscript of Al, giving Al₂O₃. Charge check: 2 × (3+) = 6+ and 3 × (2−) = 6−, balanced!

## Part C: Worked Formula Examples

In every example below, the positive radical goes first and the negative radical goes second.

**Example 1: Sodium chloride (Na⁺ and Cl⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 1 and 1. Criss-cross gives Na₁Cl₁, and we omit 1s.
- Formula: **NaCl**

**Check:** Positive = 1 × 1 = 1; negative = 1 × 1 = 1 ✅.

**Example 2: Magnesium chloride (Mg²⁺ and Cl⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 2 and 1. Criss-cross puts 1 under Mg and 2 under Cl.
- Formula: **MgCl₂**

**Check:** Positive = 1 × 2 = 2; negative = 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

**Example 3: Aluminium oxide (Al³⁺ and O²⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 3 and 2. Criss-cross gives Al₂O₃.
- Formula: **Al₂O₃**

**Check:** Positive = 2 × 3 = 6; negative = 3 × 2 = 6 ✅.

**Example 4: Magnesium oxide (Mg²⁺ and O²⁻) — reducing to the lowest ratio**

- Charge numbers: 2 and 2. Criss-cross gives Mg₂O₂.
- Divide both subscripts by 2 to get Mg₁O₁.
- Formula: **MgO** (never Mg₂O₂, because ionic formulas are always written in the lowest ratio).

**Check:** Positive = 1 × 2 = 2; negative = 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

**Example 5: Calcium carbonate (Ca²⁺ and CO₃²⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 2 and 2. Criss-cross gives Ca₂(CO₃)₂. Divide both outside subscripts by 2 to get Ca₁(CO₃)₁.
- Since CO₃ appears only once, no brackets are needed.
- Formula: **CaCO₃**

**Check:** Positive = 1 × 2 = 2; negative = 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

**Example 6: Calcium hydroxide (Ca²⁺ and OH⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 2 and 1. Criss-cross puts 1 under Ca and 2 under OH.
- Because the compound radical OH appears **2** times, enclose it in brackets: Ca(OH)₂.
- Formula: **Ca(OH)₂**

**Check:** Positive = 1 × 2 = 2; negative = 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

**Example 7: Potassium carbonate (K⁺ and CO₃²⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 1 and 2. Criss-cross puts 2 under K and 1 under CO₃.
- Since CO₃ appears only once, no brackets are used.
- Formula: **K₂CO₃**

**Check:** Positive = 2 × 1 = 2; negative = 1 × 2 = 2 ✅.

**Example 8: Ammonium sulphate (NH₄⁺ and SO₄²⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 1 and 2. Criss-cross puts 2 under NH₄ and 1 under SO₄.
- Because the compound radical NH₄ appears **2** times, enclose it in brackets: (NH₄)₂. SO₄ appears once, so no brackets around SO₄.
- Formula: **(NH₄)₂SO₄**

**Check:** Positive = 2 × 1 = 2; negative = 1 × 2 = 2 ✅. (Atom count: 2 N, 8 H, 1 S, 4 O = 15 atoms total.)

**Example 9: Iron(III) oxide (Fe³⁺ and O²⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 3 and 2 (Roman numeral III tells us to use Fe³⁺). Criss-cross gives Fe₂O₃.
- Formula: **Fe₂O₃**

**Check:** Positive = 2 × 3 = 6; negative = 3 × 2 = 6 ✅. *(Note: Iron(II) oxide uses Fe²⁺ and O²⁻, which reduces to **FeO** — a different compound.)*

**Example 10: Aluminium sulphate (Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 3 and 2. Criss-cross puts 2 under Al and 3 under SO₄.
- Because SO₄ appears **3** times, enclose it in brackets: (SO₄)₃.
- Formula: **Al₂(SO₄)₃**

**Check:** Positive = 2 × 3 = 6; negative = 3 × 2 = 6 ✅. (Atom count: 2 Al, 3 S, 12 O = 17 atoms total.)

**Example 11: Calcium phosphate (Ca²⁺ and PO₄³⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 2 and 3. Criss-cross puts 3 under Ca and 2 under PO₄.
- Ca is a single-atom ion, so write Ca₃ without brackets. PO₄ is a compound radical appearing twice, so write (PO₄)₂ with brackets.
- Formula: **Ca₃(PO₄)₂**

**Check:** Positive = 3 × 2 = 6; negative = 2 × 3 = 6 ✅. (Atom count: 3 Ca, 2 P, 8 O = 13 atoms total.)

**Example 12: Aluminium hydroxide (Al³⁺ and OH⁻)**

- Charge numbers: 3 and 1. Criss-cross puts 1 under Al and 3 under OH.
- Formula: **Al(OH)₃**

**Check:** Positive = 1 × 3 = 3; negative = 3 × 1 = 3 ✅.

Summary of all 12 worked examples:

| Positive | Negative | After Criss-Cross | Final Formula | Total (+) | Total (−) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na⁺ | Cl⁻ | Na₁Cl₁ | NaCl | 1 | 1 |
| Mg²⁺ | Cl⁻ | Mg₁Cl₂ | MgCl₂ | 2 | 2 |
| Al³⁺ | O²⁻ | Al₂O₃ | Al₂O₃ | 6 | 6 |
| Mg²⁺ | O²⁻ | Mg₂O₂ → divide by 2 | MgO | 2 | 2 |
| Ca²⁺ | CO₃²⁻ | Ca₂(CO₃)₂ → divide by 2 | CaCO₃ | 2 | 2 |
| Ca²⁺ | OH⁻ | Ca₁(OH)₂ | Ca(OH)₂ | 2 | 2 |
| K⁺ | CO₃²⁻ | K₂(CO₃)₁ | K₂CO₃ | 2 | 2 |
| NH₄⁺ | SO₄²⁻ | (NH₄)₂SO₄ | (NH₄)₂SO₄ | 2 | 2 |
| Fe³⁺ | O²⁻ | Fe₂O₃ | Fe₂O₃ | 6 | 6 |
| Al³⁺ | SO₄²⁻ | Al₂(SO₄)₃ | Al₂(SO₄)₃ | 6 | 6 |
| Ca²⁺ | PO₄³⁻ | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ | Ca₃(PO₄)₂ | 6 | 6 |
| Al³⁺ | OH⁻ | Al₁(OH)₃ | Al(OH)₃ | 3 | 3 |

## Part D: Visualizing Charge Balance

Why does the criss-cross method always work? Because it automatically makes the total positive charge equal the total negative charge ("pins = slots"):

```
   Ca(OH)₂ :   Ca²⁺     OH⁻     OH⁻
                (+ +)    (-)     (-)
               2 plus   1 minus  1 minus
               ----------------------
               +2   =   -1 + -1 = -2  (net charge = 0)

   Al₂O₃  :   Al³⁺ Al³⁺          O²⁻ O²⁻ O²⁻
              (+++)(+++)        (--)(--)(--)
              6 plus     =       6 minus   (net charge = 0)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Charge balance: in Ca(OH)₂, the 2+ charge of one Ca²⁺ ion is balanced by two OH⁻ ions (1− each). In Al₂O₃, the 6+ charge of two Al³⁺ ions is balanced by three O²⁻ ions (2− each). Equal positive and negative charges produce an electrically neutral compound.

Always perform Step 6 (the charge check). If your total positive and negative charges do not match, re-check your radical charges and subscripts.

## Part E: Working Backwards: Naming Compounds From Formulae

Reading a formula and writing its chemical name is even simpler!

**Rules for naming ionic compounds:**

1. **Name the positive radical first, followed by the negative radical.**
2. **Do not include subscript counts in the name.** Whether the formula is NaCl, MgCl₂, or AlCl₃, the name is simply *sodium chloride*, *magnesium chloride*, or *aluminium chloride*.
3. Use the radical endings from Topic 4: **-ide** for single-atom anions (chloride, oxide, sulphide), **-ate** for the higher-oxygen group (sulphate SO₄²⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻, phosphate PO₄³⁻), and **-ite** for the lower-oxygen group (sulphite SO₃²⁻).
4. For metals with variable valency (such as Fe and Cu), include the **Roman numeral** in parentheses to show the metal's charge: *iron(II)*, *iron(III)*, *copper(I)*, *copper(II)*. You can deduce the metal's charge from the total negative charge of the anion(s).

| Formula | Charge Balance: (+) = (−) | Chemical Name |
|---|---|---|
| NaCl | 1 = 1 | sodium chloride |
| K₂S | 2 = 2 | potassium sulphide |
| Na₂SO₄ | 2 = 2 | sodium sulphate |
| Na₂SO₃ | 2 = 2 | sodium sulphite |
| KNO₃ | 1 = 1 | potassium nitrate |
| NaHCO₃ | 1 = 1 | sodium hydrogencarbonate |
| Mg(OH)₂ | 2 = 2 | magnesium hydroxide |
| AgNO₃ | 1 = 1 | silver nitrate |
| ZnSO₄ | 2 = 2 | zinc sulphate |
| CuSO₄ | 2 = 2 | copper(II) sulphate |
| Fe(OH)₃ | 3 = 3 | iron(III) hydroxide |

How did we find the Roman numerals in the last two rows?
- In **CuSO₄**, the sulphate ion (SO₄²⁻) has a charge of 2−, and there is 1 copper ion balancing it, so copper must be **Cu²⁺** → **copper(II) sulphate**.
- In **Fe(OH)₃**, three hydroxide ions (OH⁻) give a total negative charge of 3−, so the single iron ion must be **Fe³⁺** → **iron(III) hydroxide**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Forgetting brackets around a compound radical.**
   Writing **CaOH₂** instead of **Ca(OH)₂** is wrong. CaOH₂ would mean 1 Ca, 1 O, and 2 H atoms! Brackets around `(OH)₂` show that the **entire OH group** is doubled. Similarly, write **(NH₄)₂SO₄**, never NH₄₂SO₄.

2. **Mistake 2: Changing subscripts arbitrarily.**
   You cannot change **NaCl** to **NaCl₂** just because you want 2 chlorine atoms. Subscripts are fixed by the charges of the ions; changing a subscript changes the formula into a different (or impossible) compound. If you want *two units* of NaCl, write a **coefficient in front**: **2NaCl** (which we will use extensively in Topic 6).

3. **Mistake 3: Forgetting to reduce subscripts to the lowest whole-number ratio.**
   Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ give **MgO**, not Mg₂O₂. Always divide outside subscripts by any common factor.

4. **Mistake 4: Changing the subscript *inside* a radical's own formula.**
   Never change **(NH₄)₂SO₄** into "(NH₂)₂SO₂" or "NH₄SO₄"! The 4 inside NH₄⁺ and the 4 inside SO₄²⁻ are part of the identity of those radicals and must **never** be divided or altered. You only simplify the *outside* ratio of how many radicals combine (for example, Ca₂(CO₃)₂ simplifies to CaCO₃).

5. **Mistake 5: Leaving charge signs (+ or −) inside the finished formula.**
   Write **NaCl** and **MgCl₂**, never Na⁺Cl⁻ or Mg²⁺Cl⁻₂.

6. **Mistake 6: Being careless with uppercase and lowercase letters.**
   Remember Topic 1: **CO** (carbon and oxygen) and **Co** (cobalt) are completely different. Write every symbol carefully.

## Quick Recap

- A **chemical formula** is a compound's recipe: it shows which elements/radicals are present and in what ratio using subscripts (1 is never written).
- **Criss-cross method:** Write positive radical first and negative second with charges → cross the charge numbers diagonally down as subscripts → simplify to lowest whole-number ratio → put brackets around any compound radical that has a subscript of 2 or more.
- **Charge check:** Total positive charge must equal total negative charge; no charges are written in the final formula.
- Essential formulas to know: NaCl, MgCl₂, MgO, Al₂O₃, CaCO₃, Ca(OH)₂, K₂CO₃, (NH₄)₂SO₄, Fe₂O₃, Al₂(SO₄)₃, Ca₃(PO₄)₂, Al(OH)₃.
- **Naming compounds:** Positive radical name first, negative radical name second (*-ide*, *-ate*, *-ite*), with Roman numerals for variable-valency metals like iron(II/III) and copper(I/II).
- **Subscripts** define the identity of the compound; **coefficients** (written in front) count how many units of the compound are present.

Check: 1 = 1 ✅. (j) Ca²⁺ and S²⁻: Ca₂S₂ reduces to **CaS**. Check: 2 = 2 ✅.

3. (a) Missing brackets around OH: **Ca(OH)₂**. (b) Not reduced to lowest ratio: **MgO**. (c) Al³⁺ needs three Cl⁻ ions: **AlCl₃** (check: 3 = 3 ✅). (d) Missing brackets and subscript 2 on NH₄: **(NH₄)₂SO₄**. (e) Na⁺ and Cl⁻ both have charge 1: **NaCl**.

4. Subscripts are determined by the ionic charges: Na⁺ (1+) and Cl⁻ (1−) balance in a 1 : 1 ratio, so sodium chloride is always **NaCl**. 7. Three sulphate radicals (SO₄²⁻) have a total negative charge of 3 × (2−) = 6−. Since two X ions balance this with 6+, each X ion has a charge of 6 ÷ 2 = **3+** (X³⁺). A real element that fits is **aluminium** (Al₂(SO₄)₃) or **iron(III)** (Fe₂(SO₄)₃).

8. (a) Al₂(SO₄)₃: 2 Al, 3 S, 12 O = **17 atoms**. (b) Ca₃(PO₄)₂: 3 Ca, 2 P, 8 O = **13 atoms**. (c) (NH₄)₂SO₄: 2 N, 8 H, 1 S, 4 O = **15 atoms**. (d) Ca(OH)₂: 1 Ca, 2 O, 2 H = **5 atoms**.

9. **MgCl₂** (2 = 2 ✅), **Mg(OH)₂** (2 = 2 ✅), **Mg(NO₃)₂** (2 = 2 ✅), **MgCO₃** (2 = 2 ✅), **Mg₃(PO₄)₂** (3 × 2 = 6 and 2 × 3 = 6 ✅).

10. (a) **False** ❌. Charges are not written in the final formula of a compound. (b) **True** ✅. Positive radical name first, negative second. (c) **False** ❌. The correct formula is **MgO** (lowest whole-number ratio). (d) **True** ✅. 1 Ca + 2 O + 2 H = 5 atoms. (e) **False** ❌. The subscript inside a radical (such as 4 in SO₄) is part of the radical's identity and must never be changed; only outside subscripts can be simplified.
