# Topic 6: Chemical Equations: The Recipe of a Reaction

In Topic 5, we learned how to combine symbols and valencies/charges to write the **formula** of any compound (NaCl, MgCl₂, Al₂O₃, CaCO₃...). So far, we have learned the **letters** (symbols) and **words** (formulas) of the language of chemistry. Now it is time to put those words together into **complete sentences**. In chemistry, a complete sentence describes a chemical reaction — telling us **which starting substances react and which new substances are formed**. Chemists call this sentence a **chemical equation**. And most importantly, the atom count on both sides of the equation must balance to the last atom.

---

## Scaling a Pudding Recipe Card

The Miller family was hosting a weekend dinner party, and Maya was in the kitchen with her grandmother looking at a handwritten recipe card for rice pudding. The card listed the exact quantities for **4 servings**:

**50 g rice + 1 L milk + 100 g sugar  →  rice pudding (for 4 people)**

Twenty guests were coming to dinner. Maya asked, "Grandma, what if we just multiply the milk by 5 and keep the rice the same?"

Her grandmother laughed. "If you use 5 times the milk with only 50 g of rice, you'll end up with sweet warm milk instead of pudding! A recipe has a **fixed ratio**. To scale a 4-person recipe for 20 people, you multiply **every ingredient by 5**: 250 g rice, 5 L milk, and 500 g sugar."

Mr. Miller looked over and smiled. "Maya, a chemical reaction works just like Grandma's recipe card. Substances react in a **fixed ratio of particles**. And just as you keep the recipe in balance by scaling whole batches of ingredients, chemists balance a reaction by counting atoms on both sides of the arrow. Let's see how it works."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reactants** | The starting substances that undergo a chemical reaction (written on the left side of the arrow) | Hydrogen and oxygen |
| **Products** | The new substances formed as a result of the reaction (written on the right side of the arrow) | Water |
| **Word equation** | A reaction written using the everyday names of substances | hydrogen + oxygen → water |
| **Skeleton (unbalanced) equation** | A reaction written with correct chemical formulas, before atom counts are balanced | H₂ + O₂ → H₂O |
| **Balanced equation** | A chemical equation in which the number of atoms of each element is equal on both sides of the arrow | 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O |
| **Coefficient** | A number placed **in front of** a formula showing how many molecules or formula units take part | The 2 in 2H₂O |
| **State symbols** | Abbreviations in parentheses showing physical state: **(s)** solid, **(l)** liquid, **(g)** gas, **(aq)** aqueous (dissolved in water) | H₂O(l) |
| **Balancing by inspection (trial and error)** | Adjusting coefficients step by step until every element has equal atom counts on both sides | The method used in this topic |

## Part A: From Word Equation to Skeleton Equation

We start by describing a chemical reaction in plain words: **reactants** on the left, **products** on the right, and an arrow (**→**, read as *"yields"* or *"forms"*) in the middle.

**Word equation:**

hydrogen + oxygen → water

Next, we replace each name with its correct chemical formula (remember from Topic 2 that hydrogen and oxygen gases are diatomic, **H₂** and **O₂**, and water is **H₂O**):

**Skeleton equation:**

H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Every formula in this skeleton equation is chemically correct, yet something is wrong with the arithmetic. Count the atoms on each side: on the left there are **2 hydrogen atoms** and **2 oxygen atoms**, while on the right there are **2 hydrogen atoms** and only **1 oxygen atom**. One oxygen atom seems to have vanished into thin air! That cannot happen in a real chemical reaction.

---

## Part B: Accounting for Atoms: Nothing Disappears

During a chemical reaction, atoms are **neither created nor destroyed**. They simply **rearrange their partnerships** — just like dancers on a dance floor who switch partners while the total number of dancers in the room stays the same. Therefore, **the number of atoms of each element must be identical on the left and right sides of the arrow.** (In the next chapter, *Atoms and Molecules*, we will meet the formal law behind this rule — the Law of Conservation of Mass.)

Look at the atom count for the unbalanced skeleton equation H₂ + O₂ → H₂O:

```
   H₂  +  O₂   ──►   H₂O

   Left side (Reactants)  Right side (Products)
   +---------------+      +---------------+
   │ H H    O O    │      │ H O H         │
   +---------------+      +---------------+
   H = 2, O = 2           H = 2, O = 1
                 ^
            O is NOT balanced!
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Atom-counting boxes for the unbalanced skeleton equation H₂ + O₂ → H₂O: the left side has O = 2, while the right side has O = 1. Because an oxygen atom cannot simply vanish, the equation must be balanced.

To fix the count, we **never alter the formulas themselves** — instead, we place **coefficients in front of the formulas**. Let's see how.

---

## Part C: Balancing Equations by Inspection (Trial and Error)

**The Golden Rule of Balancing:** Placing a coefficient in front of a formula scales the number of molecules just like scaling Grandma's pudding recipe: writing **2H₂O** means **2 complete molecules of water**, each still having the formula H₂O. You must **never change a subscript** (changing H₂O to H₂O₂ changes water into hydrogen peroxide, a completely different substance!).

**Step-by-step balancing strategy:**

1. Write the **skeleton equation** using the correct formula for every reactant and product.
2. Count the atoms of each element on both sides of the arrow.
3. Leave any element that appears in multiple formulas (often oxygen or hydrogen) for **last**. Start by balancing elements that appear in only one formula on each side.
4. Place a **coefficient** in front of a formula to match the atom count, then **recount all elements** affected by that coefficient.
5. Make sure the final coefficients are in the **smallest whole-number ratio** (for instance, reduce 4, 2, 4 to 2, 1, 2).
6. **Final check:** Count every element on both sides one last time to verify.

### Example 1: H₂ + O₂ → H₂O

Initial count: Left: H = 2, O = 2. Right: H = 2, O = 1.

- To balance O, put a coefficient of **2** in front of H₂O on the right: H₂ + O₂ → **2**H₂O. Now the right side has O = 2, and H = 2 × 2 = 4.
- To balance H, put a coefficient of **2** in front of H₂ on the left: **2**H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.

**Balanced equation:** 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O

Check: H: left 2 × 2 = 4, right 2 × 2 = 4 ✅. O: left 2, right 2 × 1 = 2 ✅.

```
   2H₂  +  O₂   ──►   2H₂O

   Left side (Reactants)         Right side (Products)
   +-------------------+        +-------------------+
   │ H H   H H   O O   │        │ H O H     H O H   │
   +-------------------+        +-------------------+
   H = 4, O = 2                 H = 4, O = 2
                    Balanced on both sides!
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Atom-counting boxes for the balanced equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O: both sides have H = 4 and O = 2. The exact same atoms are present before and after; only their bonding partnerships have changed.

### Example 2: Mg + O₂ → MgO

Initial count: Left: Mg = 1, O = 2. Right: Mg = 1, O = 1.

- Balance O first by putting **2** in front of MgO: Mg + O₂ → **2**MgO. Now the right side has O = 2 and Mg = 2.
- Balance Mg by putting **2** in front of Mg on the left.

**Balanced equation:** 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO

Check: Mg: 2 = 2 ✅. O: 2 = 2 ✅.

### Example 3: Fe + O₂ → Fe₂O₃

Initial count: Left: Fe = 1, O = 2. Right: Fe = 2, O = 3.

Notice that oxygen comes in pairs on the left (**O₂**, multiples of 2) and in threes on the right (**Fe₂O₃**, multiples of 3). Whenever an element has **2** on one side and **3** on the other, use their **least common multiple (LCM)**: LCM(2, 3) = **6**.

- To get O = 6 on the left, write **3**O₂ (3 × 2 = 6).
- To get O = 6 on the right, write **2**Fe₂O₃ (2 × 3 = 6).
- Now the right side has Fe = 2 × 2 = 4, so put **4** in front of Fe on the left.

**Balanced equation:** 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃

Check: Fe: 4 = 2 × 2 = 4 ✅. O: 3 × 2 = 6 = 2 × 3 = 6 ✅.

### Example 4: N₂ + H₂ → NH₃

Initial count: Left: N = 2, H = 2. Right: N = 1, H = 3.

- Balance N first by writing **2**NH₃ on the right. Now the right side has N = 2 and H = 2 × 3 = 6.
- Balance H by writing **3**H₂ on the left (3 × 2 = 6).

**Balanced equation:** N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Check: N: 2 = 2 × 1 = 2 ✅. H: 3 × 2 = 6 = 2 × 3 = 6 ✅.

### Example 5: CH₄ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Initial count: Left: C = 1, H = 4, O = 2. Right: C = 1, H = 2, O = 3 (2 in CO₂ + 1 in H₂O).

Helpful tip: **Save O for last**, because oxygen appears in two different products on the right.

- C is already balanced (1 = 1).
- Balance H: put **2** in front of H₂O on the right (H = 2 × 2 = 4). Now count total O on the right: 2 (from CO₂) + 2 (from 2H₂O) = **4**.
- Balance O: put **2** in front of O₂ on the left (2 × 2 = 4).

**Balanced equation:** CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O

Check: C: 1 = 1 ✅. H: 4 = 2 × 2 = 4 ✅. O: 2 × 2 = 4 = 2 + 2 = 4 ✅.

### Example 6: Zn + HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Initial count: Left: Zn = 1, H = 1, Cl = 1. Right: Zn = 1, Cl = 2, H = 2.

- Balance Cl by putting **2** in front of HCl on the left. Now the left side has Cl = 2 and H = 2.
- Check H: 2 on the left and 2 on the right — already balanced!

**Balanced equation:** Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂

Check: Zn: 1 = 1 ✅. H: 2 = 2 ✅. Cl: 2 = 2 ✅.

### Example 7: CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂ (on heating)

Initial count: Left: Ca = 1, C = 1, O = 3. Right: Ca = 1, C = 1, O = 1 + 2 = 3.

Look closely — **it is already balanced!** Not every skeleton equation needs coefficients greater than 1. Always count the atoms first before changing anything.

**Balanced equation:** CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂

Check: Ca: 1 = 1 ✅. C: 1 = 1 ✅. O: 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 ✅.

### Example 8: Al + O₂ → Al₂O₃

Initial count: Left: Al = 1, O = 2. Right: Al = 2, O = 3.

Just like Fe + O₂, oxygen has 2 on the left and 3 on the right, so use LCM(2, 3) = **6**:

- Put **3** in front of O₂ on the left (O = 6) and **2** in front of Al₂O₃ on the right (O = 6).
- Now the right side has Al = 2 × 2 = 4, so put **4** in front of Al on the left.

**Balanced equation:** 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃

Check: Al: 4 = 2 × 2 = 4 ✅. O: 3 × 2 = 6 = 2 × 3 = 6 ✅.

> **Watch out:** If someone writes "2Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃", aluminium is unbalanced (2 on the left vs 4 on the right). Recounting every element at the end catches that error immediately!

### Example 9 (Challenge): C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Propane (C₃H₈) is a common fuel gas used in camping stoves and barbecues. Even though this equation looks big, our step-by-step rule solves it cleanly.

Initial count: Left: C = 3, H = 8, O = 2. Right: C = 1, H = 2, O = 3.

- **Step 1 (Balance C):** Left has 3 C, so write **3**CO₂ on the right. Now right has C = 3, O = 3 × 2 = 6.
- **Step 2 (Balance H):** Left has 8 H, so write **4**H₂O on the right (4 × 2 = 8). Now total O on the right = 6 (from 3CO₂) + 4 (from 4H₂O) = **10**.
- **Step 3 (Balance O last):** Right has 10 O, so write **5**O₂ on the left (5 × 2 = 10).

**Balanced equation:** C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Check: C: 3 = 3 ✅. H: 8 = 4 × 2 = 8 ✅. O: 5 × 2 = 10 = (3 × 2) + (4 × 1) = 10 ✅.

Notice how effortless that was once we saved oxygen (which appears in two products) for the final step!

## Part D: What You Can Change and What You Cannot

```
   2H₂  +  O₂   ──►   2H₂O

   Coefficients (2, 1, 2): CAN be changed to balance atoms
   Subscripts (₂ in H₂, ₂ in O₂, ₂ in H₂O): MUST NEVER be changed
```

> **Figure 6.3** – When balancing a chemical equation, you may only adjust the coefficients in front of formulas; subscripts are fixed parts of each substance's chemical formula.

If you tried to balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O by changing the right side to **H₂O₂**, the atom count would match, but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide — a bleaching and antiseptic agent, not drinking water! **Never touch subscripts when balancing an equation.**

## Part E: State Symbols and Reaction Conditions

Chemists often add extra details to make an equation even more informative:

- **State symbols**, written in parentheses right after each formula:
  - **(s)** = solid
  - **(l)** = liquid
  - **(g)** = gas
  - **(aq)** = aqueous solution (dissolved in water)

For example, writing Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂ with state symbols gives:

**Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)**

This tells us at a glance that solid zinc reacts with aqueous hydrochloric acid to form aqueous zinc chloride and bubbles of hydrogen gas.

Similarly: **2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l)** and **CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)**.

- **Reaction conditions** (such as heat, temperature, pressure, or a catalyst) are written **above or below the arrow**:

```
   CaCO₃(s)   ──heat──►   CaO(s) + CO₂(g)

   N₂(g) + 3H₂(g)   ──catalyst──►   2NH₃(g)
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Reaction conditions written above the arrow: the first reaction requires heating (sometimes written with the triangle symbol Δ), and the second uses a catalyst (a substance that speeds up the reaction without being used up). Both equations are fully balanced.

## Part F: What an Equation Tells Us — and What It Does Not

**A balanced chemical equation tells us:**

1. **Which substances are reactants and which are products** (e.g., Zn and HCl react to form ZnCl₂ and H₂).
2. **The exact ratio of molecules (or atoms/formula units)** taking part (e.g., 2 molecules of H₂ react with 1 molecule of O₂ to form 2 molecules of H₂O).
3. **That every atom is conserved** on both sides of the reaction.
4. **The physical state of each substance**, when state symbols (s), (l), (g), and (aq) are included.

**A balanced chemical equation does NOT tell us:**

1. **How fast the reaction occurs.** One reaction might finish in a millisecond, while another (like the rusting of iron) takes months or years.
2. **Whether the reaction actually takes place under ordinary conditions** just because the equation can be balanced on paper.
3. **How much heat is released or absorbed** (unless thermal data is added separately).
4. **How many grams of each substance react** — until we connect atoms to their atomic masses in the next chapter (*Atoms and Molecules*).

*(We will study the different **types of chemical reactions** — combination, decomposition, displacement, and double displacement — along with heat changes in **Chapter 7: "Chemical Changes and Reactions"**.)*

## Part G: Scaling an Equation Like a Recipe

Just like Maya's rice pudding recipe, a balanced chemical equation stays balanced whenever you multiply **every coefficient by the same factor**.

**Example 10:** Multiply the balanced equation 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O by 5. Is it still balanced?

- Scaled equation: **10**H₂ + **5**O₂ → **10**H₂O.
- Check: H: 10 × 2 = 20 on the left, 10 × 2 = 20 on the right ✅. O: 5 × 2 = 10 on the left, 10 × 1 = 10 on the right ✅.

The ratio of molecules remains 2 : 1 : 2 (since 10 : 5 : 10 simplifies to 2 : 1 : 2). By convention, we normally write the **smallest whole-number coefficients** (2, 1, 2), unless we are tracking a specific larger batch of molecules.

**Example 11:** The rice pudding recipe for 4 people uses 50 g rice + 1 L milk + 100 g sugar. How much of each ingredient is needed for **12 people**?

- Scaling factor = 12 ÷ 4 = **3**.
- Rice: 50 × 3 = **150 g**. Milk: 1 × 3 = **3 L**. Sugar: 100 × 3 = **300 g**.
- Check: The ratio of rice : milk : sugar was 50 : 1000 : 100 = 1 : 20 : 2 (in g and mL), and 150 : 3000 : 300 also simplifies to 1 : 20 : 2 ✅.

A balanced chemical equation works the exact same way: its coefficients lock in a fixed ratio that holds true whether you react a few molecules or trillions of molecules.

Across the six topics of this chapter, we built the entire language of chemistry from the ground up — from **letters** (symbols) to **words** (formulas) to **sentences** (balanced equations):

```
   SYMBOL           FORMULA            EQUATION
   (letter)         (word)             (sentence)

     H, O           H₂O, CO₂         2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
      │                │                    │
      +-> valency ---> radical ---> criss-cross ---> balance
```

> **Figure 6.5** – The journey through the chapter: symbols combine via valency and radicals using the criss-cross method to form formulas, and formulas are arranged and balanced to form chemical equations.

| Topic | Focus | Key Takeaway in One Line |
|---|---|---|
| Topic 1 | Symbols | Every element has an international 1- or 2-letter symbol (first letter uppercase, second lowercase, some from Latin like Na, Fe, Cu) representing one atom of that element. |
| Topic 2 | Atoms, Molecules, and Ions | An atom is the smallest particle of an element, a molecule is a bonded group of atoms (H₂, O₂, H₂O) with a definite atomicity, and an ion carries an electrical charge. |

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Changing subscripts to balance an equation.**
   Never balance H₂ + O₂ → H₂O by writing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂. Changing a subscript changes the substance itself; only adjust **coefficients** in front of formulas.

2. **Mistake 2: Balancing one element and forgetting to recount the others.**
   Putting a 2 in front of MgO in Mg + O₂ → 2MgO balances O, but also changes Mg on the right to 2! Always recount all elements after adding a coefficient: **2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO**.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing a coefficient with a subscript.**
   **2H₂O** means two water molecules (4 H atoms and 2 O atoms in total), whereas H₂O₂ is a single molecule of hydrogen peroxide.

4. **Mistake 4: Leaving coefficients unsimplified.**
   While 4H₂ + 2O₂ → 4H₂O is mathematically balanced, standard chemical equations are written in the **lowest whole-number ratio**: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming a balanced equation guarantees a fast reaction.**
   Balancing only ensures atom conservation; it does not tell you how fast a reaction occurs or what conditions are required to start it.

6. **Mistake 6: Adding coefficients to an equation that is already balanced.**
   Always count atoms first! Equations like **CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂** are already balanced with coefficients of 1.

## Quick Recap

- A **word equation** writes a reaction using substance names; a **skeleton equation** replaces names with chemical formulas before balancing.
- Because atoms are neither created nor destroyed in a reaction, **every element must have the same number of atoms on both sides of the arrow**.
- **Balancing by inspection:** Count atoms on both sides, adjust **coefficients** only (never subscripts!), save elements that appear in multiple formulas (like O) for last, use LCMs (such as 6 for 2 and 3 in Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃), and do a final atom count check.
- Key balanced equations from this topic:
  - 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O
  - 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO
  - 4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃
  - N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃
  - CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O
  - Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂
  - CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂
  - 4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃
  - C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O
- **State symbols:** **(s)** solid, **(l)** liquid, **(g)** gas, **(aq)** aqueous. **Conditions** (such as *heat* or *catalyst*) are written above or below the arrow.
- An equation shows reactants, products, and particle ratios, but does not show reaction speed.

2. (a) **2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO** (Check: Mg 2 = 2 ✅, O 2 = 2 ✅). (b) **N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃** (Check: N 2 = 2 ✅, H 6 = 6 ✅). (c) **Zn + 2HCl → ZnCl₂ + H₂** (Check: Zn 1 = 1 ✅, H 2 = 2 ✅, Cl 2 = 2 ✅).

3. (a) **4Fe + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂O₃** (Check: Fe 4 = 4 ✅, O 6 = 6 ✅). (b) **4Al + 3O₂ → 2Al₂O₃** (Check: Al 4 = 4 ✅, O 6 = 6 ✅). On the left, oxygen atoms come in multiples of 2 (from O₂), while on the right they come in multiples of 3 (from O₃ in Fe₂O₃ and Al₂O₃). The smallest number divisible by both 2 and 3 is their LCM, **6**.

4. **CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O** (Check: C 1 = 1 ✅, H 4 = 4 ✅, O 4 = 4 ✅). Balancing oxygen last is smart because oxygen appears in two separate formulas on the right (CO₂ and H₂O); once C and H are fixed, the total oxygen count on the right is fixed and easy to match on the left.

5. **C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O**. Before balancing (skeleton): Left has C = 3, H = 8, O = 2; Right has C = 1, H = 2, O = 3. After balancing: Left has C = 3, H = 8, O = 5 × 2 = 10; Right has C = 3, H = 4 × 2 = 8, O = (3 × 2) + 4 = 10 ✅.

6. H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, not water! Changing a subscript alters the chemical identity of the product. To balance an equation, you may only place coefficients in front of formulas: **2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O**.

7. Atom count: Ca: 1 = 1, C: 1 = 1, O: 3 = 1 + 2 = 3 — **yes, it is already balanced** ✅. With state symbols and condition: **CaCO₃(s) ──heat──► CaO(s) + CO₂(g)**.

8. **Zn(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂(g)**. **(s)** shows zinc is a solid, **(aq)** shows HCl and ZnCl₂ are dissolved in water (aqueous solutions), and **(g)** shows hydrogen escapes as a gas.

9. (a) **True** ✅. (b) **False** ❌. You may only change coefficients; changing subscripts changes the substance itself. (c) **False** ❌. A balanced equation does not indicate reaction speed. (d) **True** ✅. 2 × H₂O gives 2 × 2 = 4 H atoms and 2 × 1 = 2 O atoms.

10. Multiplying by 3 gives **6H₂ + 3O₂ → 6H₂O** (Check: H 12 = 12 ✅, O 6 = 6 ✅). For the pudding recipe: 16 ÷ 4 = 4 batches, so we need 50 × 4 = **200 g rice**, 1 × 4 = **4 L milk**, and 100 × 4 = **400 g sugar**.
