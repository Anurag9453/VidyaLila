# Topic 2: Solids, Liquids, and Gases: Understanding the Three States of Matter

In Topic 1 we concluded that all matter is built from tiny **particles**. Those particles are microscopic, have empty spaces between them, move ceaselessly, and pull on one another through **forces of attraction**. That raises a natural question: if everything is made of particles like these, why is a stone hard and rigid, why does water pour and splash, and why is air completely invisible and compressible? In this topic, we will use our **particle picture** to understand the three classic states of matter.

---

## Three Objects at a Sunday Street Fair

One Sunday afternoon, Ms. Ross took her nephew Leo to a neighborhood street fair. Leo usually found textbook science a bit dry, but everyone loves a fair.

First they passed a **balloon vendor** filling bright balloons from a tall metal helium tank. Next they stopped at a **picnic table**, where Ms. Ross unpacked a **stainless-steel lunchbox** filled with warm soup. Finally, Leo pulled out his **water bottle** and took a long drink.

Ms. Ross pointed at the table. "Leo, right now you are holding three things: the steel lunchbox, the water in your bottle, and the gas inside that balloon. Without using any textbook jargon, tell me—how do those three behave differently?"

Leo thought about it. "Well, the steel lunchbox is **rigid**—it keeps its own shape no matter where I put it. The water changes shape to match the bottle or my cup. And the gas inside the balloon spreads out to fill the entire balloon."

"Congratulations—you just described the three states of matter," Ms. Ross laughed. "Now let's figure out **why** they behave so differently: which ones keep their shape, which ones can be squeezed into a smaller space, and which ones flow. Every single difference comes down to how their particles are arranged."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **State of matter** | The physical form in which matter exists: solid, liquid, or gas | Steel (solid), water (liquid), air (gas) |
| **Solid** | A state of matter with a fixed shape and a fixed volume | Steel lunchbox, rock, book |
| **Liquid** | A state of matter with a fixed volume but no fixed shape; it takes the shape of its container | Water, milk, cooking oil |
| **Gas** | A state of matter with neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume; it expands to fill its entire container | Air, helium, cooking gas (LPG) |
| **Volume** | The amount of three-dimensional space an object occupies | 1 liter of water |
| **Compressibility** | How much a substance's volume can be reduced by squeezing or applying pressure | Gases are highly compressible; solids are almost incompressible |
| **Rigidity** | The tendency of a solid to keep its shape and resist bending or deforming | A steel ruler or stone block |
| **Fluidity** | The ability to flow. Both liquids and gases can flow, so both are called **fluids** | Water and air |
| **Density** | How much mass is packed into a given volume (**density = mass ÷ volume**) | Iron has a much higher density than air |
| **Diffusion** | The automatic mixing of particles of one substance through another (Topic 1) | Scent of a candle spreading through air |

## Part A: The Particle Map of the Three States

In Topic 1 we built a mental picture of particles. Now let us see how those particles are arranged in solids, liquids, and gases. Remember that two opposing factors are always competing inside matter: **interparticle attraction** (which tries to pull particles tightly together) and **particle motion** (which tries to make particles break free and fly apart). Whichever side wins determines the state of matter!

```
   SOLID: steel lunchbox       LIQUID: bottled water        GAS: air in a balloon

   ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●            ●  ● ●   ●  ●               ●             ●
   ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●             ● ●  ●●  ●                      ●
   ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●            ●  ●  ●  ● ●                          ●
   ●  ●  ●  ●  ●  ●             ●●  ●   ●                ●
                                                                    ●     ●
   packed in neat rows,        closely packed, but          very far apart,
   locked in place,            able to slide past           zipping around rapidly
   vibrating on the spot       one another                  in all directions
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Particle map of the three states. In a solid, particles are packed closely in a regular pattern and vibrate in fixed positions. In a liquid, particles are still close together with no regular pattern, sliding past one another. In a gas, particles are very far apart and move rapidly at random.

Notice two crucial details in this diagram:

- **In both solids and liquids, particles are packed almost equally close together.** There is very little empty space between them. That is why both solids and liquids have a fixed volume and cannot be easily compressed.
- **In a gas, particles are separated by huge distances.** The empty space between gas particles is **many times larger than the particles themselves**. That is why a gas can expand to fill any room or be squeezed into a small tank.

In the Physics chapter on Heat, you saw how raising the temperature makes particles move faster. Here we hold the temperature steady and ask: **at the same room temperature**, how do the three states compare, and why?

### How Do Particles Move in Each State?

| State | Particle Arrangement | Particle Motion | Force of Attraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Solid** | Regular, tightly packed | Vibrate back and forth in fixed positions; cannot leave their spots | Very strong |
| **Liquid** | Closely packed, but random (no regular rows) | Slide and roll past one another | Moderate—strong enough to keep particles together, weak enough to let them slide |
| **Gas** | Very far apart, completely random | Fly rapidly in straight lines until they collide and bounce off | Negligible (almost zero) |

(Remember, these "particles" may be individual atoms or molecules—a distinction we will explore in the chapter *Atoms and Molecules*.)

## Part B: Shape and Volume: What Changes and What Stays Fixed?

### Solids: Fixed Shape and Fixed Volume

Set a steel lunchbox on a table, on the floor, or inside a backpack—its shape never changes. Why? Because its particles are **locked in place** by strong forces of attraction. No particle can break away to flow somewhere else. That is why a solid is **rigid**.

### Liquids: Variable Shape, Fixed Volume

Suppose you have **1 liter (1,000 mL) of water**. Watch what happens when you pour it into different containers:

| Container | What Happens |
|---|---|
| Tall, narrow bottle | The water takes a tall, narrow column shape |
| Wide, shallow tray | The water spreads out into a wide, thin layer |
| Four small cups (250 mL each) | The water divides evenly among the four cups |

In all three cases, the **shape is completely different**, yet the **total volume is still 1,000 mL**.

**Check:** Four cups × 250 mL per cup = **1,000 mL** ✅

**Why?** Liquid particles can slide over one another, so gravity pulls them down to match the shape of whatever container holds them. However, because the force of attraction still keeps the particles close together, the average distance between particles does not change—so the total volume stays fixed.

### Gases: Neither Fixed Shape Nor Fixed Volume

For a gas, both shape and volume depend entirely on the container. Inside a round balloon, helium takes the shape and volume of the balloon; inside a bicycle tire, air takes the shape and volume of the tire; released into a bedroom, it spreads out to fill the entire room. Why? Gas particles move **at high speeds** and experience almost no attraction toward one another. With nothing holding them back, they keep spreading out until they hit the walls of their container. Therefore, **a gas always expands to fill the entire volume of whatever closed container it is in**.

```
   Same 1 L of water in 2 containers       Same amount of gas in 2 containers

   ┌──┐     ┌──────────┐                     ┌────────┐     ┌────────────────┐
   │  │     │          │                     │ ●   ●  │     │ ●      ●     ● │
   │~~│     │          │                     │   ●  ● │     │    ●      ●    │
   │~~│     │~~~~~~~~~~│                     │ ●   ●  │     │ ●      ●     ● │
   └──┘     └──────────┘                     └────────┘     └────────────────┘
   bottle   wide tray                        small box      large box
   volume = volume =                         the gas fills both containers
   1 liter  1 liter                          completely!
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Left: A liquid takes the shape of its container while keeping the same volume. Right: A gas spreads out to fill the entire volume of any closed container. (Not drawn to scale.)

Here is a one-line memory aid:

> **Solid:** fixed shape, fixed volume. **Liquid:** container's shape, fixed volume. **Gas:** container's shape, container's volume.

## Part C: Compressibility (Can You Squeeze It?)

### The Syringe Experiment—With Numbers

In Topic 1 we tried squeezing air and water inside a needle-free plastic syringe. Now let us look at that experiment quantitatively across all three states:

- **Syringe filled with air:** When you block the nozzle and push the plunger, it moves a long way inward. Suppose you start with **100 mL** of air and push the plunger until the air occupies only **25 mL**. The exact same number of air particles are now packed into one-fourth the volume—meaning they are **four times closer together** on average. (Your exact reading depends on how hard you push.)
- **Syringe filled with water:** Block the nozzle and push. The plunger barely moves at all, even when you press hard.
- **Solid (e.g., a wooden dowel or stone):** You cannot squeeze a solid block into a smaller volume by hand at all.

```
   Before pushing (gas)          After pushing (compressed gas)

   ┌──────────────────┐         ┌─────────┐
   │ ●    ●   ●    ●  │ ◀── P   │ ●●●● ●  │ ◀── P
   │   ●      ●    ●  │         │ ● ●●  ● │
   │ ●     ●     ●    │         └─────────┘
   └──────────────────┘          particles are closer together;
    particles far apart           only the empty space shrank!
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Compressing a gas inside a syringe reduces the empty space between particles. The particles themselves do not shrink—they simply move closer together. (P = pressure from the plunger.)

### Why Does This Happen?

- **Gases** have **huge empty spaces** between their particles, so there is plenty of room to push them closer together. Gases have **high compressibility**.
- **Liquids** and **solids** have particles that are already nearly touching. Since there is almost no empty space left to eliminate, liquids are **nearly incompressible**, and solids even more so.

### Real-World Applications: LPG, CNG, and Scuba Tanks

High compressibility of gases is enormously useful in daily life:

- **LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas) cylinders:** Camp stoves, outdoor grills, and many home kitchens use steel cylinders filled with LPG. Because gas can be compressed, a huge volume of fuel gas is squeezed into a compact steel cylinder (in fact, under pressure it turns into a liquid inside the tank, as we will see in Topic 3).
- **CNG (Compressed Natural Gas):** Many city buses and low-emission vehicles run on CNG, which is natural gas compressed to about **200 times normal atmospheric pressure** so that a practical amount of fuel fits in the vehicle's tank.
- **Bicycle and car tires, hospital oxygen cylinders, and scuba diving tanks:** All of these pack large volumes of gas into sturdy, portable containers by compressing the empty space between gas particles.

> Remember: when you compress a gas into a cylinder, the individual gas particles do **not** get smaller—only the empty gaps between them shrink.

## Part D: Fluidity (Flowing) vs. Rigidity (Holding Shape)

- **Solids:** Particles are locked in fixed positions, so a solid **does not flow**—it is **rigid**.
- **Liquids:** Particles slide past one another, so a liquid **flows**. Tilt a pitcher of juice and it pours smoothly into a glass.
- **Gases:** Particles move freely and independently, so a gas **flows** even more easily than a liquid. Wind is simply air flowing from one place to another.

Because **both liquids and gases flow**, scientists group them together under a single word: **fluids**. Whenever you hear the word "fluid" in physics or chemistry, remember it means **both liquids and gases**, not just liquids!

## Part E: Density: How Much Mass Is Packed into a Given Space?

**Density** tells us how tightly matter is packed into a given volume: **density = mass ÷ volume**. The closer the particles are packed (and the heavier each particle is), the higher the density.

Compare the approximate mass contained in **1 liter (1,000 cm³)** of three everyday substances:

| Substance | Approximate Mass of 1 Liter |
|---|---|
| Air (gas) | about 1.2 grams |
| Water (liquid) | 1 kilogram (1,000 grams) |
| Steel / Iron (solid) | about 7.8 kilograms (7,800 grams) |

**Same 1-liter space, vastly different masses!** Because air particles are far apart, 1 liter of air contains relatively few particles and weighs only about 1.2 g. Water particles are packed closely together, so 1 liter of water weighs 1,000 g. Iron particles are packed tightly and each iron particle is heavier, giving 7,800 g per liter.

**Reasoning Example 1:** Roughly how many times denser is liquid water than air?
For the same 1-liter volume, water has a mass of 1,000 g and air has a mass of about 1.2 g:
1,000 ÷ 1.2 ≈ **833**—meaning liquid water is **more than 800 times denser** than air!
**Check:** 1.2 g × 833 ≈ 1,000 g ✅. That huge ratio reflects how far apart gas particles are compared to liquid particles.

As a general rule, **solids have the highest density, liquids have moderate density, and gases have the lowest density** (with one famous exception—ice floating on liquid water—which we will explain in Topic 3).

## Part F: Diffusion Across the Three States

In Topic 1 we learned what diffusion is. Now let us compare how fast diffusion happens in gases, liquids, and solids:

- **Fastest in gases:** Think back to Maya smelling the scented candle across the room in a minute or two. Because gas particles move at high speeds and have huge empty spaces between them, one gas diffuses through another very quickly.
- **Slower in liquids:** A drop of food coloring in a still glass of water takes several minutes (or longer) to spread evenly. Liquid particles move more slowly and are crowded close together, so diffusing particles get bumped and delayed constantly.
- **Extremely slow in solids:** Because solid particles only vibrate in fixed positions, diffusion between two solids at room temperature is practically unobservable in daily life (if two smooth metal blocks are clamped tightly together for years, only a microscopic layer of atoms will cross the boundary).

## Part H: Tricky Everyday Puzzles Explained

### 1. A kitchen sponge can be squeezed easily—why is it still called a solid?

A sponge **is a solid**. However, it is full of **tiny pores (holes) filled with air**. When you squeeze a sponge, you are forcing the **air** out of those holes and bending the thin solid walls around them—you are not compressing the solid material of the sponge itself. When you release your grip, the solid walls spring back and air refills the pores.

### 2. A rubber band changes shape when stretched—is it really a solid?

Yes, a rubber band **is a solid**. It changes shape only while an **outside force** (your fingers pulling it) acts on it. The moment you let go, the strong attractions between its particles pull it right back to its **original fixed shape**. Unlike a liquid, it never pours or takes the shape of a bowl on its own.

### 3. Sugar and salt take the shape of whatever jar you pour them into—are they liquids?

No, both are **solids**. Look closely at a **single tiny crystal (grain)** of sugar or salt under a magnifying glass: that individual crystal has sharp, fixed edges and **never changes its shape**, whether it sits on a spoon or inside a jar. A pile of sugar pours and takes the shape of a jar only because millions of tiny solid grains **slide over one another**, just as a bucket of gravel or marbles would.

### 4. What about candle wax and window glass?

At room temperature, both wax and glass are **solids**—they keep their shape and do not flow.

**Debunking a popular myth:** You may have heard people claim that *"antique cathedral windows are thicker at the bottom because glass is actually a slow-flowing liquid that sagged over centuries."* **That is a myth!** Modern materials science has shown that room-temperature glass would take longer than the age of the universe to flow measurably. Centuries ago, artisans made window panes by spinning or blowing molten glass by hand, which produced sheets of uneven thickness. Builders naturally installed the heavier, thicker edge at the bottom for stability. Glass is a genuine solid.

### 5. Why does a gas fill its entire container?

Because gas particles move at high speeds in all directions and have almost zero attraction pulling them together. Nothing holds them in a puddle at the bottom, so they spread out until the container walls stop them.

### 6. Why does a liquid have no fixed shape, yet a fixed volume?

Its particles can **slide past one another** (so it changes shape to fit the container), but moderate forces of attraction keep the particles **closely packed at a constant average spacing** (so the total volume stays fixed).

### 7. Why can a gas be compressed so much more than a liquid?

Because a gas has **large empty spaces** between its particles that can be squeezed down, whereas a liquid's particles are already nearly touching with almost no empty space left.

## Part I: Solved Examples (Reasoning and Quick Calculations)

**Example 1 (Pouring a liquid):** You have **1.5 liters** of juice and want to pour it equally into **500 mL** bowls. How many bowls will it fill, and what is the volume of juice in each bowl?

- Convert to milliliters: 1.5 liters = 1,500 mL
- Number of bowls = 1,500 mL ÷ 500 mL = **3 bowls**
- Volume in each bowl = **500 mL**

**Check:** 3 × 500 mL = 1,500 mL ✅. Even though the juice takes the wide shape of each bowl, the total volume remains 1,500 mL because **a liquid has a fixed volume**.

**Example 2 (Compressing air in a syringe):** A syringe holds **60 mL** of air. You block the tip and push the plunger until the air occupies **20 mL**. What happened to the particles inside?

- Volume decreased from 60 mL to 20 mL: 60 ÷ 20 = **3 times smaller volume**
- The same number of air particles now occupy one-third the space, so they are packed **3 times more densely**
- The **empty space** between particles shrank; the particles themselves did not shrink

**Check:** 20 mL × 3 = 60 mL ✅. If the syringe had been filled with 60 mL of water, you could not have pushed the plunger down to 20 mL because liquid particles are already closely packed.

**Example 3 (Identifying the state of matter):** Identify the state of matter in each case and give a one-line reason: (a) air inside an inflated bicycle tire, (b) a hardcover book, (c) thick honey pouring slowly from a spoon, (d) a single grain of table salt.

- (a) **Gas**: it is compressed and expands to fill the entire tire tube.
- (b) **Solid**: it has a fixed shape and a fixed volume.
- (c) **Liquid**: even though it flows slowly (high viscosity), it still flows and takes the shape of its container.
- (d) **Solid**: the individual crystal keeps its own fixed shape and volume.

**Check:** Honey flows and takes its container's shape (liquid); a book and a salt crystal do not flow (solid) ✅

## Part J: The Three States Working Together in Daily Life

- **Solids:** Steel beams, bricks, wooden tables, lunchboxes. Because their particles are locked in place, solids keep their shape and support heavy loads.
- **Liquids:** Water, milk, cooking oil, gasoline. Because they flow and take the shape of containers, liquids can be pumped through pipes and stored in bottles or tanks.
- **Gases:** Air (for breathing), LPG (for cooking), CNG (for vehicles), helium (for balloons). Because gases are compressible, large amounts can be stored in compact metal cylinders.
- **In the kitchen:** A steel pot (**solid**) holding simmering soup (**liquid**) over a gas burner fed by an LPG tank (**gas**)—all three states working together at once!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "A sponge is compressible, so it cannot be a solid."**

A sponge is a solid. When you squeeze it, you are expelling **air** from its tiny open pores, not compressing the solid material itself.

**Mistake 2: "Sugar and salt take the shape of a jar, so they are liquids."**

Each individual crystal of sugar or salt is a rigid solid whose shape never changes. Only the bulk pile changes shape as grains slide over one another.

**Mistake 3: "Old window panes are thicker at the bottom because glass is a slow-flowing liquid."**

False. Glass at room temperature is a solid and does not flow. Old panes were uneven because of historic hand-manufacturing methods, and glaziers installed the thicker edge at the bottom.

**Mistake 4: "A gas has a fixed volume."**

No. A gas always expands or contracts to match the exact volume of whatever closed container holds it.

**Mistake 5: "A liquid's volume changes when you pour it into a larger container."**

No. Only the liquid's **shape** changes. One liter of water poured into a 5-liter bucket still occupies only 1 liter at the bottom of the bucket.

**Mistake 6: "Compressing a gas squeezes the individual gas particles into smaller particles."**

No. The particles themselves stay the exact same size; compression only reduces the **empty space** between them.

## Quick Recap

- The three classic states of matter—**solid, liquid, and gas**—differ in how their particles are arranged, how they move, and how strongly they attract one another.
- **Solids:** Particles are tightly packed in a regular arrangement and vibrate in fixed positions. Fixed shape, fixed volume, rigid, nearly incompressible.
- **Liquids:** Particles are closely packed in a random arrangement and slide past one another. Fixed volume, no fixed shape (takes container's shape), fluid, nearly incompressible.
- **Gases:** Particles are very far apart and move rapidly at random. Neither fixed shape nor fixed volume (fills entire container), fluid, highly compressible.
- Both liquids and gases can flow, so both are called **fluids**.
- **Density** (mass ÷ volume) is usually highest in solids, moderate in liquids, and lowest in gases (water is ~800 times denser than air).
- **Diffusion** is fastest in gases, slower in liquids, and extremely slow in solids.
- Sponges (air pores), rubber bands (elastic recovery), sugar/salt crystals (rigid individual grains), wax, and window glass are all **solids**.
