# Topic 3: Changing States of Matter: Interconversion, Temperature, and the Role of Pressure

In Topic 2 we compared the three states of matter side by side: solid particles vibrate in fixed positions, liquid particles stay close together while sliding past one another, and gas particles fly around far apart. At the end of Topic 2, Ms. Ross pointed at a melting snow cone and asked how solid ice turns into liquid water without any new substance being added. Clearly, ice, liquid water, and steam are **not three different substances—they are three states of the exact same substance**. How do we make matter switch from one state to another? What "dials" do we turn? And can some substances skip the liquid state altogether and jump straight from solid to gas? Let us step backstage at a school theater production to find out.

---

## The "Fog Bowl" Backstage at the School Play

Mr. Alvarez took his niece Elena backstage before the opening night of her high school's drama production. Near the stage entrance sat a large metal prop cauldron, and thick **white fog** was pouring over its rim and rolling across the floor like a spooky river. Elena peered into the cauldron from a safe distance: inside sat several white, ice-like pellets, and the stage manager had just poured a cup of warm water over them to make the fog billow even faster.

Elena reached out a hand toward one of the white pellets on a tray, but Mr. Alvarez immediately stopped her. "Wait! Don't touch that—it's **dry ice**. **Never touch dry ice with bare skin, and never put it near your mouth.** It is so freezing cold that it causes instant frostbite, just like a burn. Always observe it from a safe distance."

Elena pulled her hand back and looked closer. "Uncle Carlos, regular ice melts into a puddle of water. Why isn't there a pool of melted dry ice at the bottom of that tray? The white pellets are shrinking and vanishing straight into the air! Where are they going?"

Mr. Alvarez smiled. "Great observation. That cauldron holds the secret to today's entire lesson. Those pellets aren't melting into a liquid at all—they are **turning directly from a solid into a gas**. Today we will map out all six pathways between the states of matter and solve the mystery of the backstage fog."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Interconversion of states** | A substance changing from one state of matter to another and back | Ice melting into water and freezing back into ice |
| **Melting (fusion)** | Changing from a solid to a liquid | Ice melting into water; candle wax melting |
| **Freezing (solidification)** | Changing from a liquid to a solid | Water freezing into ice cubes |
| **Vaporisation** | Changing from a liquid to a gas (vapor) | Water turning into steam |
| **Boiling** | Rapid vaporisation throughout the entire liquid at a fixed temperature | Water boiling in a kettle at 100 °C |
| **Condensation** | Changing from a gas to a liquid | Water droplets forming on the outside of a cold glass |
| **Sublimation** | Changing directly from a solid to a gas without becoming a liquid | Dry ice, camphor, or mothballs (naphthalene) vanishing into gas |
| **Deposition** | Changing directly from a gas to a solid without becoming a liquid | Frost forming on cold grass or a car windshield on a freezing morning |
| **Melting point** | The fixed temperature at which a pure solid melts into a liquid (at atmospheric pressure) | Ice melts at 0 °C |
| **Boiling point** | The fixed temperature at which a pure liquid boils into a gas (at atmospheric pressure) | Water boils at 100 °C |
| **Kelvin (K)** | The SI (scientific) unit of temperature | 0 °C = 273 K |

## Part A: The Six Pathways Between States of Matter

Between the three states of matter—solid, liquid, and gas—there are **six possible transitions**, each with its own scientific name:

| Change of State | Name | What Happens to Heat? | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Solid → Liquid | **Melting** | Heat is **added** (absorbed) | Ice melting into water |
| Liquid → Solid | **Freezing** | Heat is **removed** (released) | Water freezing into ice |
| Liquid → Gas | **Vaporisation** (includes boiling) | Heat is **added** (absorbed) | Boiling water turning into steam |
| Gas → Liquid | **Condensation** | Heat is **removed** (released) | Steam condensing into droplets on a cool lid |
| Solid → Gas | **Sublimation** | Heat is **added** (absorbed) | Dry ice turning directly into CO₂ gas |
| Gas → Solid | **Deposition** | Heat is **removed** (released) | Water vapor in cold air forming frost |

```
                     sublimation (add heat)
        ┌────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
        │                                                ▼
      SOLID ──melting──▶ LIQUID ──vaporisation──▶       GAS
            ◀─freezing──        ◀─condensation──
        ▲                                                │
        └────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                     deposition (remove heat)

   (melting, vaporisation, sublimation: add heat;
    freezing, condensation, deposition: remove heat)
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The three states of matter and the six pathways connecting them. Moving to the right requires **adding heat**; moving to the left requires **removing heat**.

Here is an easy way to remember the direction: **adding heat "opens up" the particle arrangement** (solid → liquid → gas), while **removing heat "packs" the particles back together** (gas → liquid → solid).

### Why Do States Change? The Particle Explanation

You met the particle picture of phase changes in the Physics chapter ([heat-and-temperature-part5.md](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part5.md)). Here is a quick chemistry recap:

- **When heat is added:** Particles gain energy and vibrate faster. At the **melting point**, solid particles vibrate so vigorously that they overcome the rigid hold of interparticle attraction and begin sliding past one another—the solid **melts** into a liquid. Add still more heat, and at the **boiling point**, liquid particles move fast enough to break free of each other's attraction completely and fly apart—the liquid **vaporises** into a gas.
- **When heat is removed:** Particles slow down. Eventually, interparticle attraction pulls fast-moving gas particles back together into a liquid (**condensation**), and further cooling locks sliding liquid particles into fixed positions (**freezing**).
- **Sublimation and deposition:** In certain solids, the forces holding particles in the solid lattice are arranged such that at normal atmospheric pressure, warming gives surface particles enough energy to escape **directly into the gas phase** without ever forming a liquid puddle (**sublimation**). When that gas is cooled, its particles lock directly back into a solid crystal (**deposition**).

```
   SOLID                 LIQUID                 GAS
  (add heat ──▶)        (add heat ──▶)

  o o o o o             o  o   o                o         o
  o o o o o              o   o  o                  o
  o o o o o             o  o  o  o            o          o
                         o  o   o                  o   o

  fixed, tightly        close together,         far apart, moving
  packed                sliding past            freely at high speed
  ◀── (remove heat)     ◀── (remove heat)
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Adding heat makes particles move faster and break free of one another (solid → liquid → gas). Removing heat slows particles down so attraction pulls them back together. The particles themselves never change—only their arrangement and speed change.

## Part B: Melting Point and Boiling Point

Every pure substance has a characteristic fixed temperature at which it melts (**melting point**) and a fixed temperature at which it boils (**boiling point**) under standard atmospheric pressure. (For a pure substance, the freezing point is the exact same temperature as the melting point.)

For pure water at normal sea-level atmospheric pressure:

- **Melting point (and freezing point):** **0 °C**
- **Boiling point:** **100 °C**

Different pure substances have very different melting and boiling points because the strength of attraction between their particles differs:

| Substance | Melting Point | Boiling Point | State at Room Temperature (~25 °C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Water | 0 °C | 100 °C | Liquid |
| Ethanol (rubbing/drinking alcohol) | −114 °C | 78 °C | Liquid |
| Mercury (a metal!) | −39 °C | 357 °C | **Liquid** |
| Paraffin wax (candle wax) | ~55 °C | >300 °C | Solid |
| Iron | 1,538 °C | 2,862 °C | Solid |
| Nitrogen | −210 °C | **−196 °C** | Gas |

Look at what this table tells us:

- **Mercury** has a melting point of −39 °C. Because ordinary room temperature (25 °C) is well above −39 °C (and well below 357 °C), mercury is a **liquid at room temperature**—making it the famous shiny **liquid metal** used in traditional lab thermometers and barometers.
- **Ethanol (alcohol)** boils at 78 °C, well below water's boiling point of 100 °C. That reflects weaker attraction between alcohol particles and explains why alcohol-based hand sanitizer evaporates so quickly.
- **Nitrogen** boils at −196 °C! At room temperature (25 °C), nitrogen is far above its boiling point, so the nitrogen that makes up 78% of our air is a gas. To turn nitrogen gas into **liquid nitrogen**, you must cool it down to −196 °C—so cold that only trained technicians with cryogenic gear handle it.
- **Iron** has extremely strong forces of attraction between its particles, so it does not melt until heated to **1,538 °C** inside a steelmaking furnace.

**An essential reminder:** While a pure substance is actively changing state (melting or boiling), **its temperature stays completely constant**. Melting ice stays at 0 °C until the very last crystal has melted, and boiling water stays at 100 °C until all the water has turned to steam. Where does the added heat go? It goes into overcoming the forces of attraction between particles rather than raising their temperature. Scientists call this **latent heat ("hidden heat")**, which you studied in detail in the Physics chapter ([Topic 5](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part5.md)).

## Part C: Two Temperature Scales: Celsius (°C) and Kelvin (K)

In everyday life we often measure temperature in **degrees Celsius (°C)**. In science, the standard SI unit of temperature is the **Kelvin (K)**. As you saw in Physics, converting between the two is straightforward:

> **K = °C + 273**

(More precisely, 0 °C = 273.15 K, but for Class 9 calculations we round to **273**.)

Two important facts about the Kelvin scale:
1. **There are no negative numbers on the Kelvin scale.** The lowest possible temperature in nature—called **absolute zero**—is **0 K** (about −273 °C).
2. **We never write or say "degrees" with Kelvin.** Write **273 K** ("273 kelvins"), never "273 °K".

Let us work through five quick conversion examples:

**Example 1 (Melting point of ice):** Convert 0 °C to Kelvin.
- K = °C + 273 = 0 + 273 = **273 K**
- **Check:** 273 − 273 = 0 °C ✅

**Example 2 (Room temperature):** Convert 25 °C to Kelvin.
- K = 25 + 273 = **298 K**
- **Check:** 298 − 273 = 25 °C ✅

**Example 3 (Boiling point of water):** Convert 100 °C to Kelvin.
- K = 100 + 273 = **373 K**
- **Check:** 373 − 273 = 100 °C ✅

**Example 4 (Boiling point of liquid nitrogen):** Convert −196 °C to Kelvin.
- K = −196 + 273 = **77 K**
- Notice that even a freezing-cold negative Celsius temperature becomes a positive number in Kelvin!
- **Check:** 77 − 273 = −196 °C ✅

**Example 5 (Converting Kelvin back to Celsius):** A gas is at 300 K. What is its temperature in °C?
- °C = K − 273 = 300 − 273 = **27 °C**
- **Check:** 27 + 273 = 300 K ✅

Here is a handy reference table:

| Benchmark | In °C | In K |
|---|---|---|
| Melting point of ice | 0 °C | 273 K |
| Standard room temperature | 25 °C | 298 K |
| Boiling point of ethanol | 78 °C | 351 K |
| Boiling point of water | 100 °C | 373 K |
| Boiling point of liquid nitrogen | −196 °C | 77 K |

## Part D: Sublimation and Deposition: Skipping the Liquid State

Now let us return to Elena's backstage cauldron. Most familiar substances go from solid → liquid → gas when heated. However, a few special substances **skip the liquid stage completely** at normal atmospheric pressure.

### Sublimation: Solid Directly to Gas

When heated (or even just left out in a warm room), these solids turn directly into gas:

- **Dry ice (solid carbon dioxide, CO₂):** Used for stage fog and keeping frozen shipments cold.
- **Camphor:** Fragrant white tablets that slowly shrink and vanish when left in an open dish, filling the room with scent without ever melting into a liquid.
- **Naphthalene (mothballs):** Small white spheres placed in storage chests to protect woolens from moths; over weeks they slowly shrink and disappear by turning directly into vapor. (Keep mothballs away from children and pets—they are toxic if swallowed.)

### Why Is Dry Ice Called "Dry"?

When ordinary water ice warms up, it melts into liquid water, soaking whatever it touches. **Dry ice is frozen carbon dioxide (CO₂).** At normal atmospheric pressure, solid CO₂ **never melts into a liquid**—instead, at **−78.5 °C (about −78 °C)**, it **sublimes directly into carbon dioxide gas**. Because no liquid puddle ever forms, everything stays completely dry—hence the name **dry ice**!

Because dry ice is at **−78 °C** (far colder than freezer ice at 0 °C), strict safety rules apply:
- **Never touch dry ice with bare skin:** It causes severe frostbite in seconds. Always use insulated gloves or tongs.
- **Never swallow or put dry ice in your mouth:** It is extremely dangerous.
- **Never seal dry ice inside an airtight bottle or jar:** As it sublimes into gas, it expands nearly 800-fold and will cause a sealed container to explode violently.
- **Always use it in a well-ventilated space.**

**Wait—what is the white "fog" coming out of the cauldron?**
Carbon dioxide gas itself is **completely invisible**! What you see rolling across the stage is **not** CO₂ gas. Because the subliming dry ice is bitterly cold (−78 °C), it chills the surrounding air. The invisible **water vapor (humidity)** already present in the room air cools down and **condenses into millions of microscopic liquid water droplets**—just like a low-lying cloud or morning fog. Pouring warm water into the cauldron speeds up sublimation and adds extra water vapor, making the fog even thicker!

```
   DRY ICE (solid CO₂)            Directly into gas (no liquid stage!)

   o o o o                         o         o
   o o o o   ── absorbs heat ──▶        o   o
   o o o o      (at −78 °C)          o       o       o
                                          o
   particles locked in place      particles far apart, moving freely
```

> **Figure 3.3** – At normal atmospheric pressure, dry ice particles absorb heat and jump **directly from solid to gas** (sublimation), skipping the liquid phase entirely.

### Deposition: Gas Directly to Solid

The exact reverse of sublimation—**a gas turning directly into a solid without becoming a liquid first**—is called **deposition**. The classic everyday example is **frost**. On a freezing winter night when grass and car windshields drop below 0 °C, water vapor in the air does not condense into liquid dew; instead, it crystallizes **directly into delicate white ice crystals** on the cold surface.

## Part E: The Role of Pressure: Turning a Gas into a Liquid

So far we have changed states by changing the **temperature** (adding or removing heat). There is a second dial we can turn: **pressure**.

In Topic 2 we saw that gases are highly compressible because their particles are far apart. What happens if we **compress a gas under high pressure** while also **cooling it**?

> **Applying high pressure and lowering the temperature forces gas particles close enough together for interparticle attraction to take hold—turning the gas into a liquid!**

```
   Gas at normal pressure         Under high pressure + cooling

   o      o      o                    o o  o o
      o      o                         o  o o  o
   o      o      o      ─────▶        o  o o  o
      o      o                         o o  o o
   particles far apart               particles close together (liquid)
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Compressing and cooling a gas brings its particles close together so it liquefies.

Everyday examples where pressure and temperature control the state of matter:

- **LPG (Liquefied Petroleum Gas) cylinders:** Inside a camping stove canister or home cooking-gas cylinder, fuel gas is compressed under moderate pressure until it turns into a **liquid**. That allows a large mass of fuel to fit in a small tank. When you turn the valve open, pressure drops inside the nozzle, and the liquid instantly vaporises back into **gas** to feed the burner flame.
- **CNG (Compressed Natural Gas):** Stored in vehicle tanks at very high pressure (~200 atmospheres) so a large amount of fuel fits in a compact space (though at room temperature, methane stays a dense compressed gas rather than liquefying).
- **CO₂ fire extinguishers and liquid CO₂:** At normal atmospheric pressure, CO₂ goes straight between solid (dry ice) and gas. Only if you seal CO₂ under high pressure (more than 5 times atmospheric pressure) can liquid CO₂ exist!

Here is how our two "dials"—temperature and pressure—work together:

| Action | Effect on Particles and State |
|---|---|
| **Increase temperature** | Particles move faster; state "opens up" (solid → liquid → gas) |
| **Decrease temperature** | Particles slow down; state "packs together" (gas → liquid → solid) |
| **Increase pressure (on a gas)** | Pushes particles closer together; helps turn a gas into a liquid |
| **Decrease pressure** | Allows particles to spread apart; makes it easier for a liquid or solid to turn into a gas |

## Part F: One Substance, Three States, Same Particles

Think about **ice, liquid water, and steam**. Outwardly they look completely different: ice is hard and cold, water flows in a glass, and pure steam is an invisible hot gas. Yet chemically they are **the exact same substance**, made of the **exact same water particles**—only the **arrangement and speed** of those particles have changed!

| Feature | Ice (Solid) | Water (Liquid) | Steam (Gas) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Identity of particles** | Water particles | Same water particles | Same water particles |
| **Particle arrangement** | Regular, locked in place | Closely packed, sliding | Far apart, moving freely |
| **Particle speed / energy** | Lowest (vibrate in place) | Moderate | Highest |
| **Shape** | Fixed | Takes container's shape | Fills entire space |

**A famous exception: Why does ice float on water?** In Topic 2 we noted that solids are usually denser than their liquids. Water is a remarkable exception! When liquid water freezes into ice, its particles lock into a special **open hexagonal cage structure** with extra empty space between the particles. That makes solid ice slightly less dense than liquid water—so ice cubes and icebergs float! (You also met this anomalous expansion of water in the Physics chapter [heat-and-temperature-part2.md](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part2.md).)

**Reasoning Example A: Does melting create a new substance?**
When ice melts into water, is a new substance formed? **No.** The particles themselves are unchanged; only their arrangement has changed, and freezing the water restores the exact same ice. Scientists call a change of state a **physical change** (we will contrast physical and chemical changes in the next chapter).

**Reasoning Example B: Why can we see the white fog from dry ice if CO₂ gas is invisible?**
Because the white fog is **not** CO₂ gas! It consists of tiny **liquid water droplets** formed when moisture in the warm room air condenses in the freezing air around the dry ice.

**Reasoning Example C: Both mercury and ethanol are liquids at room temperature (25 °C). Does that mean their particles attract each other equally?**
Not at all! Mercury boils at 357 °C whereas ethanol boils at 78 °C. That tells us the attraction between mercury particles is **much stronger** than the attraction between ethanol particles. Every pure substance has its own unique melting and boiling points.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Dry ice is just super-cold water ice that melts into water."**

No. Dry ice is **solid carbon dioxide (CO₂)**, not frozen water. At normal pressure it sublimes directly into CO₂ gas without ever forming a liquid.

**Mistake 2: "Touching dry ice with bare hands is harmless—it's just like holding an ice cube."**

Dangerous! Dry ice is around **−78 °C** and causes severe frostbite within seconds. **Never touch it with bare skin and never put it in your mouth.**

**Mistake 3: "The white cloud rolling off dry ice is carbon dioxide gas."**

CO₂ gas is completely colorless and invisible. The white cloud is **condensed water droplets** (fog) formed from moisture in the surrounding air.

**Mistake 4: Writing "°K" for Kelvin or getting negative Kelvin answers.**

We write **273 K**, never "273 °K". Also, the Kelvin scale starts at absolute zero (0 K), so a Kelvin temperature can never be negative.

**Mistake 5: Adding 273 when converting from Kelvin to Celsius (or subtracting when going from Celsius to Kelvin).**

Remember **K = °C + 273**. To go from °C to K, **add 273** (25 °C + 273 = 298 K). To go from K to °C, **subtract 273** (300 K − 273 = 27 °C).

**Mistake 6: "The temperature of boiling water keeps rising above 100 °C as long as the stove is on."**

No. While a pure liquid is boiling at atmospheric pressure, its temperature stays **constant** at its boiling point (100 °C for water) because all the added heat goes into breaking particles apart into gas (latent heat).

**Mistake 7: "All metals are solids at room temperature."**

**Mercury** is a metal, and its melting point is −39 °C—so at room temperature (25 °C) mercury is a **liquid**.

## Quick Recap

- There are **six state changes**: **melting** (solid → liquid), **freezing** (liquid → solid), **vaporisation/boiling** (liquid → gas), **condensation** (gas → liquid), **sublimation** (solid → gas directly), and **deposition** (gas → solid directly).
- **Adding heat** makes particles move faster and break apart; **removing heat** slows particles down so attraction pulls them together.
- Every pure substance has a fixed **melting point** and **boiling point** (water: 0 °C and 100 °C; ethanol boils at 78 °C; mercury melts at −39 °C; liquid nitrogen boils at −196 °C).
- During any change of state, the **temperature remains constant** until the change is complete.
- **K = °C + 273** (and **°C = K − 273**). 0 °C = 273 K; 25 °C = 298 K; 100 °C = 373 K; −196 °C = 77 K.
- **Sublimation** examples: dry ice (solid CO₂), camphor, naphthalene (mothballs). **Deposition** example: frost forming on a freezing morning.
- Dry ice is at −78 °C: **never touch it with bare hands or put it in your mouth**, and remember that its visible "fog" is condensed water droplets from the air, not CO₂ gas.
- Gases can be liquefied by **applying high pressure and cooling** (e.g., LPG in fuel cylinders).
- Ice, liquid water, and steam contain the **exact same particles**; changing state is a **physical change**.
