# Topic 4: Evaporation: How Liquids Dry and Why Evaporation Cools Things Down

In Topic 3 we saw that when a liquid is heated to its **boiling point**, it bubbles vigorously throughout and turns into a gas (vapor). Yet that leaves an everyday mystery unsolved: when you hang a wet cotton shirt on a balcony clothesline, there is no stove underneath it and the temperature never reaches 100 °C—yet a couple of hours later the shirt is completely dry! Its liquid water **turned into water vapor and escaped into the air** far below the boiling point. How does that happen, and why does it depend so strongly on the weather? Let us use our particle model to find out.

---

## The Mystery of the Balcony Jersey

One sunny Saturday morning, Mrs. Bennett washed her son Liam's cotton soccer jersey and hung it spread out on the balcony clothesline. The afternoon was warm, dry, and breezy. Liam was studying at his desk, glancing out at the jersey every now and then because he wanted to wear it to practice. Just one hour later he touched the fabric: **bone dry**.

Two months later, during a rainy, overcast week, Mrs. Bennett washed the exact same cotton jersey and hung it on the exact same balcony line. Outside, the sky was gray, the air felt muggy and damp, and there was no breeze at all. By evening the jersey was still **damp and heavy**. Even by noon the next day, the seams felt slightly clammy.

Liam was puzzled. "Mom, it's the exact same jersey and the exact same spin cycle on the washing machine. Why did it dry in one hour that Saturday, and take more than a full day this week? And where does the water actually go when it dries?"

Mrs. Bennett smiled. "The water didn't vanish into thin air—well, actually, it **became** part of the air as invisible water vapor! And to understand why it escaped so fast on a sunny, breezy day and so slowly on a damp, still day, you have to picture what the water particles are doing right at the surface of the cloth."

Let us zoom in and see.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Evaporation** | The slow change of a liquid into vapor **only from its surface**, at **any temperature below its boiling point** | Wet laundry drying on a line; puddles drying after rain |
| **Vapour (vapor)** | The gaseous state of a substance that is normally a liquid at room temperature | Invisible water vapor in the air |
| **Surface area** | The area of the top exposed surface of a liquid in contact with air | Water spread on a wide tray has a larger surface area than the same water in a narrow cup |
| **Humidity** | The amount of water vapor already present in the air | Humidity is high on a muggy, rainy day and low on a dry desert day |
| **Cooling effect of evaporation** | The drop in temperature of the remaining liquid and its surroundings as fast-moving particles escape | Sweating cools your skin when a breeze blows |
| **Surroundings** | Everything in contact with or around the evaporating liquid (air, cloth, your skin, a floor) | Your palm when rubbing alcohol evaporates from it |
| **Volatile liquid** | A liquid that evaporates very rapidly at room temperature | Rubbing alcohol, nail-polish remover (acetone) |

## Part A: How Does Evaporation Work at the Particle Level?

Recall our particle picture of a liquid from Topics 2 and 3: liquid particles are packed closely together, sliding past one another in constant random motion. Now here is a key insight we have not mentioned yet:

> **At any given moment, not all particles in a liquid are moving at the same speed!**

Just like cars on a busy highway or runners in a park, as particles constantly collide with one another, some get knocked into moving **much faster than average**, most move at moderate speeds, and some move more slowly. (The liquid's temperature simply measures the **average** kinetic energy of all its particles.)

Now think about the particles sitting right at the **top surface** of the liquid. Particles deep inside the liquid are surrounded and pulled back by neighbors on all sides. A particle at the **surface**, however, has liquid neighbors pulling it only from below and the sides—above it is open air! Whenever a **fast-moving particle** at the surface happens to have enough kinetic energy to overcome the downward pull of its neighbors, it **breaks free and flies out into the air** as a gas (vapor) particle.

```
         air above (particles far apart)
      .       o  <--- fast surface particle escapes!       .
                 \
   ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~
     o   o  O  o   o   o  O  o   o      <--- liquid surface
    o  o   o   o  o    o   o   o  o
      o   o    o    o   o    o   o
    o   o  o    o  o   o     o   o
        bulk liquid (interior particles are pulled from all sides)
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Fast-moving particles at the surface (shown as larger **O**) have enough energy to overcome the attraction of neighboring particles and escape into the air as vapor. That is **evaporation**. Interior particles are pulled from every direction and cannot easily escape.

Three central features of evaporation follow directly from this picture:

1. **Evaporation is a surface phenomenon:** Only particles right at the exposed surface have an open escape route into the air.
2. **Evaporation happens at any temperature:** You do not need to heat water to 100 °C. At 30 °C, 20 °C, or even 5 °C, a small fraction of surface particles always happen to be moving fast enough to break free.
3. **Evaporation is gradual and quiet:** Because only the fastest surface particles escape bit by bit, evaporation happens slowly without any bubbling.

That solves the first half of Liam's question: his wet jersey did not need to boil. All afternoon, the fastest water particles at the surface of the wet cotton fibers kept breaking free into the air until the cloth was dry.

### Evaporation vs. Boiling: How Are They Different?

In Topic 3 we studied **boiling**, and in the Physics chapter ([heat-and-temperature-part5.md](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part5.md)) we briefly contrasted it with evaporation. Now look at how the two compare at the particle level:

```
   EVAPORATION                         BOILING

  ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~              ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~
   o   o   o   o   o                  o   O   o   O   o
  o   o   o   o   o                     o   (  )   o
    o   o   o   o                      o  (    )  o  O
  o   o   o   o   o                      o  (  )   o
   particles escape                   vapor bubbles form deep
   only from the surface              inside the entire liquid!
```

> **Figure 4.2** – In **evaporation**, particles escape quietly from the top surface only. In **boiling**, the liquid is hot enough that particles throughout the entire bulk push apart to form **vapor bubbles** inside the liquid, which rise and burst at the top.

Here is a side-by-side comparison table:

| Feature | Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Occurs at **any temperature** below the boiling point | Occurs **only at the fixed boiling point** (e.g., 100 °C for water) |
| **Where it happens** | **Surface phenomenon** (only at the exposed top surface) | **Bulk phenomenon** (throughout the entire liquid) |
| **Speed** | Slow and gradual | Rapid and vigorous |
| **Bubbles** | No bubbles form | Vapor bubbles form throughout the liquid |
| **Temperature effect** | **Cools** the remaining liquid and surroundings | Temperature stays constant at the boiling point |
| **Source of heat** | Absorbs heat from the liquid itself and its immediate **surroundings** | Requires a continuous external heat source (stove, heater) |
| **Everyday example** | Wet clothes drying; sweat drying on skin | Water boiling in a kettle on a stove |

> **Key takeaway:** Both evaporation and boiling turn a liquid into a gas (so both are forms of **vaporisation**), but **evaporation is not boiling**. Evaporation works quietly at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the entire liquid at its fixed boiling point.

## Part B: Four Factors That Control the Rate of Evaporation

Now let us explain the second half of Liam's puzzle: why did his jersey dry in 1 hour on the sunny, breezy Saturday, and take all day during the damp, still rainy week? Four factors control how fast a liquid evaporates.

### 1. Surface Area (How Spread Out the Liquid Is)

Because evaporation happens only at the surface, **increasing the exposed surface area gives more particles a chance to escape at the same time**. That is why water spread out on a wide plate dries much faster than the same volume of water in a narrow cup, and why you **spread a wet towel out** on a clothesline instead of leaving it crumpled in a ball.

**Home Activity 1 (Safe):** Measure **50 mL of water** into a narrow drinking glass, and another **50 mL of water** into a wide dinner plate. Set both side by side in the same room. Check them after several hours (or the next day): the water on the wide plate will have evaporated much faster!

### 2. Temperature (How Warm the Liquid and Air Are)

When temperature rises, the **average kinetic energy** of the liquid particles increases—meaning a much larger fraction of surface particles are moving fast enough to break free of their neighbors' attraction. Therefore, **higher temperature speeds up evaporation**.

**Home Activity 2 (Safe):** Wet two identical cotton handkerchiefs and wring them out equally. Hang one in a **warm, sunny spot** and the other in a **cool, shaded spot** (with similar airflow). The one in the warm sun dries much faster.

### 3. Humidity (How Much Water Vapor Is Already in the Air)

Air can hold only a certain amount of water vapor at a given temperature. When the air is **humid** (already crowded with water vapor, as on a rainy or muggy day), water particles trying to escape from wet cloth have a harder time staying in the air—in fact, many vapor particles in the damp air **collide with the wet surface and get recaptured**, slowing net evaporation to a crawl. When the air is **dry** (low humidity), very few vapor particles return to the cloth, so net evaporation is fast.

That is a major reason Liam's jersey took so long to dry on the rainy day!

### 4. Wind Speed (How Fast Air Is Moving Across the Surface)

As water evaporates from a wet shirt, the thin layer of air right next to the fabric becomes humid with escaped water vapor. If the air is completely still, that humid air blanket sits around the cloth and slows further evaporation. When a **breeze or fan blows**, it **sweeps the humid air away** and replaces it with fresh, drier air—allowing surface particles to keep escaping rapidly.

**Home Activity 3 (Safe):** Wet two identical cotton cloths equally. Hang one in a room **under a running fan** (or in a breeze) and the other in a **still room with no airflow**. The cloth in the moving air dries much faster.

### Summary Table: The Four Factors

| Factor | When This Factor Increases, Evaporation Becomes... | Particle Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Surface area** | **Faster** | More particles are at the surface with an open escape route |
| **2. Temperature** | **Faster** | More particles have enough kinetic energy to break free |
| **3. Humidity** | **Slower** | Air is already crowded with water vapor; more vapor particles return to the liquid |
| **4. Wind speed** | **Faster** | Moving air sweeps escaped vapor particles away from the surface |

### Solved Reasoning Examples

**Example 1 (Comparing drying times on two days):** Suppose Liam's freshly washed jersey holds **100 g** of water after the spin cycle. On the sunny, breezy Saturday, all **100 g** of water evaporated in **1 hour**. On the humid, windless rainy day, only **25 g** of water evaporated in 1 hour. Assuming a steady evaporation rate on each day, how long will the jersey take to dry completely on the rainy day, and how many times faster was evaporation on Saturday?

- Saturday evaporation rate = 100 g ÷ 1 hour = **100 g/hour**
- Rainy-day evaporation rate = 25 g ÷ 1 hour = **25 g/hour**
- Time to dry on the rainy day = 100 g ÷ 25 g/hour = **4 hours**
- Ratio of rates = 100 ÷ 25 = **4 times faster** on Saturday

**Check:** 25 g/hour × 4 hours = 100 g ✅. (In real life the rate varies slightly as the cloth dries, but the math clearly shows how lower evaporation rate stretches drying time.)

**Example 2 (Three wet handkerchiefs—which dries first?):** Three identical wet handkerchiefs (P, Q, and R) each contain the same amount of water:
- **P:** Spread wide open and hung in the **warm sun**.
- **Q:** Rolled into a tight bundle and left in the **warm sun**.
- **R:** Spread wide open and hung in the **cool shade**.

Which handkerchief will dry **first**?
- **P will definitely dry first** because it has **both** a large surface area **and** a high temperature working in its favor.
- **Q** has high temperature but a tiny exposed surface area (water trapped inside the bundle cannot easily escape).
- **R** has a large surface area but a lower temperature.
- Both Q and R will take longer than P (which of Q or R finishes last depends on how tightly Q is bundled versus how cool the shade is).

**Check:** P beats Q on surface area and beats R on temperature → P > Q and P > R ✅

## Part C: Why Does Evaporation Cause Cooling?

Step out of a swimming pool into a breeze, and you immediately shiver—even on a warm afternoon! Dab a drop of rubbing alcohol or nail-polish remover on the back of your hand, and that spot feels icy cold. Wipe your forehead with a damp cloth when you have a fever, and your skin cools down. All of these share one fundamental principle: **evaporation causes cooling**. Why?

### 1. Only the Fastest (Highest-Energy) Particles Escape

Remember from Part A that only the **fastest-moving particles** at the surface have enough energy to overcome interparticle attraction and escape into the air. When the fastest runners leave a group, what happens to the **average speed** of the runners left behind? **It drops!**

In the same way, when the highest-energy particles escape during evaporation, the **average kinetic energy of the particles remaining in the liquid decreases**. Since temperature is a measure of average particle kinetic energy, **the remaining liquid becomes cooler**.

### 2. The Evaporating Liquid Absorbs Heat from Its Surroundings

Now look at what happens next: as the liquid cools slightly below the temperature of whatever it is touching, it **absorbs heat energy from its surroundings** (your skin, the cloth, a terracotta pot, the air) to replace the energy carried away by the escaping vapor particles. Because the surroundings lose heat energy, **the surroundings cool down**!

> **Evaporation carries away high-energy particles and absorbs latent heat from the surroundings—making both the remaining liquid and its surroundings cooler.**

### Everyday Examples of Evaporative Cooling

1. **Sweating cools the human body:** When you exercise or sit in hot weather, your sweat glands release water onto your skin. As that sweat evaporates, it absorbs heat directly from your skin, cooling your body and keeping your core temperature safe.

2. **Terracotta / Earthenware water coolers (clay pots):** In many warm regions of the world, drinking water is stored in unglazed clay pots (often called *matkas* or *ollas*). The clay walls contain millions of microscopic pores. A tiny fraction of the water slowly seeps through these pores to the outer surface and **evaporates into the air**, drawing the heat needed for evaporation from the bulk water inside the pot—keeping the drinking water refreshingly cool without electricity!

3. **Sprinkling water on hot patios or courtyards:** On a scorching summer afternoon, spraying water onto hot pavement or a sunny patio cools the ground down as the water rapidly evaporates and draws heat out of the stone.

4. **Why cotton clothes feel cooler in summer:** Cotton fibers readily **absorb sweat** from your skin and spread it over a larger **surface area** exposed to the air. The sweat evaporates quickly, cooling your body. Many non-breathable synthetic fabrics do not absorb water well, leaving sweat trapped on your skin and making you feel hot and sticky.

5. **Why a fan cools you down (even though it doesn't chill the room air):** A ceiling fan does not lower the thermometer reading of an empty room! Instead, when you sit under a fan, the moving air **sweeps away the humid air layer** next to your skin and dramatically **speeds up the evaporation of your sweat**—cooling *you* much faster.

6. **Evaporative ("swamp") coolers work best in dry climates:** In dry desert climates (like Arizona, inland Australia, the Middle East, or northern India in May), evaporative coolers pull hot, **dry air** through wet pads. Because humidity is low, water evaporates rapidly and chills the air. On a **humid, muggy day**, the air is already nearly saturated with water vapor, so very little water evaporates from the pads and the cooler barely cools the air at all.

7. **Rubbing alcohol or acetone (nail-polish remover) on your palm:** Volatile liquids like acetone or rubbing alcohol have weaker attractions between their particles, so they evaporate **much faster** than water at room temperature. Because so many particles escape from your skin in just a few seconds, they draw heat out of your palm rapidly, making your skin feel noticeably cold.

> **Safety note:** Never inhale the fumes of acetone or rubbing alcohol, keep them away from flames (they are flammable), and never taste them. A safe way to feel this effect at home is to put **one drop of plain water** on the back of your left hand and **one drop of alcohol-based hand sanitizer** on the back of your right hand—the hand sanitizer evaporates faster and feels noticeably cooler!

**Home Activity 4 (Measuring evaporative cooling with a thermometer):** Wrap a small piece of wet cotton around the bulb of a room thermometer (or hold a damp cloth over a digital weather sensor) and place it in front of a running fan alongside a dry thermometer. Within a few minutes, the **wet-bulb thermometer will read several degrees lower** than the dry one!

### Solved Calculation Example: Estimating the Cooling Limit of a Clay Pot

**Example 3 (Upper-limit cooling calculation):** A terracotta pot holds **1 kg (1,000 g)** of water. Suppose **20 g** of water seeps through the pores and evaporates completely. **Assuming** all the heat needed for that evaporation comes solely from the remaining 1 kg of water inside the pot (ignoring heat leaking in from the warm room air), by at most how many degrees Celsius could the water cool? Use latent heat of vaporisation of water ≈ **2,260 J/g** and specific heat capacity of water = **4,200 J/(kg·°C)** (from your Physics chapter on Heat).

- Heat absorbed by 20 g of evaporating water: Q = 20 g × 2,260 J/g = **45,200 J**
- Maximum temperature drop of 1 kg of water: ΔT = Q ÷ (m × c) = 45,200 J ÷ (1 kg × 4,200 J/kg·°C) = 45,200 ÷ 4,200 ≈ **10.8 °C**

**Check:** 4,200 × 10.8 ≈ 45,360 J ≈ 45,200 J ✅. (In real life, warm room air continuously transfers some heat back into the pot, so the actual cooling is a few degrees rather than the full 10.8 °C theoretical limit. Still, it shows how just **20 g** of evaporating water can cool **1,000 g** of water by several degrees!)

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Evaporation happens only when water is warm or in direct sunlight."**

No. Evaporation occurs at **every temperature** below the boiling point—even cold water in a glass on a shaded table slowly evaporates over several days.

**Mistake 2: "Evaporation and boiling are two words for the exact same thing."**

Both turn liquid into gas, but evaporation happens quietly **at the surface only, at any temperature**, and **cools** the liquid; boiling happens **throughout the bulk liquid with bubbles at a fixed boiling point**.

**Mistake 3: "When wet clothes dry, the water is destroyed."**

Matter is never destroyed during drying. The liquid water simply changes state into invisible **water vapor (gas)** and mixes into the air.

**Mistake 4: "Turning on a ceiling fan lowers the temperature of the air in an empty room."**

A fan only moves air around (in fact, its electric motor adds a tiny bit of heat!). A fan makes *people* feel cooler because moving air speeds up the **evaporation of sweat** from their skin.

**Mistake 5: "Evaporation stops completely on a rainy or humid day."**

Unless the air is 100% saturated, evaporation still happens—just **much more slowly** because many water vapor particles in the damp air bounce back into the wet cloth.

## Quick Recap

- **Evaporation** is the gradual change of a liquid into vapor **from its surface**, occurring at **any temperature below its boiling point**.
- Because particles in a liquid move at a range of speeds, the **fastest-moving particles at the surface** can overcome interparticle attraction and escape into the air.
- Unlike **boiling** (a rapid bulk process with bubbles at a fixed boiling point), evaporation is a quiet surface process.
- **Four factors affect the rate of evaporation:**
  1. **Surface area** ↑ → evaporation ↑
  2. **Temperature** ↑ → evaporation ↑
  3. **Humidity** ↑ → evaporation ↓
  4. **Wind speed** ↑ → evaporation ↑
- **Evaporation causes cooling** because the highest-energy particles escape, lowering the average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid, which then draws heat from its surroundings.
- Everyday applications: sweating, porous clay water pots, sprinkling water on hot pavements, wearing breathable cotton in summer, sitting under a fan, evaporative desert coolers, and the cold feel of rubbing alcohol or acetone on skin.

## Practice Questions

1. What is evaporation? Using the particle model, explain why evaporation can occur at any temperature whereas boiling occurs only at the boiling point.

2. Explain why evaporation takes place only from the surface of a liquid and not from deep inside it.

3. Using the particle picture, give one clear reason for each observation:
   (a) Wet laundry dries faster when spread out wide than when left crumpled.
   (b) A puddle dries faster on a hot sunny afternoon than on a cool morning.
   (c) Wet clothes dry very slowly on a humid, rainy day.
   (d) Wet hair dries faster when a breeze is blowing.

4. Two identical wet bath towels each hold 200 g of water. Towel A loses all its water in 2 hours. Towel B loses 50 g of water per hour. Assuming steady evaporation rates:
   (a) How long will Towel B take to dry completely?
   (b) How many times faster does Towel A dry compared to Towel B?

5. Three identical wet shirts are hung up at the same time: Shirt X is spread out in the sun, Shirt Y is spread out in a cool dark closet with the door shut, and Shirt Z is rolled into a tight ball and left in the sun. Which shirt will dry first? Explain why.

6. Why does drinking water stored in a porous clay pot stay cooler than water stored in a sealed stainless-steel bottle in the same room?

7. Why do cotton clothes keep us more comfortable on a hot summer day than non-absorbent synthetic clothes?

8. Why does an evaporative ("swamp") cooler cool a room effectively on a hot, dry desert day, but work poorly on a muggy, humid coastal day?

9. A drop of nail-polish remover (acetone) or rubbing alcohol placed on your palm feels much colder than a drop of water at the same room temperature. Explain why.

10. A homeowner sprinkles 30 g of water onto a hot patio stone, and all 30 g evaporates. If each gram of evaporating water absorbs about 2,260 J of heat, how much heat energy is removed from the patio stone? (If that same amount of heat were removed from 2 kg of liquid water instead, by approximately how many degrees Celsius would the water cool? Use c = 4,200 J/kg·°C.)

11. State True or False, and correct the false statements:
    (a) Water evaporates only when it reaches 100 °C.
    (b) During evaporation, the remaining liquid becomes cooler.
    (c) Bubbles forming at the bottom of a pot are a sign of simple surface evaporation.
    (d) Evaporation is faster when the surrounding air has high humidity.

### Answers

1. **Evaporation** is the slow change of a liquid into vapor from its surface at any temperature below its boiling point. Because liquid particles move at a wide range of speeds, even at lower temperatures a few fast-moving surface particles have enough energy to break free. **Boiling** requires particles throughout the entire bulk liquid to have enough energy to form vapor bubbles inside the liquid, which happens only at the boiling point.

2. Particles deep inside the liquid are surrounded and pulled back by neighboring particles on all sides. Particles right at the top surface are pulled only from below and the sides, giving fast surface particles an open escape route into the air above.

3. (a) Spreading the cloth increases the **surface area**, exposing more water particles to the air at once.
   (b) Higher **temperature** gives a larger fraction of water particles enough kinetic energy to break free.
   (c) On a humid day, the air is already crowded with water vapor particles, many of which collide with the cloth and return to the liquid, slowing net escape.
   (d) Moving air (**wind**) sweeps escaped water vapor away from the surface, keeping the air right next to the hair dry so more particles can escape.

4. Towel A's rate = 200 g ÷ 2 hours = 100 g/hour. Towel B's rate = 50 g/hour.
   (a) Time for Towel B = 200 g ÷ 50 g/hour = **4 hours**.
   (b) Ratio of rates = 100 ÷ 50 = **2 times faster**.
   Check: 100 g/h × 2 h = 200 g ✅ and 50 g/h × 4 h = 200 g ✅

5. **Shirt X** will dry first because it benefits from **both** a large exposed surface area and a higher temperature in the sun. Shirt Y suffers from lower temperature and still, humid air in the closed closet; Shirt Z suffers from a very small exposed surface area.

6. Tiny amounts of water seep through the microscopic pores of the clay pot and evaporate from its outer surface. As that water evaporates, it draws latent heat from the pot and the water inside, cooling the drinking water. Stainless steel has no pores, so no evaporation can occur through the bottle walls.

7. Cotton absorbs sweat from the skin and spreads it over a wide surface area exposed to the air. As the sweat evaporates rapidly, it draws heat from the skin and cools the body. Many synthetic fabrics do not absorb sweat well, slowing evaporation.

8. An evaporative cooler cools air by evaporating water from wet pads. On a dry day (low humidity), water evaporates rapidly and absorbs a lot of heat from the air. On a humid day, the air already holds a lot of water vapor, so evaporation is very slow and little cooling occurs.

9. Acetone and rubbing alcohol are highly **volatile** liquids—their particles have weaker forces of attraction and evaporate much faster than water. Rapid evaporation draws heat out of your palm very quickly, creating a strong cooling sensation.

10. Heat removed = 30 g × 2,260 J/g = **67,800 J**. If removed from 2 kg of water: ΔT = 67,800 ÷ (2 kg × 4,200 J/kg·°C) = 67,800 ÷ 8,400 ≈ **8.07 °C** (about **8 °C** cooler).
    Check: 8,400 × 8.07 ≈ 67,788 J ≈ 67,800 J ✅

11. (a) **False** ❌. Evaporation occurs at any temperature below 100 °C. (b) **True** ✅. High-energy particles escape, lowering the average energy of the remaining liquid. (c) **False** ❌. Bubbles forming inside the liquid are a sign of **boiling**, not surface evaporation. (d) **False** ❌. High humidity **slows down** evaporation.
