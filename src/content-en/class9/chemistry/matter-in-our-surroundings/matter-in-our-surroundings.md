# Topic 1: What Is Matter and How Is It Made of Particles?

This is the very first topic of Class 9 Chemistry, so there is no previous chemistry lesson to look back on. Chemistry begins with a simple question: **what are the things around us actually made of?** A pen, water, air, hot cocoa, soil—what are they made of, and if we could peer inside that "stuff," what would we find? Today we will explore exactly that: first, what scientists mean by the word "matter," and second, how four clear pieces of experimental evidence prove that all matter is built from unimaginably tiny particles.

(You may have caught a brief glimpse of particles in the Physics chapter [heat-and-temperature-part5.md](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part5.md). Here we will build the idea from the ground up from a chemistry perspective, backed by hands-on evidence.)

---

## From One Corner of the Room to the Other

Mr. Parker ran an after-school chemistry study group in a small classroom. One afternoon his student Maya arrived a few minutes early. Mr. Parker lit a **vanilla-scented candle** (or an incense stick) on a shelf in one corner of the room and walked over to the opposite corner to check his notes. Maya was sitting near the door at the far end of the room. The windows and door were closed, and the ceiling fan was switched off.

Within a minute or two, Maya sniffed the air. "Mr. Parker, I can smell the vanilla all the way over here! Performers use smoke machines on stage, sure, but the candle is sitting right there in the corner. Something from that corner must have traveled through the air to reach me."

Mr. Parker smiled. "Spot on. Now tell me: what actually traveled from that corner to your nose? And how did it move across a completely still room? Today's entire lesson rests on answering that single question. And the answer is so powerful that it will explain everything from the scent of freshly baked bread to how sugar disappears into your morning tea."

We will return to Maya's observation halfway through this topic, once we uncover a process called **diffusion**.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Matter** | Anything that has **mass** and **takes up space (volume)** | A pen, water, air, soil |
| **Mass** | The amount of "stuff" in an object, measured on a balance | A 1 kg bag of sugar has a mass of 1 kg |
| **Particles** | Extremely tiny building blocks of matter, invisible to the naked eye | Particles of water, particles of air |
| **Interparticle space** | The empty space between particles of matter | Sugar particles fitting into the spaces between water particles |
| **Diffusion** | The automatic mixing of particles of two substances on their own | The scent of a candle or freshly baked bread spreading across a room |
| **Force of attraction** | The pull that holds particles of matter together | Water droplets merging into a larger drop |
| **Dilution** | Making a solution weaker (less concentrated) by adding more water | Adding water to concentrated fruit squash |

## Part A: What Do We Mean by Matter?

Not everything in our experience is matter. Scientists call something **matter** only if it has **two essential properties**:

1. It has **mass** (it contains "stuff" that can be weighed on a scale).
2. It **takes up space** (it has **volume**).

> **Matter = anything that has mass and occupies space.**

Look at how everyday things sort into two groups:

| Is Matter | Is Not Matter |
|---|---|
| Pen, book, soil, water | **Light** |
| **Air** | **Sound** |
| Milk, tea, sugar, salt | **Heat** |
| Phone, bicycle, your own body | **Love, anger, happiness (emotions)** |

Light, sound, and heat are **forms of energy**. They have no mass and do not take up space. Similarly, you cannot weigh happiness or curiosity on a balance, so thoughts and emotions are not matter either.

### Air Is Matter Too: Two Safe Home Activities

Because air is invisible, it can be hard to believe that it is real matter. Let us prove both properties of air right at home.

**Activity 1: Air takes up space.**
Fill a deep bucket or large bowl with water. Crumple a dry paper towel or tissue and push it firmly into the bottom of a dry drinking glass so it does not fall out when you turn the glass upside down. Now hold the glass **straight upside down** (mouth pointing straight down) and push it slowly into the water. Do not tilt the glass.

When you pull the glass straight back out, you will find that the **paper towel is still completely dry** (or nearly dry). Why? Because **air is already occupying the space inside the glass**, blocking the water from entering. If you tilt the glass slightly while it is underwater, you will see **air bubbles escaping** to the surface, and only then does water rush in to take its place.

**Activity 2: Air has mass.**
Take a long wooden ruler or a light stick and tie a piece of string around its exact center so it hangs horizontally in **balance**. Take two identical **balloons**. Tape one uninflated balloon to each end of the stick; the stick stays balanced. Now remove one balloon, blow it up with air, tie it shut, and tape it back to the same spot. That end of the stick **tips downward**.

This shows that the inflated balloon has **more mass** because the mass of the trapped air has been added to it. Since air has mass and takes up space (it inflates the balloon), **air is definitely matter**.

## Part B: Matter Is Made of Tiny Particles

Now for the deeper question: imagine taking a glass of water and dividing it in half, then dividing that half in half again, and repeating this millions of times. Would you be able to keep dividing forever, or would you eventually reach a "smallest possible piece" that cannot be split any further as water?

Through many careful experiments, scientists discovered that **all matter is made up of tiny discrete building blocks called particles.** (In a later chapter, *Atoms and Molecules*, you will learn that these particles are sometimes **atoms** and sometimes **molecules**. For now, calling them **particles** is all we need.)

How do these particles behave? There are **four key pieces of evidence**. Let us examine them one by one.

---

## Part C: Evidence 1: Particles Are Extremely Small

### Activity: The Serial Dilution Experiment

You can perform this experiment in a school lab or at home. In a lab, teachers often use a tiny crystal of **potassium permanganate (KMnO₄)** (handle with care under adult or teacher supervision; never touch it with bare hands or taste it). At home, a **single drop of dark food coloring or fountain-pen ink** works wonderfully.

**How to do it:**

1. Pour about 100 mL of water into a clear glass and add one tiny crystal of KMnO₄ or one drop of dark food coloring. Stir well. The water turns a **deep purple or rich color**.
2. Using a dropper or a small measuring spoon, take **1 mL** of this dark solution and pour it into a second glass containing **9 mL of clean water**. Total volume = 10 mL. The color becomes **lighter**, but it is still clearly visible.
3. Take **1 mL** from this second glass and mix it into **9 mL of clean water** in a third glass. The color becomes paler still.
4. Repeat this step **5 to 8 times**, using a fresh glass of 9 mL clean water each time.

**What do you observe?** With each transfer the color fades, yet even **after 5 to 8 dilutions**, a faint tint is still visible—all from one tiny starting crystal or drop!

**Why?** This proves that a **single tiny crystal or drop contains millions upon millions of particles**, and each individual particle is so unimaginably small that even a tiny fraction of them can spread out and color an entire glass of water.

### Reasoning Example: Tracking the Dilution Factor

**Example 1 (How many times diluted?):** In each step, 1 mL of solution is mixed with 9 mL of water to make a total of 10 mL. That means each step makes the solution **10 times weaker (10 times diluted)**. If you repeat this process **n times**, by what factor is the original solution diluted?

After Step 1: 10 times diluted.
After Step 2: 10 × 10 = **100** times diluted.
After Step 3: 10 × 10 × 10 = **1,000** times diluted.
So **after n steps: 10ⁿ times diluted.**

Suppose the starting concentration ("strength") of the solution is **100 units**:

| Step | Concentration (units) | Dilution factor from start |
|---|---|---|
| 0 (start) | 100 | 1× |
| 1 | 10 | 10× |
| 2 | 1 | 100× |
| 3 | 0.1 | 1,000× |
| 4 | 0.01 | 10,000× |
| 5 | 0.001 | 100,000× |

**Check:** At each step we divide the concentration by 10: 100 ÷ 10 = 10; 10 ÷ 10 = 1; 1 ÷ 10 = 0.1; 0.1 ÷ 10 = 0.01; 0.01 ÷ 10 = 0.001. After 5 steps the dilution factor is 10⁵ = 100,000, and 100 ÷ 100,000 = 0.001 ✅

Notice that **after just 5 steps, the solution is 100,000 times weaker** than at the start, yet our eyes can still detect the color. Each particle must be astonishingly tiny!

Here is one more variation: if you mix **1 mL** of solution with **99 mL** of clean water (total 100 mL), you dilute it by a factor of **100** in a single step. Two such steps dilute it by 100 × 100 = **10,000 times**.

### Dissolving Sugar or Salt Is Evidence Too

Stir a spoonful of **salt or sugar** into a glass of water. The solid grains **disappear** completely. You cannot see a single grain anymore, yet every sip of the water tastes salty or sweet. That means each grain broke apart into particles too small to see, and those particles are **still right there** throughout the water. (Salt and sugar are safe kitchen ingredients; of course, never taste anything in a chemistry laboratory.)

```
  A single crystal (tiny grain)     After dissolving in water
  
      +---------+                   o  o   o    o   o
      | o o o o |                  o    o    o  o     o
      | o o o o |   on dissolving    o   o     o   o
      | o o o o |   ------------>  o    o   o      o   o
      +---------+                    o     o   o
   (visible grain)                 (particles spread everywhere;
                                    individual particles are invisible)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – When a crystal dissolves in water, its particles separate and spread throughout the water. Individual particles are too small to see, but their color or taste tells us they are present.

## Part D: Evidence 2: There Is Empty Space Between Particles

If the particles of matter were packed with zero room between them, **nothing could ever mix into anything else**. Yet substances mix all the time. That tells us there must be **empty space (interparticle space)** between particles. Let us look at three pieces of evidence.

### Evidence 2a: Water Level When Sugar or Salt Dissolves

Pour 100 mL of water into a clear glass and mark the exact water level on the outside with a marker or a strip of tape. Now add a spoonful of **sugar** and stir until it dissolves completely. Look closely at the mark: the **water level remains almost unchanged** (or rises far less than the volume of the spoonful of sugar). Where did the sugar go?

**Why?** Because there are **tiny empty spaces between the particles of water**. When the sugar dissolves, its separated particles **slip into those empty spaces**, much like smaller objects fitting into the gaps between larger ones.

**An everyday analogy:** Fill a jar to the brim with **glass marbles**. The jar looks completely full. Now pour **fine sand** into the jar and tap it gently—the sand trickles down into the **gaps between the marbles**, and the total level in the jar barely changes. In the same way, sugar particles fit into the spaces between water particles.

### Evidence 2b: Compressing Air in a Syringe

Take a clean **plastic medical syringe** without a needle (the kind used for measuring liquid medicine). Pull the plunger halfway back so the barrel fills with air. Now press your **fingertip firmly over the nozzle** to seal the tip, and push the plunger inward.

You will notice that the plunger **moves inward quite a bit**—the air inside is **compressed** into a smaller volume. That means there was **plenty of empty space** between the air particles, and pushing the plunger forced those particles closer together.

Now fill the same syringe with **water**, seal the nozzle with your finger, and try pushing the plunger. This time the plunger **barely budges**, no matter how hard you push. That tells us that water particles have **much less empty space** between them than air particles do.

> For now, we simply note that air has large spaces between its particles while water has very small spaces. In Topic 2, we will compare solids, liquids, and gases in full detail.

### Evidence 2c: Mixing Two Liquids (Lab Observation)

In a laboratory, if a teacher carefully measures **50 mL of water** and **50 mL of rubbing alcohol (ethanol)** and mixes them together, the combined volume turns out to be **slightly less than 100 mL** (around 97 mL). Why doesn't 50 mL + 50 mL equal 100 mL? Because the particles of one liquid **tuck into the empty spaces** between the particles of the other liquid.

```
  Before mixing                  After mixing

  Water particles   Alcohol        Mixed together
    o  o  o          x   x         o x  o  x o
    o  o  o          x   x         x o  x  o x
    o  o  o          x   x         o x  o  x o

   (spaces in       (spaces in     (particles of one slip into the
    between)         between)       spaces of the other; total
                                    volume is slightly smaller)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – When water and alcohol are mixed, particles of one liquid fit into the interparticle spaces of the other, making the total volume slightly less than the sum of the two separate volumes.

## Part E: Evidence 3: Particles Are Constantly Moving

Particles of matter **never sit completely still**; they are in continuous motion. Our opening story gave us our first clue.

### Diffusion: The Scent Across the Room

Mr. Parker explained to Maya, "When the scented candle warms up—or when an incense stick burns—some of its **fragrance particles escape into the air**. The air particles are constantly moving, and so are the fragrance particles. They zip around, collide with one another, bounce off in random directions, and gradually **spread throughout the entire room**. When those particles reach your nose, you smell the vanilla."

**Maya:** "But Mr. Parker, if gas particles move so fast, why didn't I smell the candle the very instant you lit it? Why did it take a minute?"

**Mr. Parker:** "Because a fragrance particle cannot travel across the room in a straight, unobstructed line. At every tiny fraction of a millimeter, it **collides with billions of air particles** and gets knocked zigzagging in new directions. So even though individual particles move fast, spreading across the whole room takes a little time."

This automatic mixing of particles of two substances on their own is called **diffusion**.

> **Diffusion = the spontaneous mixing of particles of two substances due to their continuous motion, without any stirring.**

```
  At first (scent particles in one corner)     After a short while

  +---------------------------+              +---------------------------+
  | *                         |              |  *   .  o  *  .  o   .    |
  | * *                       |              |   . *  o   .  *  .  o  .  |
  | * * *                     |   ------->   |  o  .  *  o  .  * .    o  |
  |                           |              |   .  o   * . o   .  *  .  |
  +---------------------------+              +---------------------------+
   (scent particles * clustered)              (scent particles * spread across
                                               the room among air particles . and o)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Diffusion: fragrance particles (*) move and collide among air particles (. and o), gradually spreading throughout the entire room.

### Diffusion Happens in Liquids Too

Place a glass of water on a table and let it sit until the water is completely still. Gently place **one drop of food coloring or ink** (or a crystal of KMnO₄) into the water. **Do not stir.**

Watch patiently: on its own, ribbons of color slowly spread out until the **entire glass of water is evenly colored**. That is diffusion happening in a liquid—though you will notice it is **much slower than in air**.

> This proves that particles are constantly moving in **liquids** too, just at a slower pace and in tighter quarters than in gases.

### Heating Speeds Up Diffusion

Take two identical clear glasses. Fill one with **cold water** (chilled in the fridge) and the other with **warm water** (warm to the touch, not scalding hot). At the exact same moment, gently add **one drop of food coloring or ink** to each glass without stirring.

**What do you observe?** The ink spreads **much faster in the warm water** and **much more slowly in the cold water**.

**Why?** When temperature increases, particles move **faster**. Faster-moving water and ink particles jostle and spread through one another much more quickly.

> **Higher temperature → faster particle motion → faster diffusion.**

### Reasoning Example: Comparing Rates of Diffusion

**Example 2 (Warm vs. cold water):** In a classroom experiment, a drop of ink takes **180 seconds** to spread evenly through a glass of **cold water**. In an identical glass of **warm water**, an identical drop of ink takes only **60 seconds** to spread evenly. How many times faster is diffusion in the warm water? And if the ink travels an average distance of 6 cm from top to bottom in 180 s in cold water, what is its average spreading speed in each glass?

(These numbers are illustrative values for this specific setup; real times depend on glass size, temperature, and ink type.)

- Time in cold water = 180 s; time in warm water = 60 s
- How many times faster = 180 ÷ 60 = **3 times faster**

Now let us check the average spreading speed over the **same 6 cm distance**:
- Cold water speed = 6 cm ÷ 180 s = **0.033 cm/s**
- Warm water speed = 6 cm ÷ 60 s = **0.1 cm/s**

**Check:** Ratio of speeds = 0.1 ÷ 0.033 ≈ **3** times faster ✅. Because the particles in warm water cover the same distance in one-third the time, their average rate of diffusion is 3 times greater.

## Part F: Evidence 4: Particles Attract One Another

If all particles are constantly moving, why doesn't everything around us just fly apart into dust? Why doesn't an iron nail crumble in your hand? Because there is a **force of attraction** between particles that holds them together. However, the **strength of this attraction varies** from one substance to another.

### Attraction Is Not Equally Strong in Everything

Compare three everyday items: an **iron nail**, a piece of **chalk**, and a glass of **water**.

| Object | What happens when you try to break or separate it | Strength of particle attraction |
|---|---|---|
| **Iron nail** | Virtually **impossible** to break by hand | **Very strong** attraction |
| **Piece of chalk** | Snaps easily between your fingers | **Weaker** attraction |
| **Water** | Your finger passes right through it; it flows easily | **Even weaker** attraction (though still present!) |

### Activity: Steel Wire, Chalk, and a Rubber Band

1. Take a piece of **thin steel wire** (or a sturdy metal paperclip) and try to pull it apart by hand. It refuses to break because the attraction between steel particles is **extremely strong**. (Be careful not to poke your fingers.)
2. Take a piece of **chalk** and snap it in two. It breaks easily because the attraction holding its particles together is much weaker.
3. Stretch a **rubber band**. It lengthens under your pull, and when you let go, it snaps right back to its original shape—showing that its particles pull on one another to stay together.

### Why Do Water Droplets Merge?

Place **two small drops of water** close together on a clean waxed plate or plastic lid, and gently nudge one drop toward the other with a toothpick. The moment they touch, they **snap together into a single larger drop**. That happens because water particles **attract one another**.

```
   Before touching          After touching

    ( o o )   ( o o )         ( o o o o )
    ( o o )   ( o o )         ( o o o o )

   two separate drops       one combined drop
```

> **Figure 1.4** – When two small water droplets come into contact, the force of attraction between water particles pulls them together into a single larger drop.

Keep this big picture in mind: this **interparticle attraction** is what holds matter together, and its strength **differs from substance to substance**. That is why iron is rigid and strong, rubber is stretchy, and water flows.

## Part G: Bringing All Four Pieces of Evidence Together

At the end of the session, Mr. Parker turned back to Maya. "Alright, Maya—now put the whole puzzle together. How did the vanilla scent from the candle in my corner reach you by the door?"

Maya thought for a moment and replied: "Air is matter, so it is made of particles. There is **empty space** between those air particles, which lets the candle's fragrance particles slip in. Both kinds of particles are **constantly moving**, so they collide and spread across the room by diffusion. And the individual fragrance particles are **so tiny** that I can't see them floating in the air, even though my nose can detect them!"

Mr. Parker applauded. "Bravo! You just used three of our four pieces of evidence in a single explanation."

| Evidence | What It Shows | Our Key Activity / Observation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Particles are **extremely small** | Serial dilution: color stays visible even after 5–8 tenfold dilutions |
| 2 | Particles have **empty space** between them | Sugar dissolving without raising the water level; compressing air in a syringe |
| 3 | Particles are **constantly moving** | Diffusion of candle scent in air and ink in warm vs. cold water |
| 4 | Particles **attract one another** | Iron nail vs. chalk vs. water; two water droplets merging |

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Air is not matter because it is invisible."**

Being visible is not a requirement for matter. Air has mass (the inflated balloon tips the balance) and takes up space (the upside-down glass keeps the tissue dry). Therefore, air is matter.

**Mistake 2: "Light, sound, and heat are also forms of matter."**

No. Light, sound, and heat are forms of **energy**. They have neither mass nor volume. Only things that have both mass and volume are matter.

**Mistake 3: "When sugar dissolves in water, it vanishes into nothing."**

It does not vanish. The sugar grains break down into microscopic particles that slip into the empty spaces between water particles. That is why the water tastes sweet (and if you evaporate the water, you get the solid sugar right back).

**Mistake 4: "Diffusion only happens if we stir or fan the substance."**

No. Diffusion happens **on its own** because particles are already in constant motion. Stirring or fanning mixes things faster in bulk, but true diffusion happens even in a completely still glass or room.

**Mistake 5: "Water looks continuous and full, so there cannot be any empty space inside it."**

The fact that a spoonful of sugar dissolves into water without noticeably raising the water level proves that tiny interparticle spaces exist between water particles.

**Mistake 6: "A 10-fold dilution means adding 10 mL of water to 1 mL of solution."**

Watch your totals! Mixing **1 mL** of solution with **9 mL** of water gives a **total volume of 10 mL**, which is a **10-fold dilution**. If you add 10 mL of water to 1 mL of solution, the total volume becomes 11 mL, which is an **11-fold** dilution, not 10-fold.

## Quick Recap

- **Matter** is anything that has **mass** and **takes up space (volume)**. Air is matter; light, sound, heat, and emotions are not.
- The upside-down glass activity proves air **takes up space**; the balanced balloon activity proves air has **mass**.
- All matter is made of tiny **particles** (which you will later know as atoms or molecules).
- **Evidence 1 (Particles are tiny):** A single crystal of KMnO₄ or drop of ink still shows color after 5–8 tenfold dilutions. After *n* tenfold steps, a solution is **10ⁿ times diluted**.
- **Evidence 2 (Space between particles):** Dissolving sugar in water barely changes the water level; air in a sealed syringe can be compressed; mixing 50 mL water + 50 mL alcohol gives slightly less than 100 mL.
- **Evidence 3 (Particles are constantly moving):** Particles spread and mix on their own (**diffusion**). Heating makes particles move faster, speeding up diffusion.
- **Evidence 4 (Particles attract each other):** The force of attraction is very strong in an iron nail, weaker in chalk, and weaker still in water (yet strong enough to pull water droplets together).

## Practice Questions

1. Define matter. Classify each of the following as matter or not matter: sound, an iron nail, air, heat, a feeling of joy, milk.

2. Describe a simple home activity to prove that air takes up space.

3. A dark dye solution is diluted by taking 1 mL of it and mixing it with 9 mL of clean water. This process is repeated 4 times in total. By what factor is the original solution diluted? If its starting concentration was 200 units, what is its final concentration?

4. 2 mL of food-coloring solution is mixed with 98 mL of clean water. By what factor has the solution been diluted?

5. When a spoonful of sugar dissolves in water, the water level barely rises. What does this tell us about the particles of water? Name one more activity that demonstrates the same property.

6. In a sealed syringe, air can be compressed easily, whereas water can barely be compressed at all. What does this tell us about the spaces between particles in air versus water?

7. A drop of ink takes 240 s to spread evenly through a glass of cold water and 80 s through an identical glass of warm water. How many times faster is diffusion in the warm water? What does this tell you about how temperature affects particle motion?

8. Explain why you can smell freshly baked bread or dinner cooking in the kitchen from another room even when no fan is running.

9. Why is a steel wire very difficult to break by hand while a stick of chalk snaps easily? Which property of particles does this illustrate?

10. State True or False, and correct the false statements:
    (a) Light is matter because it fills a room.
    (b) Diffusion occurs only in gases.
    (c) Particles of matter are in continuous motion.
    (d) Cold water shows faster diffusion than warm water.

### Answers

1. **Matter** is anything that has **mass** and **takes up space (volume)**. **Matter:** iron nail, air, milk. **Not matter:** sound, heat, a feeling of joy (these are forms of energy or emotions and have neither mass nor volume).

2. Crumple a dry paper towel into the bottom of a dry glass, turn the glass straight upside down, and push it into a bucket of water. The paper stays dry because the air inside the glass occupies space and prevents water from entering.

3. Each step dilutes the solution by a factor of 10. After 4 steps, the dilution factor is 10⁴ = **10,000 times**. Final concentration = 200 ÷ 10,000 = **0.02 units**.
   Check: 200 ÷ 10 = 20; 20 ÷ 10 = 2; 2 ÷ 10 = 0.2; 0.2 ÷ 10 = 0.02 ✅

4. Total volume = 2 mL + 98 mL = 100 mL. Dilution factor = 100 ÷ 2 = **50 times**.
   Check: 2 mL × 50 = 100 mL ✅

5. It tells us that there are **empty spaces (interparticle spaces)** between water particles, and the sugar particles fit into those spaces. Another activity: compressing air in a sealed syringe (or mixing 50 mL water with 50 mL alcohol).

6. Air has **large empty spaces** between its particles, allowing them to be pushed closer together. Water has **very little empty space** between its particles, so they cannot be pushed much closer.

7. Diffusion in warm water is 240 ÷ 80 = **3 times faster**.
   Check: 80 × 3 = 240 ✅. This shows that particles move faster at higher temperatures, causing faster mixing.

8. Aroma particles from the hot food evaporate into the air and collide with moving air particles, spreading across the house by **diffusion** until they reach your nose.

9. The **force of attraction** between particles is very strong in steel, holding them tightly together, whereas it is much weaker in chalk. This illustrates that **particles of matter attract one another, and the strength of attraction varies from substance to substance**.

10. (a) **False** ❌. Light is a form of energy; it has no mass and does not occupy volume, so it is not matter. (b) **False** ❌. Diffusion also occurs in liquids (such as ink spreading in water), just more slowly than in gases. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌. Warm water shows faster diffusion than cold water because particles move faster at higher temperatures.
