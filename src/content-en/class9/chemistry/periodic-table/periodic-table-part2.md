# Topic 2: Mendeleev's Periodic Table

In our previous topic, we traced the earliest attempts to bring order to the chemical elements. Dobereiner grouped elements into triads (such as Lithium, Sodium, and Potassium), but only a handful of elements fit into his scheme. Newlands arranged elements by increasing atomic mass and discovered that properties repeated every eighth element, but his Law of Octaves broke down immediately after Calcium and forced incompatible elements into the same columns. 

Yet from these early stumbles came a profound insight: **a truly successful classification does not merely file away known facts—it possesses predictive power.** 

Today's story belongs to a visionary Russian chemist whose chart did something unprecedented: **he left deliberate blank spaces for undiscovered elements, accurately predicted their properties years before they were found, and watched the scientific community marvel as nature proved him right.**

## The Torn Calendar and the Missing Day

Liam was sorting through an old box of stationery when he found a **torn monthly calendar page** from November 2026. A small section of the paper had been ripped away, leaving a blank hole where the date of the **18th** should have been. 

Liam thought for a moment and smiled at his older sister, Elena. "Elena, even though this piece of paper is torn, I know exactly what day of the week the 18th fell on."

Elena raised an eyebrow playfully. "How can you be sure? The date box is completely gone."

Liam pointed to the surrounding grid. "Look at the pattern! The 17th is clearly marked as a **Tuesday**, and the 19th is a **Thursday**. The missing day must sit between Tuesday and Thursday—so it has to be a **Wednesday**. Furthermore, look down the Wednesday column: dates 4, 11, and 25 are all there. The 18th fits squarely into that same vertical column."

Elena clapped her hands. "Excellent deductive reasoning! You recognized the fundamental **repeating pattern** of a calendar: days of the week line up vertically, and the structure of the surrounding grid tells you what must occupy the empty space."

Now, imagine someone creating a "calendar" of the chemical elements with deliberate blank spots, boldly declaring: *"An undiscovered element belongs in this empty space, and here are its exact mass, density, and chemical reactions."* A few years later, prospectors and chemists find the exact element in a mineral mine! That visionary was the Russian chemist **Dmitri Mendeleev**. Let us discover how he achieved this historic feat.

## Part A: Mendeleev's Revolutionary Concept (1869)

In 1869, **Dmitri Mendeleev** set out to write a new textbook of chemistry. At that time, approximately 63 elements were known, but they resembled an unorganized catalog of disjointed facts. Mendeleev wrote the name, atomic mass, and characteristic properties of each known element onto individual cards and spent weeks shuffling them like a deck of playing cards.

From this card-sorting investigation emerged three revolutionary principles:

1. **Order of atomic masses:** When elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic mass, chemical and physical properties repeat at regular intervals.
2. **Horizontal periods and vertical groups:** Whenever properties began to repeat, Mendeleev started a new horizontal row (**period**), aligning elements with similar chemical behaviours in the same vertical column (**group**).
3. **Chemical behaviour takes precedence over numerical mass:** Mendeleev insisted that chemistry must dictate the arrangement. If placing an element strictly by atomic mass put it into a column where it did not chemically belong, he concluded that either the atomic mass had been measured incorrectly or the order needed slight adjustment. Most boldly, if a position was vacant, **he left a blank space**, asserting that nature simply had not yet yielded that particular element to human discovery.

In Mendeleev's era, one of the most reliable ways to evaluate chemical behaviour was by analyzing **oxide formulas**—the compounds formed when elements react with oxygen:

| Group | Group I | Group II | Group III | Group IV |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Example Element** | Sodium ($\text{Na}$) | Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) | Aluminium ($\text{Al}$) | Silicon ($\text{Si}$) |
| **Valency** | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
| **Oxide Formula** | $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$ | $\text{MgO}$ | $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ | $\text{SiO}_2$ |

*Verification:*
- In $\text{Na}_2\text{O}$: two $\text{Na}^+$ ions balance one $\text{O}^{2-}$ ion: $2 \times 1 = 2$ ✅.
- In $\text{MgO}$: one $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ion balances one $\text{O}^{2-}$ ion: $2 = 2$ ✅.
- In $\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$: two $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ions balance three $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions: $2 \times 3 = 6$ and $3 \times 2 = 6$ ✅.
- In $\text{SiO}_2$: one $\text{Si}^{4+}$ ion balances two $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions: $4 = 2 \times 2$ ✅.

Mendeleev noticed that as you move from Group I to Group IV, the element's combining capacity with oxygen increases systematically: Group I forms $\text{R}_2\text{O}$, Group II forms $\text{RO}$, Group III forms $\text{R}_2\text{O}_3$, and Group IV forms $\text{RO}_2$. This regular progression allowed him to predict the exact formula of compounds formed by undiscovered elements.

**Mendeleev's Periodic Law:**
> *"The physical and chemical properties of the elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses."*

In simple terms: as atomic mass increases, elements with similar personalities reappear in a recurring cycle, just like days of the week reappear on a calendar grid.

### A Compact Mendeleev-Style Table

Here is a simplified excerpt representing how Mendeleev organized his rows and columns, with approximate atomic masses. The question marks (**?**) indicate **deliberate gaps** left for undiscovered elements:

```
 Group:    I         II        III       IV        V         VI        VII

 Row 3:    Na (23)   Mg (24)   Al (27)   Si (28)   P (31)    S (32)    Cl (35.5)

 Row 4:    K (39)    Ca (40)   ? (44)    Ti (48)   V (51)    Cr (52)   Mn (55)

 Row 5:    Cu (63.5) Zn (65)   ? (68)    ? (72)    As (75)   Se (79)   Br (80)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – A section of Mendeleev's periodic arrangement (Rows 3 to 5, Groups I to VII). Notice how elements in each vertical group share similar chemistry: Group I contains reactive metals ($\text{Na}, \text{K}, \text{Cu}$), while Group VII contains halogens ($\text{Cl}, \text{Br}$). The three question marks represent intentional gaps: one between Calcium and Titanium (predicted mass $\sim 44$), and two between Zinc and Arsenic (predicted masses $\sim 68$ and $\sim 72$).

Why did Mendeleev leave a gap between Calcium ($\text{Ca} = 40$) and Titanium ($\text{Ti} = 48$)? Because Titanium forms an oxide $\text{TiO}_2$, showing a valency of 4—it behaved chemically like Silicon, not like Aluminium! If Mendeleev had placed Titanium immediately after Calcium, it would have fallen into Group III, ruining the chemical harmony of the column. By boldly leaving slot 44 blank, Titanium fell naturally under Silicon in Group IV, and Mendeleev declared that an undiscovered Group III element with an atomic mass near 44 would eventually be discovered.

## Part B: Three Famous Predictions: Eka-Boron, Eka-Aluminium, Eka-Silicon

Mendeleev did not merely say "something goes here." He published detailed dossiers predicting the atomic mass, physical state, density, and chemical formulas for the missing elements. To name them, he attached the Sanskrit prefix **eka-** (meaning "first after") to the element directly above the gap:
- **Eka-boron:** The missing element directly below Boron.
- **Eka-aluminium:** The missing element directly below Aluminium.
- **Eka-silicon:** The missing element directly below Silicon.

How did Mendeleev estimate these atomic masses? He used the mathematical averages of the surrounding elements in the grid:
- *Vertical average:* Aluminium (27) and Indium (114.8): $(27 + 114.8) / 2 = \mathbf{70.9}$.
- *Horizontal average:* Zinc (65) and Arsenic (75): $(65 + 75) / 2 = \mathbf{70.0}$.

Both cross-averages hover around 70 to 71, leading Mendeleev to predict an atomic mass of approximately **68** for Eka-aluminium. This was not guesswork; it was mathematical interpolation guided by nature's pattern!

### Predicted versus Real

| Predicted Element | Mendeleev's Prediction (Mass) | Actual Element Discovered | Year of Discovery | Actual Mass | Absolute Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Eka-aluminium** | $\approx 68$ | **Gallium ($\text{Ga}$)** | 1875 | 69.7 | 1.7 |
| **Eka-boron** | $\approx 44$ | **Scandium ($\text{Sc}$)** | 1879 | 45.0 | 1.0 |
| **Eka-silicon** | $\approx 72$ | **Germanium ($\text{Ge}$)** | 1886 | 72.6 | 0.6 |

*Accuracy check:*
- For Gallium: $|69.7 - 68| = 1.7$ (a percentage difference of only $2.4\%$).
- For Scandium: $|45.0 - 44| = 1.0$ (a percentage difference of only $2.2\%$).
- For Germanium: $|72.6 - 72| = 0.6$ (an astounding percentage error of less than $0.8\%$).

### Eka-Aluminium and Gallium: The Metal That Melts in Your Hand

Let us place Mendeleev's 1871 prediction side by side with the experimental measurements obtained when French chemist Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran discovered **Gallium** in 1875:

```
 Property                Mendeleev's Prediction (1871)    Actual Gallium Discovered (1875)
                         (Eka-aluminium)                  (Discovered by Lecoq de Boisbaudran)
 ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
 Atomic Mass             $\approx 68$                     69.7
 Density                 $\approx 5.9\text{ g/cm}^3$      $5.94\text{ g/cm}^3$
 Melting Behaviour       Low melting point                Melts at $29.8^\circ\text{C}$
 Oxide Formula           $\text{Ea}_2\text{O}_3$          $\text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3$
                         (Group III valency = 3)          (Valency = 3)
 Chloride Formula        $\text{EaCl}_3$                  $\text{GaCl}_3$
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Side-by-side comparison between Mendeleev's theoretical prediction (Eka-aluminium) and the experimental reality of Gallium. Notice the remarkable agreement: predicted density of $5.9\text{ g/cm}^3$ vs. measured $5.94\text{ g/cm}^3$, and identical trivalent compound formulas.

**The fascinating property of Gallium:** Gallium has an exceptionally low melting point of just **$29.8^\circ\text{C}$** ($85.6^\circ\text{F}$). Normal human body temperature is approximately **$37.0^\circ\text{C}$**. 

Because $37.0 - 29.8 = \mathbf{7.2^\circ\text{C}}$, a piece of solid gallium placed gently on the palm of your hand will absorb body heat and **melt into a shimmering liquid puddle of metal** right before your eyes! On a warm summer afternoon in many parts of the world, ambient room temperature can easily exceed $30^\circ\text{C}$, keeping gallium in liquid form on a laboratory shelf. (Gallium should only be handled under teacher supervision in school labs.)

### Eka-Silicon and Germanium

For Eka-silicon, Mendeleev predicted an atomic mass of $\approx 72$, a density of $\approx 5.5\text{ g/cm}^3$, and an oxide formula of $\text{EsO}_2$. In 1886, German chemist Clemens Winkler isolated **Germanium ($\text{Ge}$)**: its mass was **72.6**, density was $5.32\text{ g/cm}^3$, and its oxide was **$\text{GeO}_2$**. Because Silicon has valency 4, its oxide is $\text{SiO}_2$; Germanium followed the identical formula $\text{GeO}_2$ with textbook precision!

### Eka-Boron and Scandium

For Eka-boron, Mendeleev forecasted an atomic mass of $\approx 44$. In 1879, Lars Fredrik Nilson discovered **Scandium ($\text{Sc}$)** with an atomic mass of **45.0** and an oxide formula $\text{Sc}_2\text{O}_3$, confirming Group III valency behaviour.

### The Moral of This Story

The fulfillment of these three predictions sent shockwaves through the scientific world. When Gallium, Scandium, and Germanium were unearthed and found to match Mendeleev's descriptions down to physical densities and chemical formulas, skepticism evaporated. Scientists realized that Mendeleev's periodic table was not a clever human invention—it reflected a **profound, universal law of nature**.

## Part C: Merits of Mendeleev's Periodic Table

1. **Unrivaled predictive power:** Mendeleev successfully predicted the existence and properties of undiscovered elements (Gallium, Scandium, Germanium), leaving deliberate gaps rather than forcing elements into artificial slots.
2. **Systematic grouping:** Elements were organized into logical families with shared valencies and formulas (such as oxides and hydrides), turning chemistry into a predictable science.
3. **Correction of doubtful atomic masses:** The table's pattern helped identify inaccurately measured atomic masses. For instance, Beryllium had previously been assigned an atomic mass of 13.5 (assuming a valency of 3). Mendeleev argued that Beryllium's chemistry placed it in Group II with valency 2, meaning its true atomic mass was $2 \times 4.5 = 9.0$, which experimental tests soon confirmed.
4. **Smooth accommodation of noble gases:** When unreactive noble gases (Helium, Neon, Argon) were discovered in the 1890s, they could be placed into a newly added column (**Group 0**) without disturbing the position of a single existing element.

## Part D: Anomalies and Limitations of Mendeleev's Table

No scientific pioneer achieves perfection on the first attempt. Mendeleev's table contained several critical flaws that pointed toward a need for a deeper organizing principle:

1. **The anomalous position of Hydrogen:** 
   Hydrogen behaves like an alkali metal (Group I) because it has 1 valence electron, forms positive ions ($\text{H}^+$), and combines with non-metals to form halides ($\text{HCl}$) and oxides ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$). However, Hydrogen also behaves like a halogen (Group VII) because it is a diatomic gas ($\text{H}_2$), a non-metal, and can gain an electron to form hydrides ($\text{NaH}$, like $\text{NaCl}$). Mendeleev could not assign Hydrogen a fixed, unambiguous position.

2. **No place for isotopes:** 
   As we learned in our atomic structure unit, **isotopes** are atoms of the same element that have the same atomic number but different atomic masses (e.g., Chlorine-35 and Chlorine-37). If a periodic table is organized strictly by increasing atomic mass, Chlorine-35 and Chlorine-37 would each require **their own separate slots**. Yet chemically, both isotopes behave identically! Mendeleev's table offered no logical way to house isotopes.

3. **Inverted pairs (anomalous mass order):** 
   To preserve chemical groupings, Mendeleev was forced to place several slightly heavier elements **before** lighter elements:

| Inverted Pair | Atomic Masses | Expected Mass Order | Mendeleev's Chemical Order | Reason for Inversion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Ar and K** | $\text{Ar} = 39.95$, $\text{K} = 39.10$ | $\text{K}$ before $\text{Ar}$ | $\text{Ar}$ before $\text{K}$ | $\text{Ar}$ is a noble gas; $\text{K}$ is an alkali metal |
| **Co and Ni** | $\text{Co} = 58.93$, $\text{Ni} = 58.69$ | $\text{Ni}$ before $\text{Co}$ | $\text{Co}$ before $\text{Ni}$ | Chemical reactivity and resemblance to family |
| **Te and I** | $\text{Te} = 127.6$, $\text{I} = 126.9$ | $\text{I}$ before $\text{Te}$ | $\text{Te}$ before $\text{I}$ | $\text{I}$ must sit under Halogens ($\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}$) |

Let us examine the **Argon-Potassium pair** in detail:

```
 If strictly ordered by ATOMIC MASS:       Actual CHEMICAL REALITY:

 Potassium (39.10) placed first            Argon (2, 8, 8)     Unreactive noble gas (Octet)
 Argon (39.95) placed second               Potassium (2, 8, 8, 1) Highly reactive alkali metal

 Potassium would sit in the Noble Gas row,  Argon belongs with Helium and Neon.
 Argon would sit with Lithium and Sodium.   Potassium belongs with Lithium and Sodium.
 Both chemical families would be RUINED!    Order MUST be inverted: Argon first, Potassium second.
```

> **Figure 2.3** – The inverted pair dilemma for Argon (39.95) and Potassium (39.10). Ordering strictly by mass places potassium in the inert gas group and argon with flammable alkali metals. Mendeleev rightly prioritized chemical behaviour over mass, but this broke his own periodic law!

Mendeleev initially assumed that the atomic masses of Tellurium and Iodine had been measured with experimental error. But when repeated re-measurements confirmed the masses, chemists realized an uncomfortable truth: **atomic mass is not the true governing parameter of chemical periodicity.**

4. **Unknown fundamental cause of periodicity:** 
   In 1869, the internal structure of the atom was completely unknown. The electron was not discovered until 1897 (J.J. Thomson) and the atomic nucleus until 1911 (Ernest Rutherford). Mendeleev could show *that* properties repeated, but he could not explain *why* they repeated.

5. **Grouping dissimilar elements together:** 
   In some subgroups, unreactive coinage metals like Copper ($\text{Cu}$), Silver ($\text{Ag}$), and Gold ($\text{Au}$) were placed in Group I alongside furiously reactive alkali metals like Lithium and Sodium, simply because both can exhibit a valency of 1.

## Part E: Reasoning Solved Examples

### Example 1: Predicting Atomic Mass Across a Gap
In Group III, Aluminium has an atomic mass of 27 and Indium has an atomic mass of 114.8. In Row 5, Zinc has an atomic mass of 65 and Arsenic has an atomic mass of 75. Use cross-averages to estimate the mass of the missing element Gallium in that slot.

- Vertical average: $\frac{27 + 114.8}{2} = \mathbf{70.9}$
- Horizontal average: $\frac{65 + 75}{2} = \mathbf{70.0}$
- Both averages hover near 70. Actual mass of Gallium is 69.7. The difference is only $70.0 - 69.7 = \mathbf{0.3}$ units ✅.

### Example 2: Predicting Oxide Formulas
Eka-silicon belongs to Group IV directly below Silicon. What is the expected formula of its oxide?
- Silicon forms the oxide $\text{SiO}_2$ with a valency of 4.
- Because Eka-silicon belongs to the same group, its valency is 4. Oxygen has a valency of 2.
- Applying criss-cross valencies: $\text{Es}_2\text{O}_4 \rightarrow \mathbf{\text{EsO}_2}$ (which matches actual Germanium oxide, $\mathbf{\text{GeO}_2}$) ✅.

### Example 3: The Argon-Potassium Paradox
Potassium has an atomic mass of 39.10, whereas Argon has an atomic mass of 39.95. Why did Mendeleev place Argon before Potassium despite Argon being heavier?
- Potassium has an electron arrangement of 2, 8, 8, 1 with 1 valence electron; it is a violent alkali metal that must sit in Group I with Lithium and Sodium.
- Argon has an electron arrangement of 2, 8, 8 with a complete octet; it is an unreactive noble gas that must sit in Group 0.
- Placing them strictly by mass would have put Potassium in the noble gas group and Argon in the alkali metal group. Chemical reality forced Mendeleev to invert the pair ✅.

### Example 4: Tellurium and Iodine Inversion
Tellurium has a mass of 127.6 and Iodine has a mass of 126.9. Explain why Iodine was placed after Tellurium.
- Iodine is a halogen: it forms diatomic purple vapour, has valency 1, and chemically mirrors Chlorine and Bromine.
- Tellurium resembles Sulfur and Selenium (Group VI, valency 2).
- Placing Iodine before Tellurium based on mass would put Iodine in Group VI and Tellurium in Group VII, destroying both chemical families. Mendeleev correctly placed Tellurium first and Iodine second ✅.

### Example 5: Was Mendeleev Wrong to Invert Pairs?
A student argues: "By placing Argon before Potassium and Tellurium before Iodine, Mendeleev cheated on his own periodic law." How would you respond?
- Mendeleev made the scientifically correct choice. He recognized that chemical similarity is the primary truth of nature, while atomic mass was merely an imperfect proxy. This courageous decision signaled that another, more fundamental physical property was waiting to be discovered.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming all elements were known in Mendeleev's day.**
   In 1869, only about 63 elements were known. Entire families—such as the noble gases—and key transition metals had not yet been discovered. Leaving gaps was Mendeleev's greatest strength.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking Mendeleev's predictions were exact to every decimal.**
   Mendeleev predicted values with astonishing closeness (e.g., predicted 68 vs. actual 69.7 for Gallium), but they were approximations based on trends, not exact mathematical certainties.

3. **Mistake 3: Believing subatomic particles were understood in 1869.**
   Electrons (1897), protons, and atomic numbers (1913) were discovered decades after Mendeleev published his table. Mendeleev operated purely on macroscopic chemical observations and measured masses.

4. **Mistake 4: Dismissing Mendeleev's table because of inverted pairs.**
   The inverted pairs were not careless mistakes; they were deliberate, correct decisions prioritizing chemistry. They highlighted the limitations of atomic mass and paved the way for Moseley's discovery of atomic number.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating "Eka-aluminium" and "Eka-silicon" as real elemental symbols.**
   The prefix "Eka-" was merely a provisional placeholder name. Once discovered, the elements received permanent international names and symbols (Gallium, $\text{Ga}$; Germanium, $\text{Ge}$; Scandium, $\text{Sc}$).

## Quick Recap

- **Mendeleev (1869)** organized elements in order of increasing atomic mass while aligning chemically similar elements into vertical **groups**; horizontal rows are called **periods**.
- **Mendeleev's Periodic Law:** The properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses.
- Mendeleev left **deliberate gaps** and made specific quantitative predictions for three missing elements:
  - **Eka-aluminium** (predicted mass 68) $\rightarrow$ discovered as **Gallium** (69.7) in 1875.
  - **Eka-boron** (predicted mass 44) $\rightarrow$ discovered as **Scandium** (45.0) in 1879.
  - **Eka-silicon** (predicted mass 72) $\rightarrow$ discovered as **Germanium** (72.6) in 1886.
- **Gallium** has a remarkably low melting point of **$29.8^\circ\text{C}$**; body heat ($37^\circ\text{C}$) easily melts it in your hand.
- **Merits:** High predictive power, systematic organization of compounds (oxides/hydrides), corrected inaccurate atomic masses, and smoothly accommodated newly discovered noble gases.
- **Anomalies:** No fixed position for Hydrogen, no logical place for isotopes, three inverted mass pairs ($\text{Ar}-\text{K}$, $\text{Co}-\text{Ni}$, $\text{Te}-\text{I}$), and no theoretical explanation for why periodicity occurs.
- These anomalies proved that **atomic mass is not the fundamental property** governing chemical behaviour.

### Answers

1. Mendeleev arranged elements in order of increasing atomic mass while grouping chemically similar elements vertically. A **group** is a vertical column containing elements with similar chemical properties; a **period** is a horizontal row across which properties change progressively.

2. **Mendeleev's Periodic Law:** The properties of elements are periodic functions of their atomic masses. Just as Wednesday recurs every seven days in a calendar column, chemical properties recur after a regular interval of atomic masses.

3. Mendeleev left gaps because the next known element was too heavy or possessed completely incompatible chemical properties for that slot. For each gap, he predicted: (i) the approximate **atomic mass**, (ii) the **density**, and (iii) the **formulas of its oxide and chloride**.

4. 
   - **Eka-aluminium:** Predicted 68, actual Gallium 69.7 $\rightarrow$ Difference: $|69.7 - 68| = \mathbf{1.7}$.
   - **Eka-boron:** Predicted 44, actual Scandium 45.0 $\rightarrow$ Difference: $|45.0 - 44| = \mathbf{1.0}$.
   - **Eka-silicon:** Predicted 72, actual Germanium 72.6 $\rightarrow$ Difference: $|72.6 - 72| = \mathbf{0.6}$.
   The closest prediction was **Eka-silicon (Germanium)** with an error of only 0.6 mass units ✅.

5. Temperature difference: $37.0^\circ\text{C} - 29.8^\circ\text{C} = \mathbf{7.2^\circ\text{C}}$. Because body temperature is higher than Gallium's melting point, heat flows from the hand into the metal, causing solid Gallium to melt into a liquid.

6. Eka-aluminium belongs to Group III with a valency of 3, while Oxygen has a valency of 2. Applying the criss-cross rule:
   $$\text{Ea}^{3+} \quad \text{O}^{2-} \implies \mathbf{\text{Ea}_2\text{O}_3} \quad (\text{actual: } \mathbf{\text{Ga}_2\text{O}_3})$$
   $2 \times 3 = 6$ positive charges balance $3 \times 2 = 6$ negative charges ✅.

7. Three merits: (i) Successfully predicted the existence and properties of undiscovered elements (Gallium, Scandium, Germanium); (ii) Systematically organized elements into families by common valencies and compounds; (iii) Corrected erroneously measured atomic masses (such as Beryllium).

8. Noble gases were unreactive and had not been isolated in 1869. When discovered in the 1890s, they fit naturally as a brand-new group (**Group 0**) without requiring any existing element or group to be shifted.

9. Hydrogen has 1 valence electron and forms positive ions ($\text{H}^+$) like alkali metals ($\text{Li}, \text{Na}$), but it is also a non-metallic diatomic gas ($\text{H}_2$) that forms covalent compounds and hydrides like halogens ($\text{F}, \text{Cl}$). Because it shares traits with two distinct groups, it had no single home.

10. Isotopes are atoms of the same element with identical chemical properties but different atomic masses (e.g., Chlorine-35 and Chlorine-37). In a mass-based table, each isotope would require a separate position, which would scatter identical chemical elements across different columns.

11. Potassium ($2, 8, 8, 1$) has 1 valence electron and reacts violently with water—it belongs in Group I with Lithium and Sodium. Argon ($2, 8, 8$) has a complete outer octet and is completely inert—it belongs in the noble gas group. Ordering strictly by mass would have put Potassium in the noble gas slot and Argon in the alkali metal slot. Mendeleev inverted them to keep chemical families pure.

12. The three inverted pairs are: **$\text{Ar}-\text{K}$**, **$\text{Co}-\text{Ni}$**, and **$\text{Te}-\text{I}$**. In every case, Mendeleev prioritized **chemical behaviour and family similarity** over numerical atomic mass.

13. In Mendeleev's era, atoms were believed to be indivisible solid spheres. The subatomic particles responsible for chemical properties were discovered decades later: the **electron in 1897** (J.J. Thomson) and the **atomic nucleus in 1911** (Ernest Rutherford). Without knowledge of electronic configurations, the physical cause of periodicity remained a mystery.

14. (a) **False** ❌. Mendeleev's table was organized by atomic mass, not atomic number.
    (b) **True** ✅. Gallium was discovered in 1875, four years after Mendeleev predicted it.
    (c) **False** ❌. "Eka-aluminium" was a provisional placeholder name; the actual element is Gallium ($\text{Ga}$).
    (d) **True** ✅. Argon was placed before Potassium to ensure both fell into their chemically matching families.
