# Topic 4: Patterns Inside the Table: Periodic Trends

In the previous topic, we organized the elements into 7 horizontal rows (periods) and 18 vertical columns (groups) based strictly on their atomic numbers. The period number matched the count of electron shells, while the group number reflected the number of valence electrons.

However, the periodic table is far more than a tidy directory of atomic addresses. The real scientific marvel is that **properties change in regular, predictable patterns as you navigate across a period or down a group**. These repeating patterns of behaviour are called **periodic trends**. 

In this topic, we will uncover how atomic size, valency, metallic character, and chemical reactivity evolve across the table, using an intuitive mental model based on nuclear pull and electron shielding.

---

## The Winter Bonfire

On a chilly winter evening, Liam and Elena joined a community gathering around a roaring **central bonfire** in a village square. People had arranged their folding chairs in concentric circular rows around the fire.

Elena observed the crowd: "Notice how the people in the **very front row** have hot faces and glowing cheeks, while the people in the **outermost third row** are wrapped tightly in blankets and still shivering."

"Why the stark difference?" Liam asked. "The fire is burning bright for everyone."

Elena explained, "Two reasons! First, the farther away you sit from a heat source, the weaker the radiant warmth. Second, the people sitting in the inner rows physically block part of the warmth, **shielding** the folks in the back row. Now imagine what would happen if the festival organizers threw five large oak logs onto the fire, making the blaze twice as powerful."

Liam smiled. "The heat would become so intense that everyone in every row would instinctively pull their chairs closer together, making the whole circle shrink!"

"Spot on!" Elena beamed. "And that is an exact picture of an atom. The central bonfire is the **nucleus** of protons, the people are **electrons**, and the concentric circular rows are **electron shells**. Keep that bonfire in mind, because it explains every periodic trend across the entire periodic table."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Periodic trend** | A regular, systematic pattern of change in physical or chemical properties across a period or down a group | Valency cycling as $1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$ |
| **Nuclear pull** | The attractive electrostatic force exerted by positively charged protons in the nucleus on surrounding electrons | Greater atomic number $\rightarrow$ stronger nuclear pull |
| **Atomic size (atomic radius)** | The distance from the center of the nucleus to the outermost electron shell | $\text{K}$ is large; $\text{Li}$ is small |
| **Metallic character** | The ease with which an atom loses valence electrons to form positive ions (electropositive nature) | High in Sodium ($\text{Na}$); absent in Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$) |
| **Non-metallic character** | The ease with which an atom gains or shares electrons to complete its outer shell (electronegative nature) | High in Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$); absent in Sodium ($\text{Na}$) |
| **Metalloid** | An element with physical and chemical properties intermediate between metals and non-metals | Boron ($\text{B}$) and Silicon ($\text{Si}$) |
| **Staircase (zigzag line)** | The diagonal dividing line on the periodic table separating metals on the left from non-metals on the right | The boundary along $\text{B}$ and $\text{Si}$ |

## Part A: The Bonfire Mental Model

Let us translate our winter bonfire into the anatomy of an atom:
- **The central blaze (The Nucleus):** The more protons packed inside the nucleus, the stronger the positive electrostatic attraction pulling on all electrons.
- **The seating rings (Electron Shells):** The farther an electron shell is from the nucleus, the weaker the attractive pull felt by those electrons.
- **Inner seating rows (Inner Shell Shielding):** Full inner shells ($K, L, \text{etc.}$) act as an electrostatic screen, partially shielding the outermost valence electrons from the full attractive charge of the nucleus.
- **The outermost row (Valence Shell):** These outermost electrons govern all chemical bonding. Their distance from the nucleus determines whether an atom easily gives up electrons (acting as a metal) or hungrily pulls them in (acting as a non-metal).

```
   Lithium (2 occupied shells):      (+) ) e
                                           ^ Outermost electron is close to nucleus:
                                             stronger pull, compact atomic size.

   Potassium (4 occupied shells):    (+) ) ) ) e
                                               ^ Outermost electron is far from nucleus:
                                                 weaker pull, larger atomic size.

   (+) = Positive Nucleus (Fire)     ) = Filled Inner Shells (Shielding)     e = Valence Electron
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The Bonfire Model comparing Lithium ($\text{Li}$) and Potassium ($\text{K}$). In Lithium, with only 2 shells, the valence electron sits close to the central nuclear pull. In Potassium, with 4 shells, the outermost electron sits far away, heavily shielded by three full inner shells. As a result, Potassium is much larger and loses its valence electron far more readily.

From this physical picture, two golden rules govern periodic trends:
1. **Adding shells increases atomic size:** More shells place valence electrons farther away, making the atom larger and weakening the nuclear grip on outer electrons.
2. **Adding protons within the same shell decreases atomic size:** With no new shells added, an increased positive nuclear charge pulls the existing shells inward, shrinking the atom.

## Part B: Across a Period from Left to Right (Exemplified by Period 3)

Let us follow Period 3 from Sodium ($\text{Na}$) across to Argon ($\text{Ar}$). Every atom in Period 3 has exactly **3 occupied electron shells** ($K, L, M$). The difference is that with each step to the right, **1 proton is added to the nucleus and 1 electron is added to the same outermost $M$-shell**:

```
   Period 3:      Na      Mg      Al      Si      P       S       Cl      Ar

   Protons (Z)    11      12      13      14      15      16      17      18
   Configuration  2,8,1   2,8,2   2,8,3   2,8,4   2,8,5   2,8,6   2,8,7   2,8,8
   Valence e-     1       2       3       4       5       6       7       8
   Valency        1       2       3       4       3       2       1       0
   Shells         3       3       3       3       3       3       3       3

   -------->  Nuclear charge increases; shells remain constant  -------->
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Progression across Period 3. As atomic number $Z$ increases from 11 to 18, valence electrons advance from 1 to 8. Valency rises from 1 to 4 (electrons lost or shared) and then falls from 3 to 0 (electrons gained or octet filled: $8 - \text{valence } e^-$). Because all electrons reside in the same 3 shells while nuclear charge rises steadily, the electron cloud is pulled tighter and tighter.

This identical rhythm occurs in Period 2 ($\text{Li}$ to $\text{Ne}$): $\text{Li} (1), \text{Be} (2), \text{B} (3), \text{C} (4), \text{N} (3), \text{O} (2), \text{F} (1), \text{Ne} (0)$.

### Summary of Trends Across a Period (Left to Right)

| Property | Left Side ($\text{Na}, \text{Li}$) | Right Side ($\text{Cl}, \text{F}$) | Physical Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Valence Electrons** | 1 | 7 (then 8 for noble gas) | Each step adds one electron to the outermost shell |
| **Valency** | 1 (rises up to 4) | Falls to 1, then 0 at noble gas | Up to 4, electrons are lost/shared; beyond 4, electrons are gained ($8 - \text{valence } e^-$) |
| **Atomic Size** | Largest | Smallest | Shells stay constant while nuclear charge increases, pulling the shell closer |
| **Metallic Character** | Highest | Lowest | Stronger nuclear pull makes losing valence electrons increasingly difficult |
| **Non-Metallic Character** | Lowest | Highest | Stronger nuclear pull makes attracting external electrons much easier |

**Why does atomic size shrink across a period?**
This is often counter-intuitive to students: Chlorine has 17 electrons, yet it is significantly **smaller** than Sodium with 11 electrons! Why? 
Both Sodium and Chlorine have 3 electron shells. But Chlorine's nucleus packs **17 protons** compared to Sodium's **11 protons**. That extra positive pull exerts a powerful inward tug on the entire electron cloud. Returning to our bonfire: if the fire suddenly burns with 17 logs instead of 11, the people in row 3 huddle much closer to the center. (Note: Noble gases are excluded from this atomic radius comparison because their radii are measured using non-bonded van der Waals distances rather than covalent bonding radii.)

**Why does metallic character decrease across a period?**
Sodium's lone valence electron feels a relatively weak nuclear hold across 3 shells, so it is surrendered easily during reactions, exhibiting classic metallic behaviour. By contrast, Chlorine's 7 valence electrons are held tightly by 17 protons; losing 7 electrons is energetically impossible, but its intense nuclear attraction hungrily captures 1 external electron to complete its octet.

## Part C: Down a Group from Top to Bottom

Down any vertical group, all elements share the **same number of valence electrons**, but each successive period adds **one brand-new electron shell**:

| Element | Configuration | Occupied Shells | Valence $e^-$ | Valency | Group |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **$\text{Li}$** | 2, 1 | 2 ($K, L$) | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| **$\text{Na}$** | 2, 8, 1 | 3 ($K, L, M$) | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| **$\text{K}$** | 2, 8, 8, 1 | 4 ($K, L, M, N$) | 1 | 1 | 1 |

| Element | Configuration | Occupied Shells | Valence $e^-$ | Valency | Group |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **$\text{F}$** | 2, 7 | 2 ($K, L$) | 7 | 1 | 17 |
| **$\text{Cl}$** | 2, 8, 7 | 3 ($K, L, M$) | 7 | 1 | 17 |

| Element | Configuration | Occupied Shells | Valence $e^-$ | Valency | Group |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **$\text{Be}$** | 2, 2 | 2 ($K, L$) | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| **$\text{Mg}$** | 2, 8, 2 | 3 ($K, L, M$) | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| **$\text{Ca}$** | 2, 8, 8, 2 | 4 ($K, L, M, N$) | 2 | 2 | 2 |

Applying the Bonfire Model: with each step down, an entirely new layer of chairs is placed behind the existing rows. The outermost electrons sit progressively farther from the nucleus, shielded by an increasing number of full inner shells:

| Property | Trend Down a Group | Underlying Physical Cause |
|---|---|---|
| **Valence Electrons** | Remains constant | Defines group identity |
| **Valency** | Remains constant | Determined directly by valence electron count |
| **Occupied Shells** | Increases by 1 each step | $\text{Period number} = \text{number of shells}$ |
| **Atomic Size** | Increases steadily | Successive shells place outer electrons farther from nucleus |
| **Metallic Character** | Increases steadily | Outermost electrons are farther away and held loosely, making loss easier |

In Group 1, Potassium ($\text{K}$, 4 shells) surrenders its single valence electron far more readily than Lithium ($\text{Li}$, 2 shells). In Group 2, Calcium ($\text{Ca}$) is distinctly more metallic and electropositive than Beryllium ($\text{Be}$).

## Part D: Metals, Non-Metals, and Metalloids: The Geographic Map of the Table

Periodic trends divide the table into distinct chemical territories:
- **Metals:** Located on the **left and bottom** of the table. They have 1, 2, or 3 valence electrons, large atomic radii, low attraction for valence electrons, and readily form positive ions (cations).
- **Non-metals:** Located in the **upper right** corner. They have 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons, small atomic radii, strong nuclear pull, and readily gain or share electrons to form anions or covalent bonds.
- **Metalloids:** Clustered along an amphitheater-like **diagonal zigzag staircase** separating metals from non-metals. They show dual behaviour: shiny like metals, but brittle and semi-conductive like non-metals.

```
   Group       1    2     13   14   15   16   17   18
   Period 1    H                                   He
   Period 2    Li   Be   [B]   C    N    O    F    Ne
   Period 3    Na   Mg    Al  [Si]  P    S    Cl   Ar
   Period 4    K    Ca

   Territory Map (M = Metal, N = Non-metal, S = Metalloid):

   Group       1    2     13   14   15   16   17   18
   Period 1    N                                   N
   Period 2    M    M     S    N    N    N    N    N
   Period 3    M    M     M    S    N    N    N    N
   Period 4    M    M

   The Staircase: Runs diagonally through Boron [B] and Silicon [Si].
   To the left and below the staircase: METALS (M)
   To the right and above the staircase: NON-METALS (N)
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Geographic classification of the first 20 elements. Notice that **Boron ($\text{B}$) and Silicon ($\text{Si}$) are the only two metalloids** among the first 20 elements. Hydrogen is stationed at Group 1 for valence electron consistency, but it is chemically a non-metal gas.

Reading Period 3 from left to right demonstrates this smooth chemical transition:
$$\underbrace{\text{Na}, \quad \text{Mg}, \quad \text{Al}}_{\text{Metals (Lose } e^-)} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \underbrace{\text{Si}}_{\text{Metalloid}} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \underbrace{\text{P}, \quad \text{S}, \quad \text{Cl}}_{\text{Non-metals (Gain/Share } e^-)} \quad \longrightarrow \quad \underbrace{\text{Ar}}_{\text{Inert Noble Gas}}$$

## Part E: Chemical Reactivity: Alkali Metals and Halogens

Periodic trends explain why chemical families react with differing vigour:

### Group 1 (Alkali Metals: $\text{Li}, \text{Na}, \text{K}$): Reactivity Increases Down the Group
- Alkali metals react by **giving away** their single outermost valence electron.
- Down the group ($\text{Li} \rightarrow \text{Na} \rightarrow \text{K}$), new shells are added, pushing that valence electron farther from the nucleus while inner shells provide extra shielding.
- As the nuclear grip weakens, the valence electron is stripped away with greater ease.
- Therefore, reactivity increases down Group 1:
  $$\mathbf{\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K}}$$
  Potassium reacts violently with water, while Sodium reacts vigorously, and Lithium reacts steadily.

### Group 17 (Halogens: $\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$): Reactivity Decreases Down the Group
- Halogens react by **attracting and capturing** an incoming electron to complete their octet.
- At the top of the group, Fluorine ($\text{F}$) has only 2 shells; its nucleus sits very close to the outer edge, exerting an intense electrostatic pull on any incoming electron.
- Down the group ($\text{F} \rightarrow \text{Cl} \rightarrow \text{Br} \rightarrow \text{I}$), each added shell places the outer boundary farther from the nucleus, and inner electrons shield the positive nuclear charge.
- Consequently, pulling in an extra electron becomes more difficult.
- Therefore, reactivity decreases down Group 17:
  $$\mathbf{\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}}$$
  Fluorine is the most reactive non-metal in existence, followed by Chlorine, Bromine, and Iodine.

> **Safety Reminder:** Alkali metals react exothermically and explosively with moisture and air; halogens such as Chlorine and Bromine are corrosive and toxic. These substances are strictly demonstration materials handled only by professional educators in ventilated fume hoods—never attempted at home.

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Comparing Atomic Size: Sodium vs. Magnesium
Which atom is larger: Sodium ($\text{Na}$) or Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$)?
- Sodium ($Z = 11$, configuration: $2, 8, 1$) has 3 shells and 11 protons.
- Magnesium ($Z = 12$, configuration: $2, 8, 2$) has 3 shells and 12 protons.
- Both have the same number of shells, but Magnesium's 12 protons exert a stronger nuclear pull than Sodium's 11 protons, pulling its shells closer to the center.
- Therefore, **Sodium is larger than Magnesium ($\text{Na} > \text{Mg}$)** ✅.

### Example 2. Comparing Metallic Character: Sodium vs. Magnesium
Which element is more metallic: Sodium or Magnesium?
- Sodium needs to lose only 1 valence electron from a weaker nuclear hold. Magnesium must lose 2 valence electrons against a stronger nuclear charge of 12 protons.
- Therefore, **Sodium is more metallic than Magnesium** (it loses its valence electron more readily) ✅.

### Example 3. Lithium vs. Potassium: Size and Reactivity
Compare Lithium ($\text{Li}$) and Potassium ($\text{K}$) in size and reactivity.
- Lithium ($Z = 3$): configuration $2, 1$ (2 shells).
- Potassium ($Z = 19$): configuration $2, 8, 8, 1$ (4 shells).
- Potassium has 2 additional shells, making its radius substantially larger: **$\text{K}$ is larger than $\text{Li}$**.
- Because Potassium's valence electron sits far from the nucleus, it is lost with far greater ease: **$\text{K}$ is more reactive than $\text{Li}$**.
- Both have identical valency = 1 ✅.

### Example 4. Fluorine vs. Chlorine
Compare Fluorine ($\text{F}$) and Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$) in terms of size and reactivity.
- Fluorine has 2 shells ($2, 7$), while Chlorine has 3 shells ($2, 8, 7$).
- With an extra shell, **Chlorine is larger than Fluorine**.
- In Group 17, chemical reactivity depends on pulling in an electron. Fluorine's nucleus is closer to its outer boundary, so it attracts electrons much more aggressively: **Fluorine is more reactive than Chlorine ($\text{F} > \text{Cl}$)** ✅.

### Example 5. Beryllium vs. Magnesium
Compare Beryllium ($\text{Be}$, $Z = 4$) and Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$, $Z = 12$).
- Beryllium: $2, 2$ (2 shells, Group 2). Magnesium: $2, 8, 2$ (3 shells, Group 2).
- Both have 2 valence electrons and a valency of 2.
- Magnesium has 3 shells versus Beryllium's 2 shells, so **Magnesium is larger and more metallic** than Beryllium ✅.

### Example 6. Arranging Period 3 Elements by Decreasing Atomic Size
Arrange the elements $\text{Na}, \text{Mg}, \text{Al}, \text{Si}, \text{P}, \text{S}, \text{Cl}$ in order of decreasing atomic size.
- All 7 elements occupy Period 3 with 3 shells.
- Across a period, increasing nuclear charge pulls the shells tighter.
- Decreasing size order:
  $$\mathbf{\text{Na} > \text{Mg} > \text{Al} > \text{Si} > \text{P} > \text{S} > \text{Cl}}$$
  Sodium is the largest atom in Period 3; Chlorine is the smallest ✅.

### Example 7. Ordering $\text{K}, \text{Na}, \text{Li}$, and $\text{Be}$ by Size
Arrange $\text{K}, \text{Na}, \text{Li}$, and $\text{Be}$ from largest to smallest.
- Shell counts: $\text{K}$ has 4 shells; $\text{Na}$ has 3 shells; $\text{Li}$ and $\text{Be}$ have 2 shells.
- More shells yield larger atoms: $\text{K} > \text{Na} > (\text{Li}, \text{Be})$.
- Between $\text{Li}$ (3 protons) and $\text{Be}$ (4 protons): both have 2 shells, but $\text{Be}$ has more protons, making it smaller than $\text{Li}$.
- Final decreasing order:
  $$\mathbf{\text{K} > \text{Na} > \text{Li} > \text{Be}}$$ ✅.

### Example 8. Identifying Element with Configuration $2, 8, 5$
An element has the electron configuration $2, 8, 5$. Identify its identity, period, group, valency, and metallic nature.
- Total electrons $= 2 + 8 + 5 = 15 \rightarrow Z = 15$: **Phosphorus ($\text{P}$)**.
- 3 occupied shells $\rightarrow$ **Period 3**.
- 5 valence electrons $\rightarrow \text{Group} = 5 + 10 = \mathbf{15}$.
- Valency $= 8 - 5 = \mathbf{3}$.
- Located to the right of the staircase $\rightarrow$ **Non-metal** ✅.

### Example 9. The Mystery Element
An element belongs to Period 3, has a valency of 4, and sits directly on the staircase dividing metals from non-metals. Identify it.
- In Period 3, the element with 4 valence electrons is Silicon ($Z = 14$, configuration: $2, 8, 4$).
- Sitting on the staircase, it is a **metalloid**: **Silicon ($\text{Si}$)** ✅.

### Example 10. Magnesium vs. Calcium
Compare Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$) and Calcium ($\text{Ca}$) in terms of metallic character.
- Magnesium ($2, 8, 2$) has 3 shells; Calcium ($2, 8, 8, 2$) has 4 shells.
- Both have 2 valence electrons.
- Calcium's valence electrons are farther from the nucleus, experiencing greater inner shielding, and are lost more readily.
- Therefore, **Calcium is more metallic and reactive than Magnesium** ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing atomic size increases from left to right across a period.**
   Adding electrons within the same shell does not expand the atom. Because additional protons increase nuclear attraction, the electron cloud is drawn inward, making atoms **smaller** across a period.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking valency increases down a group.**
   Valency is dictated by valence electrons, which remain constant down a group. Lithium, Sodium, and Potassium all have a valency of 1.

3. **Mistake 3: Equating total electron count with atomic size across a period.**
   Chlorine has 17 electrons and Sodium has only 11, but Chlorine is smaller because 17 protons pull the 3 shells much tighter than 11 protons do.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing all metals react with the same intensity.**
   Metallic reactivity varies dramatically. Group 1 alkali metals react with cold water instantly, while Group 2 metals react more moderately, and transition metals like gold are practically inert.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming reactivity trends are identical for alkali metals and halogens.**
   Alkali metals react by *losing* electrons, so reactivity increases down the group. Halogens react by *gaining* electrons, so reactivity decreases down the group.

6. **Mistake 6: Searching for metalloids outside the staircase line.**
   Among the first 20 elements, only **Boron ($\text{B}$)** and **Silicon ($\text{Si}$)** are classified as metalloids.

7. **Mistake 7: Misclassifying noble gases as reactive non-metals.**
   Although noble gases sit on the non-metallic right side of the table, their full outer octets give them a valency of 0 and exceptional chemical inertness.

## Quick Recap

- **Periodic trend:** The regular, predictable variation of chemical and physical properties across periods and down groups.
- **The Bonfire Model:** The nucleus acts as a central fire; electron shells act as seating rows; inner shells shield outer electrons from nuclear pull.
- **Across a Period (Left to Right):**
  - Shell count remains constant.
  - Nuclear charge ($Z$) increases by 1 each step.
  - Valence electrons increase from 1 to 8.
  - Valency rises from 1 to 4, then drops to 0 ($1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$).
  - Atomic size **decreases**.
  - Metallic character **decreases**; non-metallic character **increases**.
- **Down a Group (Top to Bottom):**
  - Valence electrons and valency remain constant.
  - Number of shells increases by 1 each period.
  - Atomic size **increases**.
  - Metallic character **increases** as valence electrons are held more loosely.
- **Geography:** Metals dominate the left and lower regions; non-metals occupy the upper right; metalloids ($\text{B}$ and $\text{Si}$ among the first 20) line the diagonal staircase.
- **Reactivity differences:**
  - Group 1 (Alkali Metals): Reactivity **increases** down the group ($\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K}$).
  - Group 17 (Halogens): Reactivity **decreases** down the group ($\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$).

8. Metals are positioned on the **left and bottom** of the table; non-metals are located in the **upper right**; metalloids sit along the **diagonal boundary staircase**. The two metalloids among the first 20 elements are **Boron ($\text{B}$)** and **Silicon ($\text{Si}$)**.

9. In Group 1, reactivity **increases** down the group ($\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K}$) because metals react by losing their valence electron, which becomes easier as distance and shielding increase. In Group 17, reactivity **decreases** down the group ($\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$) because halogens react by pulling in an external electron, which becomes harder as the valence shell moves farther from the nucleus. These chemicals are hazardous (alkali metals react violently with moisture; halogens are toxic and corrosive) and must never be tested outside professional laboratories.

10. Configuration $2, 8, 6 \rightarrow Z = 16$: **Sulphur ($\text{S}$)**. 3 shells $\rightarrow$ **Period 3**. 6 valence electrons $\rightarrow 6 + 10 = \mathbf{\text{Group 16}}$. Valency $= 8 - 6 = \mathbf{2}$. It sits to the right of the staircase $\rightarrow$ **Non-metal**.

11. Period 2 indicates 2 shells; Group 14 indicates 4 valence electrons $\rightarrow$ configuration $2, 4 \rightarrow Z = 6$: **Carbon ($\text{C}$)**. It is a non-metal with a valency of **4**.

12. (a) **False** ❌. Atomic size decreases across a period from left to right due to increasing nuclear charge.
    (b) **True** ✅. Fluorine is smaller and attracts incoming electrons more aggressively.
    (c) **False** ❌. Valency rises from 1 to 4, then drops back to 0.
    (d) **True** ✅. Outer electrons are held more loosely down a group, increasing metallic character.

13. Plotting valency against atomic number yields a repeating **sawtooth / mountain-wave pattern**: rising from 1 to 4 and falling back to 0 across Period 2 ($\text{Li}$ to $\text{Ne}$), then repeating the exact same $1 \rightarrow 4 \rightarrow 0$ cycle across Period 3 ($\text{Na}$ to $\text{Ar}$). This repeating geometric wave is the visual definition of **periodicity**.
