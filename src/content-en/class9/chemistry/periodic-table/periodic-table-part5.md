# Topic 5: Families of Elements: Alkali Metals, Halogens, and Noble Gases

In our previous topic, we established that moving down any vertical column adds new electron shells while keeping the number of valence electrons completely constant. Because valence electrons dictate chemical bonding and reactivity, **all elements residing in the same vertical group behave like biological siblings—sharing striking personality traits, similar compounds, and predictable patterns of reactivity**.

In chemistry, these distinct vertical columns are celebrated as **chemical families**. Today, we will explore four of the most famous families on the periodic table: **Group 1 (Alkali Metals)**, **Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals)**, **Group 17 (Halogens)**, and **Group 18 (Noble Gases)**.

---

## The Family Reunion Lunch

At a weekend family reunion picnic in the park, Liam and Elena were introduced to dozens of distant cousins. Liam had never met most of them before, but as the afternoon progressed, he noticed something fascinating.

Over by the picnic tables, three cousins were gathered around a board game. Whenever someone made a funny move, all three cousins laughed in the exact same distinctive manner—a quiet chuckle followed by a loud, infectious burst of laughter.

"Elena," Liam whispered, "those three sitting at table one must be brothers!"

Elena smiled knowingly. "They certainly are. Members of the same family often share common traits—similar laughs, similar facial expressions, and shared habits. Look at table two: those cousins are all quiet and reflective, while the group by the lawn games is bursting with boundless, fiery energy."

Liam thought about the analogy. "So if you observe the behaviour of just one family member, you already know what to expect from the rest of the household?"

"Precisely," said Elena. "And that is exactly how the periodic table operates. Each vertical column represents a distinct **chemical family**. Because they all inherit the **same count of valence electrons**, understanding one member of the family allows you to predict how the entire group behaves in nature."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Chemical family (group)** | A vertical column of elements sharing identical valence electron counts and similar chemical personalities | Group 1, Group 17 |
| **Alkali metals** | The highly reactive, soft metals of Group 1 with a valency of 1 | Lithium ($\text{Li}$), Sodium ($\text{Na}$), Potassium ($\text{K}$) |
| **Alkaline earth metals** | The reactive, divalent metals of Group 2 with a valency of 2 | Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), Calcium ($\text{Ca}$) |
| **Halogens** | The reactive, salt-forming non-metals of Group 17 with a valency of 1 (*halo* = salt, *gen* = producer) | Fluorine ($\text{F}$), Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$), Bromine ($\text{Br}$), Iodine ($\text{I}$) |
| **Noble gases** | The chemically inert, non-metallic gases of Group 18 with completely filled outermost electron shells | Helium ($\text{He}$), Neon ($\text{Ne}$), Argon ($\text{Ar}$) |
| **Inert** | Chemically unreactive under ordinary conditions due to a stable electron configuration | Noble gases having valency 0 |
| **Alkaline solution** | A basic solution that turns red litmus paper blue, produced when alkali metals react with water | Aqueous Sodium Hydroxide ($\text{NaOH}$) |

## Part A: The Four Major Families at a Glance

Recall our foundational rule of chemistry: **valence electrons dictate chemical personality**. 
- Every element in Group 1 possesses **1 valence electron** (valency 1).
- Every element in Group 2 possesses **2 valence electrons** (valency 2).
- Every element in Group 17 possesses **7 valence electrons** (valency 1).
- Every element in Group 18 possesses a **stable complete outer shell** (8 electrons, or 2 for helium; valency 0).

```
   GROUP 1            GROUP 2            GROUP 17           GROUP 18
   Alkali Metals      Alkaline Earth     Halogens           Noble Gases
                      Metals
   +---------+        +---------+        +---------+        +---------+
   | (H)     |        |         |        |         |        | He  (2) |
   | Li  (1) |        | Be  (2) |        | F   (7) |        | Ne  (8) |
   | Na  (1) |        | Mg  (2) |        | Cl  (7) |        | Ar  (8) |
   | K   (1) |        | Ca  (2) |        | Br  (7) |        |         |
   |         |        |         |        | I   (7) |        |         |
   +---------+        +---------+        +---------+        +---------+

   Valence e-: 1      Valence e-: 2      Valence e-: 7      Valence e-: 8 (He: 2)
   Valency: 1         Valency: 2         Valency: 1         Valency: 0

   Descending:        Descending:        Descending:        Completely INERT:
   Reactivity         Reactivity         Reactivity         Virtually zero
   INCREASES          INCREASES          DECREASES          chemical reactivity
   Li < Na < K        Be < Mg < Ca       F > Cl > Br > I
```

> **Figure 5.1** – The four classic chemical families. Numbers in parentheses indicate valence electrons. In Groups 1 and 2, reactivity increases down the column as valence electrons are held more loosely. In Group 17, reactivity decreases down the column as nuclear pull on incoming electrons weakens. Group 18 elements have saturated outer shells and are chemically inert. Note: Hydrogen is placed atop Group 1 for valence electron consistency, but it is a non-metallic gas.

Notice how Group 1 and Group 17 are perfect chemical partners: Group 1 metals want to **surrender 1 electron**, while Group 17 halogens urgently need to **gain 1 electron** to complete their octet. When they meet, an electron is transferred completely, forging a strong **ionic bond** (such as $\text{NaCl}$).

## Part B: Group 1, Alkali Metals ($\text{Li}, \text{Na}, \text{K}$)

Group 1 elements are known as the **alkali metals** because they react vigorously with water to form soluble hydroxides that produce strongly **alkaline (basic)** solutions.

### Characteristic Family Traits
1. **Exceptionally soft and lustrous:** Unlike typical structural metals like iron or copper, alkali metals can be sliced easily with a standard butter knife. When freshly cut, they display a brilliant, silvery metallic luster that tarnishes within seconds upon exposure to atmospheric oxygen.
2. **Extremely low density:** They are remarkably light metals. Lithium is the least dense solid element known ($0.53\text{ g/cm}^3$, nearly half the density of water), and both Sodium and Potassium float on water while reacting!
3. **Valency of 1:** Each has a single valence electron ($s^1$) which is readily lost to form univalent positive cations ($\text{Li}^+, \text{Na}^+, \text{K}^+$).
4. **Fierce chemical reactivity:** They react rapidly with oxygen, halogens, and moisture. Consequently, **Sodium and Potassium must be stored permanently submerged under mineral oil or kerosene** to isolate them from atmospheric oxygen and moisture.

### Violent Reaction with Water (Strictly Theoretical Knowledge)
When dropped into water, an alkali metal skitters rapidly across the surface, fizzing intensely as it displaces hydrogen gas:
$$2\text{Na} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} \longrightarrow 2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2\uparrow + \text{Heat}$$
- **Lithium** reacts steadily with gentle effervescence.
- **Sodium** melts into a shimmering sphere that darts across the surface, generating sufficient heat to occasionally ignite the liberated hydrogen with a characteristic yellow-orange flame.
- **Potassium** reacts instantly and violently; the reaction heat ignites the hydrogen immediately with a vivid lilac (pale violet) flame.

Because this reaction is exothermic and poses severe splattering hazards, **alkali metal demonstrations are conducted strictly behind blast shields by certified chemistry instructors in fume hoods. Students must never touch or handle raw alkali metals.**

### Reactivity Trend Down the Group
$$\mathbf{\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K}}$$
As we descend Group 1, atomic radius expands ($\text{Li}: 2\text{ shells} \rightarrow \text{Na}: 3\text{ shells} \rightarrow \text{K}: 4\text{ shells}$). The solitary valence electron sits progressively farther from the nucleus, shielded by inner shells. It is surrendered with less energy, driving reactivity steadily upward.

### Characteristic Ionic Compounds
Alkali metals form stable, white, water-soluble crystalline salts:
- **$\text{NaCl}$** ($\text{Na}^+ + \text{Cl}^-$): Common table salt.
- **$\text{KBr}$** ($\text{K}^+ + \text{Br}^-$): Potassium bromide.
- **$\text{LiF}$** ($\text{Li}^+ + \text{F}^-$): Lithium fluoride.

*Charge balance verification:* In every case, one $+1$ cation perfectly neutralizes one $-1$ anion ✅.

## Part C: Group 2, Alkaline Earth Metals ($\text{Mg}, \text{Ca}$)

Group 2 elements are called **alkaline earth metals** because their oxides produce alkaline solutions when mixed with water and were historically extracted from insoluble terrestrial minerals ("earths").

### Characteristic Family Traits
1. **Harder and denser than Group 1:** Beryllium, Magnesium, and Calcium are silver-white metals. They cannot be sliced with a butter knife and have noticeably higher melting points than alkali metals.
2. **Valency of 2:** Each atom contains 2 valence electrons and readily loses both to form divalent cations ($\text{Mg}^{2+}, \text{Ca}^{2+}$).
3. **Reactive, but less explosive than Group 1:** Losing 2 valence electrons requires more energy than losing 1. Thus, within any period, Group 2 metals are less reactive than their Group 1 neighbors ($\text{Mg}$ is less reactive than $\text{Na}$).
4. **Reactivity increases down the group:** $\mathbf{\text{Be} < \text{Mg} < \text{Ca}}$. Calcium reacts steadily with cold water to release hydrogen gas, whereas Magnesium reacts only very sluggishly with cold water, requiring steam or burning to react vigorously.

### Hallmark Reactions
In our study of chemical changes, we observed Magnesium burning in air with an intense, blinding white flame:
$$2\text{Mg} + \text{O}_2 \longrightarrow 2\text{MgO}$$
$\text{MgO}$ is an ionic compound composed of $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ ions in a $1:1$ ratio ($2 = 2$ charge balance ✅).

### Real-World Compounds
- **Calcium compounds:** Vital structural components of animal bones, teeth, eggshells, and seashells ($\text{CaCO}_3$). Dissolved calcium ions are essential for muscle contraction and blood clotting.
- **Chlorides:** Each $+2$ metal ion requires two $-1$ chloride ions: **$\text{MgCl}_2$** and **$\text{CaCl}_2$** (used industrially as a de-icing agent on winter highways).

## Part D: Group 17, Halogens ($\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$)

The name **halogen** is derived from the Greek words *hals* (meaning "sea salt") and *gen* (meaning "to produce"). When these elements react with metals, they generate classic salts like $\text{NaCl}$ and $\text{KBr}$.

All halogens have **7 valence electrons** and an identical **valency of 1**. They exist naturally as **diatomic molecules** ($\text{F}_2, \text{Cl}_2, \text{Br}_2, \text{I}_2$) bound by a single covalent bond.

### The Only Family Spanning All Three States of Matter!
One of the most extraordinary curiosities in chemistry is that Group 17 contains elements in **all three states of matter at room temperature ($25^\circ\text{C}$)**:

```
        FLUORINE           CHLORINE            BROMINE            IODINE
        F2                 Cl2                 Br2                I2
        (Z = 9)            (Z = 17)            (Z = 35)           (Z = 53)

        Pale yellow        Greenish-yellow     Reddish-brown      Lustrous dark purple-
        gas                gas                 liquid             black solid

        GAS                GAS                 LIQUID             SOLID
        (Room temp)        (Room temp)         (Room temp)        (Room temp)

        Reactivity: Highest -----------------------------------------> Lowest
        (Reactivity decreases steadily down the group)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Physical states, colours, and reactivity progression of the halogens at room temperature. Notice the gradual transition from light gas ($\text{F}_2$) to dense gas ($\text{Cl}_2$) to volatile liquid ($\text{Br}_2$) to crystalline solid ($\text{I}_2$). Down the group, colours darken and reactivity decreases.

### Reactivity Trend Down the Group
$$\mathbf{\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}}$$
Halogens react by **capturing an electron** into their valence shell. In Fluorine, the incoming electron is captured into the compact 2nd shell very close to the nucleus. Down the group, new shells push the outer boundary farther away, and inner shells shield the nuclear charge. The attraction for an incoming electron weakens, so reactivity drops. Fluorine is the most aggressive oxidizing non-metal known.

### Halogen Displacement Reaction
Because Fluorine and Chlorine are more reactive than Bromine and Iodine, a more reactive halogen will displace a less reactive halogen from its salt solution:
$$\text{Cl}_2 + 2\text{KBr} \longrightarrow 2\text{KCl} + \text{Br}_2$$
Chlorine takes Bromine's place, freeing reddish-brown liquid bromine. 
*Atom count verification:* Left side has $2\text{ Cl}, 2\text{ K}, 2\text{ Br}$; right side has $2\text{ K}, 2\text{ Cl}, 2\text{ Br}$ ✅.

### Critical Safety Precautions
- **Chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$)** is a suffocating, dense, greenish-yellow gas that severely irritates mucous membranes and causes pulmonary damage if inhaled.
- **Bromine ($\text{Br}_2$)** is a heavy, reddish-brown fuming liquid whose corrosive vapours attack the eyes and lungs, causing severe skin burns on contact.
- **Fluorine ($\text{F}_2$)** is extremely toxic and violently reactive.
- Students must never attempt to handle free halogens outside professional chemical facilities.

### Real-World Uses
- **Chlorine:** Municipal water treatment plants add tiny, precisely metered quantities of chlorine (a few parts per million) to kill pathogenic bacteria, making drinking water safe. It is also used to manufacture bleaching powder ($\text{CaOCl}_2$) for whitening paper and textiles.
- **Iodine:** Dissolved in alcohol-water solution as **tincture of iodine**, it serves as a common topical antiseptic for cuts and abrasions. Minute traces of potassium iodate are added to commercial table salt (**iodized salt**) to prevent goiter and thyroid deficiency.
- **Bromine:** Used in specialized flame retardants and photographic emulsions.

## Part E: Group 18, Noble Gases ($\text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$)

Group 18 elements are known as the **noble gases** (or inert gases). Like royalty standing aloof from common squabbles, noble gases rarely engage in chemical reactions with other elements.

### The Secret of Inertness: Saturated Electron Shells
Every noble gas possesses a **completely filled outermost electron shell**:
- **Helium ($\text{He}$, $Z = 2$):** $K = 2$ (a stable duplet completing the first shell).
- **Neon ($\text{Ne}$, $Z = 10$):** $K = 2, L = 8$ (a stable outer octet).
- **Argon ($\text{Ar}$, $Z = 18$):** $K = 2, L = 8, M = 8$ (a stable outer octet).

```
    HELIUM (Z = 2)           K shell: 2                  Completely filled duplet
        (2)
    NEON (Z = 10)            K: 2   L: 8                 Completely filled octet
        (2, 8)
    ARGON (Z = 18)           K: 2   L: 8   M: 8          Completely filled octet
        (2, 8, 8)

    Visual Shell Diagram:
       He:  [2]
       Ne:  [2] [8]
       Ar:  [2] [8] [8]

    Zero vacancies exist in the valence shell.
    Therefore, valency = 0; no electrons are gained, lost, or shared!
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Electron configurations of the first three noble gases: Helium ($2$), Neon ($2, 8$), and Argon ($2, 8, 8$). Because their outermost energy levels are completely saturated, they experience zero chemical drive to react under standard conditions.

### Noble Gas Applications
Because they are completely non-flammable and unreactive, noble gases have unique modern applications:
- **Helium:** Used to inflate weather and party balloons. Unlike Hydrogen (which is flammable and explosive), Helium is completely non-combustible and safe. It is also blended with oxygen in deep-sea diving breathing tanks to prevent decompression sickness ("the bends").
- **Neon:** Used in bright advertising signs. When high-voltage electricity passes through low-pressure neon gas in a sealed glass tube, its electrons become excited and emit a brilliant, vibrant reddish-orange glow.
- **Argon:** Used as an unreactive shielding gas inside incandescent light bulbs to prevent the hot tungsten metal filament from burning in atmospheric oxygen. It makes up approximately **$0.93\%$** of Earth's atmosphere.

## Part F: Other Main-Group Families in Brief

The intermediate columns of the periodic table also form recognizable families:
- **Group 13 (Boron Family):** Boron ($\text{B}$) and Aluminium ($\text{Al}$). Characterized by **3 valence electrons** and a valency of 3. Aluminium is a lightweight, corrosion-resistant metal used in cooking foil, beverage cans, and airplane bodies.
- **Group 14 (Carbon Family):** Carbon ($\text{C}$) and Silicon ($\text{Si}$). Characterized by **4 valence electrons** and a valency of 4. Carbon is the foundational backbone of all organic life; Silicon is the semiconductor backbone of modern computer chips.
- **Group 15 (Nitrogen Family / Pnictogens):** Nitrogen ($\text{N}$) and Phosphorus ($\text{P}$). Possess **5 valence electrons** and a valency of 3 ($8 - 5 = 3$). Nitrogen makes up $78\%$ of atmospheric air; Phosphorus is essential for agricultural fertilizers and DNA.
- **Group 16 (Oxygen Family / Chalcogens):** Oxygen ($\text{O}$) and Sulphur ($\text{S}$). Possess **6 valence electrons** and a valency of 2 ($8 - 6 = 2$). Oxygen supports cellular respiration; Sulphur is used in vulcanizing rubber and manufacturing sulfuric acid.

*(Note: Groups 3 to 12 contain the transition metals, which exhibit multiple valencies and will be studied in senior secondary chemistry.)*

## Part H: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Family Resemblance: $\text{K}, \text{Mg}$, and $\text{Cl}$
Which of the elements Potassium ($\text{K}$), Magnesium ($\text{Mg}$), or Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$) will behave most similarly to Sodium ($\text{Na}$)?
- Sodium ($Z = 11$, configuration $2, 8, 1$) belongs to Group 1 with 1 valence electron.
- Potassium ($Z = 19$, configuration $2, 8, 8, 1$) also belongs to Group 1 with 1 valence electron.
- Because both belong to the alkali metal family, **Potassium will behave most similarly to Sodium** ✅.

### Example 2. Compound Formation Between Calcium and Chlorine
Predict the chemical formula of the compound formed between Calcium and Chlorine.
- Calcium belongs to Group 2 with 2 valence electrons, forming a $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ cation.
- Chlorine belongs to Group 17 with 7 valence electrons, needing 1 electron to form a $\text{Cl}^-$ anion.
- Two $\text{Cl}^-$ ions are required to balance one $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ion:
  $$\text{Ca}^{2+} + 2\text{Cl}^- \longrightarrow \mathbf{\text{CaCl}_2}$$
  Charge balance: $+2 + 2(-1) = 0$ ✅.

### Example 3. Compound of Sodium and Bromine
Write the formula of the compound formed between Sodium and Bromine.
- Sodium forms $\text{Na}^+$; Bromine forms $\text{Br}^-$.
- One-to-one ratio yields **$\text{NaBr}$ (Sodium Bromide)** ✅.

### Example 4. Reactivity Comparison: $\text{Na}$ vs. $\text{K}$, and $\text{F}$ vs. $\text{Cl}$
Between Sodium and Potassium, which is more reactive? Between Fluorine and Chlorine, which is more reactive?
- In Group 1, reactivity increases down the group $\rightarrow$ **Potassium ($\text{K}$) is more reactive than Sodium ($\text{Na}$)**.
- In Group 17, reactivity decreases down the group $\rightarrow$ **Fluorine ($\text{F}$) is more reactive than Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$)** ✅.

### Example 5. Identifying Family from Configuration $2, 8, 8$
An element has the electron configuration $2, 8, 8$ ($Z = 18$). Identify its family and valency.
- The outermost shell contains 8 electrons (a complete octet).
- It belongs to **Group 18, the Noble Gas family (Argon)**. Its chemical valency is **0** ✅.

### Example 6. Identifying Family from Configuration $2, 8, 7$
An element has the electron configuration $2, 8, 7$ ($Z = 17$). State its period, group, family, and valency.
- Occupies 3 shells $\rightarrow$ **Period 3**.
- 7 valence electrons $\rightarrow 7 + 10 = \mathbf{\text{Group 17}}$: **Chlorine, Halogen family**.
- It gains 1 electron to complete its octet, giving it a **valency of 1** ✅.

### Example 7. Bonding Nature of Sodium and Chlorine
When Sodium and Chlorine react, what type of chemical bond is formed? Explain.
- Sodium ($2, 8, 1$) donates its single valence electron to Chlorine ($2, 8, 7$).
- This creates a stable $\text{Na}^+$ cation and $\text{Cl}^-$ anion, bound together by strong electrostatic attraction into an **ionic bond ($\text{NaCl}$)** ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing alkali metals can be touched safely with bare hands.**
   Alkali metals react exothermically with moisture on human skin to form caustic hydroxides that cause severe chemical burns. They are strictly teacher-demonstration materials.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming halogens become more reactive down the group.**
   This is the exact opposite of reality. While alkali metals become more reactive down the column, halogens become **less** reactive down the column ($\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$).

3. **Mistake 3: Believing chlorine is safe to breathe because it is added to tap water.**
   Free chlorine gas is highly toxic and suffocating. It is added to public water supplies in microscopic, carefully regulated concentrations (parts per million) that eliminate microbes while leaving water safe for human consumption.

4. **Mistake 4: Claiming noble gases have "no valence electrons".**
   Noble gases have valence electrons—Neon and Argon have 8, and Helium has 2. Their valency is 0 because their valence shells are completely full, leaving zero vacancies for bonding.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming all elements in a family share identical physical states.**
   Chemical properties are identical within a family, but physical states can differ. In Group 17, Fluorine and Chlorine are gases, Bromine is a liquid, and Iodine is a solid.

## Quick Recap

- **Chemical Family:** A vertical group of elements with the same valence electron count and similar chemical behaviour.
- **Group 1 (Alkali Metals: $\text{Li}, \text{Na}, \text{K}$):** Soft, shiny, low-density metals with valency 1; stored under oil; react vigorously with water to produce hydrogen and alkaline solutions; reactivity increases down the group ($\text{Li} < \text{Na} < \text{K}$).
- **Group 2 (Alkaline Earth Metals: $\text{Mg}, \text{Ca}$):** Harder and denser than Group 1; valency 2; Magnesium burns with a brilliant white flame to yield $\text{MgO}$; Calcium compounds are essential for bones and teeth.
- **Group 17 (Halogens: $\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$):** Salt-forming non-metals with 7 valence electrons and valency 1; exist as diatomic molecules; spans all three states of matter ($\text{F}_2, \text{Cl}_2$ gases, $\text{Br}_2$ liquid, $\text{I}_2$ solid); reactivity decreases down the group ($\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$).
- **Group 18 (Noble Gases: $\text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$):** Fully saturated valence shells (duplet in $\text{He}$, octet in $\text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$); valency 0; completely inert; used safely in balloons ($\text{He}$), illuminated signs ($\text{Ne}$), and light bulbs ($\text{Ar}$).
- **Ionic bonding:** Group 1 metals transfer an electron to Group 17 halogens to form stable ionic salts ($\text{NaCl}, \text{KBr}, \text{LiF}$).

### Answers

1. A **chemical family** is a vertical column (group) of elements in the periodic table. Elements in the same group possess the **same number of valence electrons**. Because valence electrons determine chemical bonding and reactivity, elements in the same group exhibit very similar chemical properties.

2. 
   (a) Alkali metals: **$\text{Li}, \text{Na}, \text{K}$**.
   (b) Alkaline earth metals: **$\text{Be}, \text{Mg}, \text{Ca}$**.
   (c) Halogens: **$\text{F}, \text{Cl}, \text{Br}, \text{I}$**.
   (d) Noble gases: **$\text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$**.

3. 
   - Group 1: **1 valence electron, valency 1**.
   - Group 2: **2 valence electrons, valency 2**.
   - Group 17: **7 valence electrons, valency 1**.
   - Group 18: **8 valence electrons (Helium has 2), valency 0**.

4. Three physical properties: (i) soft enough to cut with a knife; (ii) silvery metallic luster on freshly cut surfaces; (iii) low density (Lithium, Sodium, and Potassium float on water). Sodium and Potassium are stored under kerosene or mineral oil to **isolate them from moisture and oxygen in the air**, with which they react rapidly.

5. **Potassium ($\text{K}$) is more reactive than Lithium ($\text{Li}$)**. Potassium has 4 electron shells while Lithium has 2. Potassium's single valence electron is located much farther from the positive nucleus and is shielded by three full inner shells ($K, L, M$). The nuclear electrostatic hold on that electron is much weaker, allowing Potassium to lose it with far greater ease.

6. Alkali metals react vigorously and exothermically with water and air, releasing flammable hydrogen gas that can ignite spontaneously. Contact with skin moisture forms corrosive alkali hydroxides that cause severe burns. Therefore, they are handled solely by trained instructors behind safety shields.

7. 
   - **Fluorine ($\text{F}_2$):** Pale yellow gas.
   - **Chlorine ($\text{Cl}_2$):** Greenish-yellow gas.
   - **Bromine ($\text{Br}_2$):** Volatile reddish-brown liquid.
   - **Iodine ($\text{I}_2$):** Lustrous dark purple-black solid.

8. **Fluorine is the most reactive**, and **Iodine is the least reactive**. Reactivity **decreases** down Group 17 ($\text{F} > \text{Cl} > \text{Br} > \text{I}$) because each added shell increases the distance from the nucleus and enhances inner electron shielding, weakening the attraction for an incoming electron.

9. Chlorine uses: (i) municipal water disinfection; (ii) manufacture of bleaching powder. Iodine use: topical antiseptic (tincture of iodine) and prevention of goiter in iodized salt. Safety rules: free chlorine and bromine gases are toxic and corrosive—never inhale their vapours; halogens should only be handled in certified laboratory fume hoods.

10. 
    (a) **$\text{NaCl}$**: $\text{Na}^+ (+1) + \text{Cl}^- (-1) = 0$ ✅.
    (b) **$\text{KBr}$**: $\text{K}^+ (+1) + \text{Br}^- (-1) = 0$ ✅.
    (c) **$\text{CaCl}_2$**: $\text{Ca}^{2+} (+2) + 2\text{Cl}^- (-2) = 0$ ✅.
    (d) **$\text{MgO}$**: $\text{Mg}^{2+} (+2) + \text{O}^{2-} (-2) = 0$ ✅.
    (e) **$\text{LiF}$**: $\text{Li}^+ (+1) + \text{F}^- (-1) = 0$ ✅.

11. Noble gases are inert because their outermost electron shells are completely filled to maximum capacity (duplet in $\text{He}$, octet in $\text{Ne}$ and $\text{Ar}$). They have no energetic drive to lose, gain, or share electrons. Configurations:
    - **$\text{He}$:** $K = 2$.
    - **$\text{Ne}$:** $K = 2, L = 8$.
    - **$\text{Ar}$:** $K = 2, L = 8, M = 8$.

12. Helium: non-flammable inflation of weather and party balloons. Neon: advertising display signs. Argon: filling incandescent light bulbs to protect the filament.

13. 
    - Group 13: Boron family, valency 3.
    - Group 14: Carbon family, valency 4.
    - Group 15: Nitrogen family (Pnictogens), valency 3.
    - Group 16: Oxygen family (Chalcogens), valency 2.

14. (a) **False** ❌. Halogens become **less** reactive down the group.
    (b) **True** ✅. Helium has 2 valence electrons in its single, complete $K$-shell.
    (c) **True** ✅. Chlorine is a gas and Bromine is a liquid at room temperature.
    (d) **False** ❌. Alkali metals react with skin moisture and cause severe burns; they must never be touched directly.
