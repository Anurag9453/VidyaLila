# Topic 2: Models of the Atom: From Thomson to Rutherford

In Topic 1, we met the three subatomic particles inside the atom: the negatively charged **electron**, the positively charged **proton**, and the uncharged **neutron**. We also saw why an intact atom is electrically neutral. Yet a huge question remains: **how are these particles arranged inside the atom?** Are they blended together in a solid ball, or separated into different regions? Because a single atom is far too tiny to see through an ordinary microscope, how could scientists possibly find out? This topic tells that detective story: first an **early model that turned out to be wrong**, then a **clever scattering experiment**, and finally a **new model** built directly from experimental evidence.

---

## What Is Hidden Behind the Curtain?

One Saturday afternoon, Mr. Vance set up a guessing game in the backyard for his nephew Lucas and his friends. He hung a long opaque curtain across the lawn and hid **one mystery object** behind it. Nobody knew whether the hidden object was a wide wooden board, a narrow iron post, or nothing at all.

"Here are the rules," Mr. Vance announced. "You aren't allowed to peek behind the curtain. Instead, roll tennis balls under and through the curtain gap from a distance and watch what happens to them. From their paths alone, tell me what is hidden behind the curtain."

Lucas and his friends rolled dozens of balls across the line. **Almost all of the balls rolled straight through** to the other side without slowing down. A few balls **glanced off at small angles**. And just **one ball** out of dozens hit something head-on and **bounced straight back** toward Lucas.

Lucas thought for a moment. "Uncle, since almost every ball went straight through, most of the space behind the curtain must be completely empty. Turning a few balls aside and bouncing one straight back means there is something **very hard and heavy** in there—and it must be **very narrow**, or else way more balls would have hit it!"

"Spot on!" Mr. Vance pulled back the curtain to reveal a thin, heavy iron pole standing in the middle. "Without ever looking behind the curtain, you mapped out what was inside just by watching how projectiles scattered. A physicist named Ernest Rutherford did the exact same thing to map the inside of an atom."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Model** | A conceptual picture or representation built from evidence to explain something we cannot see directly | Thomson's model, Rutherford's model |
| **Alpha particle (α-particle)** | A fast-moving, positively charged particle (a helium nucleus, Charge +2, Mass 4 u) emitted by certain radioactive substances | The "projectiles" used in the gold-foil experiment |
| **Gold foil** | An extremely thin sheet of hammered gold, only about 1,000 atoms thick | The target in Rutherford's experiment |
| **Deflection** | A change in direction; bending away from a straight-line path | An alpha particle veering off at an angle |
| **Nucleus** | The tiny, dense, positively charged central core of an atom | The "iron post" at the heart of the atom |
| **Empty space** | The vast region of the atom containing no heavy particles | Makes up almost the entire volume of an atom |
| **Dense** | Packing a large amount of mass into a very tiny volume | The atomic nucleus |

## Part A: Thomson's Model: Plum Pudding and Blueberry Muffin

In Topic 1, we saw that J. J. Thomson discovered the negatively charged electron in **1897**. That immediately raised a puzzle: if an atom contains negative electrons, and the whole atom is electrically neutral, **how is the positive charge arranged?** In **1904**, Thomson proposed the first model of the internal structure of the atom.

His picture was simple: an atom is a **uniform sphere of positive charge** in which the negative **electrons are embedded**, like plums or raisins in a traditional English Christmas pudding—or like blueberries studded throughout a blueberry muffin (or seeds in a watermelon). Because the total positive charge of the sphere equals the total negative charge of the embedded electrons, the atom as a whole is electrically neutral.

```
        Thomson's "Plum Pudding" Model

        +  +  +  +  +  +
     +  +  -  +  +  -  +  +
     +  -  +  +  +  +  -  +
     +  +  +  -  +  +  +  +
     +  -  +  +  +  -  +  +
     +  +  -  +  +  +  +  +
        +  +  +  +  +  +

     "+" = positive charge (spread evenly throughout the sphere)
     "-" = electrons (embedded like raisins in a pudding or berries in a muffin)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Thomson's model of the atom: positive charge is spread evenly throughout the entire sphere, with negative electrons studded inside it like raisins in a pudding or chocolate chips in a muffin. In this model, there is no empty space—the entire atom is a soft, evenly filled sphere.

Thomson's model was an important first step because it accounted for three known facts: atoms contain electrons, atoms contain positive charge, and whole atoms are electrically neutral. In science, however, a good model must also be **testable**. Thomson's model made a clear prediction: **mass and positive charge are spread thinly and evenly across the entire atom.** That prediction was put to the test by Ernest Rutherford.

## Part B: Rutherford's Gold-Foil Experiment

Around **1911, Ernest Rutherford** and his co-workers (Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden) carried out one of the most famous experiments in the history of science. Their strategy was the same as Mr. Vance's backyard curtain game: since you cannot see inside an atom directly, fire tiny high-speed **projectiles** at a thin sheet of atoms and watch where the projectiles go.

**Experimental set-up:**

- **Projectiles:** **Alpha (α) particles**—fast-moving, relatively heavy particles carrying a **positive charge** (each alpha particle has a mass of about 4 u and a charge of +2).
- **Target:** An **extremely thin sheet of gold foil**. Gold was chosen because it is so malleable that it could be hammered into a film only about **1,000 atoms thick**.
- **Detector:** A circular screen coated with **zinc sulphide (ZnS)** placed around the gold foil. Whenever an alpha particle struck the screen, it produced a tiny pinpoint flash of light (a scintillation), allowing the scientists to see exactly where each particle went.

Rutherford expected Thomson's model to hold true. If an atom were a soft, diffuse sphere of evenly spread positive charge, the heavy, fast-moving alpha particles should plough straight through every atom with at most tiny deflections. What actually happened astonished everyone.

```
   Alpha-particle                 Thin gold foil
   source (positive, fast)        (with tiny central nuclei N)

                                          N
   ===(a)=====================================================>  passed straight through (most)

                                          N
   ===(b)==================================\
                                             \___.            deflected by a small angle (some)
                                          N

   ===(c)===============================> N
   <===================================                          rebounded straight back (very few)

                                          N
```

> **Figure 2.2** – The three types of paths observed in Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (schematic sketch, not to scale): (a) most alpha particles passed straight through empty space, (b) a small fraction passed close to a nucleus and were deflected through small or moderate angles, and (c) a tiny fraction collided almost head-on with a nucleus and rebounded backward. In reality, the nucleus is about 100,000 times smaller than the atom.

## Part C: Three Observations, Three Conclusions

Let us examine each experimental observation, the reasoning behind it, and the conclusion it forced scientists to draw.

### Observation 1: Most of the alpha particles passed straight through the gold foil

When thousands of alpha particles were fired at the foil, **almost all of them** went straight through without any deflection at all.

**Reasoning:** Alpha particles are fast and massive. If atoms were solid spheres filled evenly with matter as in Thomson's model, nearly every alpha particle would encounter resistance passing through 1,000 layers of atoms. Passing straight through untouched is possible only if there is **nothing in their way**.

**Conclusion 1: Most of the space inside an atom is completely empty.**

Just like Lucas's tennis balls rolling under the curtain, most projectiles went straight through because most of the target region was open space.

### Observation 2: Some alpha particles were deflected through small angles

A small fraction of the alpha particles veered away from their straight-line path by small or moderate angles.

**Reasoning:** An alpha particle carries a **positive charge**. Recall the fundamental rule of electrostatics from Physics: **like charges repel each other.** For a positive alpha particle to be pushed sideways, it must have passed close to a concentrated region of **positive charge**.

**Conclusion 2: The positive charge of the atom is not spread evenly throughout the sphere; it is concentrated in a single region.**

If the positive charge were spread thinly across the whole atom (as Thomson thought), the electric push at any point would be too weak to noticeably bend the path of a fast, heavy alpha particle.

### Observation 3: A very tiny fraction of alpha particles rebounded almost straight back

Roughly **1 in every 12,000** (or 1 in 20,000) alpha particles bounced back by nearly 180°, right back toward the source. Rutherford later remarked that it was *"almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch artillery shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you."*

**Reasoning:** To stop a heavy, high-speed alpha particle dead in its tracks and hurl it backward requires a tremendous repulsive force and a target much heavier than the projectile itself. Moreover, since only about **1 in 12,000** particles suffered such a head-on rebound, that heavy, positively charged target must occupy an **extremely tiny volume** inside the atom.

**Conclusion 3: All the positive charge and almost all the mass of the atom are concentrated in an extremely small, dense central core called the nucleus.**

### All Three in One Table

| Observation | Reasoning (Why did it happen?) | Conclusion about the Atom |
|---|---|---|
| Most alpha particles passed straight through | They encountered no obstacle along their path | Most of the atom is **empty space** |
| Some alpha particles were deflected through small angles | Positive alpha particles were repelled when passing near a positive charge | The positive charge is **concentrated**, not spread out |
| Very few (~1 in 12,000) rebounded straight back | They scored a near head-on hit against something tiny, massive, and positive | The atom has a tiny, dense, positively charged **nucleus** at its centre |

What about the electrons? Remember from Topic 1 that an electron is about 1840 times lighter than a proton (and over 7,000 times lighter than an alpha particle). An electron cannot deflect a speeding alpha particle any more than a gnat can deflect a bowling ball! Thus, the deflections are caused entirely by the heavy, positive **nucleus**, while the tiny electrons occupy the vast empty space surrounding the nucleus.

## Part D: How Tiny Is the Nucleus? Sizes and an Everyday Scale

Let us put real numbers on the size of an atom and its nucleus:

- Approximate radius/size of an **atom**: **$10^{-10}\text{ m}$**
- Approximate radius/size of a **nucleus**: **$10^{-15}\text{ m}$**

**Example 1: How many times larger across is the atom than the nucleus?**

$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{10^{-10}\text{ m}}{10^{-15}\text{ m}} = 10^{-10 + 15} = 10^5 = \mathbf{100,000}$$

That means the diameter of the nucleus is only about **1/100,000** of the diameter of the atom!

**Example 2: Stadium / Everyday Scale (Approximate).**

Imagine magnifying a single atom until its nucleus is the size of a **small marble or pea** (about **1 cm** across) placed on the centre spot of a giant sports stadium—or in the middle of a city park. How wide would the whole atom be on that same scale?

$$1\text{ cm} \times 100,000 = 100,000\text{ cm} = 1,000\text{ m} = \mathbf{1\text{ km}}$$

If the nucleus were a 1 cm marble, the outer edge of the atom would be **1 kilometre across** (a 500 m radius in every direction), with nearly all of that vast space completely empty except for a few tiny electrons whizzing around!

**Example 3: Where is the mass located?**

Each proton and neutron has a mass of about 1 u, whereas each electron has a mass of only about 1/1840 u (Topic 1). Since all the protons and neutrons sit inside the nucleus, **more than 99.9% of an atom's mass is packed into its tiny nucleus.** Packing nearly all the mass into 1/100,000th of the width makes the nucleus unimaginably **dense**.

**Example 4: How many alpha particles bounce back?**

Suppose 24,000 alpha particles are fired at the gold foil, and roughly 1 in 12,000 rebounds backward. How many rebound?

$$24,000 \div 12,000 = \mathbf{2\text{ alpha particles}}$$

The remaining ~23,998 particles pass straight through or suffer only slight deflections, because the nucleus is such a tiny target.

## Part E: Rutherford's Model of the Atom

Putting his three conclusions together, Rutherford proposed his **nuclear model of the atom** (also called the planetary model):

1. At the centre of the atom lies an extremely small, dense, positively charged core called the **nucleus**, which contains all the **positive charge (protons)** and **nearly all the mass** of the atom.
2. The negatively charged **electrons** lie **outside** the nucleus and **revolve around it** at high speed in circular paths (orbits), much like planets orbiting the Sun.
3. **Most of the volume of the atom is empty space** between the tiny nucleus and the revolving electrons.
4. The total positive charge of the nucleus equals the total negative charge of the surrounding electrons, so the atom as a whole is **electrically neutral**.
5. The **electrostatic attraction** between the positive nucleus and the negative electrons keeps the electrons in orbit around the nucleus.

```
                 Rutherford's Nuclear Model

                   o   electron
                      .
                .           .
              .                .
             .       +++        .      o
        o   .       +++++        .
             .       +++        .
              .                .
                .           .
                  .   o   .

        Centre: nucleus (extremely tiny, dense, positively charged)
        Outside: electrons revolving around the nucleus
        In between: vast empty space
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Rutherford's nuclear model of the atom: a tiny, heavy, positively charged nucleus at the centre, surrounded by vast empty space through which light electrons revolve. This picture completely replaced Thomson's solid sphere.

*(Historical note: In 1911, the neutron had not yet been discovered—Chadwick found it in 1932, as we saw in Topic 1. Today we know the nucleus contains both protons and neutrons, except in ordinary hydrogen-1.)*

**Comparing Thomson's and Rutherford's Models at a Glance**

| Feature | Thomson's Model (1904) | Rutherford's Model (1911) |
|---|---|---|
| Positive charge | Spread evenly throughout the entire sphere | Concentrated in a tiny central **nucleus** |
| Location of electrons | Embedded inside the positive sphere | Revolving in orbits outside the nucleus |
| Space inside the atom | Completely filled | Mostly empty space |
| Agreement with the gold-foil experiment | Fails (cannot explain why some alpha particles bounce straight back) | Succeeds (explains all three observations) |

## Part F: A Limitation of Rutherford's Model

Rutherford's nuclear model brilliantly explained the gold-foil experiment, and its discovery of the nucleus remains a cornerstone of modern science. However, it ran into one serious theoretical difficulty regarding **atomic stability**.

According to classical electromagnetic theory, **any charged particle moving in a curved path (accelerating) continuously radiates energy**. If an orbiting electron constantly loses energy as radiation, its orbit should steadily shrink, causing the electron to **spiral inward and crash into the positive nucleus** in less than a billionth of a second!

Yet we know from everyday life that **atoms are completely stable**. The atoms in your desk, the water you drink, and the air you breathe have existed for billions of years without collapsing.

So while Rutherford was completely right about the **nucleus**, something more was needed to explain **how** electrons move around the nucleus without losing energy and spiralling inward.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking Rutherford discovered the electron.**
   J. J. Thomson discovered the electron (1897). Rutherford discovered the **atomic nucleus** via the gold-foil experiment (1911).

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking alpha particles are negatively charged.**
   Alpha particles carry a **positive charge** (+2). That is why they are repelled by the positively charged nucleus.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking "most particles went straight through" means the atom contains nothing at all.**
   It means *most* of the atom's volume is empty space—but at the very centre sits a tiny, massive nucleus that deflected and bounced back the few particles that came close to it.

4. **Mistake 4: Thinking the nucleus must be large because it holds almost all the mass.**
   The nucleus holds over 99.9% of the mass, yet its diameter is only about **1/100,000** of the atom's diameter. Having a huge mass in a tiny volume means the nucleus is extraordinarily **dense**, not large.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating textbook diagrams as drawn to true scale.**
   No page is big enough to draw an atom to scale: if the nucleus were a 1 mm dot on this page, the outer electrons would be 100 metres away! Textbook diagrams enlarge the nucleus so you can see it.

## Quick Recap

- **Thomson's model (1904):** The atom is a uniform sphere of positive charge with negative electrons embedded in it like plums in a pudding or blueberries in a muffin.
- **Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911):** Fast, positively charged alpha particles were fired at an ultra-thin gold foil (~1,000 atoms thick) surrounded by a zinc sulphide detector screen.
- **Observation 1:** Most alpha particles passed straight through → **most of the atom is empty space**.
- **Observation 2:** Some alpha particles were deflected at small angles → **positive charge is concentrated**, repelling the positive alpha particles.
- **Observation 3:** Very few (~1 in 12,000) bounced straight back → **all positive charge and nearly all mass sit in a tiny, dense central nucleus**.
- **Sizes:** Atom $\approx 10^{-10}\text{ m}$; nucleus $\approx 10^{-15}\text{ m}$ (the atom is ~100,000 times wider than its nucleus—like a 1 cm marble in the middle of a 1 km circle).
- **Rutherford's nuclear model:** A tiny, dense, positive nucleus at the centre, electrons revolving around it in vast empty space, with equal total positive and negative charges.
- **Limitation:** Classical physics predicts an orbiting electron should lose energy and spiral into the nucleus, leaving the stability of atoms unexplained—a puzzle solved in Topic 3.

### Answers

1. In Thomson's model, the atom is a **uniformly positively charged sphere** with negatively charged **electrons embedded inside it**. The positive sphere corresponds to the pudding (or muffin batter), and the electrons correspond to the raisins or blueberries. The atom is neutral because the total positive and negative charges are equal.

2. **Projectiles:** Fast-moving, positively charged alpha particles. **Target:** A sheet of gold foil hammered to about 1,000 atoms thick. **Detector:** A circular screen coated with zinc sulphide (ZnS) that produced a tiny flash of light wherever an alpha particle struck it.

3. (i) Most alpha particles passed straight through → most of the atom is **empty space**. (ii) Some particles were deflected through small angles → the positive charge is **concentrated** in one place. (iii) Very few (~1 in 12,000) rebounded straight back → almost all the mass and all the positive charge are packed into a **tiny, dense central nucleus**.

4. In Thomson's model, positive charge and mass are spread thinly and evenly across the entire atom, so there is no concentrated charge or heavy core to give an alpha particle a strong push. It completely failed to explain why **some alpha particles rebounded nearly straight back**.

5. Both the alpha particle and the gold nucleus carry a **positive charge**. Because **like charges repel each other**, an alpha particle passing near the nucleus experiences a strong electrostatic repulsion and is deflected aside.

6. An electron has a mass of only about 1/1840 u—over 7,000 times lighter than an alpha particle (4 u). Just as a gnat cannot bounce back a speeding bowling ball, a tiny electron cannot cause a heavy alpha particle to rebound; only the massive nucleus can do that.

8. $36,000 \div 12,000 = \mathbf{3}$ alpha particles (approximately).

9. (i) A tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus at the centre holds all the protons and nearly all the mass. (ii) Electrons revolve around the nucleus at high speed. Unlike Thomson's solid, evenly filled sphere, Rutherford's atom is mostly empty space with a concentrated central nucleus.

10. Classical electromagnetic theory states that an accelerating (orbiting) charged particle continuously radiates energy.
