# Topic 3: Bohr's Model: Electron Shells

At the end of Topic 2, we were left with a nagging puzzle. In Rutherford's model, electrons revolve around the central nucleus. According to classical physics, however, a revolving charged particle should continuously lose energy and spiral into the nucleus—meaning no atom should be stable! Yet the atoms all around us last for billions of years. In this topic, we will see how the Danish physicist **Niels Bohr** resolved this paradox with a bold, simple idea, and we will learn the exact rules that govern how electrons arrange themselves in **shells** around the nucleus.

---

## The Elevator That Only Stops at Floors

Ms. Chen took her niece Aria to visit her tenth-floor office in a downtown high-rise. As they stepped into the glass elevator and rode upward, Aria watched the floor indicator jump from 1 to 2, then 3, then 4. She noticed that the elevator never stopped **between** two floors—it stopped cleanly at Floor 3 or Floor 4, never at "Floor 3.5."

"Auntie, why can't the elevator stop halfway between Floor 3 and Floor 4?" Aria asked. "On a ramp you can stand at any height you want—why not in an elevator?"

Ms. Chen smiled. "Because a building only has **specific, fixed floors**. You can stand on the 3rd floor, or you can stand on the 4th floor, but there is no 3.5th floor to stand on. Notice something else: to go up from Floor 3 to Floor 4, the elevator motor has to supply a **specific amount of electrical energy**, and going down releases energy. Each floor represents a fixed energy level."

Aria thought about it. "So the floors are like rungs on a ladder—you can stand on one rung or the next, never floating in the air between them."

"Exactly," Ms. Chen said. "And that is the heart of how Niels Bohr explained electrons inside an atom. Just replace the elevator with an **electron** and the building floors with **shells**."

```
        Office Building                        Atom
        (only fixed floors exist)              (only fixed shells exist)

   Floor 4  -----------------  <-- can stop here                  N shell
   Floor 3  -----------------  <-- can stop here                  M shell
   Floor 2  -----------------  <-- can stop here                  L shell
   Floor 1  -----------------  <-- can stop here                  K shell
   Ground   [ elevator ]                                          nucleus (+)

   Stopping at "Floor 2.5" between floors: impossible.
   An electron staying between the L and M shells: impossible.
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The elevator analogy. Just as an elevator can stop only at designated floors and never in between, an electron in an atom can occupy only specific allowed shells (energy levels), never the space between them. The farther a shell is from the nucleus, the higher its energy. (Keep in mind this is an analogy—real shells are three-dimensional regions around the nucleus, not flat floors.)

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Shell (orbit)** | A fixed, allowed circular path (or region) around the nucleus in which electrons move | K, L, M, N shells |
| **Energy level** | Another name for a shell, because each shell has a specific, fixed energy | $n = 1, 2, 3, 4$ |
| **$n$ (shell number)** | The number of a shell counting outward from the nucleus ($1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$) | For the K shell, $n = 1$ |
| **Capacity** | The maximum number of electrons a given shell can hold ($2n^2$) | The capacity of the L shell is 8 |
| **Outermost shell (valence shell)** | The farthest occupied shell from the nucleus in an atom | The M shell in a sodium atom |
| **Electronic arrangement (configuration)** | The distribution of an atom's electrons across the K, L, M, N shells | Carbon: 2, 4 |
| **Bohr-Bury rules** | The set of rules governing how electrons fill the shells (for the first 20 elements) | Covered in Part C of this topic |

## Part A: Bohr's Postulates (1913)

To fix the stability problem in Rutherford's model, **Niels Bohr (1913)** proposed a new model of the atom based on three key postulates:

1. Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain **fixed, allowed circular paths** called **discrete orbits**, **shells**, or **energy levels**.
2. **While an electron remains in one of these allowed shells, it does not radiate (lose) energy.** Because it loses no energy, it never spirals into the nucleus—explaining why atoms are stable!
3. Each shell has a fixed amount of energy. Shells closer to the nucleus have lower energy, and shells farther out have higher energy. An electron can jump from one shell to another only by absorbing or emitting the exact difference in energy between the two shells—never stopping in between, just like our elevator.

Because Bohr's model neatly resolves Rutherford's stability puzzle and explains the chemical behaviour of the elements clearly, it is the primary model we use in Class 9 Chemistry. (In higher classes, you will see how quantum mechanics refines this model further for larger, more complex atoms.)

Starting from the innermost shell closest to the nucleus and moving outward, the shells are named **K, L, M, N** (or numbered **$n = 1, 2, 3, 4$**).

```
   .-------------------------------------------.
   :  N shell (n = 4)                          :
   :   .-----------------------------------.   :
   :   :  M shell (n = 3)                  :   :
   :   :   .---------------------------.   :   :
   :   :   :  L shell (n = 2)          :   :   :
   :   :   :   .-------------------.   :   :   :
   :   :   :   :  K shell (n = 1)  :   :   :   :
   :   :   :   :         (+)       :   :   :   :
   :   :   :   :       nucleus     :   :   :   :
   :   :   :   '-------------------'   :   :   :
   :   :   '---------------------------'   :   :
   :   '-----------------------------------'   :
   '-------------------------------------------'
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Bohr's shells nested outward from the nucleus (+): innermost is the K shell ($n = 1$), followed by L ($n = 2$), M ($n = 3$), and N ($n = 4$). This diagram is schematic (drawn as nested boxes for clarity, whereas real shells are concentric rings/spheres and are not equally spaced). Electrons can reside only on these shells, never in the gaps between them.

## Part B: How Many Electrons in a Shell? The Formula 2n²

Think of each shell as a concert-hall tier with a fixed number of seats. Niels Bohr and Charles Bury showed that the maximum number of electrons that can fit into the $n$-th shell is given by a simple formula:

> **Maximum number of electrons in a shell $= 2n^2$**

Here **$n$** is the shell number ($1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$), and $n^2$ means $n \times n$. Let us calculate the capacity of the first four shells:

- **$n = 1$ (K shell):** $n^2 = 1 \times 1 = 1 \implies 2n^2 = 2 \times 1 =$ **2 electrons**.
- **$n = 2$ (L shell):** $n^2 = 2 \times 2 = 4 \implies 2n^2 = 2 \times 4 =$ **8 electrons**.
- **$n = 3$ (M shell):** $n^2 = 3 \times 3 = 9 \implies 2n^2 = 2 \times 9 =$ **18 electrons**.
- **$n = 4$ (N shell):** $n^2 = 4 \times 4 = 16 \implies 2n^2 = 2 \times 16 =$ **32 electrons**.

| Shell | Shell Number ($n$) | $n^2$ | Maximum Capacity ($= 2n^2$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| K | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| L | 2 | 4 | 8 |
| M | 3 | 9 | 18 |
| N | 4 | 16 | 32 |

Check: Total capacity across all four shells $= 2 + 8 + 18 + 32 = 60$ electrons ✅

Remember: **capacity** means the *maximum* number of electrons a shell can hold—a shell does not have to be completely full in every atom. Next, let us see the rules for how shells fill up one by one.

---

## Part C: The Bohr-Bury Rules (For the First 20 Elements)

How do electrons distribute themselves across the shells of an atom? **Bohr and Bury (1921)** formulated three straightforward rules:

1. **Maximum capacity is $2n^2$:** No shell can hold more than $2n^2$ electrons (K holds at most 2, L holds at most 8, M holds at most 18, N holds at most 32).
2. **The outermost shell can hold at most 8 electrons** (and if the K shell is the outermost shell, it can hold at most 2).
3. **Inner shells fill first (stepwise filling):** Electrons occupy the lowest-energy (innermost) available shell first. A new outer shell does not start filling until the inner shells have reached their required count.

These three rules work cleanly for the **first 20 elements** (from hydrogen, with 1 electron, up to calcium, with 20 electrons), which is our complete scope in Class 9.

**Pay special attention to Rule 2 after Argon (18 electrons):** Even though the M shell ($n = 3$) has a theoretical maximum capacity of 18 electrons, **it cannot hold more than 8 electrons while it is the outermost shell** (Rule 2). Therefore, once argon reaches **2, 8, 8**, the M shell pauses at 8, and the next electron goes into the **N shell**:
- **Potassium (19 electrons):** **2, 8, 8, 1** (NOT 2, 8, 9!)
- **Calcium (20 electrons):** **2, 8, 8, 2** (NOT 2, 8, 10!)

**An honest note on scope:** Up to calcium (20 electrons), this simple scheme works without exception. Beyond calcium (starting from element 21, scandium), additional electrons begin filling the remaining spots in the inner M shell (up toward 18), following subshell rules that you will study in higher classes.

```
   Order of filling electrons (for the first 20 elements):

   K (up to 2) -> L (up to 8) -> M (up to 8 first) -> N (1, then 2)
   fill first     fill next      pauses at 8          starts after Ar

   Ar : 2, 8, 8       K : 2, 8, 8, 1       Ca : 2, 8, 8, 2
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Order of filling for the first 20 elements: fill K (2), then L (8), then M up to 8, and then place the 19th and 20th electrons into the N shell (rather than putting a 9th electron into M).

## Part D: Drawing Atomic Diagrams (Step by Step)

In this topic, we will take the **total number of electrons** in a neutral atom as given (in Topic 4 we will see how that number is determined by the element's atomic number). To write the electronic arrangement and sketch the atom:

- **Step 1:** Note the total number of electrons.
- **Step 2:** Put up to **2** electrons in the innermost **K shell**.
- **Step 3:** Put the remaining electrons in the **L shell**, up to **8**.
- **Step 4:** Put any remaining electrons in the **M shell**, up to **8**. If 1 or 2 electrons still remain (for 19 or 20 electrons), place them in the **N shell**.
- **Step 5:** Add the shell counts to verify that their sum equals the total number of electrons.

**Example 1: Hydrogen (1 electron).** K gets 1. Arrangement: **1**. (Just one shell containing 1 electron.)

**Example 2: Helium (2 electrons).** K gets 2. Arrangement: **2**. (The K shell is now completely full.)

**Example 3: Lithium (3 electrons).** K gets 2, leaving 1 for L. Arrangement: **2, 1**. Check: $2 + 1 = 3$ ✅

**Example 4: Carbon (6 electrons).** K gets 2, leaving 4 for L. Arrangement: **2, 4**. Check: $2 + 4 = 6$ ✅

**Example 5: Oxygen (8 electrons).** K gets 2, leaving 6 for L. Arrangement: **2, 6**. Check: $2 + 6 = 8$ ✅

**Example 6: Neon (10 electrons).** K gets 2, leaving 8 for L (L is now completely full). Arrangement: **2, 8**. Check: $2 + 8 = 10$ ✅

```
   Lithium (Li): 2, 1          Carbon (C): 2, 4

               e                           e
            .-----.                     .-----.
           /       \                   /       \
          (  e (+) e )               e (  e (+) e )  e
           \       /                   \       /
            '-----'                     '-----'
                                           e

   Neon (Ne): 2, 8

               e
        e   .-----.   e
           /       \
      e   (  e (+) e )   e
           \       /
        e   '-----'   e
               e
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Shell diagrams for Lithium (2, 1), Carbon (2, 4), and Neon (2, 8). At the centre is the nucleus (+), surrounded by the inner K shell holding 2 electrons (`e`), and outside it the L shell holding 1, 4, and 8 electrons respectively. Check the totals: Li $= 2 + 1 = 3$, C $= 2 + 4 = 6$, Ne $= 2 + 8 = 10$ electrons. (These sketches are schematic diagrams for counting shells.)

**Example 7: Sodium (11 electrons).** K gets 2 ($11 - 2 = 9$ left), L gets 8 ($9 - 8 = 1$ left), M gets 1. Arrangement: **2, 8, 1**. Check: $2 + 8 + 1 = 11$ ✅

**Example 8: Chlorine (17 electrons).** K gets 2, L gets 8, leaving $17 - 10 = 7$ for M. Arrangement: **2, 8, 7**. Check: $2 + 8 + 7 = 17$ ✅

**Example 9: Argon (18 electrons).** K gets 2, L gets 8, leaving 8 for M. Arrangement: **2, 8, 8**. Notice M holds 8 here, because as the outermost shell it cannot exceed 8 (Rule 2).

**Example 10: Potassium (19 electrons).** K gets 2, L gets 8, M gets 8, leaving 1 electron. Because M cannot hold 9 as the outermost shell, the 19th electron enters the **N shell**. Arrangement: **2, 8, 8, 1**. Check: $2 + 8 + 8 + 1 = 19$ ✅

**Example 11: Calcium (20 electrons).** K gets 2, L gets 8, M gets 8, leaving 2 for the N shell. Arrangement: **2, 8, 8, 2**. Check: $2 + 8 + 8 + 2 = 20$ ✅

```
   Atom      K        L                   M                  N

   Na (11)   e e      e e e e e e e e     e
   Cl (17)   e e      e e e e e e e e     e e e e e e e
   K  (19)   e e      e e e e e e e e     e e e e e e e e    e
   Ca (20)   e e      e e e e e e e e     e e e e e e e e    e e
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Shell-by-shell electron counts for larger atoms: Na (2, 8, 1), Cl (2, 8, 7), K (2, 8, 8, 1), and Ca (2, 8, 8, 2). Each `e` represents one electron in that shell. Notice how in K and Ca, the M shell stops at 8 and the N shell begins filling.

One more reminder: all of these diagrams are built from **identical electrons**. Sodium does not have "sodium-flavoured electrons"—what distinguishes sodium from chlorine or neon is simply the total number of protons and electrons, and how those electrons are distributed across the shells.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing the "K shell" with the element potassium (K).**
   The **K shell** is the innermost electron shell ($n = 1$) in every atom, whereas **K** is the chemical symbol for the element potassium (from *kalium*). Always check the context!

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming a shell must always be filled to its maximum capacity.**
   The M shell has a capacity of 18, yet in sodium it holds only 1 electron (2, 8, 1) and in argon it holds 8 (2, 8, 8). Capacity is an upper limit, not a requirement.

3. **Mistake 3: Writing potassium's arrangement as 2, 8, 9 (or calcium's as 2, 8, 10).**
   By Bohr-Bury Rule 2, the outermost shell can never contain more than 8 electrons. Potassium is **2, 8, 8, 1** and calcium is **2, 8, 8, 2**.

4. **Mistake 4: Filling outer shells before inner shells are full.**
   Electrons always fill the lowest-energy inner shells first. Lithium (3 electrons) is **2, 1**, never 1, 2 or 0, 3.

5. **Mistake 5: Calculating $(2n)^2$ instead of $2n^2$.**
   In $2n^2$, square $n$ first and then multiply by 2. For $n = 3$, $2 \times (3^2) = 2 \times 9 = 18$, NOT $(2 \times 3)^2 = 36$.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking the Class 9 shell rules apply unchanged past calcium.**
   The simple 2, 8, 8, 2 rule is exact for the first 20 elements; from element 21 onward, inner shells resume filling up to 18, which is covered in senior classes.

## Quick Recap

- Rutherford's model could not explain why orbiting electrons don't lose energy and spiral into the nucleus. **Niels Bohr (1913)** proposed that electrons move in **fixed shells (energy levels)** and **do not lose energy** while remaining in a shell.
- **Elevator analogy:** An elevator stops only at fixed floors, never between floors; similarly, an electron occupies only fixed shells, never the space between shells.
- The shells are named **K, L, M, N** ($n = 1, 2, 3, 4$) moving outward from the nucleus.
- Maximum capacity of a shell $= \mathbf{2n^2}$: **K = 2, L = 8, M = 18, N = 32**.
- **Bohr-Bury rules:** (1) Maximum capacity of shell $n$ is $2n^2$; (2) the outermost shell holds at most **8** electrons (2 if K is outermost); (3) inner shells fill first.
- Sample arrangements: H (1), He (2), Li (2, 1), C (2, 4), O (2, 6), Ne (2, 8), Na (2, 8, 1), Cl (2, 8, 7), Ar (2, 8, 8), K (2, 8, 8, 1), Ca (2, 8, 8, 2).
- After argon (2, 8, 8), the next electron enters the **N shell** (potassium: 2, 8, 8, 1; calcium: 2, 8, 8, 2).

11. State **True** or **False**, giving a reason for each:
    (a) "K shell" and the symbol "K" for potassium mean the same thing.

### Answers

1. In Rutherford's model, a revolving electron should continuously radiate energy and spiral into the nucleus. Bohr resolved this by proposing that electrons revolve only in **discrete, fixed shells** and **do not radiate energy** as long as they remain in a given shell, keeping the atom stable.

2. (i) Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain fixed circular orbits called shells or energy levels. (ii) While in an allowed shell, an electron does not lose or radiate energy. (iii) An electron jumps from one shell to another only by absorbing or emitting the exact energy difference between the two shells, never remaining between shells.

3. Just as an elevator can stop only at built floors (Floor 3 or Floor 4, never 3.5), an electron can exist only in allowed energy shells and never in the gap between them. The analogy breaks down because an elevator is a large everyday object moving up a straight shaft with equally spaced floors, whereas electrons are subatomic particles moving in three-dimensional shells whose energy spacings are not equal.

4. $n = 1$: $2 \times 1^2 = \mathbf{2}$ (**K shell**). $n = 2$: $2 \times 2^2 = \mathbf{8}$ (**L shell**). $n = 3$: $2 \times 3^2 = \mathbf{18}$ (**M shell**). $n = 4$: $2 \times 4^2 = \mathbf{32}$ (**N shell**).

5. (1) The maximum number of electrons in shell $n$ is $2n^2$. (2) The outermost shell can hold at most 8 electrons (or at most 2 if K is the outermost shell). (3) Inner shells must be filled before outer shells begin filling.

6. (a) 5 electrons: K = 2, L = 3 → **2, 3**. (b) 9 electrons: K = 2, L = 7 → **2, 7**. (c) 12 electrons: K = 2, L = 8, M = 2 → **2, 8, 2**. (d) 16 electrons: K = 2, L = 8, M = 6 → **2, 8, 6**. Check totals: $2+3=5$, $2+7=9$, $2+8+2=12$, $2+8+6=16$ ✅

7. Writing `2, 8, 9` violates Rule 2, which states that the outermost shell cannot hold more than 8 electrons. Once the M shell reaches 8, the 19th electron goes into the N shell, giving **2, 8, 8, 1**. Check: $2 + 8 + 8 + 1 = 19$ ✅

8. (a) 15 electrons: **2, 8, 5** (outermost shell is **M**, with **5** electrons). (b) 18 electrons: **2, 8, 8** (outermost shell is **M**, with **8** electrons). (c) 20 electrons: **2, 8, 8, 2** (outermost shell is **N**, with **2** electrons).

9. First, the K (2) and L (8) shells fill up first, taking 10 of argon's 18 electrons and leaving 8 for the M shell. Second, by Rule 2, the outermost shell can never hold more than 8 electrons anyway; 18 is the M shell's maximum capacity when inner shells of even larger atoms fill later.

10. The K shell ($n = 1$) has a maximum capacity of $2(1^2) = 2$ electrons, so it can never hold 8 electrons. Filling K with 2 and L with 8 leaves 1 electron for the M shell, giving sodium's correct arrangement: **2, 8, 1**. Check: $2 + 8 + 1 = 11$ ✅

11. Both statements are **False** ❌. (a) The "K shell" is the innermost shell ($n = 1$) of any atom, whereas "K" is the chemical symbol for the element potassium. (b) An occupied shell does not have to be completely full—for example, sodium's M shell holds just 1 electron even though its capacity is 18.

12. Up to calcium (`2, 8, 8, 2`), the rule of pausing the M shell at 8 and starting the N shell works directly. Beyond calcium (from element 21 onward), additional electrons begin filling the remaining spaces in the inner M shell (up to 18), which requires subshell rules taught in higher classes.
