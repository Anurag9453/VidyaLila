# Topic 4: Atomic Number and Mass Number

In Topic 3, we saw that protons and neutrons sit inside the central nucleus while electrons occupy surrounding shells. Now comes a fundamental question: what determines **which element** a particular atom belongs to? After all, a sodium atom and a chlorine atom are built from the exact same three kinds of particles (protons, neutrons, and electrons)—only their counts differ. In this topic, we will master two simple whole numbers—the **atomic number ($Z$)** and the **mass number ($A$)**—that let us immediately find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in any atom or ion, and explain why the first 20 elements were listed in that exact serial order back in Chapter 3.

---

## Student ID Numbers and the Class Total

On Monday morning, Ms. Alvarez took attendance in homeroom. Every student on her roster had a unique roster number, and Ethan's number was 11. Peeking at the attendance sheet, Ethan asked, "Ms. Alvarez, my roster number is 11, and there are 40 students in our class. Between 11 and 40, which number is my real identifier?"

Ms. Alvarez smiled. "Number 11 is your unique identifier. No other student in this class has roster number 11. The number 40 is just the **total headcount** in the room. Even if two students are absent tomorrow so the room total drops to 38, or two new students join so the total becomes 42, your roster number is still 11—and you are still Ethan."

Ethan nodded. "So the roster number tells us **who** someone is, while the total tells us **how many** are in the room altogether?"

"Exactly," Ms. Alvarez said. "And atoms work the same way. Every element has a unique 'roster number' that defines its **identity**, and each atom also has a 'headcount total' telling us how many heavy particles sit inside its nucleus. Let's give both numbers their proper scientific names."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atomic number ($Z$)** | The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom; defines the element's identity | For sodium (Na), $Z = 11$ |
| **Mass number ($A$)** | The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus ($p + n$) | For common sodium, $A = 23$ |
| **Nucleon** | Any particle that lives in the nucleus (either a proton or a neutron) | A $^{23}\text{Na}$ nucleus has 23 nucleons |
| **Neutral atom** | An atom with equal numbers of protons and electrons, so its net charge is 0 | Neutral Na: 11 protons, 11 electrons |
| **Ion** | A charged particle formed when an atom gains or loses one or more electrons | $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Cl}^-$ |
| **Cation** | A positively charged ion (formed by losing electrons) | $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ |
| **Anion** | A negatively charged ion (formed by gaining electrons) | $\text{O}^{2-}$ |

---

## Part A: Atomic Number Z — The Identity of an Element

**Atomic number ($Z$) = the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom.**

This single number determines which element an atom belongs to:

- Every atom with **1 proton** ($Z = 1$) is always **hydrogen**.
- Every atom with **6 protons** ($Z = 6$) is always **carbon**.
- Every atom with **11 protons** ($Z = 11$) is always **sodium**.

If an atom gains or loses an electron (or even has a different number of neutrons), it still remains the exact same element as long as its proton count does not change. Conversely, if the number of protons changes, **it becomes a completely different element**. That is why the atomic number $Z$ is the true "ID number" of an element.

Remember one more essential fact from Topic 1 (and from the [Electricity](../../physics/electricity/electricity.md) chapter in Physics): in any **neutral atom**, the positive charges of the protons and the negative charges of the electrons balance out to zero, which means:

$$\textbf{In a neutral atom: Number of electrons} = \textbf{Number of protons} = \mathbf{Z}$$

### Example 1. Atomic numbers of hydrogen, carbon, and sodium

- **Hydrogen:** 1 proton $\implies Z = 1$, and a neutral hydrogen atom has 1 electron.
- **Carbon:** 6 protons $\implies Z = 6$, and a neutral carbon atom has 6 electrons.
- **Sodium:** 11 protons $\implies Z = 11$, and a neutral sodium atom has 11 electrons.

Check: In each neutral atom, protons $=$ electrons ($1 = 1$, $6 = 6$, $11 = 11$) ✅

## Part B: Mass Number A — The Total Count in the Nucleus

Recall from Topic 1 that a proton and a neutron each have a mass of approximately $1\text{ u}$, whereas an electron has a mass of only about $1/1840\text{ u}$ (practically zero). Therefore, almost the entire mass of an atom sits in its nucleus and is determined by the **total count of protons and neutrons** (collectively called **nucleons**).

$$\textbf{Mass number } (\mathbf{A}) = \textbf{Number of protons } (\mathbf{Z}) + \textbf{Number of neutrons } (\mathbf{n})$$

Because $A$ is a **count of particles**, the mass number of any single atom is always a **whole number**. Rearranging this equation gives the formula for finding the number of neutrons:

$$\textbf{Number of neutrons } (\mathbf{n}) = \mathbf{A - Z}$$

For most common atoms in our Chapter 4 atomic mass table, the mass number of the most abundant atom matches the atomic mass rounded to the nearest whole number: H = 1, C = 12, N = 14, O = 16, F = 19, Na = 23, Mg = 24, Al = 27, Si = 28, P = 31, S = 32, Cl = 35, K = 39, Ca = 40. *(Why is chlorine's average atomic mass $35.5\text{ u}$ when no atom can have half a neutron? Because natural chlorine is a mixture of atoms with $A = 35$ and $A = 37$—we will calculate that exact average in Topic 6!)*

Keep the distinction clear: **mass number ($A$)** is a pure whole-number count of nucleons (e.g., $23$), whereas **atomic mass** is a measured mass expressed in atomic mass units $\text{u}$ (e.g., $23\text{ u}$).

## Part C: How to Write Z and A with an Element Symbol

Chemists use a standard nuclear notation around an element's chemical symbol $\text{X}$: the **mass number $A$ is written as a superscript on the top-left**, and the **atomic number $Z$ is written as a subscript on the bottom-left** ($^A_Z\text{X}$).

```
        mass number A  (protons + neutrons)
              :
              v
             23
                 Na   <-- chemical symbol (element)
             11
              ^
              :
        atomic number Z  (protons)

   Written inline:   ²³₁₁Na
   Meaning:  A = 23,  Z = 11,  element = sodium
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Standard nuclear notation ($^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$): the top-left superscript is the mass number $A$ ($\text{protons} + \text{neutrons}$), and the bottom-left subscript is the atomic number $Z$ ($\text{protons}$). Because the symbol $\text{Na}$ already tells us the element is sodium ($Z = 11$), the subscript is sometimes omitted in shorthand (e.g., $^{23}\text{Na}$ or sodium-23).

From $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$, we can read off at a glance: protons $= 11$, neutrons $= 23 - 11 = 12$, and (in a neutral atom) electrons $= 11$.

### Example 2. Reading $^{12}_6\text{C}$

Here $A = 12$ and $Z = 6$.
Protons $= 6$, neutrons $= 12 - 6 = 6$, and electrons (in a neutral atom) $= 6$.

Check: $\text{protons} + \text{neutrons} = 6 + 6 = 12 = A$ ✅

## Part D: Counting Particles in a Neutral Atom, Step by Step

For any neutral atom $^A_Z\text{X}$, follow three simple steps:

1. Read $Z$: this gives the number of **protons**, and also the number of **electrons** (since the atom is neutral).
2. Read $A$: calculate **$\text{neutrons} = A - Z$**.
3. Check your work: verify that **$\text{protons} + \text{neutrons} = A$**.

```
   Neutral sodium atom, ²³₁₁Na

            nucleus
        ┌─────────────┐
        │  11 p+      │        shell K : 2 e-
        │  12 n       │        shell L : 8 e-
        └─────────────┘        shell M : 1 e-
                               -----------------
   protons   = Z      = 11     total e- = 2 + 8 + 1 = 11
   neutrons  = A - Z  = 23 - 11 = 12
   electrons = Z      = 11     (neutral atom, so p = e)
   total nucleons = 11 + 12 = 23 = A
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Particle count in a neutral sodium atom ($^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$): the nucleus holds 11 protons and 12 neutrons (total $= 23 = A$), while the shells outside hold $2 + 8 + 1 = 11$ electrons (by the Bohr-Bury rules from Topic 3). Because the atom is neutral, protons equal electrons.

### Example 3. Sodium, $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$

- Protons $= Z =$ **11**
- Neutrons $= 23 - 11 =$ **12**
- Electrons $=$ **11**

Check: $11 + 12 = 23$ ✅, and $11\text{ p}^+ = 11\text{ e}^-$ ✅

### Example 4. Chlorine, $^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$

- Protons $=$ **17**
- Neutrons $= 35 - 17 =$ **18**
- Electrons $=$ **17**

Check: $17 + 18 = 35$ ✅

### Example 5. Aluminium, $^{27}_{13}\text{Al}$

- Protons $=$ **13**
- Neutrons $= 27 - 13 =$ **14**
- Electrons $=$ **13**

Check: $13 + 14 = 27$ ✅

### Example 6. Calcium, $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$

- Protons $=$ **20**
- Neutrons $= 40 - 20 =$ **20**
- Electrons $=$ **20**

Check: $20 + 20 = 40$ ✅

### Example 7. Oxygen, $^{16}_8\text{O}$

- Protons $=$ **8**
- Neutrons $= 16 - 8 =$ **8**
- Electrons $=$ **8**

Check: $8 + 8 = 16$ ✅

### Example 8. Ordinary Hydrogen, $^1_1\text{H}$

- Protons $=$ **1**
- Neutrons $= 1 - 1 =$ **0**
- Electrons $=$ **1**

Check: $1 + 0 = 1$ ✅. Indeed, ordinary hydrogen-1 is the only stable atom whose nucleus has **zero neutrons** (as we noted in Topic 1).

## Part E: Working Backwards: Finding the Element from A, Z, or Protons

### Example 9. An atom has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Find Z, A, and the symbol.

- $Z = \text{protons} =$ **6**
- $A = 6 + 6 =$ **12**
- Element $Z = 6$ is carbon, so the notation is **$^{12}_6\text{C}$**.

Check: $A - Z = 12 - 6 = 6\text{ neutrons}$ ✅

### Example 10. An atom has $A = 31$ and 16 neutrons. Identify the element.

- $Z = A - \text{neutrons} = 31 - 16 =$ **15**
- Element $Z = 15$ is phosphorus, so the atom is **$^{31}_{15}\text{P}$**.

Check: $15\text{ protons} + 16\text{ neutrons} = 31$ ✅, and a neutral atom has 15 electrons.

### Example 11. Counting particles in a heavy atom: Uranium-235 ($^{235}_{92}\text{U}$)

Even for an element far beyond the first 20, the counting rules work identically:

- Protons $= Z =$ **92**
- Neutrons $= 235 - 92 =$ **143**
- Electrons (neutral) $=$ **92**

Check: $92 + 143 = 235$ ✅. Notice that heavy nuclei have significantly more neutrons than protons.

## Part F: Ions: Only Electrons Change, Never Protons

In Topic 3, we saw that electrons occupy the outer shells of an atom. During chemical changes, an atom can **lose** or **gain** one or more outer electrons to form a charged **ion** (as we first met in Chapter 3). Let us see how to count particles in an ion.

**The Golden Rule of Ions:** When an atom forms an ion, **only the number of electrons changes. The number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus NEVER changes** (so the element's identity stays the same!).

- **Losing electrons** leaves more positive protons than negative electrons, producing a **positive ion (cation)**.
- **Gaining electrons** gives more negative electrons than positive protons, producing a **negative ion (anion)**.

$$\textbf{Net charge on an ion} = \textbf{Number of protons} - \textbf{Number of electrons}$$

Compare neutral $\text{Na}$ and $\text{Cl}$ with their ions $\text{Na}^+$ and $\text{Cl}^-$:

| Species | Protons | Electrons | Net Charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| $\text{Na}$ (neutral atom) | 11 | 11 | $0$ |
| $\text{Na}^+$ (lost $1\text{ e}^-$) | 11 | 10 | $+1$ |
| $\text{Cl}$ (neutral atom) | 17 | 17 | $0$ |
| $\text{Cl}^-$ (gained $1\text{ e}^-$) | 17 | 18 | $-1$ |

Notice that the proton counts remain fixed at 11 and 17; only the electron counts change by 1.

### Example 12. Sodium ion, $\text{Na}^+$ ($A = 23, Z = 11$)

- Protons $=$ **11**
- Neutrons $= 23 - 11 =$ **12**
- Electrons $= 11 - 1 =$ **10**

Check: $\text{Charge} = 11 - 10 = \mathbf{+1}$ ✅

### Example 13. Chloride ion, $\text{Cl}^-$ ($A = 35, Z = 17$)

- Protons $=$ **17**
- Neutrons $= 35 - 17 =$ **18**
- Electrons $= 17 + 1 =$ **18**

Check: $\text{Charge} = 17 - 18 = \mathbf{-1}$ ✅

### Example 14. Magnesium ion, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($A = 24, Z = 12$)

- Protons $=$ **12**
- Neutrons $= 24 - 12 =$ **12**
- Electrons $= 12 - 2 =$ **10**

Check: $\text{Charge} = 12 - 10 = \mathbf{+2}$ ✅

### Example 15. Oxide ion, $\text{O}^{2-}$ ($A = 16, Z = 8$)

- Protons $=$ **8**
- Neutrons $= 16 - 8 =$ **8**
- Electrons $= 8 + 2 =$ **10**

Check: $\text{Charge} = 8 - 10 = \mathbf{-2}$ ✅

### Example 16. Aluminium ion, $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ($A = 27, Z = 13$)

- Protons $=$ **13**
- Neutrons $= 27 - 13 =$ **14**
- Electrons $= 13 - 3 =$ **10**

Check: $\text{Charge} = 13 - 10 = \mathbf{+3}$ ✅

### Example 17. Different ions with the exact same electron count: $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{O}^{2-}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$

Look closely at Examples 12, 14, 15, and 16: $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{O}^{2-}$, and $\text{Al}^{3+}$ all have **10 electrons**, even though they have 11, 12, 8, and 13 protons respectively! Because their proton counts differ, they are still four different elements. Why do all four stop at exactly 10 electrons? We will uncover the reason in Topic 5!

Check: $\text{Na}^+: 11 - 1 = 10$ ✅, $\text{Mg}^{2+}: 12 - 2 = 10$ ✅, $\text{O}^{2-}: 8 + 2 = 10$ ✅, $\text{Al}^{3+}: 13 - 3 = 10$ ✅

### Example 18. Potassium ion $\text{K}^+$ and Calcium ion $\text{Ca}^{2+}$

- $\text{K}^+$ ($A = 39, Z = 19$): protons $=$ **19**, neutrons $= 39 - 19 =$ **20**, electrons $= 19 - 1 =$ **18**.
- $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ ($A = 40, Z = 20$): protons $=$ **20**, neutrons $= 40 - 20 =$ **20**, electrons $= 20 - 2 =$ **18**.

Check: $\text{K}^+$ charge $= 19 - 18 = +1$ ✅; $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ charge $= 20 - 20 = +2$ ✅ (and both ions have 18 electrons!).

## Part G: Why Elements Are Ordered by Atomic Number Z

In Chapter 3, our table of the first 20 elements listed them in a numbered serial order from $\text{H} = 1$ and $\text{He} = 2$ up to $\text{Ca} = 20$. We promised then to explain where that serial number came from. Now you know: that serial number is simply the **atomic number $Z$** (the number of protons). Just as Ms. Alvarez orders her class roster by student ID number, chemists order the elements by their proton count $Z$.

Why not order elements by their mass number $A$ instead? Look at **argon** and **potassium**:

- **Argon ($Z = 18$)** has a common mass number of **$A = 40$**.
- **Potassium ($Z = 19$)** has a common mass number of **$A = 39$**.

Even though argon has fewer protons than potassium ($18 < 19$), a common argon atom has more neutrons ($22$ vs. $20$), making its mass number slightly higher ($40 > 39$). If we ordered elements by mass number, reactive potassium metal would be placed *before* inert argon gas, scrambling the chemical families! Ordering by **atomic number $Z$** puts argon ($18$) before potassium ($19$), where each belongs chemically.

### Example 19. Comparing Argon and Potassium

| Property | Argon ($^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$) | Potassium ($^{39}_{19}\text{K}$) |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic number $Z$ (protons) | 18 | 19 |
| Mass number $A$ | 40 | 39 |
| Neutrons ($A - Z$) | 22 | 20 |
| Electrons (in neutral atom) | 18 | 19 |

Check: $\text{Ar}: 40 - 18 = 22$ ✅; $\text{K}: 39 - 19 = 20$ ✅. Ordered by $Z$, $\text{Ar}$ ($18$) comes before $\text{K}$ ($19$).

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Swapping atomic number ($Z$) and mass number ($A$).**
   $Z =$ **protons only** (the smaller number, written at the bottom-left); $A =$ **protons $+$ neutrons** (the larger number, written at the top-left: $^A_Z\text{X}$).

2. **Mistake 2: Adding $A + Z$ to find the number of neutrons.**
   Always **subtract**: $\text{neutrons} = A - Z$.

3. **Mistake 3: Changing the number of protons when an ion forms.**
   In $\text{Na}^+$, the proton count is still **11**, never 10! Forming an ion only ever changes the **electron** count. Changing the protons would turn sodium into a different element.

4. **Mistake 4: Adding electrons for a positive ion (or subtracting for a negative ion).**
   Because electrons carry a negative charge, a **positive ion ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$) has LOST electrons** ($12 - 2 = 10\text{ e}^-$), while a **negative ion ($\text{O}^{2-}$) has GAINED electrons** ($8 + 2 = 10\text{ e}^-$).

5. **Mistake 5: Writing a decimal for a mass number $A$.**
   The mass number $A$ of a single atom is always a whole number (like 35 or 37) because you cannot have half a proton or neutron. Chlorine's $35.5\text{ u}$ is an *average atomic mass* across isotopes, not a mass number.

6. **Mistake 6: Including electrons when calculating the mass number $A$.**
   $A = \text{protons} + \text{neutrons}$. Electrons are ~1840 times lighter and are never counted in $A$.

## Quick Recap

- **Atomic number ($Z$)** $=$ number of protons in the nucleus; it defines the **identity** of the element.
- **Mass number ($A$)** $=$ protons $+$ neutrons; therefore, **$\text{neutrons} = A - Z$**.
- Standard nuclear notation places $A$ top-left and $Z$ bottom-left: $^A_Z\text{X}$ (e.g., $^{23}_{11}\text{Na}$).
- In a **neutral atom**, $\text{electrons} = \text{protons} = Z$.
- In an **ion**, only the number of electrons changes: cations have fewer electrons than protons, anions have more. **$\text{Charge} = \text{protons} - \text{electrons}$.**
- $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{O}^{2-}$, and $\text{Al}^{3+}$ all have 10 electrons (we will see why in Topic 5).
- Elements are ordered by **atomic number $Z$** (not mass number $A$), which correctly places $\text{Ar}$ ($Z = 18, A = 40$) before $\text{K}$ ($Z = 19, A = 39$).

## Practice Questions

1. What is the atomic number of an element? What does it tell us about an atom, and why is it called the "unique identity" of an element?

2. Define mass number ($A$). Write the formula connecting mass number ($A$), atomic number ($Z$), and the number of neutrons ($n$).

3. Write the standard nuclear notation ($^A_Z\text{X}$) and find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in a neutral atom of: (a) carbon ($Z = 6, A = 12$), (b) oxygen ($Z = 8, A = 16$), (c) aluminium ($Z = 13, A = 27$), (d) calcium ($Z = 20, A = 40$).

4. A neutral atom has 17 protons and 18 neutrons. Find its atomic number, its mass number, and identify the element.

5. An atom has a mass number of 31 and contains 16 neutrons. Find its atomic number $Z$, name the element, and state how many electrons are in the neutral atom.

6. Find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in each of the following ions: (a) $\text{Na}^+$ ($A = 23, Z = 11$), (b) $\text{Cl}^-$ ($A = 35, Z = 17$), (c) $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ ($A = 24, Z = 12$), (d) $\text{O}^{2-}$ ($A = 16, Z = 8$), (e) $\text{Al}^{3+}$ ($A = 27, Z = 13$).

7. A student writes, "$\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has 14 electrons because the $+2$ means we add 2." Explain the mistake and find the correct number of electrons.

8. Why do $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{O}^{2-}$, and $\text{Al}^{3+}$ all have 10 electrons even though they belong to four different elements? How do their nuclei differ?

9. Argon has a mass number of 40 and an atomic number of 18. Potassium has a mass number of 39 and an atomic number of 19. In which order should they be placed in the periodic table, and why?

10. State **True** or **False**, and correct the false statements:
    (a) The atomic number equals the number of neutrons in the nucleus.
    (b) In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the number of protons.
    (c) The mass number of an atom is always a whole number.
    (d) When an atom forms an ion, its number of protons changes.

### Answers

1. The atomic number ($Z$) is the **number of protons** in the nucleus of an atom. Because every element has its own fixed, unique number of protons ($\text{H} = 1, \text{C} = 6, \text{Na} = 11$), $Z$ uniquely identifies which element the atom belongs to.

2. Mass number ($A$) is the total number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) in the nucleus: $A = Z + n$, so **$\text{neutrons } (n) = A - Z$**.

3. (a) $^{12}_6\text{C}$: **6** protons, $12 - 6 =$ **6** neutrons, **6** electrons. (b) $^{16}_8\text{O}$: **8** protons, $16 - 8 =$ **8** neutrons, **8** electrons. (c) $^{27}_{13}\text{Al}$: **13** protons, $27 - 13 =$ **14** neutrons, **13** electrons. (d) $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$: **20** protons, $40 - 20 =$ **20** neutrons, **20** electrons. Check ($\text{protons} + \text{neutrons} = A$): 12, 16, 27, 40 ✅

4. $Z = \text{protons} = \mathbf{17}$. $A = 17 + 18 = \mathbf{35}$. Element $Z = 17$ is **chlorine** ($^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$). Check: $17 + 18 = 35$ ✅

5. $Z = A - \text{neutrons} = 31 - 16 = \mathbf{15}$. Element $Z = 15$ is **phosphorus** ($^{31}_{15}\text{P}$). A neutral atom has **15 electrons**. Check: $15 + 16 = 31$ ✅

6. (a) $\text{Na}^+$: **11** protons, **12** neutrons, $11 - 1 =$ **10** electrons. (b) $\text{Cl}^-$: **17** protons, **18** neutrons, $17 + 1 =$ **18** electrons. (c) $\text{Mg}^{2+}$: **12** protons, **12** neutrons, $12 - 2 =$ **10** electrons. (d) $\text{O}^{2-}$: **8** protons, **8** neutrons, $8 + 2 =$ **10** electrons. (e) $\text{Al}^{3+}$: **13** protons, **14** neutrons, $13 - 3 =$ **10** electrons. Check ($\text{charge} = p - e$): $+1, -1, +2, -2, +3$ ✅

7. Electrons carry a negative charge, so a $+2$ charge means the atom **lost** 2 negative electrons, not gained them. A neutral $\text{Mg}$ atom has 12 electrons, so $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ has $12 - 2 = \mathbf{10\text{ electrons}}$. Check: $\text{charge} = 12 - 10 = +2$ ✅

8. All four ions have 10 electrons because $\text{Na}$ ($11$) lost 1, $\text{Mg}$ ($12$) lost 2, $\text{O}$ ($8$) gained 2, and $\text{Al}$ ($13$) lost 3. Their nuclei still have different numbers of protons (11, 12, 8, and 13), which is why they remain four different elements.

9. Elements are ordered by atomic number $Z$, so **argon ($Z = 18$)** comes first, followed by **potassium ($Z = 19$)**. Ordering by mass number $A$ would wrongly place potassium ($39$) before argon ($40$), even though proton count $Z$ is the true identity of an element.

10. (a) **False** ❌ — Atomic number equals the number of **protons** ($\text{neutrons} = A - Z$). (b) **True** ✅. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌ — When an atom forms an ion, only the number of **electrons** changes; the number of protons never changes.
