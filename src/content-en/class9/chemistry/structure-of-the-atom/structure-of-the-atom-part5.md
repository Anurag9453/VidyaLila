# Topic 5: Electronic Configuration and Valency

In Topic 4, we learned that every element's identity is fixed by its **atomic number ($Z$)**, and that a neutral atom has exactly $Z$ electrons. In Topic 3, we saw that electrons occupy **shells (K, L, M, N)** with strict capacity limits. Now we bring those two ideas together: **how the $Z$ electrons of each of the first 20 elements sit in their shells**, and how the electrons in the **outermost shell** control the atom's **combining power (valency)**. Back in Chapter 3, we had to memorize the valency table and wondered why elements were listed in that numbered serial order. Today, both mysteries will make complete sense.

---

## Seating in a Concert Hall

Mr. Brooks took his niece Chloe to an evening concert at a circular amphitheatre. As the doors opened, Chloe noticed that the ushers seated the earliest arrivals right in the **innermost ring** around the stage. Only when the first ring was completely full did the ushers open the second ring, and then the third.

"Uncle, why does everyone fill the front rings first?" Chloe asked.

"Because the innermost ring is **closest to the stage**—it's the most stable, lowest-energy spot in the hall," Mr. Brooks replied. "And look at the seating capacities: the innermost ring has only **2 seats**, the second ring has **8 seats**, and the third ring has **18 seats**. Once a ring is full, the next person moves out to the next ring."

Chloe grinned. "Just like electron shells in an atom! The K shell holds 2 electrons, L holds 8, M holds 18, and electrons always fill from the inside out."

"Exactly," Mr. Brooks said. "And remember the special rule of our atomic concert hall: whichever ring happens to be on the **very outside** can never hold more than **8** people (or 2 if it's the innermost ring). Even better, the number of people sitting in that outermost ring tells you almost everything about how the atom will interact with other atoms!"

Let us map out this entire seating chart for the first 20 elements.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electronic configuration** | The shell-by-shell distribution of an atom's electrons across K, L, M, N | Sodium (Na): 2, 8, 1 |
| **Outermost shell (valence shell)** | The farthest occupied shell from the nucleus | The M shell in Na (2, 8, 1) |
| **Valence electrons** | The electrons sitting in the outermost shell of an atom | Chlorine (2, 8, 7) has 7 valence electrons |
| **Duplet** | A complete outermost K shell containing 2 electrons | Helium (He): 2 |
| **Octet** | A complete outermost shell containing 8 electrons | Neon (Ne): 2, 8; Argon (Ar): 2, 8, 8 |

| **Valency** | The number of electrons an atom loses, gains, or shares to achieve a full outer shell | Na: 1, O: 2, Al: 3 |
| **Noble gas (inert gas)** | An unreactive gas (such as He, Ne, Ar) whose outermost shell is already full | Neon (Ne) |

## Part A: Rules for Filling Shells (Bohr-Bury Recap)

Let us briefly recall the three Bohr-Bury rules from Topic 3:

1. **Inner shells fill first:** Electrons enter the lowest-energy shell first—K fills first, then L, then M, then N.
2. **Maximum shell capacity is $2n^2$:** $\text{K} = 2,\ \text{L} = 8,\ \text{M} = 18,\ \text{N} = 32$.
3. **Outermost shell limit:** The outermost shell can never hold more than **8 electrons** (or **2** if K is the only shell).

For the **first 20 elements**, this means we fill up to **2 in K**, up to **8 in L**, then up to **8 in M**, and then place the 19th and 20th electrons into the **N shell** (because M cannot hold more than 8 while it is the outermost shell).

## Part D: Duplet and Octet — What Makes an Atom Stable?

Among the first 20 elements, three stand out because their outermost shells are **already completely full**:
- **Helium ($\text{He}$): `2`** — its K shell is completely full (a **duplet**).
- **Neon ($\text{Ne}$): `2, 8`** — its outer L shell has 8 electrons (an **octet**).
- **Argon ($\text{Ar}$): `2, 8, 8`** — its outer M shell has 8 electrons (an **octet**).

These three elements are the **noble gases** (or **inert gases**). Their most striking chemical property is that **they virtually never react or combine with other atoms**—not even with themselves (they exist as single, uncombined atoms: $\text{He}, \text{Ne}, \text{Ar}$).

From this observation comes one of the most important principles in chemistry:

> **An atom with a completely filled outermost shell—2 electrons in K (a duplet) or 8 electrons in any other outer shell (an octet)—is chemically stable.**
> Atoms of other elements react with one another in order to achieve this stable duplet or octet in their outermost shell.

```
   First shell (K): 2 seats                Outer shell (L or M): 8 seats

        e   e                                  e   e
      +-------+                             e         e
      :  He   :                             e    Ne   e
      +-------+                                e   e

       Duplet = 2                            Octet = 8
       (K shell full)                     (L shell full)
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Duplet and octet stability. In helium, the K shell is full with 2 electrons (duplet). In neon, the outer L shell is full with 8 electrons (octet). In argon (2, 8, 8), the outer M shell also holds an octet of 8 electrons. Because their outer shells are already full, noble gases have no need to gain, lose, or share electrons.

How can an atom whose outer shell is incomplete achieve a duplet or octet? It has three possible routes: **lose** outer electrons, **gain** extra electrons, or **share** electrons with another atom. In the next chapter (**Chemical Bonding**), we will see how these three routes create chemical bonds. Right now, they immediately explain **valency**!

## Part E: Finding Valency from Valence Electrons

In Chapter 3, we defined **valency** as the combining capacity of an atom (how many "hooks" it has). Now we can see where valency comes from: **valency is the number of electrons an atom must lose, gain, or share to achieve a complete outer shell (an octet, or a duplet for the K shell).**

**Rules for calculating valency from valence electrons:**

- **If an atom has 1, 2, 3, or 4 valence electrons:** It is easier to lose (or share) those electrons than to gain 7, 6, or 5. Therefore:
  $$\textbf{Valency} = \textbf{Number of valence electrons}$$
- **If an atom has 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons:** It is much easier to gain (or share) 3, 2, or 1 electron to reach 8 than to lose 5, 6, or 7. Therefore:
  $$\textbf{Valency} = \mathbf{8 - (\text{Number of valence electrons})}$$
- **If an atom already has a full outer shell** (2 for He, 8 for Ne and Ar), it loses, gains, or shares **0** electrons, so its **valency is 0**.

Let us apply this rule across all 20 elements:

| Element | Configuration | Valence Electrons | Rule Used | Valency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | 1 | 1 (lose 1 or gain/share 1 for duplet) | 1 |
| He | 2 | 2 (full K) | Duplet already complete | 0 |
| Li | 2, 1 | 1 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 1 |
| Be | 2, 2 | 2 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 2 |
| B | 2, 3 | 3 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 3 |
| C | 2, 4 | 4 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 4 |
| N | 2, 5 | 5 | $8 - 5$ | 3 |
| O | 2, 6 | 6 | $8 - 6$ | 2 |
| F | 2, 7 | 7 | $8 - 7$ | 1 |
| Ne | 2, 8 | 8 | Octet already complete | 0 |
| Na | 2, 8, 1 | 1 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 1 |
| Mg | 2, 8, 2 | 2 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 2 |
| Al | 2, 8, 3 | 3 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 3 |
| Si | 2, 8, 4 | 4 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 4 |
| P | 2, 8, 5 | 5 | $8 - 5$ | 3 |

| Cl | 2, 8, 7 | 7 | $8 - 7$ | 1 |

| K | 2, 8, 8, 1 | 1 | $= \text{valence electrons}$ | 1 |

Compare the last column with the **Chapter 3 valency table**: $\text{H, Li, Na, K} = 1$; $\text{Be, Mg, Ca} = 2$; $\text{B, Al} = 3$; $\text{C, Si} = 4$; $\text{N, P} = 3$; $\text{O, S} = 2$; $\text{F, Cl} = 1$; and $\text{He, Ne, Ar} = 0$. **Every single one of the 20 elements matches!** You no longer need to memorize that table by rote—you can derive any element's valency in seconds from its atomic number $Z$.

*(Note: In Chapter 3 we mentioned that phosphorus can also show a valency of 5 and sulphur up to 6 in certain compounds; our octet rule gives their primary valencies of 3 and 2, while expanded valencies are covered in senior classes.)*

## Part F: Solved Examples

### Example 1. Electronic configuration and valency of Nitrogen ($Z = 7$)

- Total electrons $= 7$. K takes 2, leaving 5 for L.
- Configuration: **2, 5**. Valence electrons $= 5$ (which is greater than 4).
- Valency $= 8 - 5 =$ **3**.

Check: $2 + 5 = 7 = Z$ ✅, matching nitrogen's valency of 3 in Chapter 3 ✅

### Example 2. Aluminium ($Z = 13$)

- K $= 2$, L $= 8$, leaving $13 - 10 = 3$ for M.
- Configuration: **2, 8, 3**. Valence electrons $= 3$ ($\le 4$).
- Valency $=$ **3**.

Check: $2 + 8 + 3 = 13$ ✅, matching $\text{Al} = 3$ ✅

### Example 3. Chlorine ($Z = 17$)

- K $= 2$, L $= 8$, leaving 7 for M.
- Configuration: **2, 8, 7**. Since $7 > 4$, valency $= 8 - 7 =$ **1**.

Check: $2 + 8 + 7 = 17$ ✅, matching $\text{Cl} = 1$ ✅

### Example 4. Calcium ($Z = 20$): Why does the N shell start?

- K $= 2$, L $= 8$, leaving 10 electrons. Because the outermost shell cannot exceed 8, M stops at 8, and the remaining 2 electrons enter the N shell.
- Configuration: **2, 8, 8, 2**. Valence electrons $= 2$, so valency $=$ **2**.

Check: $2 + 8 + 8 + 2 = 20$ ✅, matching $\text{Ca} = 2$ ✅

### Example 5. Comparing Magnesium and Oxygen

$\text{Mg}$ is `2, 8, 2` (2 valence electrons); $\text{O}$ is `2, 6` (6 valence electrons).

- For $\text{Mg}$, losing 2 electrons is much easier than gaining 6; once those 2 M-shell electrons leave, the full L shell (8 electrons) becomes the new outer shell (an octet). Valency $= 2$.
- For $\text{O}$, gaining 2 electrons completes its L shell ($6 + 2 = 8$). Valency $= 8 - 6 = 2$.

Check: Both have valency 2 ✅

### Example 6. Identifying an element from its configuration

An atom has the electronic configuration **`2, 8, 6`**. Which element is it, and what is its valency?

- Total electrons $= 2 + 8 + 6 = 16 = Z$.
- Element $Z = 16$ is **sulphur (S)**. With 6 valence electrons, its valency is $8 - 6 =$ **2**.

Check: Sulphur has $Z = 16$ and valency 2 ✅

### Example 7. Why is Neon chemically inert?

Neon ($Z = 10$) has the configuration **`2, 8`**.

- Its outermost shell (L) already holds 8 electrons—a complete octet.
- Therefore, neon has no tendency to lose, gain, or share electrons, and its valency is **0**.

Check: $2 + 8 = 10 = Z$ ✅

### Example 8. Why do Carbon ($Z = 6$) and Silicon ($Z = 14$) have the same valency?

- Carbon is **`2, 4`** (4 valence electrons in L). Silicon is **`2, 8, 4`** (4 valence electrons in M).
- Even though their outer shells are different (L vs. M), both atoms have **4 valence electrons**, giving both a valency of **4**.

Check: In Chapter 3, both C and Si had valency 4 ✅

### Example 9. Why do Hydrogen, Lithium, Sodium, and Potassium all have valency 1?

- $\text{H}$ (`1`), $\text{Li}$ (`2, 1`), $\text{Na}$ (`2, 8, 1`), and $\text{K}$ (`2, 8, 8, 1`) all have **1 valence electron** in their outermost shell, so all four have a valency of **1**.

## Part G: From Na to Na⁺ and Cl to Cl⁻: A Story of Electrons Only

Now we can answer the question left hanging in Topic 4: *why* does a sodium atom lose exactly 1 electron to become $\text{Na}^+$, while a chlorine atom gains 1 electron to become $\text{Cl}^-$?

**Sodium ($\text{Na}$, $Z = 11$):** Configuration **`2, 8, 1`**. Its outermost shell (M) has just **1 lone electron**. If sodium gives away that 1 electron, the M shell becomes empty and the inner **L shell (with 8 electrons)** becomes the new outermost shell—a stable **octet**, matching the electron arrangement of **neon (`2, 8`)**!

- Before: 11 protons ($+11$) and 11 electrons ($-11$) $\implies$ charge $= 0$.
- After losing $1\text{ e}^-$: 11 protons ($+11$) and **10 electrons** ($-10$) $\implies$ net charge $= \mathbf{+1}$.
- Resulting ion: **$\text{Na}^+$**, with configuration **`2, 8`**.

**Chlorine ($\text{Cl}$, $Z = 17$):** Configuration **`2, 8, 7`**. Its outermost shell (M) has **7 electrons**—just 1 short of an octet! By gaining **1 electron**, chlorine completes its M shell to **`2, 8, 8`**, matching the stable electron arrangement of **argon (`2, 8, 8`)**.

- Before: 17 protons ($+17$) and 17 electrons ($-17$) $\implies$ charge $= 0$.
- After gaining $1\text{ e}^-$: 17 protons ($+17$) and **18 electrons** ($-18$) $\implies$ net charge $= \mathbf{-1}$.
- Resulting ion: **$\text{Cl}^-$**, with configuration **`2, 8, 8`**.

```
   Na  --(loses 1 electron)-->  Na⁺
   protons  : 11                protons  : 11
   electrons: 11                electrons: 10
   shells   : 2, 8, 1           shells   : 2, 8     (neon-like octet)
   charge   : 0                 charge   : +1
   (the lone M electron leaves; the 8 electrons in L are now outermost)

   Cl  --(gains 1 electron)-->  Cl⁻
   protons  : 17                protons  : 17
   electrons: 17                electrons: 18
   shells   : 2, 8, 7           shells   : 2, 8, 8  (argon-like octet)
   charge   : 0                 charge   : −1
   (1 electron enters the M shell, completing its octet of 8)
```

> **Figure 5.4** – How $\text{Na}$ loses 1 electron to become $\text{Na}^+$ (`2, 8`, neon-like octet) and $\text{Cl}$ gains 1 electron to become $\text{Cl}^-$ (`2, 8, 8`, argon-like octet). In both cases, the number of protons in the nucleus remains completely unchanged.

Three crucial points to remember:

1. **$\text{Na}^+$ did NOT turn into neon!** It still has 11 protons in its nucleus, so it is still the element sodium—it is a *sodium ion* whose *electron arrangement* (`2, 8`) matches neon's.
2. Similarly, **$\text{Cl}^-$ is not argon** (it has 17 protons, whereas argon has 18).
3. A positive charge on an ion always comes from **losing electrons** (never from gaining protons), and a negative charge always comes from **gaining electrons**.

### Example 10. Magnesium to $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and Oxygen to $\text{O}^{2-}$

- **$\text{Mg}$ ($Z = 12$, `2, 8, 2`):** Loses 2 outer electrons to leave **`2, 8`** (10 electrons). With 12 protons and 10 electrons, the charge is $+12 - 10 = \mathbf{+2}$ ($\text{Mg}^{2+}$).
- **$\text{O}$ ($Z = 8$, `2, 6`):** Gains 2 electrons to reach **`2, 8`** (10 electrons). With 8 protons and 10 electrons, the charge is $+8 - 10 = \mathbf{-2}$ ($\text{O}^{2-}$).

Check: Both $\text{Mg}^{2+}$ and $\text{O}^{2-}$ have 10 electrons (`2, 8`, neon-like octet) ✅, and the magnitude of each ion's charge ($2$) equals its valency ($2$) ✅

### Example 11. Lithium to $\text{Li}^+$ and Calcium to $\text{Ca}^{2+}$

- **$\text{Li}$ ($Z = 3$, `2, 1`):** Loses 1 electron to leave **`2`** in the K shell—a stable **duplet** like helium! Charge $= +3 - 2 = \mathbf{+1}$ ($\text{Li}^+$).
- **$\text{Ca}$ ($Z = 20$, `2, 8, 8, 2`):** Loses 2 outer electrons to leave **`2, 8, 8`** (18 electrons, argon-like octet). Charge $= +20 - 18 = \mathbf{+2}$ ($\text{Ca}^{2+}$).

Check: Both $\text{Ca}^{2+}$ and $\text{Cl}^-$ have 18 electrons (`2, 8, 8`) ✅

### Example 12. Aluminium to $\text{Al}^{3+}$ and Fluorine to $\text{F}^-$

- **$\text{Al}$ ($Z = 13$, `2, 8, 3`):** Loses 3 electrons $\implies$ **`2, 8`** (10 electrons), charge $= +13 - 10 = \mathbf{+3}$ ($\text{Al}^{3+}$).
- **$\text{F}$ ($Z = 9$, `2, 7`):** Gains 1 electron $\implies$ **`2, 8`** (10 electrons), charge $= +9 - 10 = \mathbf{-1}$ ($\text{F}^-$).

Now Topic 4's mystery (Example 17) is completely solved: **$\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{F}^-$, and $\text{O}^{2-}$ all have 10 electrons because 10 electrons gives the stable `2, 8` neon-like octet!**

## Part H: The Repeating Pattern of Atomic Number and Valency

Look at what happens when we line up the elements from Lithium to Neon ($Z = 3\text{ to }10$) and directly below them Sodium to Argon ($Z = 11\text{ to }18$):

```
   Valency:         1   2   3   4   3   2   1   0
   Element (Row 2): Li  Be  B   C   N   O   F   Ne
   Valence e⁻:      1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8

   Valency:         1   2   3   4   3   2   1   0
   Element (Row 3): Na  Mg  Al  Si  P   S   Cl  Ar
   Valence e⁻:      1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8
```

In both rows, as the number of valence electrons climbs from **1 to 8**, the valency rises from **1 to 4** and then steps symmetrically back down to **3, 2, 1, 0**! Elements that end up in the same vertical column ($\text{Li}$ and $\text{Na}$; $\text{Be}$ and $\text{Mg}$; $\text{F}$ and $\text{Cl}$; $\text{Ne}$ and $\text{Ar}$) have the exact same number of valence electrons, the same valency, and remarkably similar chemical properties. This repeating (*periodic*) pattern is the foundation of the **Periodic Table of Elements**, which you will explore in a later chapter.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Writing calcium's configuration as `2, 8, 10` because the M shell has a capacity of 18.**
   Because the outermost shell can never hold more than 8 electrons, the M shell stops at 8 and the next 2 electrons enter the N shell: **`2, 8, 8, 2`**.

2. **Mistake 2: Equating valency directly to the number of valence electrons when there are more than 4.**
   Nitrogen (`2, 5`) has 5 valence electrons, but its valency is $8 - 5 = \mathbf{3}$, not 5! Always subtract from 8 when an atom has 5, 6, or 7 valence electrons.

3. **Mistake 3: Saying neon or argon has a valency of 8.**
   Neon (`2, 8`) and argon (`2, 8, 8`) have 8 *valence electrons*, which means their octets are already full—so their **valency is 0**.

4. **Mistake 4: Thinking protons change when an atom becomes an ion.**
   In $\text{Na} \to \text{Na}^+$, the nucleus still has 11 protons; only 1 electron is lost (from 11 down to 10).

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking $\text{Na}^+$ has turned into a neon atom.**
   $\text{Na}^+$ has the same *electronic configuration* (`2, 8`) as neon, but because its nucleus has 11 protons (not 10), it is a positively charged sodium ion, not neon gas.

6. **Mistake 6: Forgetting to add the shell numbers to check against $Z$.**
   Always verify that the sum of the numbers in your configuration equals the total electron count (e.g., for Cl: $2 + 8 + 7 = 17$).

## Quick Recap

- **Electronic configuration** is the shell-by-shell distribution of electrons across K, L, M, N. For the first 20 elements, fill K up to 2, L up to 8, M up to 8, and then N.
- **Valence electrons** are the electrons in the outermost occupied shell.
- A completely full outer shell—**2 in K (duplet)** or **8 in L, M, ... (octet)**—is chemically **stable**. The **noble gases** ($\text{He}: 2$, $\text{Ne}: 2, 8$, $\text{Ar}: 2, 8, 8$) already have full outer shells, so their valency is **0** and they are chemically unreactive.
- **Valency rule:** For 1 to 4 valence electrons, $\text{valency} = \text{valence electrons}$. For 5 to 7 valence electrons, $\text{valency} = 8 - (\text{valence electrons})$. For 8 valence electrons (or 2 in He), $\text{valency} = 0$.
- **$\text{Na} \to \text{Na}^+$** (`2, 8, 1` $\to$ `2, 8`): loses $1\text{ e}^-$, 11 protons, 10 electrons, charge $+1$. **$\text{Cl} \to \text{Cl}^-$** (`2, 8, 7` $\to$ `2, 8, 8`): gains $1\text{ e}^-$, 17 protons, 18 electrons, charge $-1$.
- As $Z$ increases, valence electrons repeat the cycle $1 \to 8$, causing valency to repeat the pattern $1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2, 1, 0$.

7. Using proton and electron counts, explain how a neutral sodium atom becomes a $\text{Na}^+$ ion. State the number of protons, electrons, and net charge before and after, and name the noble gas that has the same electron arrangement as $\text{Na}^+$.

8. Explain how a neutral chlorine atom becomes a $\text{Cl}^-$ ion, giving the proton count, electron count, charge, and electronic configuration of $\text{Cl}^-$. A student claims, "$\text{Cl}^-$ is now an argon atom." Explain what is wrong with this claim.

9. Find the total number of electrons and write the electronic configuration of each ion: (a) $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, (b) $\text{O}^{2-}$, (c) $\text{Li}^+$, (d) $\text{Ca}^{2+}$.

10. Silicon (`2, 8, 4`) and carbon (`2, 4`) have the exact same valency. Explain why, in terms of their valence electrons.

11. Fill in the blanks: $\text{Na}^+$, $\text{Mg}^{2+}$, $\text{Al}^{3+}$, $\text{F}^-$, and $\text{O}^{2-}$ all have \_\_\_ electrons and the electronic configuration \_\_\_. Are they all the same element? Explain why or why not.

12. State **True** or **False**, and correct the false statements:
    (a) Nitrogen has a valency of 5 because it has 5 valence electrons.
    (b) The valency of argon is 0.
    (c) When $\text{Na}$ becomes $\text{Na}^+$, it gains a proton.
    (d) The electronic configuration `2, 8, 8` is a stable octet arrangement.

### Answers

1. (a) Carbon ($Z = 6$): **`2, 4`** ($2 + 4 = 6$ ✅). (b) Oxygen ($Z = 8$): **`2, 6`** ($8$ ✅). (c) Magnesium ($Z = 12$): **`2, 8, 2`** ($12$ ✅). (d) Sulphur ($Z = 16$): **`2, 8, 6`** ($16$ ✅). (e) Potassium ($Z = 19$): **`2, 8, 8, 1`** ($19$ ✅).

2. The outermost shell can hold at most **8** electrons (or at most **2** if the first shell, K, is the outermost shell). In calcium ($Z = 20$), K takes 2 and L takes 8, leaving 10 electrons. Because M cannot hold 10 as the outermost shell, M stops at 8 and the remaining 2 electrons enter the N shell, giving **`2, 8, 8, 2`**.

3. **Valence electrons** are the electrons in the outermost occupied shell of an atom. $\text{Li}$ (`2, 1`): **1**; $\text{N}$ (`2, 5`): **5**; $\text{Cl}$ (`2, 8, 7`): **7**; $\text{Ar}$ (`2, 8, 8`): **8**.

4. If valence electrons $\le 4$, $\text{valency} = \text{valence electrons}$; if $> 4$, $\text{valency} = 8 - (\text{valence electrons})$ (and 0 if the shell is full). $\text{Na}$ (`2, 8, 1`): **1**; $\text{Al}$ (`2, 8, 3`): **3**; $\text{P}$ (`2, 8, 5`): $8 - 5 =$ **3**; $\text{F}$ (`2, 7`): $8 - 7 =$ **1**. All four match Chapter 3 ✅

5. Their outermost shells are already completely full ($\text{He}$ has a duplet of **2**; $\text{Ne}$ is **`2, 8`** and $\text{Ar}$ is **`2, 8, 8`**, both with an octet of 8), so they have no need to lose, gain, or share electrons. Their valency is **0**.

6. Total electrons $= 2 + 8 + 3 = 13 \implies Z = 13$, which is **aluminium ($\text{Al}$)**. With 3 valence electrons, its valency is **3**. By losing those 3 outer electrons, it forms the **$\text{Al}^{3+}$** ion (10 electrons, configuration **`2, 8`**).

7. Neutral $\text{Na}$ has 11 protons and 11 electrons (charge $0$, configuration `2, 8, 1`). Losing its 1 outer electron leaves 11 protons and **10** electrons, giving a net charge of $+11 - 10 = \mathbf{+1}$ ($\text{Na}^+$, configuration **`2, 8`**). This matches the electron arrangement of **neon**.

8. Neutral $\text{Cl}$ has 17 protons and 17 electrons (`2, 8, 7`). Gaining 1 electron gives 17 protons and **18** electrons, a net charge of $17 - 18 = \mathbf{-1}$ ($\text{Cl}^-$), and the configuration **`2, 8, 8`**. Saying "$\text{Cl}^-$ is argon" is wrong because $\text{Cl}^-$ still has **17 protons** in its nucleus (making it chlorine), whereas argon has 18 protons; only their electron arrangements are the same.

9. (a) $\text{Mg}^{2+}$: $12 - 2 =$ **10** electrons, **`2, 8`**. (b) $\text{O}^{2-}$: $8 + 2 =$ **10** electrons, **`2, 8`**. (c) $\text{Li}^+$: $3 - 1 =$ **2** electrons, **`2`** (K-shell duplet). (d) $\text{Ca}^{2+}$: $20 - 2 =$ **18** electrons, **`2, 8, 8`**.

10. Both carbon (`2, 4`) and silicon (`2, 8, 4`) have **4 valence electrons** in their outermost shell, so both require 4 electrons to complete their octet and therefore have a valency of **4**.

11. They all have **10** electrons and the electronic configuration **`2, 8`**. However, they are **not** the same element because their nuclei contain different numbers of protons (11, 12, 13, 9, and 8 respectively).

12. (a) **False** ❌ — Nitrogen has 5 valence electrons, so its valency is $8 - 5 = \mathbf{3}$. (b) **True** ✅. (c) **False** ❌ — $\text{Na}$ loses an **electron** to become $\text{Na}^+$; its proton count never changes. (d) **True** ✅ (an argon-like octet).
