# Topic 6: Isotopes, Isobars, and Their Applications + Chapter Summary

In the previous topics, we learned that an atom's identity is fixed by its **atomic number ($Z$)**—the number of protons in its nucleus—while its chemical behaviour is governed by the seating of its outer **electrons**. One fascinating question still remains: are all atoms of a given element 100% identical in every way? Recall from Chapter 4 that the atomic mass of chlorine is **$35.5\text{ u}$**, and we promised to explain how an element can have a fractional atomic mass when every individual atom has a whole number of protons and neutrons. Today we will solve that mystery, explore **isotopes** and **isobars**, and wrap up the entire chapter in a single master table.

---

## The Ancient Wooden Boat at the Museum

During the school holidays, Dr. Foster took his nephew Noah to the natural history museum. In the main hall stood a preserved wooden canoe dug up from an ancient riverbed. The museum guide explained, "Archaeologists determined the age of this wooden canoe using **carbon-14** dating."

Noah raised his hand. "Excuse me—isn't carbon just carbon? What are **carbon-12** and **carbon-14**? Are there different versions of the same element?"

Dr. Foster smiled. "Great question, Noah. Let's work it out together using what you already know. Every single carbon atom in the universe must have **6 protons** in its nucleus—otherwise it wouldn't be carbon at all. So if one carbon atom has a mass number of 12 and another has a mass number of 14, which particle inside the nucleus must be different?"

Noah thought for a second. "The **neutrons**! One has 6 neutrons and the other has 8!"

"Bullseye," Dr. Foster said. "And those 'twin' atoms of the same element are called **isotopes**."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Isotopes** | Atoms of the **same element** that have the **same atomic number ($Z$)** but **different mass numbers ($A$)** (same protons, different neutrons) | Carbon-12 ($^{12}_6\text{C}$) and Carbon-14 ($^{14}_6\text{C}$) |
| **Isobars** | Atoms of **different elements** that have **different atomic numbers ($Z$)** but the **same mass number ($A$)** | Argon-40 ($^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$) and Calcium-40 ($^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$) |
| **Mass number ($A$)** | Total number of protons plus neutrons ($Z + n$) | For carbon-12, $A = 12$ |
| **Atomic number ($Z$)** | Number of protons in the nucleus | For all carbon atoms, $Z = 6$ |
| **Average atomic mass** | The weighted average of the masses of an element's naturally occurring isotopes, based on their percentage abundance | Chlorine's average atomic mass is $35.5\text{ u}$ |
| **Radioactive isotope (radioisotope)** | An unstable isotope whose nucleus spontaneously emits radiation | Carbon-14, Iodine-131, Cobalt-60 |
| **Heavy water ($\text{D}_2\text{O}$)** | Water in which ordinary hydrogen ($^1\text{H}$) is replaced by its heavier isotope deuterium ($^2\text{H}$ or $\text{D}$) | Used as a moderator in certain nuclear reactors |

## Part A: Isotopes — Twin Atoms of the Same Element

Back in Chapter 4 ([atoms-and-molecules-part3.md](../atoms-and-molecules/atoms-and-molecules-part3.md)), Dalton's second postulate (P2) assumed that all atoms of a given element are completely identical in mass. Now we know why that postulate needed a small update: while every atom of an element has the exact same number of **protons ($Z$)**, atoms of the same element can have **different numbers of neutrons**. Changing the neutron count changes the mass number $A = Z + n$, while leaving the element's identity unchanged.

> **Isotopes** are atoms of the **same element** having the **same atomic number ($Z$)** (same number of protons) but **different mass numbers ($A$)** (different numbers of neutrons).

We name an isotope by writing the element's name followed by a hyphen and its mass number (e.g., **carbon-12**, **carbon-14**), or by using nuclear notation ($^{12}_6\text{C}$, $^{14}_6\text{C}$).

### The Three Isotopes of Hydrogen

Hydrogen is the only element whose three isotopes have special individual names:

| Isotope Name | Nuclear Symbol | Protons ($Z$) | Neutrons ($A - Z$) | Mass Number ($A$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Protium** (ordinary hydrogen) | $^1_1\text{H}$ | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| **Deuterium** (heavy hydrogen, D) | $^2_1\text{H}$ | 1 | 1 | 2 |
| **Tritium** | $^3_1\text{H}$ | 1 | 2 | 3 |

Check ($\text{neutrons} = A - Z$): $1 - 1 = 0$, $2 - 1 = 1$, $3 - 1 = 2$ ✅

```
   Protium        Deuterium       Tritium
   (A = 1)        (A = 2)         (A = 3)

     (+)           (+)(n)          (+)(n)(n)
     nucleus       nucleus         nucleus
      o             o               o
   1 electron     1 electron      1 electron

   (+) = proton,  (n) = neutron,  o = electron
```

> **Figure 6.1** – The three isotopes of hydrogen: all three have **1 proton** and **1 electron**, but they contain **0, 1, and 2 neutrons** respectively, giving mass numbers of 1, 2, and 3. Because all three have $Z = 1$, all three are hydrogen!

### Isotopes of Carbon

| Isotope | $Z$ (Protons) | $A$ (Mass Number) | Neutrons ($A - Z$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Carbon-12** ($^{12}_6\text{C}$) | 6 | 12 | $12 - 6 = 6$ |
| **Carbon-13** ($^{13}_6\text{C}$) | 6 | 13 | $13 - 6 = 7$ |
| **Carbon-14** ($^{14}_6\text{C}$) | 6 | 14 | $14 - 6 = 8$ |

All three have 6 protons (and 6 electrons in a neutral atom). In nature, almost 99% of all carbon is carbon-12, about 1% is carbon-13, and a tiny trace is carbon-14.

### The Two Isotopes of Chlorine

| Isotope | $Z$ (Protons) | $A$ (Mass Number) | Neutrons ($A - Z$) | Natural Abundance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chlorine-35** ($^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$) | 17 | 35 | $35 - 17 = 18$ | ~75% (3 out of 4 atoms) |
| **Chlorine-37** ($^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$) | 17 | 37 | $37 - 17 = 20$ | ~25% (1 out of 4 atoms) |

In nature, any sample of chlorine gas or table salt contains roughly **75% chlorine-35** and **25% chlorine-37** (a **$3 : 1$ ratio**).

## Part B: Unravelling the Mystery of Chlorine's 35.5 u

In Chapter 4, we saw that chlorine's atomic mass in the reference table is **$35.5\text{ u}$**. Yet no single chlorine atom weighs $35.5\text{ u}$! Every individual chlorine atom weighs either about **$35\text{ u}$** (if it is $^{35}\text{Cl}$) or about **$37\text{ u}$** (if it is $^{37}\text{Cl}$). The number $35.5\text{ u}$ is the **weighted average** of the two isotopes in natural chlorine.

```
   A natural sample of 100 chlorine atoms (each [] = 5 atoms)

   Cl-35 :  [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] [] []   = 75 atoms (75%)
   Cl-37 :  [] [] [] [] []                                 = 25 atoms (25%)

   In every group of 4 chlorine atoms:
        [35] [35] [35] [37]       ->  ratio 3 : 1
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Natural chlorine is a $3 : 1$ mixture of chlorine-35 (75%) and chlorine-37 (25%). The weighted average mass of this mixture is $35.5\text{ u}$.

**Method 1 (Using percentages / decimal weights):**

$$\text{Average atomic mass of Cl} = (0.75 \times 35\text{ u}) + (0.25 \times 37\text{ u})$$

- $0.75 \times 35 = 26.25\text{ u}$
- $0.25 \times 37 = 9.25\text{ u}$
- $\text{Total} = 26.25 + 9.25 = \mathbf{35.5\text{ u}}$ ✅

**Method 2 (Taking 4 representative atoms in a $3 : 1$ ratio):**

Out of every 4 chlorine atoms, 3 have mass $35\text{ u}$ and 1 has mass $37\text{ u}$:

$$\text{Average} = \frac{(3 \times 35) + (1 \times 37)}{4} = \frac{105 + 37}{4} = \frac{142}{4} = \mathbf{35.5\text{ u}}$$ ✅

Think of it like test scores in a group of 4 students: if three students score 35 and one student scores 37, the group average is $35.5$, even though no single student scored $35.5$! That completely resolves our Chapter 4 mystery.

## Part C: Why Do Isotopes Have Identical Chemical Properties?

As we learned in Topic 5, how an atom reacts chemically depends entirely on its **outer (valence) electrons**. Because all isotopes of an element have the exact same number of protons ($Z$), neutral atoms of those isotopes also have the **exact same number of electrons** and the **exact same electronic configuration**:

| Isotope | Electrons | Electronic Configuration | Valence Electrons | Valency |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chlorine-35 | 17 | 2, 8, 7 | 7 | 1 |
| Chlorine-37 | 17 | 2, 8, 7 | 7 | 1 |
| Carbon-12 | 6 | 2, 4 | 4 | 4 |
| Carbon-14 | 6 | 2, 4 | 4 | 4 |

Because their electron configurations are identical, **isotopes of an element have virtually identical chemical properties** (for instance, both $^{12}\text{C}$ and $^{14}\text{C}$ burn in oxygen to form $\text{CO}_2$). Extra neutrons sit quietly in the nucleus and do not participate in chemical bonding; they only change the atom's **mass**, so physical properties that depend on mass (such as density or rate of diffusion) differ slightly between isotopes.

## Part D: Isobars — Different Elements with the Same Mass Number

What if two atoms belong to **different elements** (different numbers of protons, $Z$), yet happen to have the **same total number of nucleons** ($A = \text{protons} + \text{neutrons}$)? Such atoms are called **isobars**.

> **Isobars** are atoms of **different elements** that have **different atomic numbers ($Z$)** but the **same mass number ($A$)**.

Compare **Argon-40** ($^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$) and **Calcium-40** ($^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$):

| Nuclide | $Z$ (Protons) | Mass Number ($A$) | Neutrons ($A - Z$) | Electronic Configuration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Argon-40** ($^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$) | 18 | 40 | $40 - 18 = 22$ | 2, 8, 8 |
| **Calcium-40** ($^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$) | 20 | 40 | $40 - 20 = 20$ | 2, 8, 8, 2 |

```
   Argon-40                    Calcium-40
   Z = 18, A = 40              Z = 20, A = 40

   protons  : 18               protons  : 20
   neutrons : 22               neutrons : 20
   ---------------             ---------------
   total A  : 40               total A  : 40   (same mass number A!)

   electrons: 18 (2, 8, 8)     electrons: 20 (2, 8, 8, 2)
```

> **Figure 6.3** – Argon-40 and calcium-40 are **isobars**: both have a mass number of $A = 40$ ($18 + 22 = 40$ and $20 + 20 = 40$), but because they have different numbers of protons ($18$ vs. $20$), they are completely different elements. Argon (`2, 8, 8`) is an unreactive noble gas, whereas calcium (`2, 8, 8, 2`) is a reactive metal!

Because isobars belong to different elements and have different electron configurations, their **chemical properties are completely different**.

## Part E: Applications of Radioactive Isotopes

Most common isotopes (such as carbon-12, oxygen-16, and chlorine-35) have completely stable nuclei. However, certain isotopes have unstable nuclei that spontaneously emit radiation; these are called **radioactive isotopes** (or **radioisotopes**). In Class 9, you only need to know a few important real-world applications of isotopes:

| Isotope | Important Application |
|---|---|
| **Carbon-14** ($^{14}\text{C}$) | **Radiocarbon dating:** determining the age of ancient wooden artifacts, bones, and fossils (like the canoe in the museum) |
| **Iodine-131** ($^{131}\text{I}$) | **Medical diagnosis and treatment** of thyroid gland disorders (such as goitre) |
| **Cobalt-60** ($^{60}\text{Co}$) | **Cancer radiotherapy** (destroying malignant tumour cells) and sterilizing surgical instruments |
| **Uranium-235** ($^{235}\text{U}$) | **Nuclear fuel** in nuclear power plants to generate electricity |
| **Deuterium** ($^2\text{H}$) | Used (in the form of **heavy water**, $\text{D}_2\text{O}$) in certain nuclear reactors |

**How carbon-14 dating works (the basic idea, without math):** All living plants and animals continuously take in carbon (including a tiny, steady fraction of carbon-14) from the environment. Once an organism dies, it stops taking in new carbon, and the carbon-14 already inside its tissues slowly decays at a known rate. By measuring how much carbon-14 remains in an old piece of wood or bone, scientists can estimate how long ago the organism died.

> **Safety note:** Radioactive materials are handled strictly by trained specialists in licensed facilities with heavy radiation shielding. They are never handled in school laboratories or at home.

## Part F: Comparing Isotopes and Isobars

| Feature | Isotopes | Isobars |
|---|---|---|
| Element | **Same** element | **Different** elements |
| Atomic number ($Z$, protons) | **Same** | **Different** |
| Mass number ($A$, protons $+$ neutrons) | **Different** | **Same** |
| Number of neutrons ($A - Z$) | **Different** | **Different** |
| Chemical behaviour | **Virtually identical** (same electron configuration) | **Completely different** (different electron configurations) |
| Examples | $^1_1\text{H}, ^2_1\text{H}, ^3_1\text{H}$; $^{35}_{17}\text{Cl}$ and $^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$ | $^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ and $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$; $^{24}_{11}\text{Na}$ and $^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}$ |

A quick memory aid:
- **Iso-TOP-es** = **same place** (*topos* in Greek) in the periodic table $\implies$ **same element, same $Z$**.
- **Iso-BAR-s** = **same weight** (*baros* in Greek) $\implies$ **same mass number $A$**.

### Solved Examples

**Example 1.** An atom has 8 protons and 9 neutrons. What are its mass number $A$, its isotope name, and its nuclear symbol?
- $Z = 8 \implies$ oxygen. $A = 8 + 9 = 17$. It is **oxygen-17** ($^{17}_8\text{O}$).

**Example 2.** Neon has $Z = 10$. How many neutrons are in neon-20 and neon-22? How are these two atoms related?
- Neon-20: $20 - 10 = \mathbf{10\text{ neutrons}}$. Neon-22: $22 - 10 = \mathbf{12\text{ neutrons}}$. Because they have the same $Z$ ($10$) and different $A$ ($20$ and $22$), they are **isotopes**.

**Example 3.** Are $^{24}_{11}\text{Na}$ and $^{24}_{12}\text{Mg}$ isotopes or isobars? Find the number of neutrons in each.
- Their atomic numbers are different ($11$ vs. $12$) while their mass numbers are the same ($A = 24$), so they are **isobars**. Neutrons: $^{24}\text{Na}$ has $24 - 11 = \mathbf{13}$; $^{24}\text{Mg}$ has $24 - 12 = \mathbf{12}$.

**Example 4.** Natural boron consists of two isotopes: boron-10 ($10\text{ u}$, abundance 20%) and boron-11 ($11\text{ u}$, abundance 80%). Calculate the average atomic mass of boron.
- $\text{Average atomic mass} = (0.20 \times 10\text{ u}) + (0.80 \times 11\text{ u}) = 2.0 + 8.8 = \mathbf{10.8\text{ u}}$ ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking isotopes have different atomic numbers ($Z$).**
   Isotopes belong to the **same element**, so they always have the **exact same $Z$ (proton count)**; only their neutron counts (and therefore their mass numbers $A$) differ.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking a single chlorine atom has a mass of $35.5\text{ u}$.**
   No single chlorine atom weighs $35.5\text{ u}$—each atom weighs about $35\text{ u}$ or $37\text{ u}$, and $35.5\text{ u}$ is the weighted average of the natural $3 : 1$ mixture.

3. **Mistake 3: Mixing up isotopes and isobars.**
   **Isotopes** = same $Z$, different $A$. **Isobars** = different $Z$, same $A$.

4. **Mistake 4: Thinking isotopes of an element have different chemical properties.**
   Because isotopes have the same number of electrons and identical outer-shell configurations, their chemical reactions are virtually identical.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming all isotopes are radioactive.**
   Every element has isotopes! Most naturally abundant isotopes (like carbon-12, hydrogen-1, oxygen-16, and chlorine-35) are completely stable and non-radioactive; only specific unstable isotopes (like carbon-14 or cobalt-60) are radioactive.

6. **Mistake 6: Taking a simple 50-50 average $(35 + 37) \div 2 = 36$ for chlorine.**
   You must weight each isotope by its natural abundance ($75\%$ and $25\%$), which gives $35.5\text{ u}$, not $36\text{ u}$.

## Quick Recap

- **Isotopes:** Atoms of the same element with the **same $Z$** (protons) and **different $A$** (neutrons). Examples: protium ($^1_1\text{H}$, 0 n), deuterium ($^2_1\text{H}$, 1 n), tritium ($^3_1\text{H}$, 2 n); carbon-12, -13, -14 (6, 7, 8 n).
- **Average atomic mass of chlorine:** $75\%$ $^{35}\text{Cl}$ (18 n) and $25\%$ $^{37}\text{Cl}$ (20 n) $\implies (0.75 \times 35) + (0.25 \times 37) = \mathbf{35.5\text{ u}}$.
- Isotopes have the same electron configuration, so their **chemical behaviour is identical**.
- **Isobars:** Atoms of different elements with the **same $A$** and **different $Z$**, such as $^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ (18 p, 22 n) and $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$ (20 p, 20 n).
- **Applications of isotopes:** Carbon-14 (archaeological dating), Iodine-131 (thyroid treatment), Cobalt-60 (cancer radiotherapy and sterilization), Uranium-235 (nuclear reactor fuel), Deuterium (heavy water).

1. Define **isotopes** and **isobars**, giving one pair of examples for each.

2. Find the number of protons, neutrons, and electrons in a neutral atom of: (a) tritium ($^3_1\text{H}$), (b) carbon-14 ($^{14}_6\text{C}$), (c) chlorine-37 ($^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$).

6. Name the three isotopes of hydrogen and state the number of protons and neutrons in each.

7. An element occurs in nature as two isotopes of masses $10\text{ u}$ and $11\text{ u}$ in the ratio $1 : 4$ (20% and 80%). Calculate the average atomic mass of the element.

8. State one important application of each of the following isotopes: (a) carbon-14, (b) iodine-131, (c) cobalt-60, (d) uranium-235.

9. A tiny sample of 8 chlorine atoms contains chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 in the natural $3 : 1$ ratio. How many atoms of each isotope are present? Calculate the average mass of these 8 atoms.

10. State **True** or **False**, and correct the false statements:
    (a) Isotopes of an element have the same number of protons.
    (b) Isobars always belong to the same element.
    (c) Chlorine-35 and chlorine-37 have the exact same electronic configuration.
    (d) Every isotope in nature is radioactive.

### Answers

1. **Isotopes** are atoms of the same element with the same atomic number ($Z$) but different mass numbers ($A$), such as $^{12}_6\text{C}$ and $^{14}_6\text{C}$. **Isobars** are atoms of different elements with the same mass number ($A$) but different atomic numbers ($Z$), such as $^{40}_{18}\text{Ar}$ and $^{40}_{20}\text{Ca}$.

2. (a) Tritium ($^3_1\text{H}$): **1** proton, $3 - 1 =$ **2** neutrons, **1** electron. (b) Carbon-14 ($^{14}_6\text{C}$): **6** protons, $14 - 6 =$ **8** neutrons, **6** electrons. (c) Chlorine-37 ($^{37}_{17}\text{Cl}$): **17** protons, $37 - 17 =$ **20** neutrons, **17** electrons.

3. $(0.75 \times 35\text{ u}) + (0.25 \times 37\text{ u}) = 26.25 + 9.25 = \mathbf{35.5\text{ u}}$ ✅

4. Chemical behaviour is determined by an atom's outer (valence) electrons. Because isotopes of an element have the same number of protons, neutral atoms have the same number of electrons and identical electronic configurations; extra neutrons only affect the mass.

5. Argon-40: $40 - 18 = \mathbf{22\text{ neutrons}}$, configuration **`2, 8, 8`**. Calcium-40: $40 - 20 = \mathbf{20\text{ neutrons}}$, configuration **`2, 8, 8, 2`**.

6. **Protium** ($^1_1\text{H}$: 1 proton, **0 neutrons**), **deuterium** ($^2_1\text{H}$: 1 proton, **1 neutron**), and **tritium** ($^3_1\text{H}$: 1 proton, **2 neutrons**).

7. $(0.20 \times 10\text{ u}) + (0.80 \times 11\text{ u}) = 2.0 + 8.8 = \mathbf{10.8\text{ u}}$.

8. (a) **Carbon-14:** dating ancient wood, bones, and fossils. (b) **Iodine-131:** diagnosing and treating thyroid disorders (such as goitre). (c) **Cobalt-60:** cancer radiotherapy and sterilizing medical instruments. (d) **Uranium-235:** fuel in nuclear power reactors.

9. A $3 : 1$ ratio out of 8 atoms gives **6 atoms of chlorine-35** and **2 atoms of chlorine-37**. Total mass $= (6 \times 35) + (2 \times 37) = 210 + 74 = 284\text{ u}$. Average mass $= 284 \div 8 = \mathbf{35.5\text{ u}}$ ✅

10. (a) **True** ✅. (b) **False** ❌ — Isobars have different atomic numbers ($Z$) and therefore belong to **different** elements. (c) **True** ✅ — Both have 17 electrons arranged as `2, 8, 7`. (d) **False** ❌ — Most common natural isotopes (like $^{12}\text{C}$ and $^{35}\text{Cl}$) are completely stable and non-radioactive.

## Chapter Summary: Structure of the Atom

Here is our entire six-topic journey through the inside of the atom in one table:

| Topic | Core Concept | Key Takeaway in One Sentence |
|---|---|---|
| **Topic 1** | Electrons, protons, and neutrons | Every atom is built from three subatomic particles—electrons ($-1$, negligible mass), protons ($+1$, $\sim 1\text{ u}$), and neutrons ($0$, $\sim 1\text{ u}$)—updating Dalton's idea of an indivisible atom. |
| **Topic 2** | Models from Thomson to Rutherford | Thomson's plum-pudding sphere was overturned by Rutherford's gold-foil experiment, which proved that an atom is mostly empty space with a tiny, dense, positively charged central **nucleus**. |
| **Topic 3** | Bohr's model of electron shells | Electrons occupy fixed energy shells (**K, L, M, N**) without losing energy; each shell holds at most $2n^2$ electrons, and the outermost shell holds at most 8. |
| **Topic 4** | Atomic number ($Z$) and mass number ($A$) | $Z =$ protons (the element's unique identity), $A = \text{protons} + \text{neutrons}$, $\text{neutrons} = A - Z$, and forming an ion changes only the electron count. |
| **Topic 5** | Electronic configuration and valency | Filling the shells of the first 20 elements reveals their valence electrons, explains why noble gases with full duplets/octets are stable, and predicts every element's valency. |
| **Topic 6** | Isotopes, isobars, and applications | **Isotopes** have the same $Z$ but different $A$ (explaining average atomic masses like chlorine's $35.5\text{ u}$); **isobars** have the same $A$ but different $Z$. |
