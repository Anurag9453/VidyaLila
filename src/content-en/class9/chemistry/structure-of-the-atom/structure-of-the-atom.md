# Topic 1: What Is Inside the Atom: Electrons, Protons, and Neutrons

In the previous chapter, John Dalton proposed that the atom is the smallest unit of matter and cannot be divided any further. However, we also noted in our comparison table that this statement is **incomplete**, and promised that the full answer would come in this chapter. That time has arrived. In this topic, we will discover how scientists proved that an atom actually contains **three** types of smaller particles inside it: **electrons, protons, and neutrons**. We will also see why an entire atom appears electrically "neutral" from the outside.

---

## The Clinging Plastic Wrap

Dr. Reynolds was standing at the kitchen counter while his niece Emma helped him pack sandwiches for a picnic. As soon as Emma pulled a sheet of plastic cling wrap off the roll, the thin film clung straight to her hand. When she shook her hand to free it, the wrap swung over and stuck firmly to the side of a stainless-steel mixing bowl.

"Uncle, it's sticking without any glue!" Emma said in surprise.

"It is," Dr. Reynolds smiled. "And notice how the more briskly you pull it off the roll, the more strongly it clings."

Emma pulled off another sheet and held it near her forearm. The fine hairs on her arm stood up slightly toward the plastic.

"That's the exact phenomenon you studied in Physics," Dr. Reynolds explained. "When two materials rub against or peel away from each other, they acquire an **electric charge**. The cling wrap picked up a charge, which is why it attracts both the bowl and your hand." (You can review the full story of electric charge and static electricity in [electricity.md](../../physics/electricity/electricity.md).)

Emma paused to think. "Where did that charge come from, though? Cling wrap is just plain plastic."

Dr. Reynolds nodded approvingly. "That is the right question. The answer is that **inside** the plastic, inside the steel bowl, and inside your own hand, **charged particles** are already present in huge numbers. Today we'll trace the detective story of how scientists discovered them."

> **Try this safely at home:** Rub a dry plastic comb through dry hair about 20 times, then bring it close to tiny torn bits of paper. The paper pieces will leap up and stick to the comb. (On a humid day, the effect will be weaker.) Use only a plastic comb and paper—never try any static experiments near electrical outlets or appliances.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Subatomic particle** | A particle smaller than an atom that lives inside the atom | Electron, proton, neutron |
| **Electron (e⁻)** | An extremely light particle carrying a negative charge | Transferred when cling wrap or a comb is rubbed |
| **Proton (p⁺)** | A heavier particle carrying a positive charge | 1 proton inside a hydrogen atom |
| **Neutron (n)** | A particle with no electric charge (neutral) | 6 neutrons inside a common carbon atom |
| **Cathode rays** | Streams of particles travelling from the negative plate in an evacuated tube | Observed in J. J. Thomson's experiment |
| **Canal rays** | Positively charged rays travelling in the opposite direction in a discharge tube | Observed in E. Goldstein's experiment |
| **Neutral** | Having a total electric charge of zero | Any ordinary, uncharged atom |
| **Relative mass** | The mass of a particle compared to a chosen standard (such as a proton) | The relative mass of a neutron is approximately 1 |

## Part A: The First Clue: Electricity Through a Gas, and a Negative Particle

Everyday observations like clinging plastic wrap show that electric charge must come from some particle inside matter. To track down that particle, late-nineteenth-century physicists used a special piece of apparatus: a **sealed glass tube** from which almost all the air had been pumped out. Two metal plates (**electrodes**) were sealed into the ends of the tube and connected to a **very high voltage** source. This device is called a **cathode ray discharge tube**.

**Safety note:** A cathode ray discharge tube uses dangerously high voltages and a vacuum-sealed glass vessel. It is strictly a specialized laboratory instrument operated by trained professionals with proper shielding. Never attempt to build or operate high-voltage equipment at home or without supervision. Here we examine the historical experiment and its results.

When high voltage was applied across the electrodes in the nearly empty tube, a beam of rays shot out from the **negative plate (cathode)** and travelled toward the positive plate (anode). Scientists named them **cathode rays**. Most remarkably, **no matter which gas was left in the tube, and no matter which metal the electrodes were made of, the cathode rays produced were always identical.**

```
   Cathode (-)                                        Anode (+)
   +-----+                                            +-----+
   :     :  e- e- e- ---- cathode rays ---->          :     :
   +-----+                                            +-----+
      :     glass tube (at very low gas pressure)        :
      :                                                  :
      +----------(-)  High voltage source  (+)-----------+
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Simplified sketch of a cathode ray tube (not to scale): a glass tube with nearly all air removed, a negative plate (cathode) at one end, and a positive plate (anode) at the other. Rays emerge from the cathode and travel toward the anode. These rays are a stream of **negatively charged particles**.

In **1897, J. J. Thomson** performed careful experiments on these rays. He found that when an electrically charged plate was brought near the path of the beam, the rays **bent toward a positively charged plate** and **curved away from a negatively charged plate**. As you learned in Physics, **opposite charges attract and like charges repel**. Because the rays were attracted toward the positive plate, Thomson concluded that the rays carried a **negative charge**.

Thomson went even further: since identical negatively charged particles came out regardless of the gas or electrode metal used, these particles could not belong to just one specific element—they had to be a **universal building block inside every atom**. They were also extraordinarily light, weighing less than one-thousandth as much as the lightest known atom (hydrogen). These particles were named **electrons**.

**Properties of the electron:**

- Charge: **negative**, taken as **−1 unit** of fundamental charge
- Mass: approximately **1/1840** of the mass of a proton (more precisely, about 1/1837)
- Symbol: **e⁻**

Keep one point in mind: Thomson did not yet know the complete blueprint of the atom. He proved that negatively charged electrons exist inside atoms; how they are arranged comes in Topic 2.

## Part B: The Second Clue: Rays Moving in the Opposite Direction and the Proton

Finding negative particles inside the atom immediately raised a second question. An ordinary atom is **electrically neutral** overall. If an atom contains negatively charged electrons, there must also be **positive charge** inside the atom to balance them. Where is that positive charge?

Part of the answer had actually been spotted earlier by **E. Goldstein (1886)**. He used a similar gas discharge tube, except that his cathode had **small perforations (holes or "canals")** drilled through it. When high voltage was applied, while cathode rays streamed toward the anode as usual, another set of glowing rays travelled **in the opposite direction, passing backward through the holes in the cathode**. Because they passed through these channels or canals, they were called **canal rays** (or anode rays).

```
   Anode (+)                                          Perforated Cathode (-)
   +-----+                                            +-----+
   :     :  <==== cathode rays (e-) =====             : o   :
   :     :                                            : o   :  <- holes (canals)
   :     :  ===== canal rays (+) ==========>          : o   :  ===> canal rays (+)
   +-----+                                            +-----+       emerging behind
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Simplified sketch of canal rays. While cathode rays travel from the negative plate toward the anode, **positively charged particles** travel in the opposite direction and pass through the holes in the perforated cathode. These were discovered by Goldstein.

Scientists noticed three key facts about canal rays:

1. They carried a **positive charge** (electric and magnetic fields deflected them in the direction opposite to cathode rays).
2. Their particles were **much heavier** than electrons.
3. Their mass **depended on the gas inside the tube**: different gases produced positive particles of different masses. The lightest positive particles of all were obtained when the tube contained **hydrogen** gas.

That lightest positive particle—obtained from hydrogen—was identified as the fundamental positive building block of matter and was later named the **proton (p⁺)** by **Ernest Rutherford**. Today we know that an **ordinary hydrogen atom** consists of **one proton and one electron, with no neutrons at all**.

**Properties of the proton:**

- Charge: **positive**, **+1 unit** (equal in magnitude to the electron's charge, but opposite in sign)
- Mass: approximately **1 u** (the unified atomic mass unit from Chapter 4)
- Symbol: **p⁺**

Notice the contrast between the two particles: an electron has a charge of −1 and a proton has a charge of +1, meaning **their charges are equal in size and opposite in sign**. Yet their masses are dramatically different—a single proton is about **1840 times heavier** than an electron.

## Part C: The Third Particle: The Uncharged Neutron

Now consider a helium atom. It contains 2 protons and 2 electrons. The electric charges balance nicely (+2 and −2). However, the measured atomic mass of helium (in the atomic mass units `u` from Chapter 4) is about **4 u**. If helium had only 2 protons inside it (and 2 electrons whose mass is practically zero), its mass should be only about 2 u. **Where does the remaining 2 u of mass come from?**

Scientists reasoned that there must be another particle inside the atom that **contributes mass but carries no electric charge**—because if it had a charge, the electrical neutrality of the atom would be upset.

In **1932, James Chadwick** solved the puzzle. By bombarding a thin sheet of beryllium with alpha particles, he discovered a new subatomic particle whose mass was almost identical to that of a proton, but which had **zero electric charge**. Because it was electrically neutral, he named it the **neutron (n)**. This immediately explained helium's mass of 4 u: 2 protons (2 u) + 2 neutrons (2 u) = 4 u (with the 2 electrons adding negligible mass).

**Properties of the neutron:**

- Charge: **zero (electrically neutral)**
- Mass: approximately **1 u** (nearly equal to a proton)
- Symbol: **n**

Why was the neutron discovered in **1932**, decades after the electron and proton? Because it carries no charge! Electric and magnetic fields easily bend the paths of electrons and protons, making them easy to detect, whereas uncharged neutrons pass straight through electric and magnetic fields unaffected.

## Part D: Summary Table of All Three Particles

| Particle | Symbol | Relative Charge | Relative Mass (compared to proton) | Approximate Mass (in u) | Location in the Atom |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Electron** | e⁻ | −1 | ~1/1840 | ~0 (negligible) | Outside the central nucleus |
| **Proton** | p⁺ | +1 | 1 | ~1 | Inside the central nucleus |
| **Neutron** | n | 0 | ~1 | ~1 | Inside the central nucleus |

Notice the word **"nucleus"** in the last column. What the nucleus is, and how scientists proved that protons and neutrons sit at the centre while electrons move outside, is the story of Rutherford's experiment in **Topic 2**. For now, remember the key takeaway:

**Essential takeaway:** Almost the entire mass of an atom comes from its **protons and neutrons**. The mass of the electrons is so tiny that it can be ignored when calculating an atom's mass.

## Part E: Why Is an Atom Electrically Neutral?

Now let us return to Emma's question in the kitchen. Cling wrap sticks to things after being pulled off the roll, yet most everyday objects around us (the steel bowl, a wooden chair, the air we breathe) do not stick to each other on their own. Why?

Because **in any normal (neutral) atom, the number of protons is exactly equal to the number of electrons.** Every +1 charge from a proton is cancelled out by a −1 charge from an electron, giving a net charge of zero.

**Example 1 (Hydrogen).** A hydrogen atom has 1 proton (+1) and 1 electron (−1). Total charge = +1 − 1 = **0** ✅

**Example 2 (Helium).** A helium atom has 2 protons (+2) and 2 electrons (−2). Total charge = +2 − 2 = **0** ✅

**Example 3 (Carbon).** A carbon atom has 6 protons (+6) and 6 electrons (−6). Total charge = +6 − 6 = **0** ✅ (A common carbon atom also has 6 neutrons, each with charge 0, which adds no charge.)

```
   A neutral atom (e.g., helium): conceptual sketch, not to scale

            (e-)
        .-----------.
       /   [p+][n]   \        2 protons (+)  = +2
      (    [n][p+]    )       2 neutrons (0) =  0
       \             /        2 electrons (-)= -2
        '-----------'         Total charge   =  0
            (e-)
```

> **Figure 1.3** – A simplified picture of a neutral helium atom. The heavy particles (2 protons and 2 neutrons) sit together in the centre while the light electrons move outside. Because the positive and negative charges are equal in number, the atom is electrically neutral overall. (This sketch only illustrates particle counts; how their exact positions were discovered is covered in Topic 2.)

**So how does cling wrap get charged?** When two materials are rubbed or peeled apart, some **electrons** are transferred from the surface of one material to the other (electrons are light and sit in the outer regions of the atom, whereas protons are locked deep inside). The material that loses electrons now has more protons than electrons, becoming positively charged. The material that gains extra electrons becomes negatively charged. This is the exact same **gain or loss of electrons** we met when studying ions in Chapter 3.

**An important rule:** Rubbing or chemical reactions only ever transfer **electrons**—never protons or neutrons, which are held tightly at the heart of the atom.

## Part F: Are the Particles Different in Different Atoms?

No—and this is one of the most elegant facts in all of science: **every atom in the universe is built from the exact same three kinds of subatomic particles.** An electron in a hydrogen atom is completely identical to an electron in a gold atom or a carbon atom. A proton is a proton, no matter which element it is in.

So what makes gold different from carbon or oxygen? **The number of particles.** Just as you can build completely different models using the same standard Lego bricks in different counts, nature builds every different element simply by combining different numbers of protons, neutrons, and electrons.

| Element | Protons | Electrons (in a neutral atom) |
|---|---|---|
| Hydrogen | 1 | 1 |
| Helium | 2 | 2 |
| Carbon | 6 | 6 |
| Oxygen | 8 | 8 |

This proton count is what gives each element its unique identity. In Topic 4, we will call this number the **atomic number**.

## Part G: Revisiting Chapter 4's Unfinished Question: "Is the Atom Indivisible?"

In Chapter 4, Dalton's first postulate (P1) stated that "atoms are indivisible and cannot be broken down into smaller particles." We now see why that postulate had to be updated: atoms are **divisible** into subatomic particles—**electrons, protons, and neutrons**.

However, Dalton was not far off for everyday chemistry. **In ordinary chemical reactions, the core of the atom never breaks apart.** Protons and neutrons stay untouched; atoms merely transfer or share a few outer **electrons** (as we will see in detail in the next chapter). That is why Dalton's idea that atoms keep their identity during chemical reactions still works beautifully in chemistry!

Dalton's second postulate (P2)—that "all atoms of a given element are identical in mass"—also needed a small correction. Two atoms of the same element always have the same number of protons, but they can have slightly different numbers of **neutrons**, giving them slightly different masses (**isotopes**, Topic 6).

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking a neutron has a negative charge.**
   The word *neutron* comes from *neutral*. A neutron has **zero** charge. Only the electron carries a negative charge.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking a proton and an electron have the same mass.**
   Their electric charges are equal in size (+1 and −1), but their masses are vastly different. A proton is about **1840 times heavier** than an electron.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking "neutral atom" means an atom has no charged particles inside it.**
   A neutral atom is packed with charged protons and electrons—it is neutral only because the **number of positive protons equals the number of negative electrons**, so their charges cancel out to zero.

4. **Mistake 4: Thinking different elements have different kinds of electrons.**
   All electrons are identical everywhere. Elements differ in the **number** of protons (and electrons), not in the type of electron.

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking a cathode ray tube experiment can be tried at home.**
   Never! It requires vacuum glass tubes and dangerously high voltages, and belongs strictly in a professional physics laboratory. Stick to safe static electricity activities like rubbing a plastic comb to pick up paper bits.

## Quick Recap

- Everyday static charge (cling wrap, a rubbed comb) shows that **charged particles** exist inside ordinary matter.
- **J. J. Thomson (1897)** studied cathode rays and discovered the **electron**: charge −1, mass ~1/1840 of a proton, present in atoms of all elements.
- **E. Goldstein (1886)** observed canal rays moving in the opposite direction; the lightest positive particle (from hydrogen) was named the **proton** by Rutherford: charge +1, mass ~1 u. An ordinary hydrogen atom has 1 proton and 0 neutrons.
- **James Chadwick (1932)** discovered the **neutron**: charge 0, mass ~1 u.
- Almost all the mass of an atom comes from its protons and neutrons; electrons have negligible mass.
- In a neutral atom, **number of protons = number of electrons**, so total charge = 0.
- All atoms are made of the same three subatomic particles; different elements simply have different **numbers** of them.
- Dalton's P1 (indivisible atoms) and P2 (identical mass for all atoms of an element) were modified after subatomic particles and neutrons were discovered.

### Answers

1. Pulling the cling wrap off the roll causes friction/separation, transferring **electrons** so the wrap acquires an electric charge and attracts the steel bowl. Only **electrons** move during static charging because they are light and lie in the outer regions of the atom.

2. When a very high voltage is applied across metal electrodes in a glass tube at very low gas pressure, a beam of rays travels from the **cathode (negative plate)** toward the **anode (positive plate)**. Thomson observed that these rays **bent toward a positive plate** and away from a negative plate. Since opposite charges attract, this proved the rays carried a **negative charge**.

3. Changing the gas inside the tube or using different metals for the electrodes always produced the **exact same** negatively charged particles. This proved that electrons are a universal component of **all atoms** of every element.

4. Canal rays are streams of **positively charged particles** that pass backward through holes in a perforated cathode in a discharge tube. Their mass depends on the gas in the tube. Because a **hydrogen** atom is the lightest of all atoms, its positive core—a single **proton**—is the lightest positive particle.

5. (a) **Electron:** J. J. Thomson, 1897, charge **−1**. (b) **Proton:** observed via canal rays by E. Goldstein (1886) and identified/named by Rutherford, charge **+1**. (c) **Neutron:** James Chadwick, 1932, charge **0**.

6. In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons = **9 electrons**. Net charge = (+9) + (−9) = **0** ✅

7. Estimated mass = (2 × 1 u) + (2 × 1 u) + (2 × 0 u) = **about 4 u**. Check: This matches helium's measured atomic mass of ~4 u ✅

8. Electron (e⁻): charge −1, relative mass ~1/1840, outside the nucleus. Proton (p⁺): charge +1, relative mass 1, inside the nucleus. Neutron (n): charge 0, relative mass ~1, inside the nucleus. It takes about **1840 electrons** to equal the mass of one proton.

9. Being electrically neutral does not mean there are no charged particles inside the atom. It means the atom contains **equal numbers of positively charged protons and negatively charged electrons**, whose charges cancel out to zero.

10. Dalton's statement is incomplete because atoms contain smaller subatomic particles (electrons, protons, and neutrons). However, during **ordinary chemical reactions**, the atom's core never splits—atoms only exchange or share outer electrons—so Dalton's idea that atoms remain intact in chemical reactions still holds true.
