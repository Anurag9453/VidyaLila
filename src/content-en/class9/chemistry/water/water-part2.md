# Topic 2: Water as a Solvent: What Dissolves and How Much

In Topic 1, we saw that water is H₂O: two hydrogen atoms and one oxygen atom joined by covalent bonds in a 1 : 8 mass ratio. We also saw how abundant water is across our planet. Now let's ask a new question: why do so many substances mix effortlessly into water? Table salt, sugar, oral rehydration salts, tea extracts, and liquid medicines all dissolve in water. In Chapter 2, we introduced solutions, solutes, solvents, and solubility, promising that we would tell the **full story of how water acts as a solvent** later on. Today is that day.

---

## Two Ingredients in a Bowl of Soup

It was Sunday afternoon. Ryan's uncle, Mr. Bennett, set down a steaming bowl of vegetable broth drizzled with golden drops of olive oil floating in little circles on the surface. Ryan stirred the soup with his spoon and paused. "Uncle, something weird is happening. The salt I stirred into the broth completely vanished. But look at these droplets of oil—no matter how hard I stir, they float right back to the top and stay separate!"

Mr. Bennett smiled. "Sharp eyes. The salt **dissolved** in the water, while the oil **refuses to dissolve**. Both met the same water in the bowl, yet behaved in completely opposite ways. And here's another twist: if you kept adding spoonful after spoonful of salt, eventually even the salt would stop dissolving."

Ryan asked, "Wait, so can water dissolve almost anything? People call it the 'universal solvent', right?" "It's a famous nickname," Mr. Bennett replied, "but it's a bit of an exaggeration. Today let's zoom in to the particle level to see what water actually does when it dissolves something, what it can and cannot dissolve, and—using a **thermometer and a graph**—how temperature changes the limit."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Soluble** | Able to dissolve in a given solvent | Table salt in water |
| **Insoluble** | Unable (or almost unable) to dissolve in a given solvent | Cooking oil or wax in water |
| **Ion** | An electrically charged particle (Chapter 6) | Na⁺, Cl⁻, Ca²⁺ |
| **Lattice** | The regular 3-D grid of ions in an ionic crystal | A crystal of NaCl |
| **Solubility** | The maximum mass of a solute (in g) that can dissolve in 100 g of water at a specific temperature | KNO₃: 64 g at 40 °C |
| **Solubility curve** | A graph plotting a substance's solubility against temperature | The steeply rising curve of KNO₃ |
| **Saturated / unsaturated** | A solution that cannot / can dissolve any more solute at that temperature (Chapter 2) | A brine solution at 20 °C |
| **Crystallisation** | Cooling a hot saturated solution to recover pure crystals of solute (Chapter 2) | Growing KNO₃ crystals |
| **Dissolved oxygen** | Oxygen gas dissolved in water, which fish and aquatic life breathe through gills | Oxygen in lake or pond water |

## Part A: How Does Dissolving Work Inside Water?

Recall Chapter 6: in an **ionic compound** (such as NaCl), positive Na⁺ ions and negative Cl⁻ ions are locked together in a regular 3-D **lattice** by strong electrostatic attraction. Because this attraction is strong, a grain of salt is a hard solid that doesn't fall apart on its own.

So how does water break it apart so effortlessly? The secret lies in the water molecules themselves: water molecules attract charged ions strongly. (In Chapter 6, we noted briefly that the ends of a water molecule carry slight partial charges; you will study polarity in detail in higher classes.)

When a salt crystal is dropped into water, water molecules cluster around the ions sitting on the **surface** of the crystal lattice and pull on them. One by one, each ion is tugged **free** from the lattice and drifts into the liquid, surrounded by a protective shell of water molecules.

```
   Before: crystal meets water         After: ions dispersed in water

     + - + - + -                       w    w            w
     - + - + - +                         (+)   w   (-)
     + - + - + -                    w          (-)    w
     - + - + - +                         (-)      (+)
                                      w     w   (+)   w
     + = Na+   - = Cl-
     w = water molecule                each ion is surrounded by water molecules
```

> **Figure 2.1** – An ionic crystal dissolving in water: on the left, + and − ions sit in a rigid crystal lattice. Water molecules surround the surface ions and pull them away one by one, dispersing the separated ions evenly throughout the water. (Particle sizes and spacings are greatly exaggerated for clarity.)

**One crucial point:** once dissolved, the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions **have not vanished**. They are still right there in the water as separated ions. That is why evaporating the water gives you all your salt back. And because those ions are free to move and carry electric charge, **salt water conducts electricity**, just as we saw with the conductivity tester in Chapter 6.

Counting ions from a formula: one formula unit of NaCl releases one Na⁺ and one Cl⁻, giving 1 + 1 = 2 ions. One formula unit of CaCl₂ releases one Ca²⁺ and two Cl⁻ ions, giving 1 + 2 = 3 ions. Therefore, dissolving 5 formula units of CaCl₂ releases 5 Ca²⁺ and 10 Cl⁻, or 15 ions in total.

## Part B: What Dissolves and What Doesn't?

Not every ionic compound dissolves well in water (for instance, chalk or marble, CaCO₃, is practically insoluble), though most common laboratory salts do. And water doesn't only dissolve ionic substances: certain covalent substances dissolve readily too—such as table sugar and rubbing alcohol—because water molecules can attract and surround their molecules as well. Here is a quick overview:

| Substance | In Water? | Type of Substance |
|---|---|---|
| Table salt (NaCl) | Dissolves readily | Ionic |
| Table sugar (sucrose) | Dissolves in large amounts | Covalent |
| Ethanol (alcohol) | Mixes completely in all proportions | Covalent |
| Washing soda, copper sulphate | Dissolve readily | Ionic |
| Chalk / limestone (CaCO₃) | Practically insoluble | Ionic |
| Sand, clay | Do not dissolve; settle at the bottom | Covalent network / mineral |
| Cooking oil, butter, candle wax | Do not dissolve | Covalent |
| Oxygen, carbon dioxide (gases) | Dissolve in small amounts | Covalent |

Notice the pattern: **substances whose particles interact favourably with water molecules dissolve**, while others do not. That is why calling water the "universal solvent" is a slight exaggeration: water dissolves **a vast range of substances**, **not literally everything**. Greasy substances like oil, wax, and plastics require non-aqueous solvents (which students should never handle without supervision).

**Activity 1 (at home with an adult):** Take three clear glasses of cold tap water. Stir one teaspoon of salt into the first, one teaspoon of sugar into the second, and one teaspoon of cooking oil into the third. Observe what happens. The salt and sugar disappear into clear solutions, while the oil forms floating droplets. **Do not taste anything**—just observe. Even if you stir the oil glass vigorously to break the oil into tiny droplets, wait a few minutes and watch them merge back into a floating layer on top.

**Check:** Why did the olive oil float on Ryan's soup while the salt disappeared? ✅ Water molecules surround and separate the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions of salt so they disperse evenly, whereas oil molecules do not mix with water and float on top because oil is less dense than water.

## Part C: Solubility: Exactly "How Much" Can Dissolve?

Recall from Chapter 2: at a given temperature, **the maximum number of grams of a solute that can dissolve in 100 g of water** is called the **solubility** of that solute at that temperature. Let's compare experimental figures for two famous salts across different temperatures. (These are approximate standard values; slight variations appear across reference tables.)

| Temperature | Potassium nitrate, KNO₃ (g per 100 g water) | Sodium chloride, NaCl (g per 100 g water) |
|---|---|---|
| 0 °C | 13 | – |
| 20 °C | 32 | 36 |
| 40 °C | 64 | – |
| 60 °C | 110 | – |
| 80 °C | 169 | 38 |

("–" means we do not need that value in this chapter.) For comparison, table sugar has a solubility of about 204 g per 100 g of water at 20 °C.

Two striking facts jump right out of the table:

1. **The solubility of KNO₃ shoots up dramatically with temperature.** From 13 g at 0 °C to 169 g at 80 °C: 169 ÷ 13 = **13 times** as much!
2. **The solubility of NaCl stays almost flat.** From 36 g at 20 °C to just 38 g at 80 °C—warming the water by 60 °C allows only 2 extra grams of salt to dissolve.

We can think of KNO₃ as "highly temperature-sensitive" and table salt as "nearly indifferent to temperature". Warming water barely increases how much table salt it can hold.

### Reading a Solubility Curve

Plotting the table values on a graph gives a **solubility curve**. Below is a character-grid graph where each horizontal step represents 2 °C and each **vertical row represents 10 g**. Because each row spans 10 g, use the graph to see the visual trend (to the nearest 10 g), and **always read exact values from the table**. Uppercase `K` and `N` mark plotted data points, while lowercase `k` and `n` trace the curves between them.

> **Figure 2.2** – Solubility curves (approximate): the curve for KNO₃ rises steeply from 13 g at 0 °C to 169 g at 80 °C, whereas the line for NaCl is nearly flat (36 g to 38 g). The two curves cross near 22.5 °C: below that temperature, NaCl is more soluble; above it, KNO₃ is far more soluble. (Because each row represents 10 g, the NaCl line sits in the 40 g row even though its exact values are 36 g to 38 g.)

Three simple rules for reading any solubility curve:

- **Any point on the curve** represents a **saturated solution** (holding the maximum possible solute at that temperature).
- **Any point below the curve** represents an **unsaturated solution** (more solute can still dissolve).
- **Any point above the curve** represents excess solute that cannot stay dissolved at equilibrium—the extra mass settles out as solid crystals at the bottom.

To **estimate** (interpolate) a value halfway between two table temperatures, take the average of the two endpoints. For example, at 50 °C (halfway between 40 °C and 60 °C), we estimate the solubility of KNO₃ as (64 + 110) ÷ 2 = 87 g per 100 g of water. This is a linear estimate; the true curve bows slightly, so the actual value is marginally different.

## Part D: Solved Examples

(All calculations use the table values from Part C; intermediate temperatures use linear estimation.)

**Example 1: Reading directly from the table.** How much KNO₃ dissolves in 100 g of water at 60 °C, and how much NaCl dissolves at 20 °C?

- KNO₃ at 60 °C = **110 g**
- NaCl at 20 °C = **36 g**

**Check:** Match the 60 °C and 20 °C rows in the table: 110 and 36 ✅.

**Example 2: Midpoint estimation (interpolation).** Estimate the solubility of KNO₃ in 100 g of water at 50 °C.

- At 40 °C it is 64 g; at 60 °C it is 110 g. Midpoint: (64 + 110) ÷ 2 = 174 ÷ 2 = **87 g**

**Check:** 64 < 87 < 110 ✅, and 87 − 64 = 23 while 110 − 87 = 23 (equally spaced) ✅.

**Example 3: How much more can dissolve?** At 40 °C, 30 g of KNO₃ is stirred into 100 g of water. Is the solution saturated or unsaturated, and how much more KNO₃ can dissolve?

- Maximum at 40 °C = 64 g.
- Since 30 < 64, the solution is **unsaturated**.
- Additional solute that can dissolve = 64 − 30 = **34 g**

**Check:** 30 + 34 = 64 ✅.

**Example 4: Scaling up the amount of water.** At 60 °C, how much KNO₃ is needed to make a saturated solution in 200 g of water? What is the total mass of that saturated solution?

- 100 g of water holds 110 g, so 200 g of water (2 times as much) holds 110 × 2 = **220 g**
- Total mass of solution = 220 + 200 = **420 g**

**Check:** 220 ÷ 200 = 1.1 and 110 ÷ 100 = 1.1 ✅ (same ratio per gram of water).

**Example 5: Cooling a hot saturated solution to grow crystals.** A saturated solution of KNO₃ in 100 g of water at 80 °C is cooled to 40 °C. How many grams of KNO₃ crystallise out?

- Dissolved at 80 °C = 169 g
- Maximum that can stay dissolved at 40 °C = 64 g
- Crystals formed = 169 − 64 = **105 g**

**Check:** 64 + 105 = 169 ✅. (Notice that the mass of water stays unchanged at 100 g; only the excess solute comes out of solution.)

**Example 6: Crystallisation with a different mass of water.** A saturated solution of KNO₃ made with 250 g of water at 60 °C is cooled to 20 °C. What mass of solid crystals separates out?

- For 100 g of water: 110 − 32 = 78 g
- For 250 g of water (2.5 times as much): 78 × 2.5 = **195 g**

**Check:** At 60 °C, 250 g of water held 110 × 2.5 = 275 g; at 20 °C it can hold 32 × 2.5 = 80 g; difference = 275 − 80 = 195 g ✅.

**Example 7: Why do we harvest sea salt by evaporation instead of cooling?** If you cool a saturated solution of NaCl in 100 g of water from 80 °C down to 20 °C, how much salt crystallises out? Compare this with KNO₃ over the same temperature drop.

- NaCl: 38 − 36 = **2 g** (tiny yield)
- KNO₃ (80 °C to 20 °C): 169 − 32 = **137 g**

**Check:** KNO₃ yields 137 ÷ 2 = 68.5 times as much crystal mass as NaCl ✅. That is why KNO₃ is purified by cooling a hot solution, whereas **table salt cannot be harvested efficiently by cooling**—instead, we evaporate the water under the sun, as in coastal salt pans.

**Example 8: Mass percentage of a saturated solution.** Find the mass percentage of solute in a saturated KNO₃ solution at 80 °C (169 g of KNO₃ in 100 g of water).

- Total mass of solution = 169 + 100 = 269 g
- Mass percent = (169 ÷ 269) × 100 = **62.8%** (approx.)

**Check:** 62.8% of 269 = 0.628 × 269 = 168.9 ≈ 169 ✅. (Remember from Chapter 2: divide by the total mass of the solution, not just the 100 g of water!)

**Example 9: What happens when you heat a mixture with undissolved solid?** At 40 °C, 70 g of KNO₃ is added to 100 g of water. Does all of it dissolve? What happens when the mixture is then heated to 60 °C?

- At 40 °C, at most 64 g dissolves, so 70 − 64 = **6 g remains undissolved** at the bottom (forming a saturated solution above it).
- At 60 °C, up to 110 g can dissolve. Since 70 < 110, **all the solid dissolves** and the solution becomes unsaturated, capable of dissolving 110 − 70 = **40 g more**.

**Check:** 64 + 6 = 70 ✅, and 70 + 40 = 110 ✅.

**Example 10: Dissolved oxygen and summer heat.** The solubility of oxygen gas in fresh water is approximately 14.6 mg per litre at 0 °C, 9.1 mg per litre at 20 °C, and 7.6 mg per litre at 30 °C. When a shallow pond warms from 20 °C to 30 °C in summer, by how much does its dissolved oxygen drop, and by what percentage?

- Drop = 9.1 − 7.6 = **1.5 mg per litre**
- Percentage drop = (1.5 ÷ 9.1) × 100 = **16.5%** (approx.)

**Check:** 9.1 × 0.165 = 1.50 ✅. Just a 10 °C rise wipes out about one-sixth of the oxygen supply—explaining why fish struggle in shallow ponds during heatwaves.

## Part E: Gases in Water: Fizzy Drinks and Fish

Unlike most solids, **the solubility of gases in water decreases as temperature rises** (as we first noted in Chapter 2). Here are two everyday examples:

- **Sparkling water and fizzy sodas:** Inside a sealed bottle, carbon dioxide (CO₂) gas is dissolved in water under high pressure. When you twist the cap off, the gas escapes as rising bubbles. A chilled soda stays fizzy much longer because cold water holds more dissolved CO₂; a warm soda loses its gas rapidly and goes flat. (You will study the exact effect of pressure on gas solubility in higher grades.)
- **Aquatic life:** Fish do not breathe air from the atmosphere; their gills extract **dissolved oxygen** directly from the water. During hot summer weather, pond water warms up, the solubility of oxygen drops (Example 10), and fish may gasp near the surface or even suffocate. Hot water discharged from power plants or factories causes the same problem (which we will revisit in Topic 6).

**Activity 2 (at home with an adult):** Pour equal amounts of sparkling water or club soda into two identical clear glasses—one chilled from the refrigerator and the other at room temperature (do not heat it on a stove). Watch closely to see which glass releases bubbles faster. **Do not drink or taste the samples** during the observation. Because CO₂ is less soluble in warmer water, the room-temperature soda fizzes out much faster.

Keep this contrast clear: warming water generally **increases** the solubility of solids, but **decreases** the solubility of gases.

```
   Hot solution (80 °C)         Cooling down...          Cool solution (20 °C)
   clear, all dissolved         (solubility limit drops) crystals settle at bottom

    [          ]                [          ]             [          ]
    [ o  o  o  ]                [ o  o  o  ]             [ o  o  o  ]
    [  o  o  o ]                [  o  o  o ]             [  o  o  o ]
    [ o  o  o  ]                [ o  o  o  ]             [ o  o  o  ]
    [__________]                [__________]             [___XXXX___]
    all KNO3 dissolved          as it cools,             XXXX = KNO3 crystals
    (169 g per 100 g)           holding capacity drops   (105 g when cooled to 40 °C)
```

> **Figure 2.3** – How crystals form on cooling: in the hot saturated solution, all the solute is dissolved (`o`). As the solution cools, the water's capacity to hold dissolved solute drops, and the excess solute separates out at the bottom as solid crystals (`X`).

## Part F: Everyday Roles of Water as a Solvent

- **Inside the human body:** Digested food breaks down into small soluble molecules that dissolve in blood plasma (mostly water) to travel to every cell. Nearly all biochemical reactions inside cells take place in aqueous solution.
- **In plants:** Plant roots can only absorb mineral nutrients from the soil when those minerals are dissolved in soil water (we will see what happens when excess fertiliser washes away in Topic 6).
- **Oral Rehydration Solution (ORS):** Dissolving measured packets of salt and glucose in clean water allows the body to absorb fluids and electrolytes rapidly during dehydration.
- **Washing and cleaning:** Water dissolves everyday dirt and sweat so it can be rinsed away (we will look at soap and hard water in Topic 3).
- **Purifying solids by crystallisation:** Compounds like KNO₃ are purified by dissolving them in hot water and cooling the solution to collect pure crystals (Example 5).
- **Harvesting sea salt:** Because NaCl solubility barely changes with temperature, sea salt is collected by **solar evaporation** rather than cooling (Example 7).
- **The downside:** Because water is such a good solvent, it can also dissolve harmful pollutants and toxic salts while remaining crystal clear. We will examine this in Topics 3, 5, and 6.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Water dissolves literally everything."**
No. Cooking oil, wax, plastics, sand, and glass are practically insoluble in water. "Universal solvent" is just a nickname highlighting how many things water *does* dissolve.

**Mistake 2: "When salt dissolves, the salt ceases to exist."**
No. The salt is still present as separated Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions dispersed through the water. Evaporating the water recovers the solid salt.

**Mistake 3: "Heating water makes every substance much more soluble."**
That is true for most solids like KNO₃, but NaCl barely changes (36 g to 38 g), and gases actually become *less* soluble when heated.

**Mistake 4: "To find mass percent, divide the solute mass by 100 g of water."**
No. In Example 8 we divided by 269 g (solute + water), not 100 g. Solubility is stated per 100 g of water, whereas mass percentage is per 100 g of total solution.

**Mistake 5: "The crystals that form on cooling come from the mass of the water."**
No. The crystals are the excess solute that can no longer stay dissolved; the mass of the liquid water remains unchanged (100 g in Example 5).

**Mistake 6: "A point above the solubility curve means the solution is unsaturated."**
No. Points *on* the curve are saturated; points *below* are unsaturated; points *above* represent excess undissolved solute settling out.

## Quick Recap

- Ionic compounds dissolve in water because water molecules attract and surround surface ions, pulling them free from the crystal lattice; the ions remain in solution.
- Covalent substances like sugar and alcohol also dissolve in water, whereas oil, wax, and chalk do not. "Universal solvent" is only a loose nickname.
- **Solubility** = maximum grams of solute that dissolve in 100 g of water at a given temperature.
- KNO₃ solubility rises sharply: 13 g (0 °C), 32 g (20 °C), 64 g (40 °C), 110 g (60 °C), 169 g (80 °C). NaCl solubility is nearly flat: 36 g (20 °C) to 38 g (80 °C).
- On the curve = saturated; below = unsaturated; above = excess solute crystallises out.
- Cooling a hot saturated solution yields crystals: crystal mass = (solute dissolved at high T) − (solute that stays dissolved at low T), scaled to the mass of water used.
- Gas solubility in water **decreases** as temperature rises (explaining flat warm sodas and fish struggling in hot summer ponds).
- Safety: only use safe kitchen items (salt, sugar, oil, chilled vs room-temperature soda) for home observations, and never taste experimental mixtures.

11. State True or False and correct the false ones:
    (a) A point lying above the solubility curve means an unsaturated solution.
    (b) When salt dissolves, the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions disappear from the solution.
    (c) The solubility of potassium nitrate increases with temperature much more than that of sodium chloride.
    (d) Water can dissolve everything, so it is truly a universal solvent.

### Answers

1. Common salt is ionic: Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions sit in a crystal lattice. Water molecules **attract and surround these surface ions**, pulling them free so they disperse evenly throughout the water. Cooking oil molecules do not interact favourably with water molecules, so they stay separate and float on top.

2. Each CaCl₂ unit releases 1 Ca²⁺ and 2 Cl⁻. For 4 units: Ca²⁺ = 4 × 1 = **4**, Cl⁻ = 4 × 2 = **8**, giving **12 ions** in total.
   Check: 4 + 8 = 12 ✅.

3. For KNO₃: 169 ÷ 13 = **13 times** larger. For NaCl: 38 ÷ 36 = 1.06 times—almost **unchanged**. Thus NaCl solubility changes very little with temperature compared with KNO₃.
   Check: 13 × 13 = 169 ✅.

4. 30 °C is halfway between 20 °C and 40 °C: (32 + 64) ÷ 2 = **48 g**. Since 40 g < 48 g, the solution is **unsaturated**, and 48 − 40 = **8 g more** can dissolve.
   Check: 40 + 8 = 48 ✅.

5. In 100 g of water, 36 g dissolves; in 250 g of water, 36 × 2.5 = **90 g** dissolves. Adding 100 g leaves 100 − 90 = **10 g** undissolved at the bottom.
   Check: 90 + 10 = 100 ✅.

6. In 100 g of water, 204 g dissolves, so in 25 g (one-quarter) 204 ÷ 4 = **51 g** dissolves. A saturated solution has 204 g sugar in 100 g water: total solution mass = 304 g, so mass percent = (204 ÷ 304) × 100 = **67.1%**.
   Check: 0.671 × 304 = 204.0 ✅.

7. For 100 g of water: 169 − 13 = 156 g crystallises. For 150 g of water (1.5 times as much): 156 × 1.5 = **234 g**.
   Check: At 80 °C, 169 × 1.5 = 253.5 g was dissolved; at 0 °C, 13 × 1.5 = 19.5 g stays dissolved; 253.5 − 19.5 = 234 g ✅.

8. Since 64 g per 100 g of water is the exact solubility of KNO₃ at 40 °C, the solution becomes **saturated at 40 °C** and crystals begin to form below that point. Cooling further to 20 °C leaves 32 g dissolved, so 64 − 32 = **32 g** crystallises out.
   Check: 32 + 32 = 64 ✅.

9. For 100 g of water: 38 − 36 = 2 g. For 500 g of water (5 times as much): 2 × 5 = **10 g** of salt (a tiny amount for half a kilogram of water). Salt makers do not use cooling because NaCl's solubility barely changes with temperature; instead, they evaporate the water using solar heat.
   Check: 5 × 2 = 10 ✅.

10. In hot summer weather, the solubility of oxygen gas in water decreases, leaving less dissolved oxygen for fish to breathe through their gills—so they can suffocate even in clean-looking water. Similarly, CO₂ gas is less soluble in warm water than in cold water, so a warm soda releases its gas bubbles much faster and goes flat.

11. (a) **False** ❌. A point **above** the curve represents excess solute that settles out; an unsaturated solution lies **below** the curve. (b) **False** ❌. The Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions remain in the solution separated from the lattice; evaporating the water recovers the salt. (c) **True** ✅. KNO₃ rises from 13 g to 169 g, while NaCl only rises from 36 g to 38 g. (d) **False** ❌. Substances like oil, wax, and plastic do not dissolve in water; "universal solvent" is only a figure of speech.
