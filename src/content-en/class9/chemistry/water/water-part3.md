# Topic 3: Hard and Soft Water

In the previous topic, we saw that water dissolves a wide variety of substances and that temperature controls how much solute can stay dissolved. Now let's ask: **do the minerals dissolved in natural water always sit quietly without causing trouble?** Sometimes they interfere with everyday chores—killing soap lather and building a stony white crust inside electric kettles and water heaters. Today we investigate why.

---

## Chalky Limescale in the Kettle and Soap That Won't Lather

At the Miller family's countryside cottage, a small mystery had been bothering everyone. Mia was visiting her uncle, Mr. Miller, for the weekend. While filling the stainless-steel electric kettle for morning tea, she noticed a **rough, chalky white crust** coating the heating element and inner walls. "Uncle, why does the inside of the kettle look like someone painted it with chalk? Didn't you scrub it last month?"

Mr. Miller chuckled. "You can scrub it clean, and a few weeks later it's right back. That's a gift from our deep groundwater well." He led Mia to the utility sink, where her aunt was washing her hands with a bar of soap. Instead of rich, fluffy bubbles, rubbing the soap produced a **sticky, greyish-white curdy film** that clung to her hands and formed a ring around the basin. "With this well water, ordinary bar soap barely lathers at all," her aunt sighed. "It wastes so much soap."

Mia was intrigued. "Uncle, what's hiding inside the well water that coats the kettle with rock and eats up all the soap bubbles?" Mr. Miller replied, "That's today's chemistry detective case. And here's the funny thing: this water is **perfectly safe to drink**—it just makes housework frustrating. Let's start with that crust inside the kettle."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Soft water** | Water containing **very little or no** dissolved calcium and magnesium salts; lathers easily with soap | Rainwater, distilled water |
| **Hard water** | Water containing **significant amounts** of dissolved calcium and magnesium salts; resists forming a lather with soap | Groundwater from limestone wells or boreholes |
| **Scum** | An **insoluble, curdy white solid** formed when soap reacts with calcium or magnesium ions in hard water | The sticky ring left around a washbasin |
| **Temporary hardness** | Hardness caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium **hydrogencarbonates** (bicarbonates); **removed by boiling** | Ca(HCO₃)₂, Mg(HCO₃)₂ |
| **Permanent hardness** | Hardness caused by dissolved calcium and magnesium **chlorides and sulphates**; **not removed by boiling** | CaCl₂, MgSO₄, CaSO₄ |
| **Limescale (kettle scale)** | The hard, chalky deposit formed on heating surfaces when temporary hard water is boiled | CaCO₃ crust inside kettles, water heaters, and boilers |
| **Washing soda** | Sodium carbonate (Na₂CO₃), used to soften both temporary and permanent hard water | Na₂CO₃ |
| **Ion-exchange** | A softening method where a resin (or zeolite) traps Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions from water and releases Na⁺ ions in exchange | Domestic water-softener units |
| **Detergent** | A synthetic cleaning agent that lathers in hard water without forming insoluble scum | Laundry powder, liquid dish soap |

## Part A: Soft Water vs Hard Water: What's the Difference?

In Topic 1, we studied chemically pure H₂O. However, natural water drawn from wells, boreholes, or rivers is **never pure H₂O**. As rainwater trickles through soil and underground rock layers—especially limestone, chalk, and dolomite—it acts as a solvent (Topic 2) and dissolves small amounts of mineral salts. Those rocks are rich in **calcium and magnesium** compounds, so the water picks up **Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions**.

Depending on how much of these salts the water has dissolved, we classify it into two types:

- **Soft water:** Contains **very little or no** dissolved calcium and magnesium salts. Examples include clean rainwater and distilled water.
- **Hard water:** Contains **appreciable amounts** of dissolved calcium and magnesium salts. Examples include water from many wells, springs, and limestone aquifers.

Keep in mind: hardness is **not pollution or poison**. Hard water is generally safe to drink (and even has some benefits, as we will see in Part G). The trouble only starts when hard water meets soap, kettles, or industrial boilers.

## Part B: The Soap Test: Why Doesn't Hard Water Lather?

Ordinary bar soap consists of **sodium salts** of long-chain fatty acids (such as sodium stearate), which dissolve in water to produce cleansing foam and lather.

When soap meets **Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ions** in hard water, a **double displacement reaction** takes place (swapping partners, just like in Chapter 7). The sodium ions swap places with calcium or magnesium ions, forming calcium or magnesium soap, which is **insoluble in water**. It precipitates out as sticky, curdy white **scum**:

> sodium soap (soluble) + Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ions (in hard water) → **calcium or magnesium soap (insoluble scum)** + sodium ions (remain in solution)

(You do not need to memorise the long chemical formula of stearate at this stage—understanding the partner swap is what matters.)

**What this means in practice:** As long as free Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions remain in the water, any soap you rub in reacts with them to form useless scum instead of bubbles. A rich lather can only begin to form **after every last Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ion has been used up**. Until then, all the soap you use is **wasted**, and the sticky scum clings to fabrics and washbasins.

What about synthetic **detergents** (laundry powders, shampoos, and liquid dish soaps)? The calcium and magnesium salts of synthetic detergents are **soluble in water**, so detergents lather freely even in hard water without forming scum.

Here is the visual comparison when equal amounts of soap are shaken 10 times in soft water versus hard water:

```

      ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~                        . ~ .
     ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~   <- thick lather     (scanty foam)
     ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~
    +-------------+                    +-------------+
    :             :                    :   o   o     :
    :   clear     :                    :  o  o  o    :  <- curdy white
    :   water     :                    :  o  o   o   :     scum; water
    :             :                    :  cloudy     :     turns cloudy
    +-------------+                    +-------------+

    Foam height: tall                  Foam height: very low

Soft water (left) produces a tall column of rich lather above clear water; hard water (right) gives almost no foam and turns cloudy with floating curdy **scum** (`o`) because most of the soap was consumed reacting with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺.

### Activity 3.1: Comparing Soap Lather in Three Water Samples

> **Activity 3.1 (at home with an adult).** Take three clean, identical **small jars with tight-fitting lids**. Using a measuring jug, pour **100 mL** of water into each jar:
> - **Jar A:** Ordinary tap or well water from your home supply.
> - **Jar B:** The same tap water after **an adult has boiled it** for several minutes and allowed it to **cool completely** (pour in the clear top water).
> - **Jar C:** **Distilled water** (from a pharmacy or auto store, or clean rainwater collected directly in a clean bowl).
>

Immediately **measure the height of the foam layer with a ruler** and record your results. **Never drink or taste** any of the water samples.
>

What you will observe (if your local tap water is hard):

1. **Jar C** (distilled water) produces the **tallest foam layer**, and the water underneath stays crystal clear.
2. **Jar A** (unboiled tap water) produces **much less foam**, with cloudy curds of scum.
3. **Jar B** (boiled and cooled tap water) often produces **more foam than Jar A**. Why does boiling help? Let's see in Part C.

(If your local tap water is naturally soft, all three jars will produce tall foam layers with very little difference—and that is a **valid scientific result** proving your local water supply is already soft!)

## Part C: Temporary Hardness: Hardness Removed by Boiling

First, let's examine **temporary hardness**. It is caused by dissolved **calcium hydrogencarbonate** and **magnesium hydrogencarbonate** (also called bicarbonates): **Ca(HCO₃)₂** and **Mg(HCO₃)₂**. (Recall the hydrogencarbonate radical HCO₃⁻ from Chapter 3.)

These two compounds are **thermally unstable**—when you heat the water to boiling, they decompose:

```
   Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂
   Mg(HCO₃)₂ → MgCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂
```

Atom balance check: Ca 1=1, H 2=2, C 2=2 (1 + 1), O 6=6 (3 + 1 + 2); the magnesium equation balances identically ✅.

Notice that this is a **thermal decomposition reaction** (Chapter 7, Topic 2): a single compound breaks down into three products. Crucially, **CaCO₃** (calcium carbonate) is **insoluble in water**, so it precipitates out as a **white solid**. **CO₂** bubbles away as a gas, and **H₂O** joins the surrounding water.

That solves the mystery of Mr. Miller's electric kettle! Every time hard well water containing dissolved **Ca(HCO₃)₂** is boiled, it decomposes into insoluble **CaCO₃**, which sticks to the hot metal heating element and inner walls. Day after day, thin layers build up into a **rough, chalky white crust**—what we call **limescale** or **kettle scale**. The exact same deposit builds up inside hot-water geysers, steam irons, and industrial boilers.

```
         BEFORE boiling                     AFTER weeks of boiling

            lid                                   lid
         _________                             _________
        /         \                           /         \
       : water     :                         : water     :
       : (dissolved:                         : (softened :
       : Ca(HCO₃)₂):                         :  water)   :
       :           :                         :###########:  <- solid CaCO₃
       :___________:                         :#limescale#:     crust
        \_________/                           \#########/
          :  heat/                              :  heat/
          v   element                           v   element

    Dissolved Ca²⁺ and HCO₃⁻ ions      Boiling precipitates solid CaCO₃ (#)
    float freely in solution           while CO₂ gas escapes
```

> **Figure 3.2** – How limescale forms inside a kettle. Boiling drives the reaction Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂. Insoluble CaCO₃ (`#`) coats the bottom and walls, removing Ca²⁺ ions from the liquid water—which is why **boiled and cooled water is softer** and gave more lather in Jar B of Activity 3.1.

### A Quick Calculation: How Much Limescale Forms?

Using atomic masses: Ca = 40, C = 12, O = 16, H = 1 (u).

- Ca(HCO₃)₂ = 40 + 2 × (1 + 12 + 3 × 16) = 40 + 2 × 61 = **162 u**
- CaCO₃ = 40 + 12 + 48 = **100 u**
- H₂O = **18 u**, CO₂ = 12 + 32 = **44 u**

Mass conservation check: 100 + 18 + 44 = **162**. Exact match ✅.

### Example 1. How much CaCO₃ forms when 8.1 g of Ca(HCO₃)₂ is boiled completely?

Every 162 g of Ca(HCO₃)₂ produces 100 g of CaCO₃. Therefore,
8.1 g produces: 8.1 × 100 ÷ 162 = **5.0 g of CaCO₃**.

Check: 8.1 ÷ 162 = 0.05, and 0.05 × 100 = 5.0 ✅.

### Example 2. Daily limescale buildup in a household kettle

Suppose (as a simplified illustration) a household's well water contains **0.162 g of Ca(HCO₃)₂ per litre**, and the family boils **2 litres** of water each day. Mass of Ca(HCO₃)₂ boiled per day: 0.162 × 2 = **0.324 g**. Mass of CaCO₃ formed per day: 0.324 × 100 ÷ 162 = **0.2 g** per day.

Over **30 days**: 0.2 × 30 = **6 g** of limescale. That may sound small, but spread across the heating element month after month, it forms a stubborn insulating crust. (In reality, some of the precipitate pours out with the water rather than sticking to the walls; this calculation shows the total mass formed.)

### A Note on Magnesium Hydrogencarbonate

Boiling Mg(HCO₃)₂ similarly precipitates insoluble **MgCO₃**. Under prolonged boiling, magnesium scale can convert further into magnesium hydroxide, which you will study in higher classes. For Class 9, the key rule is simple: **hardness caused by hydrogencarbonates is temporary and is removed by boiling.**

## Part D: Permanent Hardness: Hardness That Boiling Cannot Remove

Now let's look at **permanent hardness**. It is caused by dissolved **chlorides and sulphates of calcium and magnesium**: **CaCl₂**, **MgCl₂**, **CaSO₄**, and **MgSO₄**.

Unlike hydrogencarbonates, these salts are **thermally stable**—they do **not** decompose when you boil the water. No matter how long you boil the water, the Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions stay dissolved in solution. Therefore, **boiling cannot remove permanent hardness.**

What if boiling a water sample improves the soap lather slightly, but the water still needs extra soap compared with distilled water? That tells you the sample had **both temporary and permanent hardness**: boiling removed the **temporary part**, while the **permanent part** remained behind.

| Type of Hardness | Cause (Dissolved Salts) | Removed by Boiling? |
|---|---|---|
| Temporary hardness | Ca(HCO₃)₂, Mg(HCO₃)₂ | Yes (removed as insoluble carbonates) |
| Permanent hardness | CaCl₂, MgCl₂, CaSO₄, MgSO₄ | No (salts do not decompose on boiling) |

## Part E: Methods for Softening Hard Water

How do we remove Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions from water to make it soft? Here are four standard methods:

### Method 1: Boiling (Removes Temporary Hardness Only)

Boiling decomposes Ca(HCO₃)₂ and Mg(HCO₃)₂ into insoluble CaCO₃ and MgCO₃, which settle out or stick to the kettle walls. Filtering or decanting the cooled water gives soft water. However, this works **only for temporary hardness** and has no effect on permanent hardness.

### Method 2: Adding Washing Soda (Removes Both Types)

Adding **washing soda (sodium carbonate, Na₂CO₃)** removes **both** temporary and permanent hardness. It reacts with dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts via **double displacement** to precipitate **insoluble carbonates** (recall Chapter 7, Topic 3):

```
   CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl
   MgSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → MgCO₃ + Na₂SO₄
   Ca(HCO₃)₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaHCO₃
```

Atom balance check: First: Ca 1=1, Cl 2=2, Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 3=3 ✅. Second: Mg 1=1, S 1=1, Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 7=7 ✅. Third: Ca 1=1, H 2=2, C 3=3, O 9=9, Na 2=2 ✅.

In all three reactions, the ions swap partners and **insoluble CaCO₃ or MgCO₃ precipitates out as a solid**, pulling the troublesome Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions out of the water. The dissolved salts left behind (NaCl, Na₂SO₄, NaHCO₃) are **sodium salts**, which do not react with soap to form scum. Thus the water is now soft!

In one sentence: **Washing soda precipitates Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions out of solution as insoluble CaCO₃ and MgCO₃.**

### Example 3. How much Na₂CO₃ is needed to remove 11.1 g of CaCl₂, and how much CaCO₃ precipitates?

Formula unit masses: CaCl₂ = 40 + 2 × 35.5 = **111 u**; Na₂CO₃ = 2 × 23 + 12 + 48 = **106 u**; CaCO₃ = **100 u**; NaCl = 23 + 35.5 = 58.5 u, so 2NaCl = **117 u**.

Mass conservation check: 111 + 106 = **217**; 100 + 117 = **217**. Exact match ✅.

Since 111 g of CaCl₂ reacts with 106 g of Na₂CO₃, **11.1 g** of CaCl₂ (one-tenth as much) requires 106 ÷ 10 = **10.6 g** of Na₂CO₃, and precipitates 100 ÷ 10 = **10 g of CaCO₃**.

Check: 11.1 ÷ 111 = 0.1; 0.1 × 106 = 10.6 ✅ and 0.1 × 100 = 10 ✅.

### Method 3: Ion-Exchange Resin (Domestic Water Softeners)

Modern household **water softeners** (often fitted to whole-house plumbing or dishwashers) pass hard water through a column packed with **ion-exchange resin beads** (or natural minerals called **zeolites**). As hard water flows over the resin, the resin **traps Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions** and releases **Na⁺ ions** in exchange. To keep electric charge balanced, the resin releases **two Na⁺ ions** (1 + 1 = +2) for every **one Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ion** (+2) it captures. Because sodium ions do not form soap scum or limescale, the water leaving the unit is soft.

### Method 4: Distillation (Produces the Purest Water)

Recall distillation from Chapter 2: water is boiled into steam and condensed back into liquid in a separate collector. All dissolved salts—temporary and permanent alike—are left behind in the boiling flask, yielding **completely soft, pure distilled water**. Because boiling every litre of water into steam requires huge amounts of energy, distillation is **slow and expensive**, so it is used mainly for laboratory work, car batteries, and steam irons rather than whole-house water softening.

```
   WATER HARDNESS
         :
         +---- Temporary (Ca, Mg hydrogencarbonate salts, HCO₃⁻)
         :         :
         :         +-- Boiling (precipitates CaCO₃, MgCO₃)
         :         +-- Washing soda (Na₂CO₃)
         :         +-- Ion-exchange resin
         :         +-- Distillation
         :
         +---- Permanent (Ca, Mg chloride & sulphate salts, Cl⁻, SO₄²⁻)
                   :
                   +-- Washing soda (Na₂CO₃)
                   +-- Ion-exchange resin
                   +-- Distillation
                   (Boiling does NOT work)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Summary of water hardness types and softening methods. All four methods remove temporary hardness, whereas boiling fails for permanent hardness (leaving washing soda, ion-exchange, and distillation).

### Example 4. Choosing the Right Softening Method

| Water Sample | Best Method | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking water with Ca(HCO₃)₂ only (at home) | Boiling (by an adult) | Decomposes Ca(HCO₃)₂ into insoluble CaCO₃ without adding chemicals |
| Laundry water containing CaCl₂ (permanent) | Washing soda or ion-exchange | Boiling cannot decompose CaCl₂ |
| Well water containing both MgSO₄ and Ca(HCO₃)₂ | Washing soda or ion-exchange | Removes both temporary and permanent hardness together |
| Water needed for school chemistry lab solutions | Distillation | Removes all dissolved ions completely |

## Part F: Disadvantages of Hard Water

Even though hard water is not toxic, it causes costly problems at home and in industry:

1. **Wastes soap and leaves dingy laundry:** Soap is consumed forming insoluble scum before any lather appears (Part B), and sticky scum clings to clothes and bathtubs.
2. **Limescale buildup in household appliances:** Kettles, electric water heaters, and coffee machines become coated with chalky CaCO₃. Because **limescale is a poor conductor of heat**, the heating element has to run longer to warm the water—**wasting electricity or gas** and causing heating coils to burn out prematurely.
3. **Danger in industrial boilers:** In factory steam boilers, thick scale narrows pipes and blocks heat transfer from the furnace into the water. The metal boiler wall can overheat dangerously, weaken, and even **crack or burst under pressure**. That is why factories always soften water before feeding it into boilers.
4. **Trouble in textile dyeing and paper mills:** Calcium and magnesium ions interfere with fabric dyes, producing uneven colours and stiff fabrics.
5. **Clogged hot-water pipes:** Over years, thick scale narrows hot-water plumbing and reduces water flow.

### Example 5. Limescale buildup in a domestic water heater

Suppose a home's water supply contains **0.405 g of Ca(HCO₃)₂ per litre** and the electric water heater heats **10 litres** of water to near boiling each day. Daily Ca(HCO₃)₂ heated: 0.405 × 10 = **4.05 g**. Daily CaCO₃ formed: 4.05 × 100 ÷ 162 = **2.5 g**. Over 20 days: 2.5 × 20 = **50 g** of limescale! (Even if only part of this adheres to the heating coil, you can see why hot-water heaters in hard-water areas need periodic descaling.)

Check: 4.05 ÷ 162 = 0.025, and 0.025 × 100 = 2.5 ✅.

## Part G: Does Hard Water Have Any Benefits? (Yes!)

Calling hard water "bad" would be one-sided—it actually has two everyday advantages:

1. **Taste:** Many people prefer the crisp, mineral taste of moderately hard drinking water and find completely soft or distilled water flat and unappealing. (Of course, never taste water samples in a science lab!)
2. **Dietary calcium and magnesium:** Drinking hard water contributes small amounts of calcium and magnesium ions, which are essential minerals for strong bones, teeth, and muscle function (though most of our daily requirement comes from food).

One practical quirk of very soft water: when you wash your hands with soap in very soft water, the slippery feel takes longer to rinse off because no calcium ions are present to curdle the soap film—so a tiny dab of soap is plenty!

## Part H: Reasoning Cases: Think Like a Chemist

### Example 6. Boiling increased the lather—what does that prove?

A student tested tap water with soap, then boiled a fresh portion of the same tap water, let it cool, and tested it again with the same amount of soap. The unboiled sample gave **very little foam**, while the boiled sample gave **rich foam**.

- Because boiling reduced the hardness, the water had **temporary hardness** caused by dissolved Ca(HCO₃)₂ or Mg(HCO₃)₂.
- Had the foam height stayed **equally low** after boiling, the hardness would have been **permanent** (from chlorides or sulphates like CaCl₂ or MgSO₄).

### Example 7. Comparing two mystery water samples P and Q

Sample P gives **much more lather after boiling**. Sample Q gives **the exact same small lather before and after boiling**.

- Sample P has **mainly temporary hardness** (hydrogencarbonates).
- Sample Q has **mainly permanent hardness** (chlorides and sulphates), which boiling cannot remove.

### Example 8. What does kettle scale tell you about the water?

Mr. Miller's kettle builds up a thick white crust of **CaCO₃**. That immediately proves the well water contains **Ca(HCO₃)₂** (temporary hardness), because CaCO₃ is produced when Ca(HCO₃)₂ decomposes on boiling. (Permanent-hardness salts like CaCl₂ do not form CaCO₃ when boiled.)

### Example 9. Adding washing soda turns clear hard water milky—why?

When washing soda (Na₂CO₃) is stirred into clear water containing dissolved CaCl₂, a **milky white precipitate** appears and slowly settles to the bottom. What is that solid? **Insoluble CaCO₃**, formed via CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl. Once the white CaCO₃ settles out, the clear water above it is **soft**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Hard water is toxic and unsafe to drink"**

No. Hard water simply contains dissolved calcium and magnesium salts, which are harmless in normal drinking water. The problem is with **soap, kettles, and boilers**, not human health.

**Mistake 2: "Boiling softens every kind of hard water"**

Boiling removes **only temporary hardness** (hydrogencarbonates). **Permanent hardness** (chlorides and sulphates) is **not** removed by boiling.

**Mistake 3: "Kettle scale is rust or mud from the pipes"**

No. White kettle scale is mainly **CaCO₃** formed by the thermal decomposition of dissolved Ca(HCO₃)₂. Rust is an iron oxide, which is brown-red and completely different.

**Mistake 4: "Washing soda softens water by dissolving the calcium even more"**

Just the opposite! Washing soda reacts with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ to form **insoluble solid** CaCO₃ and MgCO₃ precipitates that settle out, **removing** those ions from the liquid.

**Mistake 5: "If soap doesn't lather, the soap bar must be defective"**

Usually the soap is fine—the **water is hard**.

**Mistake 6: Tasting or drinking water from the soap-lather activity**

Never drink or taste any water from Activity 3.1. It contains soap, and laboratory/hardware-store distilled water is not packaged for drinking.

## Quick Recap

- **Soft water:** Very little Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺; lathers easily with soap. **Hard water:** High in dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts; resists lathering.
- In hard water, soap reacts with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ to form **insoluble scum** (calcium/magnesium soap), wasting soap. **Detergents** do not form scum.
- **Temporary hardness:** Caused by Ca(HCO₃)₂ and Mg(HCO₃)₂. Removed by boiling: **Ca(HCO₃)₂ → CaCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂**. The insoluble CaCO₃ forms **kettle limescale**.
- **Permanent hardness:** Caused by chlorides and sulphates (CaCl₂, MgCl₂, CaSO₄, MgSO₄). **Not** removed by boiling.
- **Washing soda (Na₂CO₃)** removes both types via double displacement precipitation: **CaCl₂ + Na₂CO₃ → CaCO₃ + 2NaCl** and **MgSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → MgCO₃ + Na₂SO₄**.
- **Ion-exchange resins (zeolites)** swap Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions for Na⁺ ions. **Distillation** removes all dissolved salts but is energy-intensive.
- Disadvantages of hard water: wastes soap, leaves scum on clothes, builds insulating scale in kettles, water heaters, and industrial boilers. Benefits: provides trace dietary Ca and Mg and a pleasant mineral taste.
- Safety: an adult must handle boiling water in Activity 3.1, and no test samples should ever be tasted.

### Answers

1. **Hard water** contains appreciable amounts of dissolved calcium and magnesium salts and does not lather easily with soap (e.g., well or borehole water from limestone areas). **Soft water** contains very little or no calcium and magnesium salts and lathers readily with soap (e.g., rainwater or distilled water).

2. The sodium salt in soap **reacts with dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions** to form calcium or magnesium soap, which is **insoluble in water**. That insoluble solid is **scum** (**calcium or magnesium soap**). Until all Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions are used up, soap goes into making scum rather than lather.

3. | Feature | Temporary Hardness | Permanent Hardness |
   |---|---|---|
   | Cause | Hydrogencarbonates of Ca and Mg | Chlorides and sulphates of Ca and Mg |
   | Compounds | Ca(HCO₃)₂, Mg(HCO₃)₂ | CaCl₂, MgCl₂, CaSO₄, MgSO₄ |
   | Effect of boiling | Hardness is **removed** | Hardness is **not** removed |

4. **Mg(HCO₃)₂ → MgCO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.** Atom count: Mg 1=1, H 2=2, C 2=2 (1 + 1), O 6=6 (3 + 1 + 2) ✅. **MgCO₃** is the insoluble solid precipitate; H₂O is liquid water and CO₂ is a gas.

5. Mg(HCO₃)₂ = 24 + 2 × 61 = **146 u**. MgCO₃ = 24 + 12 + 48 = **84 u**. Check: 84 + 18 + 44 = 146 ✅. Since 146 g gives 84 g of MgCO₃, 7.3 g gives: 7.3 × 84 ÷ 146 = **4.2 g of MgCO₃**. Check: 7.3 ÷ 146 = 0.05, and 0.05 × 84 = 4.2 ✅.

6. (a) **MgSO₄ + Na₂CO₃ → MgCO₃ + Na₂SO₄.** Atom count: Mg 1=1, S 1=1, Na 2=2, C 1=1, O 7=7 ✅. (b) MgSO₄ = 24 + 32 + 64 = **120 u**; Na₂CO₃ = **106 u**; MgCO₃ = **84 u**; Na₂SO₄ = 46 + 32 + 64 = **142 u**. Mass check: 120 + 106 = 226 = 84 + 142 ✅. For 2.4 g of MgSO₄, scale factor = 2.4 ÷ 120 = 0.02. Na₂CO₃ needed: 0.02 × 106 = **2.12 g**. MgCO₃ formed: 0.02 × 84 = **1.68 g**.

7. Because the lather remained **the same** (low) after boiling, the hardness was **not** removed by boiling—meaning the water has **permanent hardness** caused by dissolved chlorides or sulphates (which do not decompose when boiled).

8. **Ca(HCO₃)₂** **decomposes on boiling** into insoluble CaCO₃, H₂O, and CO₂ gas, removing Ca²⁺ ions from solution as a solid precipitate. **CaCl₂** does **not decompose on boiling**: its Ca²⁺ and Cl⁻ ions stay dissolved in the water, so the hardness remains.

9. **Adding washing soda (Na₂CO₃)** and **ion-exchange** (as well as distillation). Home water softeners use **ion-exchange**: water flows through a resin or zeolite bed that **traps Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions** and releases **Na⁺ ions** in exchange (two Na⁺ for each Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺).

10. Disadvantages (any three): wastes soap, leaves curdy scum on clothes, builds heat-blocking scale inside kettles and water heaters (wasting energy), damages industrial boilers and clogs hot-water pipes, interferes with fabric dyeing. Benefit: supplies small amounts of dietary calcium and magnesium (and often has a pleasant mineral taste).

11. (i) Limescale is a **poor conductor of heat**, so less heat reaches the water and **more fuel is wasted**. (ii) Thick scale **narrows pipes** and causes the metal boiler walls to overheat dangerously, which can weaken or burst the boiler.

12. (a) **False** ❌. Hard water is not poisonous; it only causes trouble with soap, kettles, and boilers. (b) **False** ❌. Detergents **do not** form insoluble scum in hard water. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌. Boiling removes **only temporary** hardness. (e) **True** ✅. Distillation leaves all dissolved mineral salts behind.

13. **Distilled water** gives the most lather (because it contains no Ca²⁺ or Mg²⁺ ions). Boiled-and-cooled tap water gives more lather than unboiled tap water if temporary hardness was present. Safety rules: perform **with an adult**; **only an adult should boil the water**; let boiled water cool completely before handling; **never drink or taste any sample**; pour all liquids down the sink afterward, wash hands, and do not reuse the test jars for food.
