# Topic 4: Water and Crystals: Hydrates, Efflorescence, and Deliquescence

In the previous topic, we saw how dissolved calcium and magnesium salts make water **hard**, sometimes precipitating out as chalky limescale when boiled and sometimes staying dissolved. There, the salts were dissolved *in* liquid water. Now let's flip the question around: can water hide *inside* a **dry, solid crystal**? And when crystals sit in open air, why do some absorb so much moisture from the air that they turn into a liquid puddle, while others lose their own water to the air and crumble into powder? That is our investigation today.

Throughout this topic, we use the standard **rounded atomic masses** from Chapter 4: H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, S = 32, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, Fe = 56, Cu = 63.5. Thus the molecular mass of H₂O is 18 u. Percentages are rounded to two decimal places as shown in each step.

## The "Do Not Eat" Packet in the Shoebox

Alex had just unboxed a brand-new pair of running shoes. Tucked under the tissue paper at the bottom of the box was a tiny white paper pouch stamped in bold capital letters: **"SILICA GEL — DO NOT EAT"**. When he shook it gently, hard glassy beads rattled inside.

"I'll ask Ms. Lin about this," Alex thought. Ms. Lin, a high-school chemistry teacher who lived next door, was dropping by that evening.

When Ms. Lin saw the sealed pouch, she held it up without opening it. "That's **silica gel**. Never tear the packet open or put it near your mouth—it isn't food; its job is to protect whatever is inside the box."

"Protect it from what?" Alex asked. "It's just sitting in a cardboard box!"

"Think about the journey those shoes took," Ms. Lin said. "Months in a warehouse, weeks inside a shipping container crossing the ocean, then delivery trucks. Air always contains water vapour—and in humid weather, a lot of it. If damp air stays trapped in the box, mould and mildew will ruin the leather and fabric. This tiny packet **pulls water vapour out of the air** inside the box while staying bone-dry to the touch."

"Is it magic?"

"Pure chemistry! And silica gel belongs to a whole fascinating family of materials: crystals that lock water molecules inside their solid structure, crystals that give up their water to dry air, and solids so thirsty that they dissolve in the moisture they pull from humid air. Grab your notebook—let's meet the whole family."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Water of crystallisation** | A fixed number of water molecules chemically bound within the crystal lattice of a salt | The 5 H₂O in CuSO₄·5H₂O |
| **Hydrate (hydrated salt)** | A crystalline salt that contains water of crystallisation in its structure | Blue vitriol, washing soda crystals |
| **Anhydrous** | "Without water"—the salt left after all water of crystallisation has been removed | White anhydrous CuSO₄ |
| **Formula unit mass** | The sum of the atomic masses of all atoms in a formula unit (in u) | 249.5 u for CuSO₄·5H₂O |
| **Efflorescence** | The spontaneous loss of water of crystallisation from a hydrated crystal to dry air, turning it into a white powder | Washing soda crystals crumbling in dry air |
| **Deliquescence** | The property of a solid absorbing so much moisture from humid air that it dissolves in it to form a liquid solution | Anhydrous CaCl₂, NaOH pellets |
| **Hygroscopic** | Able to absorb moisture from the air without dissolving into a liquid solution (or, if already a liquid, diluting itself) | Silica gel beads, quicklime (CaO) |
| **Drying agent (desiccant)** | A hygroscopic substance used to keep air, gases, or closed containers dry | Silica gel packets, anhydrous CaCl₂ |
| **Dehydrating agent** | A substance that chemically strips hydrogen and oxygen (in the ratio of water) out of another compound (covered in higher classes) | Concentrated sulphuric acid |

(Another classic word you will see in chemistry tables is **"vitriol"**—an older historical name for certain hydrated metal sulphates, such as blue vitriol and green vitriol.)

## Part A: Water Locked Inside a Crystal: Water of Crystallisation

### How Can a Crystal Feel Dry Yet Contain Water?

If you look at crystals of blue vitriol (copper sulphate), they look like dry, hard blue gemstones—there is no wetness or liquid clinging to them. Yet if a teacher heats those dry blue crystals in a test tube, liquid water droplets condense on the cool upper walls! Where did that water come from? It was **locked into the crystal grid when the crystal grew from solution**. Recall from Chapter 2 that crystals are grown by cooling a hot concentrated aqueous solution. As the ions stack into their regular 3-D lattice, a fixed number of water molecules slot neatly into specific spaces around the ions and become part of the solid architecture. We call this **water of crystallisation**.

Two key facts to remember:

1. Water of crystallisation is **not** surface wetness like water on a damp sponge. It is a **structural part of the crystal lattice**, which is why its ratio is strictly **fixed** by a chemical formula: in blue vitriol, there are always exactly **5** H₂O molecules for every 1 CuSO₄ unit—never 4 or 6.
2. Water of crystallisation often gives the crystal its **geometric shape and colour**. Blue vitriol owes its brilliant blue colour to those 5 bound water molecules; drive them off by heating, and the blue crystal collapses into a white powder.

```
   Simplified sketch of a hydrated crystal lattice

     [CuSO₄] (H₂O)(H₂O)    [CuSO₄] (H₂O)(H₂O)
             (H₂O)(H₂O)(H₂O)       (H₂O)(H₂O)(H₂O)

     [CuSO₄] (H₂O)(H₂O)    [CuSO₄] (H₂O)(H₂O)
             (H₂O)(H₂O)(H₂O)       (H₂O)(H₂O)(H₂O)

   Every [CuSO₄] unit is accompanied by 5 (H₂O): hence CuSO₄·5H₂O
```

> **Figure 4.1** – A simplified schematic of blue vitriol (CuSO₄·5H₂O): every CuSO₄ formula unit is surrounded by a fixed count of 5 water molecules (`H₂O`) locked into the 3-D crystal lattice.

### How to Read a Hydrate Formula

In a hydrate formula, a **centred dot (·)** separates the salt formula from the water molecules: **CuSO₄·5H₂O**. Read this as "one unit of CuSO₄ combined with 5 molecules of H₂O". The number 5 after the dot multiplies the entire H₂O that follows it. When calculating the **formula unit mass**, you **add** the mass of the 5 water molecules (5 × 18 = 90 u) to the mass of CuSO₄. Never multiply by the dot—the dot means "chemically combined with", not multiplication!

Salts written with `·nH₂O` are **hydrates**; the same salts without any water molecules are **anhydrous**.

| Common Name | Chemical Name | Formula | Colour |
|---|---|---|---|
| Blue vitriol | Copper(II) sulphate pentahydrate | CuSO₄·5H₂O | Bright blue |
| Washing soda crystals | Sodium carbonate decahydrate | Na₂CO₃·10H₂O | Colourless / translucent white |
| Green vitriol | Iron(II) sulphate heptahydrate | FeSO₄·7H₂O | Pale green |
| Epsom salt | Magnesium sulphate heptahydrate | MgSO₄·7H₂O | Colourless / white |
| Gypsum | Calcium sulphate dihydrate | CaSO₄·2H₂O | White |

(Note: In Topic 3, we wrote washing soda simply as Na₂CO₃ in our water-softening equations because the water of crystallisation simply joins the surrounding water once dissolved. Fresh commercial crystals of washing soda have the full formula **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O**.)

## Part B: Formula Unit Mass and Percentage of Water in a Hydrate

Now we apply our percentage composition method from Chapter 4—except instead of finding the percentage of a single element, we find the percentage of **water of crystallisation** in the hydrate.

**Step-by-step method:**

1. Calculate the formula unit mass of the anhydrous salt.
2. Calculate the mass of the water molecules: (number of H₂O) × 18.
3. Add the two together to get the **formula unit mass of the hydrate**.
4. **Percentage of water = (mass of water ÷ formula unit mass of hydrate) × 100.**
5. **Check:** Add the percentage of water and the percentage of anhydrous salt; they must total 100%.

### Example 1. Blue vitriol, CuSO₄·5H₂O

- CuSO₄: Cu 63.5 + S 32 + O (4 × 16 = 64) → 63.5 + 32 + 64 = **159.5**.
- 5H₂O: 5 × 18 = **90**.
- Formula unit mass = 159.5 + 90 = **249.5 u**.
- Water % = (90 ÷ 249.5) × 100 = 36.07 ≈ **36.07%**. Anhydrous salt % = (159.5 ÷ 249.5) × 100 = 63.93%.

Check: 36.07 + 63.93 = 100.00 ✅

### Example 2. Washing soda crystals, Na₂CO₃·10H₂O

- Na₂CO₃: Na (2 × 23 = 46) + C 12 + O (3 × 16 = 48) → 46 + 12 + 48 = **106**.
- 10H₂O: 10 × 18 = **180**.
- Formula unit mass = 106 + 180 = **286 u**.
- Water % = (180 ÷ 286) × 100 = 62.94 ≈ **62.94%**. Anhydrous salt % = (106 ÷ 286) × 100 = 37.06%.

Check: 62.94 + 37.06 = 100.00 ✅

Look at that result: **more than half** (nearly 63%!) of the mass of dry-looking washing soda crystals is actually locked-up water!

### Example 3. Green vitriol, FeSO₄·7H₂O

- FeSO₄: Fe 56 + S 32 + O 64 = **152**.
- 7H₂O: 7 × 18 = **126**.
- Formula unit mass = 152 + 126 = **278 u**.
- Water % = (126 ÷ 278) × 100 = 45.32 ≈ **45.32%**. Anhydrous salt % = (152 ÷ 278) × 100 = 54.68%.

Check: 45.32 + 54.68 = 100.00 ✅

### Example 4. Epsom salt, MgSO₄·7H₂O and Gypsum, CaSO₄·2H₂O

- MgSO₄: 24 + 32 + 64 = 120. 7H₂O = 126. Formula unit mass = 120 + 126 = **246 u**. Water % = (126 ÷ 246) × 100 = 51.22 ≈ **51.22%**.
- CaSO₄: 40 + 32 + 64 = 136. 2H₂O = 36. Formula unit mass = 136 + 36 = **172 u**. Water % = (36 ÷ 172) × 100 = 20.93 ≈ **20.93%**.

Check: For MgSO₄·7H₂O, 51.22 + (120 ÷ 246 × 100 = 48.78) = 100.00 ✅; for CaSO₄·2H₂O, 20.93 + (136 ÷ 172 × 100 = 79.07) = 100.00 ✅

Compare FeSO₄·7H₂O and MgSO₄·7H₂O: both contain 7 H₂O molecules (126 u of water), but because MgSO₄ (120 u) is lighter than FeSO₄ (152 u), Epsom salt has a higher percentage of water by mass (51.22% vs 45.32%). When two hydrates hold the same number of water molecules, the one with the **lighter anhydrous salt** has the higher mass percentage of water.

## Part C: Teacher Demo: Heating Blue Vitriol and Rehydrating It

**Strictly for observation (teacher demonstration only). Students must not handle or heat copper sulphate themselves.** Copper sulphate is harmful if swallowed or inhaled as dust, and heating laboratory chemicals requires safety goggles and proper training.

Here is how Ms. Lin demonstrates this reversible change in the school laboratory:

1. She places a few dry **blue crystals of copper sulphate (CuSO₄·5H₂O)** at the bottom of a clean, **dry** boiling tube while students observe from a safe distance.
2. Holding the tube with a test-tube holder tilted slightly downward (pointed away from everyone), she heats the bottom gently over a burner flame.
3. As the crystals warm up, their bright blue colour fades into a **chalky white powder**, and tiny **droplets of clear liquid** condense near the cool open mouth of the tube. That liquid is the water of crystallisation driven out as steam and condensed on the cool glass.
4. Once the tube cools down, she adds **two or three drops of water** onto the white anhydrous powder. Instantly, the powder turns **bright blue** again, and the bottom of the tube feels noticeably warm!

We write this reversible process as:

> **CuSO₄·5H₂O (blue solid) ⇌ CuSO₄ (white solid) + 5H₂O**
> (forward on gentle heating; reverse on adding water)

```
         Gentle heating: water escapes as steam
       ===========================================================>

    BLUE crystals                                        WHITE powder
    CuSO₄·5H₂O                                               CuSO₄
    (hydrated)                                           (anhydrous)

       <===========================================================
         Add drops of water: water re-binds into crystal structure
                     (releases noticeable warmth)
```

> **Figure 4.2** – The reversible colour cycle of copper(II) sulphate: heating drives off the 5 water molecules, turning blue hydrated crystals into white anhydrous powder; adding drops of water restores the blue hydrated form and releases heat.

### Why This Serves as the Chemical Test for Water

Remember Topic 1? **White anhydrous copper sulphate** turns **blue** on contact with water. If a teacher places a drop of an unknown colourless liquid onto white CuSO₄ and it turns blue, the liquid definitely contains water. Remember, however, that this only proves **water is present**—it does not prove the liquid is pure water (milk, vinegar, or salty water will also turn it blue).

### Example 5. Calculating water lost from experimental demo numbers

Suppose a teacher heats **24.95 g** of blue copper sulphate crystals until all the water of crystallisation is driven off, leaving **15.95 g** of white anhydrous powder after cooling.

- Mass of water lost = 24.95 − 15.95 = **9.00 g**.
- Percentage of water = (9.00 ÷ 24.95) × 100 = **36.07%**—matching our theoretical calculation in Example 1!

Check: Since 24.95 g is exactly 0.1 × 249.5 u, water lost = 0.1 × 90 = **9.00 g** ✅ and remaining CuSO₄ = 0.1 × 159.5 = **15.95 g** ✅

### Example 6. Working backward to find the formula number *x*

Using the same experimental masses, suppose we did not know the number "5" in CuSO₄·*x*H₂O. How could we find *x*?

- Mass of water lost = 9.00 g. Moles of H₂O = 9.00 ÷ 18 = **0.5 mol** (using the Chapter 4 mole formula).
- Mass of anhydrous CuSO₄ left = 15.95 g. Moles of CuSO₄ = 15.95 ÷ 159.5 = **0.1 mol**.
- Ratio (moles of H₂O ÷ moles of salt) = 0.5 ÷ 0.1 = **5**. Thus *x* = 5, giving **CuSO₄·5H₂O** ✅

Check: 5 × 18 = 90 and 159.5 + 90 = 249.5, matching Example 1 ✅

### Example 7. Weighing out hydrated crystals for a recipe

Suppose a laboratory procedure requires **53 g of anhydrous Na₂CO₃**, but the stockroom shelf only has hydrated washing soda crystals, **Na₂CO₃·10H₂O**. What mass of hydrated crystals should the technician weigh out?

- Molar mass of anhydrous Na₂CO₃ = 106 g/mol. So 53 g = 53 ÷ 106 = **0.5 mol**.
- Each mole of Na₂CO₃·10H₂O contains 1 mole of Na₂CO₃, so we need 0.5 mol of the crystals (molar mass = 286 g/mol).
- Mass of crystals needed = 0.5 × 286 = **143 g**.

Check: In 143 g of crystals, water mass = 143 × (180 ÷ 286) = 90 g, leaving 143 − 90 = **53 g** of Na₂CO₃ ✅

## Part D: Efflorescence: Crystals That Lose Water to the Air

Now let's look at how crystals interact with ordinary room air. Air always contains some water vapour, ranging from very dry (desert summer or heated winter rooms) to very humid (rainy season or coastal air).

**Efflorescence** occurs when a hydrated crystal **spontaneously loses part or all of its water of crystallisation to dry air at room temperature**, without any heating needed. Take freshly prepared crystals of **washing soda (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O)**: at first they are clear and glassy. Leave them on an open dish in a dry room for a few days, and they lose their glassy shine, crumbling into a **fluffy white powder**. Because nearly 63% of their mass was water (Example 2), losing that water causes the crystal lattice to collapse into powder. That is why washing soda should be stored in tightly sealed containers.

In short: **Efflorescence is the loss of water of crystallisation from a hydrated salt when exposed to dry air.**

Common efflorescent salts: washing soda crystals (Na₂CO₃·10H₂O) and Glauber's salt (Na₂SO₄·10H₂O).

## Part E: Deliquescence and Hygroscopic Substances: Solids That Pull Water from the Air

Now consider the exact opposite behaviour: substances that **absorb moisture from the air**. They fall into two categories:

### Deliquescent Substances: Absorb So Much Moisture That They Dissolve into a Liquid

Suppose a chemist leaves a few dry white granules of **anhydrous calcium chloride (CaCl₂)** on a watch glass on a humid day. Within a few hours, the granules glisten with dampness, and soon they turn into a **clear liquid puddle**! The solid pulled so much water vapour out of the air that it completely **dissolved in the absorbed water**. This phenomenon is called **deliquescence**. Other classic deliquescent solids include **sodium hydroxide pellets (NaOH)** and **magnesium chloride (MgCl₂)**.

**Safety:** Students must never touch deliquescent laboratory chemicals. Sodium hydroxide is strongly caustic (causes severe chemical burns), and calcium chloride irritates skin and eyes.

### Hygroscopic Substances: Absorb Moisture Without Turning into a Solution

What about the **silica gel** beads inside Alex's shoebox? Silica gel absorbs water vapour from the air, yet the beads **remain solid and dry to the touch**—they never melt into a puddle. Substances that absorb moisture from air without dissolving into a liquid solution are called **hygroscopic**. Other examples include **quicklime (CaO)** (which absorbs moisture and reacts with it; caustic, not handled by students) and **concentrated sulphuric acid** (already a liquid, which absorbs water vapour from the air and increases in volume).

Keep the logical relationship clear: **every deliquescent solid is strongly hygroscopic, but not every hygroscopic substance is deliquescent.** Silica gel is hygroscopic, not deliquescent.

### A Brief Note on Dehydrating Agents: Concentrated Sulphuric Acid

Concentrated sulphuric acid goes one step beyond merely absorbing moisture from the air: it is also a powerful **dehydrating agent**, meaning it can chemically rip hydrogen and oxygen atoms (in a 2 : 1 ratio) right out of another compound's molecules! For example, when poured onto table sugar in a fume-hood demonstration, it strips the water elements out of the sugar molecules, leaving behind a steaming black column of pure carbon. This reaction is **extremely hazardous** and is **never performed as a student activity**; we mention the term here simply so you don't confuse a harmless *drying agent* (like a sealed silica gel packet) with a chemical *dehydrating agent* (which you will study in higher classes).

### Comparing the Three Air-Moisture Behaviours

```
               How does the substance interact with moisture in the air?
                     /                                        \
      ABSORBS water vapour from air               RELEASES water to dry air
      (gains mass in humid air)                   (from a hydrated crystal)
           /                \                            EFFLORESCENT
    HYGROSCOPIC          DELIQUESCENT             (e.g., washing soda crystals:
    (stays solid, or     (absorbs so much          crumbles into white powder)
    dilutes if liquid)   it turns into a liquid)
       silica gel,          anhydrous CaCl₂,
       quicklime,           NaOH pellets, MgCl₂
       conc. H₂SO₄
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Three ways substances interact with atmospheric moisture: efflorescent hydrates release water to dry air; hygroscopic substances absorb moisture without dissolving; deliquescent solids absorb so much moisture that they dissolve into a liquid puddle.

### Practical Uses of Drying Agents (Desiccants)

Hygroscopic substances serve as everyday **drying agents (desiccants)**:

- **Silica gel pouches:** Packed inside shoeboxes, camera bags, electronics boxes, and vitamin bottles to prevent mould, rust, and clumping. They are stamped "DO NOT EAT" so no one mistakes the pouch for a seasoning packet.
- **Anhydrous CaCl₂:** Used in laboratory drying tubes to dry gases and in commercial dehumidifier tubs (handled with proper precautions).
- **Quicklime (CaO):** Used in laboratories to dry certain gases (such as ammonia) that would react with acidic drying agents.

### Why Does Table Salt Clump in Humid Weather? (An Honest Chemistry Answer)

During damp, rainy weather, table salt in a salt shaker often clumps together or turns damp. People often say, "Salt is deliquescent." **The honest chemistry explanation is more interesting**: **chemically pure** NaCl is *not* deliquescent under normal conditions. However, natural sea salt and unrefined table salt contain **tiny trace impurities** of **magnesium chloride (MgCl₂) and calcium chloride (CaCl₂)**—both of which *are* strongly deliquescent! Those trace impurities pull moisture from humid air and coat the salt grains with a microscopic film of brine, sticking them together. (That is why commercial free-flowing table salt often includes a tiny amount of harmless "anti-caking agent".)

That also explains a classic kitchen trick: dropping a few **uncooked grains of rice** into the salt shaker. The dry starch in the rice absorbs moisture before the salt grains stick together, helping the salt pour freely.

## Part F: Home Activity (with an Adult): Weighing a Sealed Silica Gel Packet

**At home with an adult.** Find a **sealed, intact** silica gel packet from a new shoebox or electronics box. **Never tear or open the packet, never put it near your mouth, and keep it completely away from young children and pets.** With an adult's supervision:

1. Weigh the sealed packet on a sensitive digital kitchen or jewellery scale and record its starting mass (for example, 5.0 g).
2. Place the sealed packet on a small saucer in a **humid spot** (such as near a window on a rainy day, or in a steamy bathroom after a shower—without splashing liquid water onto it) for several hours. **Keep the saucer away from all food preparation areas.**
3. Weigh the sealed packet again.

In humid air, you may notice a **slight increase in mass** because the silica gel beads inside absorbed invisible water vapour through the breathable paper pouch! (If the air is dry or your kitchen scale only reads whole grams, the mass change may be too small to register.) **When finished, have an adult dispose of the packet in the household bin and wash your hands.**

## Part G: Classifying Substances by Their Behaviour in Air

Here is a summary table to help you reason through any exam question:

| Substance | What Happens in Open Air | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Washing soda crystals (in dry air) | Loses water of crystallisation; crumbles to white powder | Efflorescent |
| Anhydrous CaCl₂ granules (in humid air) | Absorbs moisture and dissolves into a liquid puddle | Deliquescent |
| NaOH pellets | Rapidly absorbs moisture and dissolves into a liquid | Deliquescent |
| Silica gel beads | Absorbs moisture while remaining dry, solid beads | Hygroscopic |
| Quicklime (CaO) | Absorbs moisture from air | Hygroscopic |
| Concentrated H₂SO₄ | Absorbs moisture; liquid volume increases | Hygroscopic |
| Blue vitriol crystals (in normal room air) | Remains unchanged under ordinary humidity | Stable hydrate |
| Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) | Remains stable in open air | Stable hydrate |

**Reasoning shortcut:** First ask whether water is **leaving** the solid (efflorescent) or **entering** from the air (hygroscopic/deliquescent). If water is entering, ask whether the solid stays solid (hygroscopic) or dissolves into a liquid puddle (deliquescent).

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Treating the dot (·) in a hydrate formula as multiplication**

In CuSO₄·5H₂O, the formula unit mass is 159.5 + (5 × 18) = 249.5 u. Multiplying 159.5 × 90 = 14,355 is completely wrong—the dot means "combined with", so you **add** the masses.

**Mistake 2: Thinking a hydrated crystal is wet to the touch**

Blue vitriol crystals contain 36.07% water by mass, yet they feel completely dry because the water molecules are locked inside the solid crystal lattice.

**Mistake 3: Mixing up efflorescence and deliquescence**

Efflorescence = crystal **loses** water to dry air (crumbles to powder). Deliquescence = solid **absorbs** water from humid air until it dissolves into a liquid solution.

**Mistake 4: Assuming every hygroscopic substance is deliquescent**

Silica gel is hygroscopic (absorbs moisture) but stays solid. Only solids that dissolve into a liquid puddle in humid air are deliquescent.

**Mistake 5: Saying copper sulphate "burns" when heated**

Gentle heating simply drives off the water of crystallisation (dehydration), leaving white anhydrous CuSO₄. Adding water turns it right back into blue CuSO₄·5H₂O.

**Mistake 6: Forgetting the 100% check in hydrate calculations**

Always check that (% water) + (% anhydrous salt) = 100%. If they don't add up to 100%, recheck your formula unit mass addition.

**Mistake 7: Thinking the white copper sulphate test proves a liquid is pure water**

Turning white CuSO₄ blue only proves that **water is present** in the liquid—not that the liquid is pure or safe to drink.

## Quick Recap

- **Water of crystallisation** is a fixed number of H₂O molecules locked inside a salt's crystal lattice (e.g., 5 in CuSO₄·5H₂O). The dot (`·`) means "chemically combined with".
- **Formula unit masses and water percentages:** CuSO₄·5H₂O = 249.5 u (36.07% water), Na₂CO₃·10H₂O = 286 u (62.94% water), FeSO₄·7H₂O = 278 u (45.32% water), MgSO₄·7H₂O = 246 u (51.22% water), CaSO₄·2H₂O = 172 u (20.93% water).
- **Percentage of water** = (number of H₂O × 18 ÷ formula unit mass of hydrate) × 100.
- **Teacher demo:** Heating blue CuSO₄·5H₂O drives off steam and leaves white anhydrous CuSO₄; adding drops of water restores the blue colour and releases warmth. White anhydrous CuSO₄ is a chemical test for the presence of water.
- **Efflorescence:** Hydrated crystals lose water to dry air and crumble into powder (washing soda). **Deliquescence:** Solids absorb so much moisture from humid air that they dissolve into a liquid solution (anhydrous CaCl₂, NaOH, MgCl₂). **Hygroscopic:** Absorbs moisture without dissolving into a solution (silica gel, quicklime, conc. H₂SO₄).
- **Drying agents (desiccants)** like silica gel packets keep enclosed spaces dry.
- Table salt clumps in humid weather mainly because of trace deliquescent impurities such as MgCl₂ and CaCl₂.
- Safety: students must never touch or taste laboratory chemicals like CuSO₄, NaOH, CaO, or conc. H₂SO₄, and must never open silica gel packets.

8. In a school lab a teacher shows white anhydrous copper sulphate turning blue after a few drops of a clear liquid are added. What can you conclude, and what can you not conclude, about the liquid?

9. Why does a family sometimes put a few grains of rice in the salt shaker during the monsoon? Give a correct, honest explanation of why salt cakes at all.

10. State True or False, and correct the false ones:
    (a) The dot in CuSO₄·5H₂O means multiplication.
    (b) Silica gel is deliquescent because it keeps absorbing moisture.
    (c) Heating blue copper sulphate crystals is a safe activity for students to do alone at home.
    (d) A deliquescent substance can turn into a solution on its own in humid air.

### Answers

1. Na₂SO₄: 2 × 23 = 46, S = 32, O = 4 × 16 = 64; sum = 142. 10H₂O = 10 × 18 = 180. Formula unit mass = 142 + 180 = **322 u**. Water % = (180 ÷ 322) × 100 = 55.90 ≈ **55.90%**. Salt % = (142 ÷ 322) × 100 = 44.10%. Check: 55.90 + 44.10 = 100.00 ✅

2. 64.4 g is 0.2 times 322 u (64.4 ÷ 322 = 0.2). Water lost = 0.2 × 180 = **36.0 g**. Anhydrous Na₂SO₄ left = 0.2 × 142 = **28.4 g**. Check: 36.0 + 28.4 = 64.4 ✅

3. Water lost = 1.08 g; moles of H₂O = 1.08 ÷ 18 = 0.06 mol. Anhydrous salt left = 2.19 − 1.08 = 1.11 g of CaCl₂. Molar mass of CaCl₂ = 40 + (2 × 35.5) = 111 g/mol, so moles of CaCl₂ = 1.11 ÷ 111 = 0.01 mol. Ratio (moles H₂O ÷ moles salt) = 0.06 ÷ 0.01 = **6**. Thus *x* = 6, giving CaCl₂·6H₂O. Check: 111 + (6 × 18) = 219 u, and 2.19 g is 0.01 mol ✅

4. CaCl₂·2H₂O: 111 + 36 = 147 u; water % = (36 ÷ 147) × 100 = **24.49%**. CaCl₂·6H₂O: 111 + 108 = 219 u; water % = (108 ÷ 219) × 100 = **49.32%**. **CaCl₂·6H₂O** has the larger percentage of water because the anhydrous salt mass is the same (111 u) while it holds 6 water molecules per unit instead of 2. Check: 24.49 + (111 ÷ 147 × 100 = 75.51) = 100.00 ✅

5. (i) The water in blue vitriol is chemically bound in the crystal lattice in a strict fixed ratio (5 H₂O per CuSO₄), whereas a wet cloth can hold any variable amount of water. (ii) Blue vitriol crystals look and feel dry to the touch; driving off their water requires heating and changes the crystal's blue colour to white powder, whereas a wet cloth feels damp and dries by simple wringing or air-drying without changing the cloth's chemical nature.

6. **Hygroscopic**: it absorbs moisture from the air inside the camera bag while remaining a dry solid, protecting the camera lens from dampness and fungus. It is labelled "DO NOT EAT" because it is a non-food desiccant that could be harmful or a choking hazard if swallowed.

7. Washing soda crystals in dry air lose their water of crystallisation and crumble into a white powder; this property is **efflorescence**. Anhydrous CaCl₂ granules in humid air absorb so much moisture that they dissolve into a liquid puddle; this property is **deliquescence**.

8. You can conclude that the liquid **contains water** (since white anhydrous CuSO₄ turns blue on contact with water). You **cannot** conclude that the liquid is **pure water** (or safe to drink), because any aqueous solution or water-containing mixture will also turn it blue.

9. Dry rice grains absorb moisture from the air inside the shaker before the salt grains stick together, keeping the salt free-flowing. Table salt clumps in humid weather because it contains tiny trace amounts of deliquescent impurities like MgCl₂ and CaCl₂ that pull moisture from the air; pure NaCl itself is not deliquescent.

10. (a) **False** ❌. The dot means "chemically combined with" (so masses are added, not multiplied). (b) **False** ❌. Silica gel is **hygroscopic** (it stays solid); a deliquescent substance dissolves into a liquid solution. (c) **False** ❌. Copper sulphate is harmful and heating it is strictly a supervised teacher demonstration. (d) **True** ✅. A deliquescent solid absorbs enough moisture from humid air to dissolve into a solution on its own.
