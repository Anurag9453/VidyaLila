# Topic 5: The Journey to Clean Water: Purification

In the previous topic, we saw that water molecules can hide inside dry crystals (water of crystallisation) and that desiccants pull moisture out of the air. In Topics 2 and 3, we also saw how easily water dissolves salts and minerals. Now let's ask a practical question that affects every single one of us: how does muddy water drawn from a river, lake, or reservoir get transformed into **clean, safe drinking water** flowing from our household taps? Today we will tour a municipal water treatment plant step by step, compare household purification methods, and build a layered bottle filter (with an adult) to see why "looking clear" and "being safe to drink" are two very different things.

---

## Brown River Water at the Treatment Plant Gate

Sofia's Class 9 science group was visiting the city's municipal water treatment plant with their teacher, Mr. Cole. Near the intake gate, a wide canal carried murky, brownish river water into the facility, with fallen leaves bobbing on the surface. Sofia wrinkled her nose. "Mr. Cole, this water looks like chocolate milk! People wouldn't even wash their hands in this—and this is what ends up in our kitchen taps?"

Mr. Cole smiled. "Walk with me to the end of the plant, and then decide." As they walked past a series of huge concrete basins, the water looked clearer at every stage, until at the final filtration gallery the plant engineer held up a glass of water that sparkled like crystal. "Look at this," the engineer said to Sofia. "Completely transparent. Now tell me: is this glass of water **safe to drink** right now?"

Sofia nodded immediately. "It's crystal clear, so it must be safe!" The engineer shook her head gently. "That's the most dangerous myth about water. Our real test happens in the **microbiology lab**—because the things that make water deadly aren't the mud particles you can see with your eyes, but the **microscopic germs you can't see at all**."

In this topic, we will see what each stage of the treatment plant removes, and why visual clarity alone never guarantees safety.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Water cycle** | The continuous natural circulation of water through evaporation, condensation into clouds, and precipitation as rain or snow | Ocean water evaporating, forming clouds, and falling as rain |
| **Raw water** | Untreated natural water drawn from a river, lake, canal, or reservoir | The murky brown water entering a treatment plant |
| **Screening** | Passing raw water through coarse metal grilles to trap large floating debris (twigs, leaves, plastic) | Intake bar screens at the plant gate |
| **Coagulation** | Adding a chemical (such as alum) to make tiny suspended particles clump together into larger, heavier clusters | Adding alum to muddy water |
| **Floc** | The fluffy, gelatinous clumps formed when fine dirt particles stick together during coagulation | Pale brownish-white clumps in the mixing basin |
| **Sedimentation** | Allowing heavy particles and floc to settle to the bottom of a quiet tank under gravity | Settling basins at a water plant |
| **Sand filtration** | Passing water downward through beds of sand and gravel to trap remaining fine particles | Rapid gravity sand filters |
| **Chlorination** | Adding a carefully controlled tiny dose of chlorine to kill disease-causing germs | The disinfection stage before storage |
| **Germs (pathogens)** | Microscopic organisms (bacteria, viruses, protozoa) invisible to the naked eye that cause disease | *Vibrio cholerae* bacteria |
| **Waterborne disease** | An illness spread through contaminated water | Cholera, typhoid, dysentery, hepatitis A (jaundice) |
| **Potable water** | Water that is clean, disinfected, and safe for human drinking | Treated municipal tap water |

## Part A: The Water Cycle: Nature's Own Distillation

Before tracing water through the treatment plant, let's ask: **where does our fresh water come from in the first place?** In Chapter 1, we studied evaporation and condensation. Those two physical changes power the planet's **water cycle**. Heat from the sun causes water from oceans, lakes, and rivers to **evaporate** into invisible water vapour. As warm, moist air rises into the cooler upper atmosphere, the water vapour **condenses** into tiny droplets that form **clouds**. When those droplets grow heavy enough, they fall back to Earth as **rain** (or snow). That water feeds rivers and lakes and seeps underground to recharge aquifers and wells—before evaporating all over again.

```
             SUNLIGHT
                \  |  /
               ---  O  ---
                /  |  \

         ,--~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~--.
        (   CLOUDS (condense)  )        <- rising vapour cools into droplets
         `--~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~--'
            ^  ^  ^        | | |
            |  |  |        v v v
    evaporation (water      RAIN & SNOW
    vapour rises)           (precipitation)
            |  |  |        | | |
     ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ v v v ~~~~~~~~~
     ~~~~ OCEAN / LAKES ~~~~~       /\    /\
     ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~    /  \  /  \   <- mountains
     ~~~~~~~~ rivers flow ~~~~~~~~/    \/    \
     ~~~~~~~ back toward the ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
     ~~~~~~~~~~~~ ocean ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
             underground: aquifers and well water
```

> **Figure 5.1** – The water cycle: solar heat drives evaporation from oceans and lakes; rising vapour cools and condenses into clouds; rain and snow replenish rivers, lakes, and underground aquifers in a continuous loop.

Notice a neat chemistry connection: natural evaporation acts like **giant solar distillation** (recall Chapter 2). When salty ocean water evaporates, all the dissolved salt stays behind in the sea, so the water vapour that condenses into clouds and falls as rain is nearly **salt-free fresh water**. However, as rainwater falls through dusty air and flows across soil, farmlands, and towns, it picks up mud, decaying leaves, dissolved minerals, sewage, and microscopic germs. That is why raw river water must be purified before anyone can drink it.

## Part B: Inside a Municipal Water Treatment Plant: Every Step and Its "Why"

To turn murky raw water into safe potable water, a city treatment plant uses a sequence of stages. **Each stage targets a specific type of impurity** and prepares the water for the next stage.

```
    RAW WATER FROM RIVER / CANAL (murky, mud, leaves, germs)
                          |
                          v
           +-----------------------------+
           |  1. SCREENING               |   large debris (branches, plastic, leaves)
           |     (coarse metal grilles)  |   stopped by bar screens
           +-----------------------------+
                          |
                          v
           +-----------------------------+
           |  2. COAGULATION             |   alum is added and gently stirred;
           |     (alum + slow mixing)    |   tiny clay particles clump into "floc"
           +-----------------------------+
                          |
                          v
           +-----------------------------+
           |  3. SEDIMENTATION           |   heavy floc settles to the bottom
           |     (large quiet basin)     |   of the settling tank as sludge
           +-----------------------------+
                          |
                          v
           +-----------------------------+
           |  4. SAND FILTRATION         |   remaining fine floc and tiny silt
           |     (sand & gravel beds)    |   are trapped in the sand layers
           +-----------------------------+
                          |
                          v
           +-----------------------------+
           |  5. CHLORINATION            |   a tiny measured dose of chlorine
           |     (disinfection)          |   kills bacteria and viruses
           +-----------------------------+
                          |
                          v
           +-----------------------------+
           |  6. STORAGE AND SUPPLY      |   water towers / reservoirs and
           |     (pipes to homes)        |   sealed mains deliver water to taps
           +-----------------------------+
                          |
                          v
               SAFE POTABLE WATER AT THE TAP
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Flow chart of a municipal water treatment plant: raw water passes through screening, coagulation, sedimentation, sand filtration, and chlorination before entering storage reservoirs and distribution pipes.

### Step 1: Screening (Trapping Large Debris)

At the river intake, raw water flows through coarse metal bar screens or mesh grilles. **Large floating objects**—tree branches, plastic bottles, wrappers, dead leaves, and weeds—are held back. This is the exact same principle as **sieving** from Chapter 2: particles larger than the openings cannot pass. Without screening, large debris would jam and damage the plant's pumps and pipes.

### Step 2: Coagulation: Why Add Alum?

Even after screening, the water looks brown and cloudy because it contains **microscopic suspended clay and silt particles**. Many of these particles are so tiny that they **refuse to settle on their own** (recall colloids and the Tyndall effect from Chapter 2, Topic 3: ultra-fine particles stay suspended indefinitely). If you sent this cloudy water straight into a sand filter, it would clog the sand bed almost immediately.

To solve this, engineers add a coagulant—traditionally **alum**. When alum dissolves in water, it forms **sticky, jelly-like microscopic clusters called floc**. As the water is stirred gently, the tiny suspended clay and dirt particles **stick to the floc**, growing into larger, heavier clumps that can settle rapidly under gravity.

In many regions, stirring a crystal of **alum (potash alum)** in a bucket of muddy water is a traditional household method to make mud settle quickly. However, remember: settling the mud does **not** kill germs!

**Link to Topic 4:** Traditional potash alum, **KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O**, is itself a classic **hydrated salt** containing 12 molecules of water of crystallisation per formula unit! (Modern treatment plants often use its close relative, aluminium sulphate.)

### Step 3: Sedimentation (Settling Out the Floc)

From the coagulation mixer, the water flows into a **large, quiet sedimentation basin** where it moves very slowly. Here, the heavy clumps of floc sink to the bottom under gravity, leaving much clearer water above. The thick mud collected at the bottom (**sludge**) is scraped out periodically. This is simply **sedimentation and decantation** from Chapter 2 operating on a massive scale. How long the water remains in the basin (the retention time) is carefully calculated, as we will see in Part D.

### Step 4: Sand Filtration (Trapping Fine Particles)

Even after sedimentation, a few light wisps of floc and fine silt particles remain suspended. The water now flows downward through deep **filter beds** made of layers of **fine sand** resting on **coarse sand and gravel**. The tiny gaps between the sand grains trap the remaining fine particles—just like filter paper in Chapter 2, but built to handle millions of litres a day. When the top of the sand bed eventually gets dirty, engineers clean it by pumping clean water backward up through the bed (backwashing).

Water leaving the sand filters looks **sparkling clear**. But as the engineer reminded Sofia, is it safe to drink yet? No—**microscopic germs are still present!**

### Step 5: Chlorination (Killing Invisible Germs)

Disease-causing **germs** (bacteria and viruses) are so microscopic that **they cannot be seen with the naked eye and slip right through the gaps between sand grains**. To destroy them, the plant adds a **carefully measured, tiny dose of chlorine** (disinfection). The dose is strictly controlled by lab technicians: high enough to kill harmful bacteria and viruses, yet low enough to be completely safe for human consumption. A tiny residual trace of chlorine stays in the water as it travels through city pipes to protect it against re-contamination along the way.

**Critical safety note:** Disinfection chemicals (chlorine gas, calcium hypochlorite / bleaching powder, chlorine tablets, and household liquid bleach) are **hazardous substances**. At treatment plants, they are handled strictly by **trained professionals**. Students must **never touch, taste, or add bleach or chlorine chemicals to water on their own.** And at home, household bleach must never be mixed with other cleaners (especially acidic toilet cleaners or vinegar, which releases deadly chlorine gas).

### Step 6: Storage and Distribution

Finally, the disinfected potable water is pumped into covered **underground reservoirs** and **elevated water towers**, from which sealed distribution pipes carry it to homes and schools. Because old, cracked pipes can sometimes allow dirty groundwater to seep in during floods or repairs, keeping household roof tanks clean—and using an extra safeguard like boiling or a certified purifier when advised—is always smart practice.

> **Check:** Water leaving the sedimentation tank before sand filtration still looks slightly hazy. Is it safe to drink? ❌ No—it still needs sand filtration to remove fine suspended particles and chlorination to kill invisible germs.

## Part C: Looking Clear vs Being Safe: Two Completely Different Things

Here is the single most important takeaway of this entire topic:

> **Clear water does NOT automatically mean safe (potable) water.**

- A glass of **crystal-clear, sparkling** water from an untreated well or stream can contain millions of invisible disease-causing bacteria and viruses, with no smell or bad taste at all.
- Conversely, water that looks slightly cloudy with harmless mineral silt might be germ-free after boiling (though we filter it first so it is pleasant to drink).
- **Dissolved** substances—including dissolved salts and toxic industrial chemicals—are also completely invisible and pass straight through cloth, filter paper, and sand filters (recall Chapter 2: true solutions pass through filters unchanged).

### Common Waterborne Diseases

When people drink water contaminated with **pathogenic germs** (bacteria, viruses, or parasites) or use it to wash raw food, **waterborne diseases** can spread rapidly. Here are four major examples:

| Disease | Typical Effects on the Body | Prevention |
|---|---|---|
| **Cholera** | Severe watery diarrhoea and vomiting, leading to rapid, life-threatening dehydration | Drink boiled or properly treated water; give ORS immediately and seek urgent medical care |
| **Typhoid** | Prolonged high fever, weakness, stomach pain, and headache | Safe drinking water, hygienic food preparation, thorough handwashing |
| **Dysentery** | Severe abdominal cramps and frequent diarrhoea (often with mucus or blood) | Safe water, handwashing with soap, keeping food covered from flies |
| **Hepatitis A (Jaundice)** | Yellowing of the eyes and skin, dark urine, extreme fatigue, nausea | Safe water and food, proper sanitation and hand hygiene |

This table is for **scientific awareness only**—never attempt to self-treat serious illness at home; always consult a **qualified doctor**. The best everyday defence is simple: **drink safe water, wash hands with soap, and eat hygienically prepared food.**

During heavy seasonal rains and floods, waterborne outbreaks spike because floodwaters wash sewage into rivers, wells, and damaged water pipes. That is why health authorities issue "boil-water advisories" after floods.

## Part D: Treatment Plant Calculations (Solved Examples)

Running a water treatment plant involves plenty of everyday math. **All numbers below are simplified, rounded figures** designed for calculation practice.

**Example 1 (Retention time in a settling basin):** A sedimentation basin has a volume of **1800 m³**, and raw water flows into it at **600 m³ per hour**. How many hours does the water stay in the basin for floc to settle?

- Retention time = volume ÷ flow rate = 1800 m³ ÷ 600 m³/h = **3 hours**

**Check:** 600 m³/h × 3 h = 1800 m³ ✅. Notice what happens if operators double the flow rate to 1200 m³/h: the retention time drops in half to 1.5 hours, giving floc less time to settle!

**Example 2 (Calculating alum dosage):** A small town plant adds alum at a dose of **25 mg per litre** of raw water. Today the plant needs to treat **400,000 L** of water. How many kilograms of alum are required?

- Total alum = 25 mg/L × 400,000 L = 10,000,000 mg
- Since 1 kg = 1000 g = 1,000,000 mg: 10,000,000 mg ÷ 1,000,000 = **10 kg**

**Check:** 10 kg = 10,000,000 mg ✅ and 10,000,000 ÷ 400,000 = 25 mg/L ✅.

**Example 3 (Chlorine dosage—plant scale):** A municipal plant doses chlorine at **3 mg per litre** of filtered water. How much chlorine is needed to disinfect **500,000 L** of water?

- Chlorine needed = 3 mg/L × 500,000 L = 1,500,000 mg = **1500 g = 1.5 kg**

**Check:** 1500 g ÷ 500,000 L = 0.003 g/L = 3 mg/L ✅. Notice how tiny that dose is: just 1.5 kg disinfects half a million litres of water!

**Example 4 (Water loss during plant operation):** In one day, a treatment plant takes in **1,200,000 L** of raw river water and delivers **1,140,000 L** of treated water (the rest is used for removing sludge and backwashing sand filters). How much water was used in processing, and what percentage of the intake is that?

- Water used/lost = 1,200,000 − 1,140,000 = **60,000 L**
- Percentage = (60,000 ÷ 1,200,000) × 100 = **5%**

**Check:** 5% of 1,200,000 = 0.05 × 1,200,000 = 60,000 ✅.

**Example 5 (Why cloth cannot stop bacteria):** The gaps between threads in a folded cotton cloth are roughly **100 µm** (0.1 mm) wide, whereas a typical bacterium is about **2 µm** long (1 µm = one-thousandth of a millimetre; approximate values). How many times smaller is the bacterium than the cloth pore, and will the cloth stop it?

- Ratio = 100 µm ÷ 2 µm = **50 times smaller**
- Because the bacterium is 50 times smaller than the hole, it **passes straight through**. Straining water through cloth only removes coarse dirt and insects, never bacteria.

**Check:** 2 × 50 = 100 ✅.

**Example 6 (Energy and time to boil water):** An adult heats 2 L of water (mass = 2 kg) from 30 °C to 100 °C in a 2000 W electric kettle. Using the Physics heat formula Q = m c ΔT (with c = 4200 J/kg °C) and assuming no heat loss, how long does it take to reach boiling?

- Q = 2 kg × 4200 J/kg °C × (100 − 30) °C = 2 × 4200 × 70 = **588,000 J**
- Time = Q ÷ P = 588,000 J ÷ 2000 J/s = **294 s** = 294 ÷ 60 = **4.9 minutes**
- Once it reaches 100 °C, keep the water at a **rolling boil** (vigorous bubbling) for **at least 1 minute** to kill germs (at high mountain altitudes where water boils at a lower temperature, health guidelines recommend boiling for about 3 minutes).

**Check:** 2000 × 294 = 588,000 ✅. Remember: boiling water must **only be handled by an adult**.

**Example 7 (Potash alum as a hydrate—linking to Topic 4):** Calculate the formula unit mass and percentage of water of crystallisation in potash alum, KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O. (Use K = 39, Al = 27, S = 32, O = 16, H = 1; H₂O = 18.)

- KAl(SO₄)₂: 39 + 27 + 2 × (32 + 4 × 16) = 39 + 27 + 2 × 96 = 39 + 27 + 192 = **258 u**
- 12H₂O: 12 × 18 = **216 u**
- Total formula unit mass = 258 + 216 = **474 u**
- % water = (216 ÷ 474) × 100 = **45.57%** (approx.)

**Check:** 258 + 216 = 474 ✅ and 216 ÷ 474 = 0.4557 ✅. Nearly 45.6% of the mass of an alum crystal is locked-up water of crystallisation!

## Part E: Household Purification Methods and Their Limits

Not every home is connected to a modern city plant, and sometimes pipes need an extra safety barrier at home. Here is how common household methods compare—and what each one **cannot** do:

| Household Method | What It Does | Limitation (What It Does NOT Do) |
|---|---|---|
| **Straining through cloth or mesh** | Removes coarse dirt, leaves, and visible particles | Does **not** remove fine clay, germs, or dissolved salts/chemicals |
| **Ceramic candle filter** | Fine pores block suspended silt and many bacteria | Must be scrubbed clean regularly or biofilm grows on it; does **not** remove dissolved salts or all viruses |
| **Boiling** | Keeping water at a rolling boil for ~1 minute **kills bacteria, viruses, and parasites** | Does **not** remove mud, dissolved salts, or heavy metals; causes temporary hard water to deposit limescale (Topic 3); requires fuel/electricity and adult handling |
| **UV (ultraviolet) purifier** | UV light destroys the DNA of bacteria and viruses | Water must be pre-filtered and clear first (suspended dirt shields germs from the UV beam); does **not** remove dissolved salts; requires electricity |
| **RO (reverse osmosis) purifier** | Forces water through an ultra-fine semi-permeable membrane that blocks dissolved salts as well as germs | Rejects a portion of the water as wastewater; requires maintenance (you will study osmosis and RO in higher classes) |

Notice why many modern home purifiers **combine several stages** inside one cabinet (for example: sediment pre-filter → activated carbon → RO or UV): just like a city plant, each stage handles a different problem.

**Key rule:** No single simple filter does everything. Always match the purification method to the actual hazard in the water!

## Part F: Hands-On Activity: Building a Layered Bottle Filter (with an Adult)

Now let's build a tabletop model of a treatment plant's sand filter: a **layered bottle filter**. **Read this warning carefully first: this activity demonstrates mechanical filtration only. The water that drips out at the bottom looks clear, but it is NOT SAFE TO DRINK because it still contains microscopic germs and dissolved substances. Never drink or taste the filtered water.**

**Perform this activity at home with an adult.** **Only an adult should cut the plastic bottle.**

**Materials:** 1 clean 1.5 L or 2 L clear plastic bottle, clean washed pebbles, coarse sand (washed), fine sand (washed), **activated charcoal granules** (from an aquarium store or pharmacy, rinsed beforehand—do not use barbecue briquettes or wood ash), a plug of clean cotton wool, a clear collecting glass, and a jug of "muddy water" made by stirring **one spoonful of garden soil into clean tap water** (never use pond water, gutter water, or sewage!).

**Steps (with an adult):**

1. Have an adult **cut off the bottom third** of the plastic bottle and **remove the cap**.
2. Turn the top two-thirds of the bottle upside down like a funnel so the **neck points downward** into the collecting glass.
3. Layer the materials from the bottom neck upward, pressing each layer gently: first a plug of **cotton wool** in the neck, then **activated charcoal**, then **fine sand**, then **coarse sand**, and finally **small pebbles** on top.
4. Flush the filter once with clean tap water to rinse the layers, and discard that first rinse water.
5. Now slowly pour your **muddy soil-water** onto the top pebble layer and observe the water dripping into the glass below.

```
          Muddy water (soil + tap water) poured slowly on top
                    \   |   /
                     v  v  v
           +-----------------------+
           |  o  O  o  O  o  O  o  |   <- 1. Small pebbles
           |                       |      (traps coarse twigs and clumps)
           |  . : . : . : . : . :  |   <- 2. Coarse sand
           |                       |
           |  . . . . . . . . . .  |   <- 3. Fine sand
           |                       |      (traps fine silt particles)
           |  # # # # # # # # # #  |   <- 4. Activated charcoal
            \  # # # # # # # # #  /       (adsorbs some odour and colour)
             \ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ /    <- 5. Cotton wool plug
              \                 /          (keeps sand grains from falling out)
                `--.        .--'
                    |  |             <- Bottle neck (cap removed)
                    v  v
               +-----------+
               |   ~ ~ ~   |         <- Collecting glass below:
               |           |            looks clear, but NOT SAFE TO DRINK!
               +-----------+
```

> **Figure 5.3** – A homemade layered bottle filter (top to bottom): small pebbles, coarse sand, fine sand, activated charcoal, and cotton wool inside an inverted bottle neck. The collected water looks much clearer but is not potable.

**What you will observe:**

- The muddy brown water enters at the top, and nearly **clear** water drips into the glass below. (If the first few drops look slightly grey from charcoal dust, pour them back through a second time.)
- Each layer plays a role: pebbles and coarse sand stop larger soil crumbs, fine sand mimics the plant's **sand filtration bed**, activated charcoal helps reduce colour and odour, and the cotton wool holds the sand bed in place.

**A bonus science test (the Tyndall effect from Chapter 2):** Darken the room slightly and shine a narrow **flashlight beam** sideways through the glass of filtered water. If you can see a glowing track where the light beam passes through, microscopic colloidal clay particles are still suspended in the water!

**What the bottle filter CANNOT remove:** bacteria, viruses, dissolved salts, and dissolved toxic chemicals. **Never drink this water**—pour it onto outdoor garden plants when finished, dispose of the used sand and cotton, and wash your hands thoroughly with soap.

## Part G: Choosing the Right Method for Each Situation

Both in engineering and in everyday life, smart water treatment means **identifying the specific problem first and matching the right method to it**:

| Situation | Main Hazard / Problem | Best Method | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cloudy tap water after heavy floods, needed for drinking | Suspended mud + disease germs | Settle/filter first, then bring to a rolling boil | Filtration removes the mud; boiling kills the germs |
| Crystal-clear well water near a leaking septic tank | Invisible pathogenic germs | Boil (or use a tested disinfection system) | Clear appearance gives zero protection against invisible bacteria |
| Brackish borewell water with high dissolved salt | Dissolved ionic salts | RO purifier or distillation | Ordinary filters and boiling cannot remove dissolved NaCl (Chapter 2) |
| Hiking in the mountains with a clear stream and a camp stove | Possible germs and parasites | Filter through clean cloth, then rolling boil for ~3 minutes and cool | Boiling kills pathogens without needing electricity |
| Slightly muddy water that won't settle on its own | Fine colloidal clay + germs | Swirl with alum, let settle, decant/filter, then boil | Alum coagulates fine clay into heavy floc; boiling kills germs |

**Check (spotting a flawed argument):** "We passed the river water through sand and activated charcoal until it looked crystal clear, so now we can drink it safely." ❌ Wrong! Neither sand nor charcoal reliably kills bacteria and viruses; disinfection (such as boiling or chlorination) is essential.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "If water looks crystal clear, it is safe to drink"**

False. Deadly bacteria and viruses are microscopic and completely invisible. That is why treatment plants disinfect water and test it in laboratories.

**Mistake 2: "Water from a homemade sand-and-charcoal bottle filter is safe to drink"**

False. A bottle filter only traps suspended dirt and reduces some odour; it does not kill germs or remove dissolved toxins. Never drink water from a DIY bottle filter.

**Mistake 3: "Boiling water removes all dissolved salts and chemical pollutants"**

False. Boiling kills living germs, but non-volatile dissolved salts and heavy metals stay right in the pot (and actually become slightly more concentrated as steam escapes).

**Mistake 4: "Adding alum purifies water completely, including killing germs"**

No. Alum is a coagulant—it only clumps fine suspended dirt so it settles faster. Killing germs requires boiling, chlorination, or UV treatment.

**Mistake 5: "Students can disinfect water at home by adding household bleach"**

Never! Chlorine and bleach chemicals are hazardous, and improper dosing or mixing with other cleaners is dangerous. Only trained adults or water-plant professionals handle chemical disinfection.

**Mistake 6: "One single filtration step removes every kind of impurity"**

No. Every method has a specific job: screens trap coarse debris, alum + sand filters remove fine particles, boiling/chlorine/UV kill germs, and distillation/RO remove dissolved salts.

## Quick Recap

- **Water cycle:** Evaporation (natural solar distillation), condensation into clouds, and rain continuously circulate Earth's water; surface runoff picks up mud and germs along the way.
- **Municipal treatment plant stages (in order):** Screening → Coagulation (alum) → Sedimentation → Sand filtration → Chlorination → Storage and supply.
- **Alum** clumps tiny suspended clay particles into heavy **floc** that settles rapidly. Potash alum, **KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O**, is a hydrated salt (formula unit mass 474 u, 45.57% water).
- **Chlorination** uses a tiny, carefully controlled dose of chlorine to kill invisible germs (handled strictly by trained professionals; students must never handle or mix chlorine/bleach chemicals).
- **Clear water ≠ safe water:** microscopic germs and dissolved chemicals are invisible to the naked eye.
- **Waterborne diseases:** Cholera, typhoid, dysentery, and hepatitis A (jaundice). Prevented by safe drinking water, handwashing with soap, and clean food.
- **Household methods:** Cloth straining (coarse dirt only), ceramic candle filter, **boiling** (rolling boil for ~1 minute, by an adult), UV purifier, and RO purifier. Each has specific limits.
- **Layered bottle filter (with an adult):** Top-to-bottom order is pebbles → coarse sand → fine sand → activated charcoal → cotton wool. Produces clear-looking water that is **not safe to drink**.

8. A filter paper (or fine cloth) has pores of about 10 µm. A bacterium is about 0.5 µm. How many times smaller is the bacterium than the pore? Will it be stopped? What does this tell you about "clear water"?

9. An adult heats 3 L of water (3 kg) from 25 °C to 100 °C in a 1500 W kettle. How much heat is needed (c = 4200 J/kg °C), and how long will it take if there is no heat loss? What else must be done for safety after reaching 100 °C?

10. A student built a bottle filter with pebbles, coarse sand, fine sand, charcoal and cotton. In which order, from top to bottom, should these layers be placed? The water coming out looks clear. Can it be drunk? Give two reasons.

11. In a bottle-filter test, 450 mL of muddy water took 6 minutes to pass through. At the same rate, how long will 1.2 L take?

12. For each situation, pick the best method and give one reason: (a) a trekker has only a stream of cloudy water and a stove, (b) a house has hard water that leaves scale in the kettle, (c) a family's water looks muddy after floods.

13. State True or False, and correct the false ones:
    (a) Boiling for about a minute kills most germs in water.
    (b) A candle filter removes all dissolved salts.
    (c) Potash alum is a hydrate.
    (d) A bottle filter made at home gives drinking water.

### Answers

1. Heat from the sun causes water from oceans and rivers to **evaporate** into water vapour, which rises, cools, and **condenses** into clouds before falling back as **rain**. Because evaporation leaves dissolved salts behind (like natural distillation), rainwater starts out much cleaner than river water; however, once it hits the ground it picks up soil, dirt, and germs, making runoff unsafe to drink directly.

2. Correct order: **screening** (blocks large debris like twigs and plastic) → **coagulation** (alum clumps fine particles into floc) → **sedimentation** (heavy floc settles to the bottom of a quiet basin) → **sand filtration** (sand and gravel beds trap remaining fine particles) → **chlorination** (tiny dose of chlorine kills bacteria and viruses) → **storage and supply** (reservoirs and sealed pipes deliver safe water to homes).

3. Alum caused the fine suspended clay particles to clump into heavy floc and settle to the bottom, making the water look clear. However, alum **does not kill germs**, and germs are microscopic and invisible—so the clear water can still cause serious illness unless it is disinfected (by boiling or chlorination).

4. Retention time = 1500 ÷ 250 = **6 hours**. If the flow rate doubles to 500 m³/h, the time drops to 1500 ÷ 500 = **3 hours** (half as long). With less time in the tank, the floc has less chance to settle completely, leaving the water cloudier.
   Check: 250 × 6 = 1500 ✅ and 500 × 3 = 1500 ✅

5. Alum needed = 30 mg/L × 250,000 L = 7,500,000 mg. Dividing by 1,000,000 mg/kg gives **7.5 kg**.
   Check: 7.5 kg = 7,500,000 mg, and 7,500,000 ÷ 250,000 = 30 mg/L ✅

6. Chlorine needed = 2 mg/L × 800,000 L = 1,600,000 mg = 1600 g = **1.6 kg**. Students must never handle or mix chlorine/bleach chemicals because they are corrosive to eyes, skin, and lungs and can release deadly chlorine gas if mixed with acidic household cleaners.
   Check: 1600 g ÷ 800,000 L = 0.002 g/L = 2 mg/L ✅

7. Water lost = 800,000 − 752,000 = **48,000 L**. Percentage lost = (48,000 ÷ 800,000) × 100 = **6%**.
   Check: 6% of 800,000 = 0.06 × 800,000 = 48,000 ✅

8. Ratio = 10 ÷ 0.5 = **20 times smaller**. Because the bacterium is 20 times smaller than the pore, it **will not be stopped** and passes right through. This shows that water can look completely free of visible particles and still contain millions of germs.
   Check: 0.5 × 20 = 10 ✅

9. Q = m c ΔT = 3 × 4200 × (100 − 25) = 3 × 4200 × 75 = **945,000 J**. Time = 945,000 ÷ 1500 = **630 s = 10.5 minutes**. For safety, keep the water at a rolling boil for **about 1 minute** after reaching 100 °C (or ~3 minutes at high mountain altitudes), and ensure an adult handles the hot kettle.
   Check: 1500 × 630 = 945,000 ✅ and 630 ÷ 60 = 10.5 ✅

10. Top to bottom: **pebbles → coarse sand → fine sand → activated charcoal → cotton wool**. The water coming out **cannot be drunk** because (1) microscopic germs slip right through the sand and charcoal pores, and (2) dissolved chemicals and salts are not removed.

11. Filtration rate = 450 ÷ 6 = 75 mL/min. Since 1.2 L = 1200 mL, time = 1200 ÷ 75 = **16 minutes**.
    Check: 75 × 16 = 1200 ✅

12. (a) Strain through clean cloth first, then **keep at a rolling boil for several minutes** (~3 minutes at high altitude), because boiling kills germs without needing electricity. (b) Soften the hard water using **washing soda, an ion-exchange softener, or distillation** (Topic 3), since simple boiling only removes temporary hardness and leaves scale inside the kettle. (c) First **settle with alum and filter**, then **boil**: filtering removes the mud, and boiling kills floodborne germs.

13. (a) **True** ✅. Keeping water at a rolling boil for about a minute kills most disease-causing germs. (b) **False** ❌. A ceramic candle filter traps suspended particles and bacteria, not dissolved salts. (c) **True** ✅. Potash alum, KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O, contains 12 molecules of water of crystallisation per formula unit. (d) **False** ❌. Water from a homemade bottle filter is not safe to drink because it can still contain germs and dissolved toxins.
