# Topic 6: Protecting Our Water: Pollution, Conservation, and Chapter Summary

In the previous topic, we saw how much engineering effort goes into making water **clean and safe to drink**: screening, alum coagulation, sedimentation, sand filtration, chlorination, and pressurised distribution pipes. That raises an obvious question: when producing clean fresh water takes so much work—and when less than 1% of Earth's water is accessible liquid fresh water—why do we **pollute** our rivers and **waste** treated water at home? In this final topic, we will examine how water becomes polluted (including how farm fertilisers can suffocate a pond), how we can conserve water, and how to turn small everyday habits—like fixing a dripping tap or harvesting rainwater—into eye-opening numbers. We will wrap up with a complete summary of the chapter.

## The Dripping Tap on the Apartment Rooftop

It was Sunday morning. Liam had gone up to the apartment building's rooftop garden with his neighbour, Mr. Evans, to check on the potted tomato plants. Near the garden hose connection, a metal tap was going **drip… drip… drip…**, leaving a dark, mossy wet streak across the concrete floor.

"Mr. Evans, how long has that tap been dripping?" Liam asked.

Mr. Evans sighed. "Probably for a month. People walk past it and think, 'It's just one tiny drop—what difference could it make?'"

Liam pulled out his phone and opened the stopwatch. "A single drop is tiny, sure—but it's falling **every second, all day and all night**!" He timed it carefully: almost exactly **one drop per second**. "Wait here, Mr. Evans—I have a graduated measuring cylinder from my science kit downstairs, and in Chapter 4 we learned that **about 20 drops of water make 1 mL**."

Mr. Evans grinned. "Alright, young scientist! Before we crunch the numbers on this tap, let's also talk about the other way we lose usable water—by polluting our rivers and lakes. Then we'll calculate just how big one little drop becomes over a month."

Liam opened his notebook and wrote the title: **"How Water Gets Ruined—and How We Save It."**

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Water pollution** | The contamination of water bodies by harmful substances that make the water unsafe for human use or toxic to aquatic life | Untreated sewage or factory waste entering a river |
| **Pollutant** | Any substance or agent (chemical, physical, or biological) that contaminates water | Plastic waste, crude oil, toxic heavy metals |
| **Sewage** | Wastewater from homes, schools, and buildings (from sinks, showers, laundries, and toilets) | Domestic wastewater in sewer drains |
| **Fertiliser runoff** | Excess farm or garden fertiliser washed by rainwater off soil and into nearby streams, ponds, or lakes | Nitrates and phosphates washing into a lake |
| **Algal bloom** | A rapid, dense overgrowth of green algae covering the surface of a nutrient-rich pond or lake | A pond turning pea-soup green in summer |
| **Dissolved oxygen** | Oxygen gas dissolved in water, essential for fish and aquatic organisms to breathe | Drops dangerously low during hot weather or decay (Topic 2) |
| **Eutrophication** | Nutrient enrichment (by nitrates and phosphates) that triggers an algal bloom, followed by bacterial decay that depletes dissolved oxygen | Fish dying of suffocation in a nutrient-choked pond |
| **Water conservation** | Using water wisely, preventing waste, and protecting water resources | Turning off the tap while brushing teeth; fixing leaks |
| **Rainwater harvesting** | Collecting and storing rainwater from rooftops into tanks or underground recharge pits | Rooftop pipes feeding a storage tank |
| **Drip irrigation** | Watering crops drop by drop directly at the plant roots through perforated pipes to minimise evaporation and runoff | Drip lines in orchards and vegetable farms |

## Part A: How Does Water Get Polluted?

In Topic 5, we learned how to take impurities *out* of water. Now let's look at the six major ways human activities put harmful pollutants *into* water:

| Pollution Source | What Happens | Impact on Water and Life |
|---|---|---|
| **Untreated domestic sewage** | Wastewater from toilets, sinks, and drains flows directly into rivers or lakes without treatment | Spreads deadly waterborne germs (cholera, typhoid); causes foul odours and depletes oxygen |
| **Industrial chemical waste** | Factories discharge toxic chemicals, acids, or heavy-metal compounds (lead, mercury) into waterways | Poisons aquatic plants and fish; toxins accumulate in the food chain |
| **Agricultural runoff (fertilisers & pesticides)** | Rain washes excess chemical fertilisers and toxic pesticides from fields into ponds and rivers | Fertilisers trigger algal blooms (eutrophication); pesticides poison fish and wildlife |
| **Plastic and solid trash** | Plastic bags, bottles, and packaging are dumped or blown into rivers and oceans | Marine animals mistake plastic for food and choke; clogs urban storm drains |
| **Oil spills and oily runoff** | Crude oil from tankers or used motor oil from roads washes into water | Because oil is insoluble and less dense than water (Topic 2), it spreads into a surface slick that blocks oxygen and coats birds' feathers |
| **Thermal pollution (hot water)** | Power plants and factories discharge hot cooling water directly into rivers | Because gases are less soluble in warm water (Topic 2), dissolved oxygen drops and fish suffocate |

Notice how Topics 2 and 5 connect directly here: just as clear water can hide germs, **chemically polluted water can also look completely clear**. You can never judge water safety by sight alone.

> **Check:** "Rainwater flowing across farmland into a stream is always pure." ❌ False. Surface runoff dissolves and carries away chemical fertilisers, pesticides, and animal waste straight into streams and ponds.

## Part B: Eutrophication: How Plant Nutrients Can Suffocate a Pond

One of the most surprising forms of water pollution is caused by **chemical fertilisers**—substances meant to help crops grow! Farm fertilisers are rich in soluble **nitrates** and **phosphates** (plant nutrients). When heavy rain washes excess fertiliser off fields into a quiet pond or lake (or when untreated sewage enters the water), the microscopic green algae floating in the pond suddenly receive a massive feast of nutrients. Watch the chain reaction that follows:

```
  [1] Excess fertiliser on fields (nitrates & phosphates)
                   |
                   v
  [2] Rain washes nutrient runoff into the pond
                   |
                   v
  [3] Nitrate and phosphate levels in the pond surge
                   |
                   v
  [4] Algae multiply explosively: ALGAL BLOOM
      (thick green mat covers surface, blocking sunlight below)
                   |
                   v
  [5] Algae die and sink; decomposing bacteria multiply
      and consume huge amounts of dissolved oxygen
                   |
                   v
  [6] Dissolved oxygen plummets:
      fish and aquatic animals die of suffocation
```

> **Figure 6.1** – The step-by-step chain of eutrophication: nitrate and phosphate runoff fuels a rapid algal bloom; when the algae die and decay, bacteria use up the pond's dissolved oxygen, suffocating fish.

Let's state this in plain, everyday words: extra plant nutrients cause algae to **overgrow into a thick green carpet (an algal bloom)** that blocks sunlight from reaching underwater plants. Soon, the short-lived algae die in huge numbers. Decomposer bacteria multiply to break down all the dead algae, and **those bacteria use up the dissolved oxygen in the water**. With the dissolved oxygen exhausted, fish and other aquatic animals suffocate. This entire process is called **eutrophication**.

Notice the link back to Topic 2: in Topic 2 we saw that **heating water lowers dissolved oxygen** physically; in eutrophication, **bacterial decay consumes dissolved oxygen** biologically. Either way, the result for fish is the same—not enough oxygen to breathe!

> **Check:** "Because algae are green plants that photosynthesise, an algal bloom permanently increases the oxygen in a pond for fish." ❌ False. Once the dense bloom blocks deeper light and the massive layer of algae dies, bacterial decomposition uses up the dissolved oxygen, causing oxygen levels to crash.

## Part C: Practical Ways to Conserve Water

Preventing water pollution goes hand in hand with **using less water and preventing waste**. Remember Topic 1: about 97% of Earth's water is salty ocean water, and roughly 70% of the remaining 2.5% fresh water is frozen in ice caps—leaving less than 1% of Earth's water easily accessible for human use.

Here are eight practical conservation measures at home, on farms, and in cities:

1. **Fix leaks promptly:** Repair dripping taps, leaking pipe joints, and running toilet cisterns right away.
2. **Turn off the tap while brushing teeth, shaving, or scrubbing dishes:** Running a tap continuously wastes litres every minute.
3. **Take shorter showers or use a bucket/measured basin:** Cuts bath water use dramatically.
4. **Reuse clean rinse water ("greywater" from rinsing vegetables or rice):** Use plain, soap-free kitchen rinse water to water potted plants or garden beds. (Avoid putting water containing harsh laundry detergents, bleach, or cooking oil onto garden soil.)
5. **Rainwater harvesting:** Channel clean rooftop rainwater into storage tanks or underground recharge pits.
6. **Drip irrigation in farming:** Deliver water drop by drop right to plant roots instead of flooding entire fields, cutting evaporation and runoff losses.
7. **Treat sewage before discharge:** Municipal sewage treatment plants remove solids and break down organic waste before returning water to rivers.
8. **Use fertilisers and pesticides sparingly:** Apply only what crops can absorb so less washes away in runoff.

Now let's do what Liam and Mr. Evans promised: **turn a tiny dripping tap into real numbers.**

## Part D: The Math of a Dripping Tap

Liam placed his graduated cylinder directly under the dripping rooftop tap.

```
         leaking tap
           |
           o   <- 1 drop every 1 second
           |
           v
      +---------+
      |         |   Graduated measuring cylinder
      |  ~~~~~  |   (marked in mL)
      |  water  |
      +---------+
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Measuring a dripping tap: at just one drop per second, collecting the water in a graduated cylinder reveals how quickly tiny drops add up over a day, a month, and a year.

**Example 1: How many drops fall in one full day (24 hours)?**
1 day = 24 hours = 24 × 60 × 60 = **86,400 seconds**.
At 1 drop per second, the tap loses **86,400 drops** in a single day.

**Example 2: How many millilitres and litres is that per day?**
Taking 20 drops = 1 mL: 86,400 ÷ 20 = **4320 mL**.
Converting to litres (1 L = 1000 mL): 4320 ÷ 1000 = **4.32 L per day**.

That means one slow, steady drip wastes **4.32 litres of treated drinking water every single day**—more than four full 1-litre water bottles! Since a standard household bucket holds about 20 L, one day's drip is 4.32 ÷ 20 ≈ 0.22 of a bucket (over one-fifth of a bucket).

**Example 3: How much is wasted in one month (30 days)?**
4.32 L/day × 30 days = **129.6 L**. Divided into 20 L buckets, that is 129.6 ÷ 20 = **6.48 buckets**—nearly six and a half full buckets of water down the drain every month from a single tap!

**Example 4: How much is wasted in one year (365 days)?**
4.32 L/day × 365 days = **1576.8 L**, or about **1.58 m³** (since 1 m³ = 1000 L).

> **Check:** 4.32 L = 4320 mL, and at 20 drops per mL, 4320 × 20 = 86,400 drops ✅.

When Liam timed the cylinder for just 1 minute (60 seconds), it collected 60 drops = 60 ÷ 20 = **3 mL**. "Only 3 mL in a minute!" Liam said. Mr. Evans nodded: "And that's why people ignore it—3 mL looks like nothing. But multiply by the **1440 minutes in a day**, and suddenly it's over 4 litres!"

## Part E: Rainwater Harvesting: From Rooftop to Recharge Pit

Now let's calculate the flip side of conservation: **capturing rainwater**. When rain falls on a building's roof, it usually rushes down the gutter into the storm drain and is lost. By piping that rooftop runoff through a coarse sand-and-gravel filter into a **storage tank** or an **underground recharge pit**, a building can store thousands of litres of soft water or replenish the local groundwater table.

```
           falling raindrops
          /   /    /    /   /
         v   v    v    v   v
      +---------------------------+
      |        ROOFTOP            |   collection area = 100 m²
      +--+------------------------+
         |    <- gutter & downpipe
         v
       [ pipe ]
         |
      +--v---+     mesh & sand filter
      |  ::  |     (first-flush dirty water
      +--+---+      is diverted away)
         |
         v
    +---------+
    |  ~~~~~  |   storage tank or groundwater recharge pit
    | ~~~~~~~ |   (clean rainwater stored)
    +---------+
```

> **Figure 6.3** – A rooftop rainwater harvesting system: rain falling on the roof flows down a gutter pipe through a filter box into a storage tank or underground recharge pit.

Let's work through a **realistic sample calculation**: suppose a house has a flat rooftop area of **100 m²**, receives **0.6 m** (60 cm or 600 mm) of rainfall over a year, and manages to capture **80%** of the water that hits the roof (allowing 20% for evaporation and first-flush diversion).

**Example 5: What total volume of rain falls on the roof in a year?**
Volume = roof area × rainfall depth = 100 m² × 0.6 m = **60 m³**.

**Example 6: If 80% is captured, how many cubic metres are collected?**
60 m³ × (80 ÷ 100) = **48 m³**.

**Example 7: How many litres is 48 m³?**
Recall that a cube measuring 1 m × 1 m × 1 m is 100 cm × 100 cm × 100 cm = 1,000,000 cm³ = 1,000,000 mL = **1000 L**.
Therefore, 48 m³ = 48 × 1000 = **48,000 L**!

**Example 8: How many days could this supply a family of 4?**
Suppose a family of **4 people** uses an average of **135 L per person per day** for all household needs.
Total daily household water use = 4 × 135 = **540 L/day**.
Number of days supplied = 48,000 L ÷ 540 L/day = **88.9 days**—nearly **3 full months** of water for the entire family from one modest rooftop!

> **Check:** 540 L/day × 88.9 days ≈ 48,006 L ≈ 48,000 L ✅.

**Important safety note:** Rooftop-harvested rainwater has flowed over dusty tiles, leaves, and bird droppings, so it is **not safe to drink directly**. It is ideal for flushing toilets, mopping floors, washing clothes (remember Topic 3: rainwater is naturally soft!), and watering gardens—and if used for drinking, it must first undergo full filtration and disinfection (Topic 5).

## Part F: Everyday Water Math and Reasoning

Liam used his notebook to compare everyday household water choices.

**Example 9: Bucket bath vs long shower.**
Taking a bath with a bucket uses about **20 L** of water. A high-flow shower running at **15 L per minute** for **8 minutes** uses 15 × 8 = **120 L**. Switching saves 120 − 20 = **100 L per day** for one person—or 100 × 30 = **3000 L = 3 m³** in a 30-day month!

**Example 10: Comparing a dripping tap to monthly family use.**
A family of 4 using 135 L per person per day uses 4 × 135 × 30 = **16,200 L (16.2 m³)** in 30 days. Our single dripping tap (129.6 L per month) wastes (129.6 ÷ 16,200) × 100 = **0.8%** of the entire family's monthly water supply. Five such leaking taps in an apartment block would waste 4% of a family's monthly budget without anyone using a drop!

**Example 11: Classify each action as "water pollution", "waste of water", or "water conservation".**
- A leaking tap dripping all night: **waste of water** (clean water lost without being used).
- Dumping plastic bottles into a canal: **water pollution**.
- Excess farm fertiliser washing into a lake: **water pollution** (causes eutrophication).
- Watering balcony plants with plain vegetable-rinsing water: **water conservation**.

**Example 12: One practical way farmers can prevent eutrophication.**
Apply only the measured amount of fertiliser that crops need (avoiding before heavy rainstorms) and leave a strip of grass or vegetation along the edges of fields to trap nutrient runoff before it reaches ponds.

> **Check:** 3000 L ÷ 1000 = 3 m³ ✅, and 4 × 135 × 30 = 540 × 30 = 16,200 ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "A single drop per second is too tiny to matter"**
One drop per second adds up to 4.32 L every day, or 129.6 L every month. Small leaks multiplied by 86,400 seconds per day become huge losses.

**Mistake 2: "If river water looks clear, it cannot be polluted"**
Dissolved toxic chemicals, heavy metals, excess nitrates, and microscopic germs are completely invisible (Topic 5).

**Mistake 3: "Since fertilisers help plants grow, they must be good for a pond"**
In a pond, excess nitrates and phosphates trigger an algal bloom whose subsequent decay robs the water of dissolved oxygen (eutrophication), suffocating fish.

**Mistake 4: "An algal bloom gives fish extra oxygen because algae are green plants"**
While live surface algae produce some oxygen in daylight, the dense mat blocks light below and when the massive bloom dies, bacteria decomposing the dead algae consume far more oxygen, causing dissolved oxygen to crash.

**Mistake 5: "1 m³ = 100 L"**
No! 1 m³ = 100 cm × 100 cm × 100 cm = 1,000,000 mL = **1000 L**.

**Mistake 6: "Rainwater collected off a roof is ready to drink straight from the tank"**
Rooftops collect dust, leaves, and bird droppings. Harvested rainwater must be filtered and disinfected (Topic 5) before drinking.

**Mistake 7: "Pouring soapy laundry water or oily dishwater onto garden plants is good conservation"**
Only reuse plain, soap-free rinse water (such as from rinsing vegetables or rice) on ordinary garden beds; strong detergents, bleach, and grease can harm soil and plant roots.

## Quick Recap

- Major sources of water pollution: untreated sewage, industrial chemical waste, agricultural fertiliser and pesticide runoff, plastic trash, oil spills, and hot water (thermal pollution).
- **Eutrophication:** Nitrate and phosphate runoff → rapid **algal bloom** → algae die and sink → bacteria decompose dead algae and consume **dissolved oxygen** → fish die of suffocation.
- Conservation habits: fix leaks promptly, turn off taps while brushing, take shorter showers or use a bucket, reuse plain kitchen rinse water on plants, harvest rainwater, use drip irrigation, and treat sewage.
- **Dripping tap math:** 1 drop/s = 86,400 drops/day = 4320 mL/day = **4.32 L/day** (using 20 drops = 1 mL), or **129.6 L in 30 days**.
- Unit conversion: **1 m³ = 1000 L**.
- **Rainwater harvesting math:** Volume collected = roof area × rainfall depth × collection efficiency. Example: 100 m² × 0.6 m × 0.8 = 48 m³ = 48,000 L (lasting a family of 4 at 540 L/day for **88.9 days**).
- Harvested rainwater must be purified before drinking.

    (a) 1 m³ is equal to 100 L.

### Answers

2. Total rain volume = 50 m² × 0.8 m = **40 m³**. Collected (70%) = 40 × 0.7 = **28 m³**. In litres: 28 × 1000 = **28,000 L**. Daily family use = 5 × 100 = 500 L/day. Days supplied = 28,000 ÷ 500 = **56 days** ✅ (500 × 56 = 28,000).

3. (a) 3.5 × 1000 = **3500 L**. (b) 250 ÷ 1000 = **0.25 m³**. (c) 6000 ÷ 1000 = **6 L**.

4. Correct sequence: **(b) → (d) → (e) → (c) → (a)**. First fertiliser runoff reaches the pond, causing algae to grow rapidly (algal bloom); then dead algae are decomposed by bacteria, which causes dissolved oxygen to fall, and finally fish die of suffocation.

5. Because oil is insoluble in water (Topic 2) and less dense than water, it floats and spreads into a thin layer across the surface. **First reason:** This surface slick blocks atmospheric oxygen from dissolving into the water below. **Second reason:** The floating oil blocks sunlight needed by underwater plants and coats the feathers of waterbirds and gills of fish.

6. In Topic 2 we learned that **gases are less soluble in warm water than in cold water**. Discharging hot water warms the river, lowering the concentration of dissolved oxygen so fish struggle to breathe.

7. (a) **waste of water** (tap left running). (b) **pollution** (plastic trash). (c) **conservation** (reusing plain rinse water). (d) **pollution** (toxic chemical runoff). (e) **waste of water** (leaking tap).

## Chapter Summary: Water

Here is the complete journey of our chapter at a glance—from a single molecule of H₂O to protecting the water supply of an entire city:

| Topic | Title | One-Line Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| Topic 1 | What Is Water: Composition and Properties | Water is H₂O (a covalent compound with a 1 : 8 mass ratio of H to O); pure water is colourless, odourless, tasteless, neutral, melts at 0 °C, and boils at 100 °C, while less than 1% of Earth's water is accessible liquid fresh water. |
| Topic 2 | Water as a Solvent | Water dissolves many ionic and polar substances (but not oil or wax); solubility is the maximum grams dissolved per 100 g of water at a given temperature, rising with temperature for most solids (like KNO₃) but falling for gases. |
| Topic 3 | Hard and Soft Water | Dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts make water hard (forming soap scum and kettle limescale): temporary hardness (hydrogencarbonates) is removed by boiling, whereas permanent hardness (chlorides/sulphates) requires washing soda, ion-exchange, or distillation. |
| Topic 4 | Hydrates, Efflorescence, and Deliquescence | Hydrated crystals lock a fixed number of H₂O molecules in their lattice; efflorescent crystals lose water to dry air, hygroscopic substances (like silica gel) absorb moisture, and deliquescent solids absorb enough moisture to dissolve into a liquid. |
| Topic 5 | Purification | Municipal plants use screening, alum coagulation, sedimentation, sand filtration, and chlorination to make water potable—because looking clear never guarantees water is germ-free. |
| Topic 6 | Protecting Our Water | Sewage, chemicals, plastic, and fertiliser runoff (eutrophication) pollute water, while fixing small leaks (1 drop/s = 4.32 L/day) and harvesting rainwater conserve thousands of litres. |
