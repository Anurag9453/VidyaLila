# Topic 1: What Is Water: Composition and Properties

In the previous chapter, we saw how hydrogen sits at the top-left corner of the periodic table while oxygen sits in Group 16, and how every element's position reveals its chemical personality. Now let's bring those two elements together and examine the most famous compound they form: **water**—something we drink every single day without ever pausing to ask, "What actually is this stuff?"

---

## A Cold Glass at the Park Fountain

It was a blazing summer afternoon. Dr. Morales and his nephew Leo were walking back from the neighbourhood sports field, the pavement shimmering in the heat. Near the park entrance, they stopped at a chilled drinking fountain. Leo filled his stainless-steel bottle, poured a cup, and drained it in one long gulp.

"Ahh! Uncle, nothing beats cold water!" Then he stared into the empty cup. "Wait—what actually *is* water? It has no colour, no taste, no smell. It looks like nothing at all, yet we can't live without it. What's inside it?"

Dr. Morales smiled. "Great question. Water actually has an 'ingredient list', just like a snack wrapper—except it's tiny: **only two elements**. And here's the wildest part: one element (hydrogen) is a flammable gas that burns explosively, the other (oxygen) is the gas that keeps every fire burning, and when the two bond together, the result—water—**puts fires out**."

Leo's eyes went wide. "Something that puts out fires is made from a gas that burns?!" "Come on," Dr. Morales said, "let's unpack the whole mystery on our walk home."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Water** | A chemical compound of hydrogen and oxygen with the formula H₂O | Tap water, river water, rainwater |
| **Compound** | Two or more elements chemically bonded in a fixed ratio by mass | H₂O, NaCl |
| **Mixture** | Substances combined physically without chemical bonding, in any ratio | Air, lemonade |
| **Molecule** | A small group of bonded atoms that exists as a single complete particle | One water molecule: 2 H and 1 O |
| **Covalent bond** | A chemical bond formed by sharing pairs of electrons between atoms | Each O–H bond in water |
| **Fresh water** | Water containing very little dissolved salt | Rivers, lakes, glacier ice |
| **Saline (salty) water** | Water with a high concentration of dissolved salts | Ocean and sea water |
| **Anhydrous** | "Without water"—a substance completely free of water | Dry white copper sulphate powder |

## Part A: The Composition of Water

### Two Elements, One Compound

The chemical formula of water is **H₂O**. This tells us that **one molecule** of water consists of **2 hydrogen atoms** chemically bonded to **1 oxygen atom**. The bond holding them together is a **covalent bond** (from Chapter 6): each hydrogen atom shares its single electron with the oxygen atom so that all three atoms achieve full outer shells—2 electrons for each hydrogen and 8 for oxygen.

```
              O
             / \
            /   \
           /     \
          H       H

   O - H bond: one shared pair of electrons (covalent)
   H - O - H : 2 hydrogen + 1 oxygen = 3 atoms in total
```

> **Figure 1.1** – A single molecule of water (H₂O): one oxygen atom bonded to two hydrogen atoms via single covalent bonds. Rather than forming a straight line, the molecule has a bent "V" shape (you will study the exact bond angle in higher grades).

Here is a crucial distinction: water is a **compound**, not a mixture. If you simply mix hydrogen gas and oxygen gas inside a jar, you get a mixture that can be combined in any proportion you like. But when they react chemically to form water, the mass ratio is **always fixed**, and the properties change completely. Hydrogen is a flammable gas, oxygen supports combustion, and water—formed from the two—**extinguishes fire**. This is a classic real-world illustration of the Law of Constant Proportions from Chapter 4.

### The Fixed Mass Ratio: 1 : 8

In Chapter 4, we learned how to calculate molecular mass. Taking the atomic mass of H as 1 u and O as 16 u:

- Molecular mass of H₂O = 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 u**
- Mass contributed by hydrogen = 2 u; mass contributed by oxygen = 16 u
- Mass ratio **H : O = 2 : 16 = 1 : 8**

```
    18 g of water (each block = 2 g)

    [ H ][ O ][ O ][ O ][ O ][ O ][ O ][ O ][ O ]
      ^    ^-------- 8 blocks of oxygen --------^
      1 block of hydrogen

    Total 9 blocks:  1 block H (2 g)  +  8 blocks O (16 g)
    Hydrogen = 1 / 9 = 11.11 %     Oxygen = 8 / 9 = 88.89 %
```

> **Figure 1.2** – The mass of water divided into 9 equal parts: 1 part is hydrogen and 8 parts are oxygen. Consequently, 11.11% of water's mass is hydrogen and 88.89% is oxygen—whether you test a single raindrop or an entire river.

Let's recall percentage composition (using the Chapter 4 method):

- Hydrogen % = (2 ÷ 18) × 100 = **11.11 %**
- Oxygen % = (16 ÷ 18) × 100 = **88.89 %**
- Total check: 11.11 + 88.89 = 100 ✅

At first glance, this feels surprising: nearly **8 out of 9 parts** of water's mass come from oxygen, even though **each molecule has 2 H atoms and only 1 O atom**. Both facts are true because **atom count** and **mass** are different things: a single oxygen atom is 16 times heavier than a single hydrogen atom.

> **Check:** Saying water has a 1 : 8 mass ratio is identical to saying 2 : 16. ✅ That is because 2 hydrogen atoms have a combined mass of 2 u while 1 oxygen atom has a mass of 16 u.

### Experimental Proof: Electrolysis of Water

In Chapter 7, we saw the experiment where an electric current splits water apart (**electrolysis**): 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂. This experiment proves two key facts:

1. Splitting water produces **only hydrogen and oxygen**, nothing else—confirming that water is made solely of these two elements.
2. The **volume ratio of the two gases is 2 : 1**: twice as much hydrogen gas is collected by volume as oxygen gas.

How can the gas volume ratio be 2 : 1 while the **mass ratio is 1 : 8**? Look at the balanced equation: 2 molecules of H₂ have a total mass of 2 × 2 = 4 u, while 1 molecule of O₂ has a mass of 32 u. The mass ratio is 4 : 32 = **1 : 8** ✅. Because hydrogen molecules are so light, even having twice as many molecules gives only one-eighth the mass of oxygen.

This experiment is performed **only as a supervised school laboratory demonstration**—never mix mains electricity and water at home.

## Part B: Physical and Chemical Profile of Water

Now that we know what water is made of, let's examine its profile of properties. Here is a summary table:

| Property | In Pure Water | In One Line |
|---|---|---|
| **Colour** | Colourless (pure water) | Shows a faint bluish tint in deep layers, invisible in a drinking glass |
| **Odour** | Odourless | Any smell in tap water comes from dissolved chlorine or minerals, not H₂O |
| **Taste** | Tasteless (pure water) | The refreshing "taste" of drinking water comes from dissolved minerals and gases |
| **Melting point** | 0 °C | Ice melts into liquid water at standard pressure |
| **Boiling point** | 100 °C | At normal atmospheric pressure (sea level) |
| **Density** | 1 g per mL (1000 kg per m³) at about 4 °C | Water reaches its maximum density at 4 °C (as studied in Physics) |
| **Specific heat capacity** | Very high: 4200 J per kg per °C | Water heats up slowly and cools down slowly (as studied in Physics) |
| **Chemical nature** | Neutral: neither acidic nor basic | Pure water does not change the colour of red or blue litmus |
| **Electrical conductivity** | Pure water is a very poor conductor | Tap water conducts electricity much better because of dissolved mineral ions (see Topic 2) |

In the Physics chapter "Heat and Temperature", we explored two of these properties in detail: water reaches its maximum density at 4 °C (allowing fish to survive under frozen winter lakes), and it has an unusually high specific heat capacity ([Topic 2](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part2.md) and [Topic 3](../../physics/heat-and-temperature/heat-and-temperature-part3.md)). We won't repeat those derivations here—just keep the one-line takeaway in mind: **densest at 4 °C, and absorbs a huge amount of heat for every degree of warming.**

Two points that are new here:

- **What "pure" means in chemistry:** All the values in the table apply strictly to **chemically pure H₂O**. Drinking fountain or tap water is not chemically pure; it contains small amounts of dissolved salts, minerals, and gases (which we will explore in the next topic).
- **Neutral:** Pure water is neither an acid nor a base. You will study acids and bases in greater detail in higher classes; for now, neutral simply means right in the middle.

### Connecting Water's Mass and Volume

Because water's density is 1 g per mL (near 4 °C), **1 mL of water has a mass of 1 g**. This gives us a remarkably handy everyday conversion:

- 1 L = 1000 mL, so **1 L of water = 1000 g = 1 kg** ✅
- Therefore, a 5 L bucket filled with water has a water mass of **5 kg**.

> **Check:** Why is a full 20 L water cooler jug so tough to lift? ✅ Because 20 L of water has a mass of 20 kg—about as heavy as a packed suitcase!

## Part C: Why Water Is So Vital—and So Scarce

Water isn't just for quenching thirst; it underpins almost everything around us.

- **About 71% of the Earth's surface is covered with water**, while the remaining 29% is land: 71 + 29 = 100 ✅.
- **Our own bodies** are mostly water; blood circulation, sweating, digestion, and cellular respiration all depend on it (remember from Chapter 7 that respiration itself produces water).
- **Agriculture, power generation, manufacturing, sanitation, and cooking** all rely heavily on water.
- **Where there is no water, there is no known life**—which is why space agencies searching for life on Mars or icy moons always look for water first.

Yet despite all this abundance, here is a sobering fact: while Earth has plenty of water, **very little of it is available as drinkable fresh water**.

```
    Divide all of Earth's water into 40 equal boxes (1 box = 2.5 %)

    [S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S]
    [S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S]
    [S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S]
    [S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][S][F]

    S = saline ocean water    39 boxes = about 97 %
    F = fresh water            1 box   = about 2.5 %
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Distribution of Earth's water (approximate): out of 40 equal boxes, only a single box (about 2.5%) represents fresh water; the rest is salty ocean water. (Exact percentages vary slightly across sources; the big picture is what matters.)

Roughly **97%** of Earth's water is salty ocean water, which cannot be used directly for drinking or irrigating crops. Only about **2.5%** is fresh water—and **most of that fresh water (around 70%) is locked away in polar ice caps and glaciers**. That means all the rivers, lakes, and accessible groundwater on the planet make up only a tiny fraction of Earth's total water. We will work through a numerical example of this in Part E.

> **Check:** "Since 71% of the Earth is covered with water, we can never run short of usable water." ❌ False. Nearly all of it is either salty ocean water or frozen in ice caps; accessible fresh water is a tiny fraction.

## Part D: Chemical Test for Water

If you are handed a clear, colourless liquid that looks just like water, how can you test chemically whether it actually contains water? Looking at it isn't enough—we use a chemical test.

**Anhydrous copper sulphate** (dry white copper sulphate powder) turns **blue** the moment it comes into contact with water. This colour change from white to blue proves the presence of water.

- White (anhydrous) CuSO₄ + a few drops of water → **blue** CuSO₄·5H₂O.

**Safety:** This is strictly a **teacher demonstration**. Copper sulphate is harmful if swallowed or touched, so students should never handle it directly, bring it near their face, or taste any laboratory liquid.

Note what this test tells us—and what it doesn't: it confirms that **water is present**, but it does **not** prove that the liquid is pure water. Any water-containing liquid, such as milk or fruit juice, will also turn white anhydrous copper sulphate blue. We will explore hydrated crystals like blue copper sulphate in full detail in Topic 4.

## Part E: Solved Examples

**Example 1: How many grams of hydrogen and oxygen are present in 36 g of water?**
The mass ratio is 1 : 8, giving 9 equal parts in total. 1 part = 36 ÷ 9 = 4 g. Hydrogen = 1 × 4 = **4 g**, oxygen = 8 × 4 = **32 g**.
Check: 4 + 32 = 36 ✅ and 32 ÷ 4 = 8 ✅.

**Example 2: Find the molecular mass and percentage composition of H₂O.**
Molecular mass = 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 u**. H % = (2 ÷ 18) × 100 = **11.11%**, O % = (16 ÷ 18) × 100 = **88.89%**. Sum = 100 ✅.

**Example 3: How much water can be formed from 5 g of hydrogen, and how much oxygen is required?**
Since 1 part of hydrogen is 5 g, 8 parts of oxygen = 8 × 5 = **40 g**, and total water formed = 5 + 40 = **45 g**.
Check: 45 ÷ 9 = 5 ✅ (hydrogen) and (45 ÷ 9) × 8 = 40 ✅.

**Example 4: During electrolysis of water, 20 mL of hydrogen gas is collected. What volume of oxygen gas is collected?**
The gas volume ratio H₂ : O₂ is 2 : 1. For 20 mL of H₂, oxygen collected = 20 ÷ 2 = **10 mL of O₂**. (Remember, this is the volume ratio; by mass, oxygen is eight times heavier.)

**Example 5: Out of 1000 L of Earth's total water, approximately how much is salty, how much is fresh, and how much of that fresh water is locked in ice?**
Salty = 97% of 1000 = **970 L**. Fresh = 2.5% of 1000 = **25 L**. About 70% of fresh water is frozen: 70% of 25 = **17.5 L** in ice and glaciers, leaving 25 − 17.5 = **7.5 L** in rivers, lakes, and groundwater (all figures approximate). Out of 1000 L, only about 7.5 L is readily accessible liquid fresh water!

**Example 6: A water dispenser jug holds 20 L of water. What is the mass of the water inside?**
Since 1 L of water has a mass of 1 kg, 20 L has a mass of **20 kg**.

**Example 7: Someone claims, "Water and a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen gas are basically the same thing." True or false?**
False. In a gas mixture, hydrogen and oxygen remain separate flammable/combustion-supporting gases and can be mixed in any ratio. Water is a chemical compound formed in a **fixed 1 : 8 mass ratio** with completely new properties—such as extinguishing fires.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

**Mistake 1: "Water contains equal masses of hydrogen and oxygen"**
No. The mass ratio is **1 : 8** (H : O). The atom ratio is 2 : 1.

**Mistake 2: "Since water is H₂O, it contains 2 grams of H and 1 gram of O"**
Subscripts tell you the number of atoms in a molecule, not the mass in grams. Two H atoms have a mass of 2 u, while one O atom has a mass of 16 u.

**Mistake 3: "Electrolysis of water gives hydrogen and oxygen in a 1 : 8 volume ratio"**
No. The gas volume ratio is **2 : 1** (H₂ : O₂); **1 : 8** is the mass ratio.

**Mistake 4: "Pure water tastes naturally sweet"**
Chemically pure water is completely tasteless. The pleasant taste of spring or tap water comes from trace dissolved minerals and gases.

**Mistake 5: "If white copper sulphate turns blue, the water is safe to drink"**
That test only proves that water is **present**—it gives zero guarantee that the liquid is pure or free of germs and toxins.

**Mistake 6: "Because 71% of Earth is covered in water, fresh water is unlimited"**
No: roughly 97% is salty ocean water, and around 70% of the small fresh-water fraction is frozen in glaciers and ice caps.

## Quick Recap

- Water is **H₂O**: a **compound** of hydrogen and oxygen joined by covalent bonds; each molecule has 2 H atoms and 1 O atom in a bent "V" shape.
- Molecular mass = 18 u; mass ratio **H : O = 1 : 8**; percentage composition is **11.11% H and 88.89% O**.
- Electrolysis (2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂) proves water consists solely of H and O; the gas volume ratio is 2 : 1, while the mass ratio is 1 : 8.
- Pure water is colourless, odourless, tasteless, and **neutral**; it melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C; its density peaks at 1 g per mL at 4 °C, and it has a very high specific heat capacity (4200 J per kg per °C).
- 1 L of water has a mass of 1 kg.
- About 71% of Earth's surface is water, but ~97% is salty; most of the ~2.5% fresh water is frozen in ice caps and glaciers.
- **Test for water:** dry white anhydrous copper sulphate turns blue on contact with water (teacher demonstration only).

## Practice Questions

1. Write the formula of water and say what it tells you about the atoms in one molecule. Which kind of bond joins them?

2. Calculate the molecular mass of water, and use it to find the percentage of hydrogen and of oxygen. Check that they add up to 100.

3. A student says that water is a mixture of hydrogen and oxygen. Give two reasons why this is wrong.

4. How much hydrogen and how much oxygen are present in 72 g of water? Check your answer.

5. How much hydrogen is needed to combine completely with 24 g of oxygen, and how much water forms?

6. A sample of water contains 7 g of hydrogen. How many grams of oxygen does it contain, and what is the mass of the sample?

7. In an electrolysis experiment (done by a teacher), 36 mL of oxygen gas is collected. How much hydrogen is collected at the other electrode? Why is the volume ratio 2 : 1 but the mass ratio 1 : 8?

8. Name three physical properties of pure water and give their values. Which two of its properties were dealt with in detail in the Physics chapter "Heat and Temperature"?

9. What is the mass of 250 mL of water at about 4 °C? What is the mass of 1.5 L? Why does a full 50 L drum feel so heavy?

10. About 71% of the Earth's surface is covered by water, yet people face water shortage. Explain using the shares of salty and fresh water.

11. Out of 4000 L of all the water on Earth, about how much is salty, and how much is fresh? Of the fresh part, about how much is frozen (take 70%)? (All figures approximate.)

12. How can you test whether a colourless liquid contains water? Why should students not do this test on their own, and why does it not prove that the liquid is drinkable?

13. State True or False, and correct the false ones:
    (a) Water contains hydrogen and oxygen in the mass ratio 2 : 1.
    (b) Pure water is neutral.
    (c) 1 mL of water has a mass of about 1 g near 4 °C.
    (d) Most of the water on Earth is fresh water.

### Answers

1. The formula is **H₂O**: one molecule contains **2 hydrogen atoms and 1 oxygen atom**. They are joined by **covalent bonds** (formed by sharing electrons).

2. Molecular mass = 2 × 1 + 16 = **18 u**. H % = (2 ÷ 18) × 100 = **11.11%**. O % = (16 ÷ 18) × 100 = **88.89%**. Sum: 11.11 + 88.89 = 100 ✅.

3. (i) In a mixture, hydrogen and oxygen can be present in any proportion, whereas in water the mass ratio is strictly **fixed at 1 : 8**. (ii) In a mixture, both gases keep their original properties (hydrogen burns, oxygen supports burning), whereas water has completely different properties and extinguishes fire. Hence water is a compound.

4. Total 9 parts, so 1 part = 72 ÷ 9 = 8 g. Hydrogen = **8 g**, oxygen = 8 × 8 = **64 g**. Check: 8 + 64 = 72 ✅.

5. Oxygen is 8 parts = 24 g, so 1 part = 24 ÷ 8 = 3 g. Hydrogen needed = **3 g**, and water formed = 3 + 24 = **27 g**. Check: 27 ÷ 9 = 3 ✅ and 3 × 8 = 24 ✅.

6. Oxygen = 8 × 7 = **56 g**. Total mass of water = 7 + 56 = **63 g**. Check: 63 ÷ 9 = 7 ✅.

7. Since the volume ratio H₂ : O₂ = 2 : 1, hydrogen collected = 2 × 36 = **72 mL**. The volume ratio is 2 : 1 because the reaction 2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ produces 2 molecules of H₂ for every 1 molecule of O₂; however, by mass, 2 H₂ molecules weigh 4 u while 1 O₂ molecule weighs 32 u, giving a mass ratio of 4 : 32 = 1 : 8 ✅ because hydrogen molecules are much lighter.

8. Pure water is colourless, odourless, and tasteless; it melts at 0 °C and boils at 100 °C; its density is 1 g per mL (1000 kg per m³) at about 4 °C; it is chemically neutral. The two properties covered in detail in the Physics chapter are **maximum density at 4 °C** (anomalous expansion) and **very high specific heat capacity (4200 J per kg per °C)**.

9. Since 1 mL = 1 g, **250 mL = 250 g**. 1.5 L = 1500 mL = **1500 g = 1.5 kg**. A full 50 L drum holds 50 kg of water, which feels very heavy because each litre adds a full kilogram of mass.

10. Although 71% of the Earth's surface is covered with water, about 97% of it is salty ocean water that cannot be used directly for drinking or farming. Only about 2.5% is fresh water, and roughly 70% of that is locked up in glaciers and polar ice caps, leaving only a tiny fraction as usable liquid fresh water.

11. Salty = 97% of 4000 = **3880 L**. Fresh = 2.5% of 4000 = **100 L**. Frozen in ice = 70% of 100 = **70 L**, leaving **30 L** in rivers, lakes, and groundwater. Check: 0.97 × 4000 = 3880 ✅, 0.025 × 4000 = 100 ✅.

12. Add a few drops of the liquid to dry white **anhydrous copper sulphate**; if it turns **blue**, water is present. Students should not perform this on their own because copper sulphate is harmful if swallowed or handled carelessly (teacher demo only). The test only proves that water is present, not that it is pure or safe to drink—harmful germs or dissolved toxins could still be present.

13. (a) **False** ❌. The mass ratio H : O is **1 : 8** (2 : 1 is the ratio of atoms). (b) **True** ✅. (c) **True** ✅. (d) **False** ❌. About 97% of Earth's water is salty; only about 2.5% is fresh water.
