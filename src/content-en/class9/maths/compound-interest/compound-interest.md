# Topic 1: Simple Interest Recap and What Is Compound Interest?

You probably already know that when we deposit money in a bank or borrow money from someone, **interest** is charged or earned. In this topic, we will quickly refresh that earlier idea (**Simple Interest**), and then meet a powerful new concept where interest itself earns more interest. This is called **Compound Interest**. Today we will focus purely on understanding the **concept**; the calculation methods will come in the topics ahead.

---

## Maya's Piggy Bank Story

For two years, **Maya** had been saving money in her piggy bank — birthday gifts, holiday allowance, and her grandmother's monthly "little bonus." When she finally opened it, she counted a full **\$10,000** in savings!

Her father said, "If you leave it sitting in a drawer, it will stay \$10,000 forever. Let us put it into a bank **savings certificate (fixed deposit)**."

At the bank, the branch manager, **Dr. Bennett**, explained, "Maya, you deposit \$10,000 with us. We put that money to work and, in return, pay you **extra money** every year. That extra money is called **interest**. Suppose we pay \$10 per year for every \$100 you deposit."

Maya did the mental maths immediately: "So in the first year, on \$10,000, I earn \$1,000 extra. Awesome!"

Dr. Bennett smiled. "Now listen to the really interesting part. Next year, a bank can calculate your interest in two different ways:

**Method 1:** Every year, we pay you interest only on your **original \$10,000**. That means \$1,000 every single year.

**Method 2:** At the end of the first year, we add that \$1,000 of interest directly into your account balance. So next year, we pay you interest on **\$11,000**. In other words, the interest you earned starts earning interest too!"

Maya's eyes lit up. "Method 2 is **interest on interest**! That earns more, right?"

"Exactly," said Dr. Bennett. "Method 1 is **Simple Interest**. Method 2 is **Compound Interest**. Today, make sure you understand the difference between the two — because over a long time, that difference becomes huge."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Principal ($P$)** | The original sum of money deposited or borrowed | Maya's \$10,000 |
| **Interest ($I$)** | The extra money paid or earned for using money | The \$1,000 earned in the first year |
| **Rate ($r\%$ per annum)** | The interest earned per \$100 in one year | $10\%$ per annum: \$10 per \$100 per year |
| **Per annum (p.a.)** | "Each year" (from Latin, meaning "by the year") | $10\%$ p.a. $= 10\%$ per year |
| **Time ($n$ or $T$)** | How many years the money is invested or borrowed | $3$ years |
| **Amount ($A$)** | The total money at the end: $A = P + I$ | $\$10,000 + \$3,000 = \$13,000$ |
| **Simple Interest (SI)** | Interest calculated only on the **original principal** | The same \$1,000 every year |
| **Compound Interest (CI)** | Interest calculated on the **principal plus all accumulated interest** | Interest on interest |

Here, $I$ stands for Interest. When we simply say "interest," we mean only the **extra earnings**, not the entire balance. The total money at the end is called the **Amount**.

## Part A: Simple Interest Recap (From Earlier Grades)

> You studied this in an earlier grade — let us quickly refresh it.

In **Simple Interest**, interest is **always** calculated on the original principal, no matter how many years pass.

**Formula:**

$$\text{SI} = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$$

Here, $P =$ principal, $R =$ annual interest rate (in percent per annum), and $T =$ time (in years). (In later topics we will write the rate as lowercase $r$ and the number of years as $n$; they mean the exact same things.)

Why does this formula work? $R\%$ per annum means "\$R earned for every \$100 in $1$ year." So on $\$P$, the interest for $1$ year is $\frac{P \times R}{100}$. Over $T$ years, you earn that same amount $T$ times, so we multiply by $T$.

And the total balance is:

$$A = P + \text{SI}$$

### Worked Examples

**Example 1:** \$5,000 is deposited at $8\%$ per annum for $3$ years. Find the Simple Interest and the Amount.

- $\text{SI} = \frac{5000 \times 8 \times 3}{100} = \frac{120{,}000}{100} = \mathbf{\$1,200}$
- $\text{Amount} = 5{,}000 + 1{,}200 = \mathbf{\$6,200}$

**Check:** $8\%$ of $\$5,000 = \$400$ per year. For $3$ years: $400 \times 3 = \$1,200$ ✅

**Example 2:** Find the SI and Amount on \$12,000 at $5\%$ per annum for $2$ years.

- $\text{SI} = \frac{12000 \times 5 \times 2}{100} = \frac{120{,}000}{100} = \mathbf{\$1,200}$
- $\text{Amount} = 12{,}000 + 1{,}200 = \mathbf{\$13,200}$

**Check:** $5\%$ of $\$12,000 = \$600$ per year. For $2$ years: $\$1,200$ ✅

**Example 3:** Find the SI and Amount on \$200,000 at $6\%$ per annum for $4$ years.

- $\text{SI} = \frac{200{,}000 \times 6 \times 4}{100} = \frac{4{,}800{,}000}{100} = \mathbf{\$48,000}$
- $\text{Amount} = 200{,}000 + 48{,}000 = \mathbf{\$248,000}$

**Check:** $6\%$ of $\$200,000 = \$12,000$ per year. For $4$ years: $\$48,000$ ✅

### One Key Trait of Simple Interest: The Same Every Year

Let us apply $10\%$ SI to Maya's \$10,000 for $3$ years:

| Year | Principal earning interest | Interest for that year |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | \$10,000 | \$1,000 |
| $2$ | \$10,000 | \$1,000 |
| $3$ | \$10,000 | \$1,000 |
| **Total** | | **\$3,000** |

The interest is **identical** every year because the principal never changes from \$10,000. If you plotted the total balance on a graph, it would form a straight line (increasing by the exact same step each year).

**Check:** $\text{SI} = \frac{10{,}000 \times 10 \times 3}{100} = \$3,000$ ✅. $\text{Amount} = \$13,000$.

## Part B: The Idea of Compound Interest

Now let us look at Dr. Bennett's **Method 2**.

In **Compound Interest**, at the end of each **compounding period** (for now, let us take $1\text{ period} = 1\text{ year}$), the interest earned is **added to the principal**. Next year's interest is then calculated on this **new, larger principal**.

Picture the chain like this:

> Year 1 principal $\rightarrow$ earns interest $\rightarrow$ added to principal $\rightarrow$ **larger Year 2 principal** $\rightarrow$ earns larger interest $\rightarrow$ added again $\rightarrow$ **even larger Year 3 principal** $\dots$

This is **"interest on interest."** Two sources of growth are working for you at once: interest on the original money, and interest on all the interest earned so far.

### Side-by-Side: \$10,000 at 10% per annum for 3 Years

For now, simply **observe** these numbers to see the difference clearly. In Topic 2, we will learn step by step how to compute them ourselves.

| Year | **Simple Interest** | | **Compound Interest** | |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | Principal | Interest | Principal | Interest |
| $1$ | \$10,000 | \$1,000 | \$10,000 | \$1,000 |
| $2$ | \$10,000 | \$1,000 | \$11,000 | \$1,100 |
| $3$ | \$10,000 | \$1,000 | \$12,100 | \$1,210 |
| **Total Interest** | | **\$3,000** | | **\$3,310** |
| **Amount** | | **\$13,000** | | **\$13,310** |

Look closely at the table:

- **In Year 1, both are equal** (\$1,000). There has not yet been any prior interest to earn interest on.
- **The gap begins in Year 2.** Under CI, the principal has grown to \$11,000, so the interest rises to \$1,100.
- By Year 3, CI earns \$1,210 in interest, while SI is still stuck at \$1,000.
- Over $3$ years, CI earns **\$310 more** than SI ($3{,}310 - 3{,}000$).

**Check:** Total SI $= 1{,}000 + 1{,}000 + 1{,}000 = 3{,}000$ ✅. Total CI $= 1{,}000 + 1{,}100 + 1{,}210 = 3{,}310$ ✅. Difference $= 3{,}310 - 3{,}000 = 310$ ✅. Amount (CI) $= 10{,}000 + 3{,}310 = 13{,}310$ ✅

### Why Does This Difference Keep Growing?

Under SI, the interest stays **constant** every year. Under CI, each year's interest is **larger than the year before**, because the principal keeps growing. Just like a small snowball rolling downhill gathers more snow and grows faster with every turn, money under compound interest accelerates over time.

## Part C: Where Does Compound Interest Appear in Real Life?

- **Savings Accounts & Certificates of Deposit:** Just like Maya's story. Banks regularly add earned interest back into your balance, letting you earn interest on interest.
- **College Funds & Long-Term Investments:** Leave savings untouched in a compounding account for many years, and the pace of growth speeds up noticeably on its own.
- **Loans:** When you borrow money and miss payments, unpaid interest can be added to the loan balance — meaning future interest is charged on that unpaid interest too. That is why paying off loans promptly is smart financial practice.
- **Credit Cards (a quick glimpse):** If you do not pay your full monthly statement on time, interest is charged on the remaining balance — and next month, interest is charged on that interest as well. That is why credit card bills should always be paid in full and on time.

Keep in mind: every bank and loan has its own rules, and many accounts compound **more often than once a year** (such as every $6$ months or every $3$ months). We will explore all of those in upcoming topics. For now, lock in the core idea: **earned interest joins the principal and goes to work too**.

## Part D: Small Rate, Long Time — Big Difference

The true power of CI shows up over long stretches of time.

Consider Maya's \$10,000 at $10\%$ per annum:

- Under **Simple Interest**, \$1,000 is added each year. For the money to double (to \$20,000), it takes **$10$ years** (since $10 \times \$1,000 = \$10,000$ interest, and $\$10,000 + \$10,000 = \$20,000$).
- Under **Compound Interest**, the money doubles in roughly **$7$ to $8$ years** — **several years sooner than SI**.

> **Note:** This "$7$ to $8$ years" figure is an **approximation** to show how much faster CI grows. We will verify the exact calculation in Topic 3 once we derive the compound interest formula.

Even better, every $7$ to $8$ years after that, the balance roughly doubles again! Under SI, earning another \$10,000 always takes another $10$ full years. That is why experienced savers say: **"Start saving early, and give compound interest time to work."**

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking SI and CI give different interest in the first year.**
   They do not. For the first year, both are **identical** ($10\%$ of \$10,000 is \$1,000 in both cases). The difference only starts in Year 2.

2. **Mistake 2: Mixing up Interest and Amount.**
   Interest is only the **extra money earned** (such as \$3,310). Amount is the **total money at the end** (\$13,310). Always remember: $A = P + I$.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking the interest rate increases each year under CI.**
   No. The rate ($10\%$) stays the same. What increases each year is the **principal on which interest is calculated**.

4. **Mistake 4: Assuming CI is always vastly larger than SI.**
   Over short periods (like $1$ year), they are **equal**. The big gap only appears when the time period is longer.

5. **Mistake 5: Forgetting to divide by $100$ in the Simple Interest formula.**
   $\text{SI} = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$. In Example 1, $5{,}000 \times 8 \times 3 = 120{,}000$, but the SI is \$1,200 because you must divide by $100$.

## Quick Recap

- **Principal ($P$)** $=$ original sum. **Rate ($R\%$)** $=$ interest per \$100 per year. **Time ($T$)** $=$ years. **Amount $= P + \text{Interest}$**.
- **Per annum (p.a.)** $=$ per year.
- **Simple Interest:** $\text{SI} = \frac{P \times R \times T}{100}$. Calculated only on the original principal, so it stays **the same every year**.
- **Compound Interest:** At the end of each period, interest is added to the principal, making next period's principal larger — **interest on interest**.
- For \$10,000 at $10\%$ over $3$ years: $\text{SI} = \$3,000$, $\text{CI} = \$3,310$. Equal in Year 1; CI pulls ahead afterward.
- CI appears in bank savings certificates, college funds, loans, and unpaid credit card balances.
- Even at a modest rate, a long time horizon makes CI grow dramatically faster than SI (doubling in roughly $7$–$8$ years at $10\%$, an approximation).
