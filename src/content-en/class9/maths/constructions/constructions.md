# Topic 1: The Geometry Box and Basic Constructions

So far in geometry, we have mostly practised **reasoning and proving**: in Lines and Angles we explored line segments and perpendiculars; in Circles we worked with radii and arcs; and in Congruence of Triangles we mastered rules such as SSS and SAS. In this chapter, we will learn how to **build figures by hand** — drawing completely accurate geometric diagrams using only a ruler and a compass, while also understanding the "why" behind every step.

Here is the neat connection: the very congruence rules we have already studied will prove that our constructions are mathematically exact.

---

## The Scout Camp Lantern

At a scout camp, Dr. Bennett stretched a long rope taut between two trees. At each tree, the rope was tied to a wooden peg: call the left peg **$A$** and the right peg **$B$**. The rope ran in a straight, tight line from $A$ to $B$.

Dr. Bennett turned to Leo, one of the scouts, and said: "Tonight we need to hang our lantern **at the exact midpoint** of this rope so the light reaches both ends equally. We do not have a measuring tape, though — only this loop of string and a stick. How can you find the exact middle?"

Leo thought for a moment. "If I just eyeball it and guess, the lantern could hang off-centre and tilt to one side." Then a clever strategy came to him. He held one end of the string at peg $A$, tied the stick to the other end, pulled the string tight, and scratched **a circular arc** onto the ground with the stick. Next, using the exact same length of string without changing it, he held one end at peg $B$ and scratched another arc. The two arcs crossed at one spot above the rope and one spot below it. When Leo stretched a straight line between those two crossing spots, the point where it met the rope was the **exact midpoint**.

Dr. Bennett folded the rope to check, placing end $A$ onto end $B$ — the crease landed right on Leo's mark. Leo hung the lantern there.

Did Leo perform magic? Not at all. He used a classic method known in geometry as a **perpendicular bisector**. By the end of this topic, we will draw this entire rope diagram step by step. First, let us get to know the tools in our geometry box.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Construction** | Drawing a figure accurately using only a ruler and a compass, never by guessing | A $6\text{ cm}$ line segment, or finding an exact midpoint |
| **Compass** | A drafting tool with a needle on one leg and a pencil on the other, used to draw arcs and circles | Drawing an arc of radius $4\text{ cm}$ from $A$ |
| **Divider** | A tool with needles on both legs used to measure and transfer lengths (a compass is often used for the same job) | Transferring the length of $AB$ onto $CD$ |
| **Arc** | A curved piece of a circle | A small circular arc drawn from centre $A$ |
| **Bisect** | To divide something into two equal parts | Bisecting segment $AB$ |
| **Midpoint** | The point halfway along a segment, dividing it into two equal parts | Point $M$ when $AM = MB$ |
| **Perpendicular bisector** | A line that cuts a segment at its midpoint at a $90^\circ$ angle | Line $PQ$ intersecting $AB$ at $M$ at $90^\circ$ |
| **Equidistant** | At the same distance from two given points or objects | Point $P$ equidistant from $A$ and $B$ ($PA = PB$) |

## Part A: What Is Inside a Geometry Box

| Tool | What it is used for |
|---|---|
| **Pencil** | Plotting points and drawing crisp lines — use a sharp HB or 2H pencil |
| **Ruler (straightedge)** | **Drawing straight lines** and **measuring lengths** only; in geometry we never use a ruler to draw arcs or angles |
| **Compass** | Drawing **arcs and circles**, and transferring a length accurately from one place to another |
| **Protractor** | Measuring and drawing angles (we will work with angle constructions in Topic 2) |
| **Set squares (two)** | Triangular tools: one $45^\circ$-$45^\circ$-$90^\circ$ and one $30^\circ$-$60^\circ$-$90^\circ$, useful for checking straight lines and $90^\circ$ angles |
| **Eraser** | Removing mistakes or stray smudges — **never erase your construction arcs**, as they are the proof of your method |

**Our core partnership throughout this chapter is: ruler (for straight lines) and compass (for arcs).** The protractor and set squares will mostly be used to verify our work.

### How to Hold a Compass

- Hold the compass **by the small knob at the top**, using your **thumb and two fingers**. Never hold it by the two legs, or the opening will shift as you draw.
- Press the needle point gently into the paper so it does not slip, without poking a large hole through the sheet.
- Make sure the needle tip and the pencil tip sit at the **same level** when the legs are closed.
- Keep the hinge screw tight. If the screw is loose, the compass will widen or narrow mid-arc.
- Swing each arc in one smooth, light, continuous motion rather than stopping and restarting.

### Keeping Your Pencil Sharp

- A sharp pencil produces thin, clean lines and tiny, unmistakable intersection points. Thick, blunt marks hide the exact crossing point.
- Sharpen your drawing pencil well, and lightly sand the tip if needed for a fine point.
- Keep the lead inside your compass sharp too, angled slightly on one side.
- Mark every key point with a crisp **dot** and label it immediately ($A$, $B$, $C$, $\dots$).

## Part B: Drawing a Line Segment of a Given Length

> **Drawing a segment:** "Construct a line segment $AB = 6\text{ cm}$."

**Step 1:** Use a ruler to draw a light, straight line. Near the left end, mark a clear dot and label it **$A$**.

**Step 2:** Place the **$0\text{ cm}$ mark** of the ruler directly on point $A$ (not the physical plastic edge of the ruler, which may be slightly offset from $0$). Along the ruler, mark a crisp dot at the **$6\text{ cm}$ mark** and label it **$B$**.

**Step 3:** Draw a firm line segment joining $A$ and $B$ along the ruler.

> **Figure 1.1** – Line segment $AB$ of length $6\text{ cm}$, with $A$ on the left and $B$ on the right.

**Check:** Re-align the ruler: $A$ sits at $0$ and $B$ sits at $6\text{ cm}$. ✅

## Part C: Copying a Line Segment Using a Compass

> **Copying a segment:** "Given a line segment $AB$ (say $5\text{ cm}$), construct an equal segment $CD$ without measuring its length with a ruler."

A compass lets us pick up a distance **without reading any numbers** and place it elsewhere.

**Step 1:** Use a ruler to draw a new straight line $l$. Mark a point **$C$** on it.

**Step 2:** Place the **needle of the compass on $A$** and open the compass until the **pencil tip touches $B$**. The compass opening now equals $AB$. Do not change this opening.

**Step 3:** With **centre $C$** and **radius equal to $AB$** (already set on your compass), draw an arc that cuts line $l$. Label the intersection point **$D$**.

> **Figure 1.2** – On line $l$, segment $CD$ equals the given segment $AB$ ($5\text{ cm}$), where $A$ and $B$ are endpoints of a separate segment.

**Check:** Measure both with a ruler: $AB = 5\text{ cm}$ and $CD = 5\text{ cm}$. They are equal. ✅

**Why is this exact?** Every point on an arc lies at the same distance (the radius) from its centre — that is the defining property of a circle ([circles.md](../circles/circles.md)). Since the radius was set to $AB$, the distance from $C$ to $D$ must equal $AB$.

## Part D: Perpendicular Bisector of a Line Segment

This is Leo's rope trick from the camp! Let us perform it formally with a ruler and compass.

> **Constructing a perpendicular bisector:** "Given segment $AB = 6\text{ cm}$, construct its perpendicular bisector."

First, draw segment $AB = 6\text{ cm}$ (as in Part B).

**Step 1:** Open the compass to **more than half** the length of $AB$. Since $AB = 6\text{ cm}$, half is $3\text{ cm}$. Anything greater than $3\text{ cm}$ works — let us choose **$4\text{ cm}$**. Keep this opening fixed.

**Step 2:** With **centre $A$** and **radius $4\text{ cm}$**, draw one arc **above** $AB$ and another arc **below** $AB$. (Make each arc long enough to extend clearly across the middle region on both sides of $AB$.)

**Step 3:** With **centre $B$** and the **same radius $4\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$ to cut the arcs from Step 2. Label the upper intersection **$P$** and the lower intersection **$Q$**.

**Step 4:** Use a ruler to join $P$ and $Q$. Label the point where line $PQ$ meets segment $AB$ as **$M$**.

> **Figure 1.3** – Segment $AB = 6\text{ cm}$; arcs of radius $4\text{ cm}$ from $A$ and $B$ intersect at $P$ above $AB$ and $Q$ below $AB$; perpendicular bisector $PQ$ cuts $AB$ at midpoint $M$ with $\angle AMP = 90^\circ$ and $AM = MB = 3\text{ cm}$.

**Check:**
- Measure $AM$ and $MB$ with a ruler: both equal $3\text{ cm}$. ✅
- Place the $90^\circ$ corner of a set square (or a protractor) at $M$: $\angle AMP = 90^\circ$. ✅
- Bonus check: measure $PA$ and $PB$ (both equal $4\text{ cm}$), and $QA$ and $QB$ (both $4\text{ cm}$). ✅

### Why "More Than Half"?

Since $AB = 6\text{ cm}$, half of $AB$ is $3\text{ cm}$:
- **Radius $2\text{ cm}$ (less than half):** The arc from $A$ reaches only $2\text{ cm}$ from $A$, and the arc from $B$ reaches only $2\text{ cm}$ from $B$. A $2\text{ cm}$ gap remains in the middle ($6 - 2 - 2 = 2$). **The arcs never meet.**
- **Radius $3\text{ cm}$ (exactly half):** The arcs just graze each other at a single point $M$ on $AB$. One point cannot determine a line.
- **Radius $4\text{ cm}$ (more than half):** The arcs cross at **two** distinct points, $P$ above and $Q$ below, defining a unique straight line $PQ$.

Therefore, always set the compass radius to **more than half of $AB$**.

### Why Does It Work?

Triangle congruence explains Leo's method completely.

**Given:** Segment $AB$. Arcs of **equal radius** from $A$ and $B$ intersect at $P$ and $Q$. Line $PQ$ meets $AB$ at $M$.

**To prove:** $AM = MB$ and $\angle AMP = 90^\circ$.

**Proof:**
1. Since $P$ lies on arcs of the same radius centred at $A$ and $B$, $AP = BP$. Similarly, for $Q$, $AQ = BQ$.
2. In $\triangle APQ$ and $\triangle BPQ$:
   - $AP = BP$ (equal radii)
   - $AQ = BQ$ (equal radii)
   - $PQ = PQ$ (common side)

   Therefore, $\triangle APQ \cong \triangle BPQ$ (by **SSS**). Thus, $\angle APQ = \angle BPQ$ (CPCT).
3. Now in $\triangle APM$ and $\triangle BPM$:
   - $AP = BP$ (equal radii)
   - $\angle APM = \angle BPM$ (from Step 2, since $M$ lies on $PQ$)
   - $PM = PM$ (common side)

   Therefore, $\triangle APM \cong \triangle BPM$ (by **SAS**). Hence, **$AM = MB$** and $\angle AMP = \angle BMP$ (CPCT).
4. Since $A, M, B$ lie on a straight line, $\angle AMP + \angle BMP = 180^\circ$ (linear pair). Because the two angles are equal, each equals **$90^\circ$**.

Thus $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$ and $PQ \perp AB$. (To review SSS and SAS, see [congruence-of-triangles-part2.md](../congruence-of-triangles/congruence-of-triangles-part2.md).)

**An extra consequence:** **Every point** on line $PQ$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$ (just as $PA = PB$). We will put this to work in Example 4!

## Part E: Dividing a Segment into 2 and 4 Equal Parts

**2 equal parts:** Simply construct the perpendicular bisector once. Point $M$ divides $AB$ into two equal halves: $AM = MB$.

**4 equal parts:** Apply the bisector **again** to each half, $AM$ and $MB$.

> **Dividing a segment into 4 equal parts:** "Divide $AB = 8\text{ cm}$ into $4$ equal parts. Each part should measure $2\text{ cm}$."

**Step 1:** Draw $AB = 8\text{ cm}$. With **centre $A$** and **radius $5\text{ cm}$** (more than half of $8\text{ cm}$, which is $4\text{ cm}$), draw arcs above and below $AB$. With **centre $B$** and **radius $5\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$ to intersect at **$P$** (above) and **$Q$** (below). Join $PQ$; it cuts $AB$ at **$M$**, giving $AM = MB = 4\text{ cm}$.

**Step 2 (bisect $AM$):** Since $AM = 4\text{ cm}$, half is $2\text{ cm}$. Choose a radius of **$3\text{ cm}$** (more than $2\text{ cm}$). With **centre $A$** and **radius $3\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$. With **centre $M$** and **radius $3\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$ to meet at **$R$** (above) and **$S$** (below). Join $RS$; it cuts $AM$ at **$X$**, giving $AX = XM = 2\text{ cm}$.

**Step 3 (bisect $MB$):** Since $MB = 4\text{ cm}$, keep the radius at **$3\text{ cm}$**. With **centre $M$** and **radius $3\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$. With **centre $B$** and **radius $3\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$ to meet at **$T$** (above) and **$U$** (below). Join $TU$; it cuts $MB$ at **$Y$**, giving $MY = YB = 2\text{ cm}$.

> **Figure 1.4** – Segment $AB = 8\text{ cm}$ with points $A, X, M, Y, B$ in order and $AX = XM = MY = YB = 2\text{ cm}$. Bisectors $PQ$ (at $M$), $RS$ (at $X$), and $TU$ (at $Y$) meet $AB$ at $90^\circ$.

**Check:** Measure from $A$ with a ruler: $AX = 2\text{ cm}$, $AM = 4\text{ cm}$, $AY = 6\text{ cm}$, $AB = 8\text{ cm}$. All four parts are $2\text{ cm}$ long: $2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8$. ✅

**Note:** Repeated bisection lets us divide a segment into $2, 4, 8, \dots$ (powers of $2$) equal parts. Dividing into $3$ or $5$ equal parts requires a different technique not covered in this chapter.

## Part F: Solving the Story — Finding the Midpoint of the Rope

Let us draw Leo's scout camp diagram step by step. Suppose the rope is $8\text{ m}$ long, and we draw it on paper at a scale of $1\text{ cm} = 1\text{ m}$, so $AB = 8\text{ cm}$. The loop of string was $6\text{ m}$ long, which becomes $6\text{ cm}$ on paper.

**Step 1:** Represent the rope as segment $AB$ ($A$ for the left peg, $B$ for the right peg). Half of $AB$ is $4\text{ cm}$. The string radius of $6\text{ cm}$ is greater than $4\text{ cm}$, so the arcs will intersect.

**Step 2:** With **centre $A$** and **radius $6\text{ cm}$ (the string length)**, draw arcs above and below the rope $AB$.

**Step 3:** With **centre $B$** and the **same radius $6\text{ cm}$** (the same unstretched string), draw arcs above and below $AB$ to meet the first arcs at **$P$** (above) and **$Q$** (below).

**Step 4:** Join $P$ and $Q$. This line cuts the rope $AB$ at **$M$**.

> **Figure 1.5** – Rope $AB = 8\text{ cm}$ (to scale); arcs of radius $6\text{ cm}$ from $A$ and $B$ intersect at $P$ and $Q$; line $PQ$ meets the rope at its midpoint $M$, with $AM = MB = 4\text{ cm}$.

**Check (Dr. Bennett's method):** Fold the rope so peg $A$ meets peg $B$ — the fold lands at $M$. On paper with a ruler: $AM = 4\text{ cm}$ and $MB = 4\text{ cm}$. ✅

The lantern hangs right at $M$ — no measuring tape and no guesswork needed!

## Part G: Solved Constructions

### Example 1: Find the midpoint of a segment

**Question:** Draw a line segment $XY = 9\text{ cm}$ and construct its midpoint.

**Step 1:** Use a ruler to draw $XY = 9\text{ cm}$. Half of $9\text{ cm}$ is $4.5\text{ cm}$, so choose a compass radius of **$5\text{ cm}$** (greater than $4.5\text{ cm}$).

**Step 2:** With **centre $X$** and **radius $5\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $XY$.

**Step 3:** With **centre $Y$** and **radius $5\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $XY$ to cut the previous arcs at **$P$** (above) and **$Q$** (below).

**Step 4:** Join $P$ and $Q$. Line $PQ$ meets $XY$ at **$M$**, which is the required midpoint.

> **Figure 1.6** – Segment $XY = 9\text{ cm}$ with midpoint $M$; perpendicular bisector $PQ$ cuts $XY$ at $M$ at $90^\circ$, giving $XM = MY = 4.5\text{ cm}$.

**Check:** With a ruler, $XM = 4.5\text{ cm}$ and $MY = 4.5\text{ cm}$ ($4.5 + 4.5 = 9$ ✅). With a protractor, $\angle XMP = 90^\circ$ ✅.

---

### Example 2: Mark a point $1.25\text{ cm}$ along a segment

**Question:** Draw $AB = 5\text{ cm}$. Without measuring $1.25\text{ cm}$ directly with a ruler, locate a point $X$ on $AB$ that is $1.25\text{ cm}$ from $A$.

**Thinking:** $5 \div 2 = 2.5$, and $2.5 \div 2 = 1.25$. That means bisecting twice!

**Step 1:** Draw $AB = 5\text{ cm}$. Half is $2.5\text{ cm}$, so set the compass to **$3\text{ cm}$**. With **centre $A$** and **radius $3\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$. With **centre $B$** and **radius $3\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$ to meet at **$P$** and **$Q$**. Join $PQ$; it cuts $AB$ at **$M$**, so $AM = 2.5\text{ cm}$.

**Step 2:** Half of $AM$ is $1.25\text{ cm}$, so set the compass to **$2\text{ cm}$** (more than $1.25\text{ cm}$). With **centre $A$** and **radius $2\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$. With **centre $M$** and **radius $2\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$ to meet at **$R$** and **$S$**. Join $RS$; it cuts $AM$ at **$X$**.

> **Figure 1.7** – Segment $AB = 5\text{ cm}$ showing midpoint $M$ and quarter-point $X$ from $A$: $AX = 1.25\text{ cm}$, $XM = 1.25\text{ cm}$, $MB = 2.5\text{ cm}$. Bisector $PQ$ passes through $M$, and bisector $RS$ passes through $X$.

**Check:** Measure $AX$ with a ruler: $1.25\text{ cm}$ ($12.5\text{ mm}$). Indeed, one-quarter of $AB$ is $5 \div 4 = 1.25\text{ cm}$. ✅

---

### Example 3: Sum of two segments

**Question:** Given $AB = 4\text{ cm}$ and $CD = 3\text{ cm}$, construct a segment $PR$ equal to $AB + CD$ by transferring lengths with a compass only.

**Step 1:** Use a ruler to draw a straight line $l$ and mark a point **$P$** on it.

**Step 2:** Place the compass needle at $A$ and the pencil at $B$ to capture $AB = 4\text{ cm}$. With **centre $P$** and **radius $AB$**, cut an arc on line $l$. Label the intersection **$Q$**, so $PQ = 4\text{ cm}$.

**Step 3:** Now place the compass needle at $C$ and the pencil at $D$ to capture $CD = 3\text{ cm}$. With **centre $Q$** and **radius $CD$**, cut an arc on line $l$ **beyond $Q$** (away from $P$). Label the intersection **$R$**, so $QR = 3\text{ cm}$.

> **Figure 1.8** – Points $P, Q, R$ in order on line $l$, with $PQ = AB = 4\text{ cm}$ and $QR = CD = 3\text{ cm}$, giving $PR = AB + CD = 7\text{ cm}$.

**Check:** Measure $PR$ with a ruler: $4 + 3 = 7\text{ cm}$. ✅

### Example 4: A fountain equidistant from two towns

**Question:** On a map, two towns $A$ and $B$ are $6\text{ cm}$ apart. Locate a point $W$ that is **$5\text{ cm}$** from both $A$ and $B$.

**Thinking:** Any point equidistant from $A$ and $B$ lies on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$. We use the same intersecting-arc method, simply setting the compass radius to $5\text{ cm}$.

**Step 1:** Draw $AB = 6\text{ cm}$. Half is $3\text{ cm}$, and since our radius $5\text{ cm}$ is greater than $3\text{ cm}$, the arcs will cross.

**Step 2:** With **centre $A$** and **radius $5\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$. With **centre $B$** and **radius $5\text{ cm}$**, draw arcs above and below $AB$. Label the upper intersection **$W$** and the lower intersection **$W'$**. Both points are $5\text{ cm}$ from $A$ and from $B$.

**Step 3:** Use a ruler to join $W$ and $W'$. This perpendicular bisector meets $AB$ at **$M$**. Also join $W$ to $A$ and $B$.

> **Figure 1.9** – Segment $AB = 6\text{ cm}$ and point $W$ above $AB$ at distance $5\text{ cm}$ from both $A$ and $B$ ($WA = WB = 5\text{ cm}$); $M$ is the midpoint of $AB$, with $WM \perp AB$ and $WM = 4\text{ cm}$. The lower point $W'$ is also $5\text{ cm}$ from both $A$ and $B$.

**Check:** With a ruler, $WA = 5\text{ cm}$ and $WB = 5\text{ cm}$ ✅. Measure $WM$: $4\text{ cm}$. Notice the classic $3$-$4$-$5$ right triangle ($AM = 3$, $WM = 4$, $WA = 5$): $3^2 + 4^2 = 9 + 16 = 25 = 5^2$ ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Setting the radius to less than half the segment.**
   If $AB = 8\text{ cm}$ and you set the radius to $3\text{ cm}$ (half is $4\text{ cm}$), the arc from $A$ reaches $3\text{ cm}$ and the arc from $B$ reaches $3\text{ cm}$, leaving a gap of $8 - 3 - 3 = 2\text{ cm}$. **The arcs will never meet.** Always choose a radius strictly greater than half of $AB$.

2. **Mistake 2: Changing the compass opening between the two arcs.**
   If you draw a $5\text{ cm}$ arc from $A$ and a $4.5\text{ cm}$ arc from $B$, they meet at a point $P$ where $PA \neq PB$. Then $PQ$ will not pass through the midpoint, and $M$ will not bisect $AB$. Both arcs must have the **exact same radius** (our proof depended on $AP = BP$).

3. **Mistake 3: Failing to mark or label intersection points.**
   Guessing where two arcs cross without placing a crisp dot causes the ruler to tilt slightly off-centre. Always mark every crossing with a small dot and label $P, Q, M$ right away.

4. **Mistake 4: Drawing with a blunt pencil.**
   A thick, blunt lead produces wide smudges, making it impossible to see the exact intersection point — even a $0.5\text{ cm}$ error can creep in. Keep both pencils sharp.

5. **Mistake 5: Using a ruler for arcs or a compass as a straightedge.**
   A ruler is strictly for straight lines and measuring lengths; a compass is for arcs and transferring lengths. Use each tool for its proper job.

## Quick Recap

- **Geometry box essentials:** sharp pencil, ruler (straight lines and measurements), compass (arcs and copying lengths), protractor, two set squares, and an eraser.
- Hold the compass by its top knob, keep the needle and pencil tips level, and keep the hinge screw tight.
- **Drawing $AB = 6\text{ cm}$:** align the $0\text{ cm}$ mark at $A$ and mark $B$ at $6\text{ cm}$.
- **Copying a segment:** place the needle at $A$ and pencil at $B$, then swing an arc of that radius from a new centre $C$.
- **Perpendicular bisector:** from $A$ and $B$, draw arcs of the **same radius (greater than $\frac{1}{2}AB$)** above and below $AB$. Join intersections $P$ and $Q$. Line $PQ$ meets $AB$ at $M$ such that **$AM = MB$** and **$\angle AMP = 90^\circ$**.
- **Why it works:** $\triangle APQ \cong \triangle BPQ$ by SSS, followed by $\triangle APM \cong \triangle BPM$ by SAS.
- Bisecting $AM$ and $MB$ again divides $AB$ into $4$ equal parts.
- Avoid pitfalls: never use a radius smaller than half, never change the opening mid-way, always mark intersections cleanly, and keep your pencil sharp.

6. Construct a point $W$ that is $5\text{ cm}$ from each of two points $A$ and $B$ with $AB = 8\text{ cm}$. How far is $W$ from the midpoint $M$ of $AB$?

7. State True or False:
   (a) A ruler can be used to draw an arc in geometry constructions.
   (b) If the compass opening is exactly half of $AB$, the arcs from $A$ and $B$ meet at two points.
   (c) The perpendicular bisector of $AB$ meets $AB$ at $90^\circ$.
   (d) It is fine to change the compass opening between the arc from $A$ and the arc from $B$.

8. Dr. Bennett's rope is $10\text{ m}$ long and Leo's string loop is $7\text{ m}$ long. Will Leo's trick work? What if the string were only $4\text{ m}$ long?

### Answers

1. $AB = 7.5\text{ cm}$, so half is $3.75\text{ cm}$. We need a radius **greater than $3.75\text{ cm}$**, such as **$4.5\text{ cm}$**. Draw $4.5\text{ cm}$ arcs from $A$ and $B$ above and below $AB$, and join intersections $P$ and $Q$. Then $AM = 7.5 \div 2 = \mathbf{3.75\text{ cm}}$.
   Why it works: because the radius exceeds half of $AB$, the arcs intersect at two distinct points.
   Check: $AM = MB = 3.75\text{ cm}$ and $\angle AMP = 90^\circ$ ✅.

2. $PQ = 12\text{ cm}$. First bisector: half is $6\text{ cm}$, so use radius **$7\text{ cm}$** ($> 6\text{ cm}$). This gives midpoint $M$ with $PM = MQ = 6\text{ cm}$. Next, bisect $PM$ and $MQ$: half of each is $3\text{ cm}$, so use radius **$4\text{ cm}$** ($> 3\text{ cm}$). Each of the four parts measures **$3\text{ cm}$**.
   Check: $3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12$ ✅.

3. Half of $AB = 10\text{ cm}$ is $5\text{ cm}$, and $4\text{ cm}$ is less than $5\text{ cm}$. The arc from $A$ reaches only $4\text{ cm}$ from $A$, and the arc from $B$ reaches only $4\text{ cm}$ from $B$. Together they cover $4 + 4 = 8\text{ cm} < 10\text{ cm}$, leaving a $2\text{ cm}$ gap in the middle, so the arcs never meet.

4. $PR = AB + CD = 4.5 + 3 = \mathbf{7.5\text{ cm}}$. $ST = AB - CD = 4.5 - 3 = \mathbf{1.5\text{ cm}}$.
   For $PR$: draw line $l$, mark $P$, cut $PQ = AB$ with centre $P$, then from centre $Q$ cut $QR = CD$ in the direction away from $P$.
   For $ST$: draw line $l$, mark $S$, cut $SU = AB$ with centre $S$, then from centre $U$ cut $UT = CD$ back toward $S$. Then $ST = AB - CD = 1.5\text{ cm}$.
   Check with ruler: $PR = 7.5\text{ cm}$ and $ST = 1.5\text{ cm}$ ✅.

5. $PB = \mathbf{7\text{ cm}}$. Every point on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ is equidistant from $A$ and $B$ ($PA = PB$, from our SSS congruence proof).

6. Half of $AB = 8\text{ cm}$ is $4\text{ cm}$, and $5\text{ cm} > 4\text{ cm}$. Arcs of radius $5\text{ cm}$ centred at $A$ and $B$ cross at $W$. Since $AM = 4\text{ cm}$ and $WA = 5\text{ cm}$ in right $\triangle WAM$, $WM = \sqrt{5^2 - 4^2} = \sqrt{25 - 16} = \sqrt{9} = \mathbf{3\text{ cm}}$.
   Check with ruler: $WM = 3\text{ cm}$ ✅.

7. (a) **False** ❌. A ruler is only for drawing straight lines and measuring lengths.
   (b) **False** ❌. If the radius is exactly half of $AB$, the arcs touch at just one point $M$. You need strictly more than half to get two intersection points.
   (c) **True** ✅. $PQ \perp AB$.
   (d) **False** ❌. Changing the opening makes $PA \neq PB$, so $PQ$ will not pass through the midpoint.

8. Half of a $10\text{ m}$ rope is $5\text{ m}$. A $7\text{ m}$ string is longer than $5\text{ m}$, so **yes, Leo's trick works** ✅. If the string were only $4\text{ m}$ long ($4 < 5$), the arcs would not meet, so **it would not work** ❌ — Leo would need a longer piece of string.
