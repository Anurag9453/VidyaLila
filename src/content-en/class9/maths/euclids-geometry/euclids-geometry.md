# Topic 1: History, Axioms, and Postulates

## A 5,000-Year-Old Story of a Farm

Imagine you are a farmer in Egypt nearly $5,000$ years ago, cultivating crops along the banks of the **Nile River**.

Every year during the rainy season, the Nile overflows its banks. When the floodwaters finally recede, a thick layer of fresh silt covers the valley, washing away every boundary marker between your land and your neighbour's. Nobody can tell where your plot ends and the next farm begins.

To prevent disputes, the royal surveyors arrive carrying **knotted measuring ropes**. They stretch the ropes taut across the fields, re-measure the boundaries, and restore each farmer's exact plot of land.

From that practical task of measuring land, a whole branch of mathematics was born: **Geometry**.

- **Geo** $=$ earth
- **Metron** $=$ to measure

Literally, **geometry means "measuring the earth."**

Of course, modern geometry goes far beyond surveying fields. In this chapter, we will see how a Greek teacher named **Euclid** transformed geometry into a logical system that has been taught in classrooms for over $2,000$ years. His secret was remarkably simple: start with a small set of **completely obvious truths** that everyone accepts without debate, and then **prove** everything else from them step by step.

## Part A: The History of Geometry

Geometry was not invented in a single place or by a single person. Civilisations across the ancient world developed geometric methods to solve real-world problems in surveying, construction, and engineering.

### Egypt

The opening story comes straight from ancient Egypt. After the annual flooding of the Nile, fields had to be re-measured and re-divided. The royal surveyors who did this work were known as **"rope-stretchers."**

Egyptian builders did far more than survey farmland: they calculated **areas** and **volumes** to construct granaries, canals, and monumental **pyramids**. However, their knowledge was largely a collection of practical **recipes and rules of thumb** — "follow this procedure to get this result" — without formal proofs explaining *why* those rules always held true.

### Indus Valley Civilisation

Around **$3000\text{ BCE}$**, cities such as Harappa and Mohenjo-daro were laid out with striking geometric precision:
- Streets were arranged in neat grids intersecting at **right angles ($90^\circ$)**.
- Covered drainage channels followed uniform gradients and alignments.
- Standardised bricks followed a strict **length : width : thickness** ratio of **$4 : 2 : 1$**, allowing walls to interlock cleanly and bear heavy loads.

None of this large-scale urban planning could have succeeded without solid geometric measurement.

### Sulbasutras (Ancient India)

Between roughly **$800\text{ BCE}$ and $500\text{ BCE}$**, texts known as the **Sulbasutras** were composed in ancient India (*sulba* means **cord** or **measuring rope**). They served as architectural manuals for constructing geometric fire altars.

- Builders had to lay out shapes such as squares, rectangles, and circles with great accuracy.
- Often, a square altar and a circular altar had to have the **exact same area**.
- Solving these problems demanded deep insight into lengths, angles, and areas.

### Greece

Ancient Greek thinkers gave geometry a dramatic new turn by asking: **"Why must this be true?"**

- **Thales** ($640$–$546\text{ BCE}$) is widely credited with giving the first formal geometric **proof**, demonstrating that a diameter always divides a circle into two equal halves.
- **Pythagoras** (around $572\text{ BCE}$), traditionally considered a student of Thales, studied properties of numbers and triangles; the famous theorem bearing his name is one you will explore in later classes.

### Euclid and the "Elements"

Now we meet the central figure of this chapter: **Euclid**.

- Euclid taught mathematics in **Alexandria**, Egypt, around **$300\text{ BCE}$**.
- He gathered all the geometric knowledge known in his era and arranged it into a single, systematic masterpiece called the **Elements**.
- The *Elements* consists of **13 books** (chapters or volumes).
- Because of this monumental work, Euclid is often called the **"Father of Geometry."**

### What Was Euclid's True Genius?

People knew plenty of geometry before Euclid, yet it existed as scattered facts and disconnected techniques. Euclid introduced a unified logical method:

1. Choose a handful of **simple, self-evident statements** that anyone will readily accept. (These are the axioms and postulates.)
2. Accept them **without requiring proof**.
3. **Prove every other result** logically, one step at a time, starting only from those foundations.

Think of it like constructing a tall **building**:

```
          [ Major Theorems ]          <- upper floors
         [ Smaller Theorems ]
        [ First Proofs       ]
   ==============================
   [ Axioms + Postulates ]            <- foundation
```

If the foundation is rock-solid, every floor built on top of it stands firm. Euclid's axioms and postulates form that foundation, and modern mathematics still follows this exact blueprint today.

## Part B: What Are Axioms and Postulates?

### The Rules of a Game Analogy

Imagine you and your friends are setting up an outdoor game of street hockey or rounders in the park. Before you start, everyone agrees on a few ground rules:
- "The oak tree marks the far boundary line."
- "If the ball bounces off the bench, play continues."

Nobody demands a mathematical "proof" for those rules. Everyone simply **agrees to accept them** so the game can begin — and the entire match runs on them.

Mathematics works the same way. We begin with a few basic statements that we **assume to be true without proof**. These starting assumptions are called **axioms** and **postulates**.

### The Difference Between the Two

Euclid sorted these starting assumptions into two groups:

| | **Axiom** (Common Notion) | **Postulate** |
|---|---|---|
| What is it? | An assumption accepted without proof | An assumption accepted without proof |
| Where does it apply? | **Everywhere** in mathematics: numbers, lengths, weights, money, and more | **Only in geometry** |
| Example | "If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal" | "A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius" |

In a single line:
> **Axiom $=$ universal rule for all of maths. Postulate $=$ specific rule for geometry.**

In modern mathematics, the two terms are often used interchangeably under the single name **axiom**. For this chapter, however, keeping Euclid's distinction clear is essential.

### One Crucial Point

Axioms and postulates are **never proved**. They are the starting blocks. New statements proved using them are called **theorems** (which we will study in Topic 4).

## Part C: Euclid's 7 Axioms

Let us examine Euclid's $7$ axioms (which he called *common notions*) one by one, using everyday situations and numbers to make each one crystal clear.

### Axiom 1: Things which are equal to the same thing are equal to one another.

**Everyday example:** Maya is the same height as Liam. Liam is the same height as Elena. Therefore, Maya and Elena are the exact same height — without even standing them back-to-back to measure.

**With numbers/algebra:** If **$a = c$** and **$b = c$**, then **$a = b$**.

**Example:** Suppose $a + b = 10$ and $a + b = c$. Then $c = 10$ (since both $10$ and $c$ equal $a + b$).

### Axiom 2: If equals are added to equals, the wholes are equal.

**Everyday example:** Both Leo and Sophia have $\$50$ in their savings accounts. Their aunt deposits an extra $\$20$ into each account. Now both have $\$70$ — their balances remain equal.

**With numbers/algebra:** If **$x = y$**, then **$x + 5 = y + 5$**.

### Axiom 3: If equals are subtracted from equals, the remainders are equal.

**Everyday example:** Two surveyors each start with a $12\text{ m}$ cable. Both trim $4\text{ m}$ off their cables. Each is left with $8\text{ m}$ — equal lengths remaining.

**With numbers/algebra:** If **$p + q = r + q$**, subtract $q$ from both sides: **$p = r$**.

### Axiom 4: Things which coincide with one another are equal to one another.

To **"coincide"** means that when you place one object directly on top of another, they **match edge-for-edge**, with nothing sticking out and no gaps left over.

**Everyday example:** Stack two identical standard playing cards directly on top of each other. Every edge and corner lines up perfectly, so the two cards are equal in size and shape.

This axiom becomes the cornerstone of comparing triangles (congruence) later in geometry.

**Watch out:** The reverse is not always true! Two shapes with equal areas do not have to coincide. A $2\text{ cm} \times 8\text{ cm}$ rectangle has an area of $16\text{ cm}^2$, and a $4\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm}$ square also has an area of $16\text{ cm}^2$. Their areas are equal, yet placing one on top of the other does not produce a match.

### Axiom 5: The whole is greater than the part.

**Everyday example:** An entire pizza is larger than a single slice cut from it. If a school orchestra has both string players and wind players, the total number of musicians in the orchestra is greater than the number of string players alone.

**With numbers/algebra:** If **$a = b + c$**, where $c$ is any positive quantity (such as a length, weight, or angle), then **$a > b$**.

**Example:** $10 = 7 + 3$. Here $10$ is the whole and $7$ is a part of it, so $10 > 7$.

### Axiom 6: Things which are double of the same things are equal to one another.

**Everyday example:** Leo's wooden beam is twice as long as a standard plank. Liam's beam is also twice as long as that same standard plank. Therefore, Leo's beam and Liam's beam have the exact same length.

**With numbers/algebra:** If **$x = 2y$** and **$z = 2y$**, then **$x = z$**.

### Axiom 7: Things which are halves of the same things are equal to one another.

**Everyday example:** A carpenter saws a $2\text{ m}$ board into two exact halves. Since both pieces are half of the same board, both pieces have the exact same length ($1\text{ m}$ each).

**With numbers/algebra:** If **$a = \frac{b}{2}$** and **$c = \frac{b}{2}$**, then **$a = c$**.

### All 7 Axioms at a Glance

| # | Axiom (in plain words) | Quick Example |
|---|---|---|
| $1$ | Equal to the same thing $\implies$ equal to each other | $a = c, b = c \implies a = b$ |
| $2$ | Equals added to equals $\implies$ equal wholes | $x = y \implies x + 5 = y + 5$ |
| $3$ | Equals subtracted from equals $\implies$ equal remainders | $p + q = r + q \implies p = r$ |
| $4$ | Coincide completely $\implies$ equal | Two identical playing cards |
| $5$ | Whole $>$ part | Whole pizza $>$ one slice |
| $6$ | Double of the same thing $\implies$ equal | $x = 2y, z = 2y \implies x = z$ |
| $7$ | Halves of the same thing $\implies$ equal | $a = b/2, c = b/2 \implies a = c$ |

## Part D: Euclid's 5 Postulates

Now let us turn to Euclid's five rules designed specifically for geometry. They mention terms such as *point*, *line*, and *circle*. For now, use your everyday intuition for these words — we will examine their formal definitions closely in **Topic 2**.

### Postulate 1: A straight line may be drawn from any one point to any other point.

```
   A •─────────────────────• B
```

**Everyday example:** Mark any two points $A$ and $B$ on a drafting sheet. Lay a straightedge across both points and draw a line connecting them. This is always possible, no matter where the points lie.

*(An interesting follow-up question: **how many** straight lines can pass through both $A$ and $B$? Just one, or more? We will answer that in **Topic 3**.)*

### Postulate 2: A terminated line can be produced indefinitely.

A **"terminated line"** (today called a *line segment*) is a straight piece with two endpoints. Euclid states that you can extend it in either direction as far as you wish.

```
   Before:         A •──────• B

   Extended:     ←───• A ──────• B ───→
```

**Everyday example:** Look at a straight $100\text{ m}$ stretch of railway track. In geometric space, there is no boundary wall stopping that straight path — you can imagine extending it straight ahead and straight behind forever.

### Postulate 3: A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius.

```
           . - ~ ~ - .
        .'             '.
       :        O •───── :     O = centre
        '.     radius  .'
           ' - ~ ~ - '
```

**Everyday example:** Place the metal point of a compass anywhere on your page and open the arms to any width you like. You can always sweep out a circle — tiny or huge, anywhere on the plane.

### Postulate 4: All right angles are equal to one another.

A right angle measures **$90^\circ$**, like the corner of a notebook, a doorframe, or the letter **L**.

**Everyday example:** Compare the corner of a postage stamp, the corner of a classroom whiteboard, and the corner where a skyscraper wall meets level ground. The objects are completely different sizes, yet every single one of those right angles has the exact same opening ($90^\circ$).

This may seem obvious, yet Euclid stated it explicitly so that every future proof involving right angles would stand on firm ground.

### Postulate 5: The Parallel Postulate (the Fifth and Most Famous)

Here is the full statement:

> If a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the **interior angles on the same side of it taken together less than two right angles ($180^\circ$)**, then the two straight lines, if produced indefinitely, **meet on that side** on which the sum of angles is less than two right angles.

That long sentence can look intimidating at first glance, so let us break it into four simple steps:

**Step 1:** Start with two straight lines, **$l$** and **$m$**.
**Step 2:** A third straight line **$n$** cuts across both of them.
**Step 3:** On the **right side** of $n$, look at the two **interior angles** trapped between $l$ and $m$: call them **$\angle 1$** and **$\angle 2$**.
**Step 4:** If **$\angle 1 + \angle 2 < 180^\circ$**, then extending $l$ and $m$ to the right guarantees they will **eventually meet** on the right side.

```
                 n
                 |
  l  ------------+------------------------.__
                 |  ∠1                        ``--.__
                 |                                   • P   (lines meet here)
                 |  ∠2                        __..--``
  m  ------------+------------------------.--``
                 |
```

**Everyday example:** Picture two steel train rails. If they are perfectly parallel, they never touch. However, if they tilt inward toward each other even slightly on the right, extending them further to the right will inevitably make them collide. On that inward-tilting side, the interior angles add up to less than $180^\circ$.

**Quick numerical check:**
- If $\angle 1 = 80^\circ$ and $\angle 2 = 85^\circ$, their sum is $80^\circ + 85^\circ = 165^\circ < 180^\circ$. Therefore, lines $l$ and $m$ **will meet on the right side**.
- If $\angle 1 = 90^\circ$ and $\angle 2 = 90^\circ$, their sum is $180^\circ$, which is *not* less than $180^\circ$. This postulate does not predict an intersection — such lines are parallel and never meet.

### A Simpler Form of Postulate 5: Playfair's Axiom

Because Euclid's fifth postulate is so wordy, the Scottish mathematician John Playfair popularized a simpler, mathematically equivalent version known as **Playfair's Axiom**:

> For every line **$l$** and for every point **$P$** **not lying on $l$**, there exists a **unique line** passing through $P$ and **parallel** to $l$.

```
             P
  m  --------•--------------------   (only one line through P parallel to l)

  l  -----------------------------
```

### The Remarkable Story of the Fifth Postulate (Beyond Class 9)

The first four postulates are short, crisp, and effortless to picture. The fifth feels more like a theorem than a basic assumption. For nearly $2,000$ years, brilliant mathematicians tried to **prove** Postulate 5 using only the first four postulates — and every single attempt failed.

Eventually, mathematicians discovered *why*: Postulate 5 cannot be proved from the first four! Even more astonishingly, when they replaced Postulate 5 with different rules, they uncovered entirely new, consistent worlds called **non-Euclidean geometries** — such as the geometry of curved surfaces like Earth's globe. That goes well beyond Class 9, yet it shows why this single postulate became one of the most famous statements in mathematical history.

### All 5 Postulates at a Glance

| # | Postulate (in plain words) |
|---|---|
| $1$ | Any two points can be joined by a straight line |
| $2$ | A terminated line can be extended indefinitely on both sides |
| $3$ | A circle can be drawn with any centre and any radius |
| $4$ | All right angles are equal to one another |
| $5$ | If interior angles on one side sum to less than $180^\circ$, the lines meet on that side |

## Part E: Putting Axioms to Work

Let us see how Euclid's axioms solve short geometric and algebraic problems. At each step, we will name the exact axiom in action.

### Example 1

Given **$a + b = 10$** and **$a + b = c$**, show that **$c = 10$**. Which axiom applies?

- Both $10$ and $c$ are equal to the same expression, $a + b$.
- Things equal to the same thing are equal to one another.
- Therefore, **$c = 10$**. This uses **Axiom 1**.

### Example 2

Given **$x = y$**, show that **$x + 7 = y + 7$**.

- We add the equal quantity $7$ to both equal quantities $x$ and $y$.
- The resulting wholes are equal: **$x + 7 = y + 7$**. This uses **Axiom 2**.

### Example 3 (Points on a Straight Line)

Four points **$A, B, C, D$** lie on a straight line in that order, and **$AC = BD$**. Show that **$AB = CD$**.

```
   A •-------• B -------• C -------• D
```

- $AC = AB + BC$ (travelling from $A$ to $C$ covers $AB$ followed by $BC$)
- $BD = BC + CD$ (travelling from $B$ to $D$ covers $BC$ followed by $CD$)
- Since $AC = BD$, we have **$AB + BC = BC + CD$**.
- Subtract **$BC$** from both sides (subtracting equals from equals).
- Therefore, **$AB = CD$**. This uses **Axiom 3**.

**Check with numbers:** Suppose $AB = 2$, $BC = 3$, and $CD = 2$. Then $AC = 5$ and $BD = 5$ (equal), and indeed $AB = CD = 2$ ✅

*(Here we simply spotted the axiom at work. In Topic 4, we will write out the full formal proof with Given, To Prove, and a Statement–Reason table.)*

### Example 4

A surveyor's tape is **$12\text{ m}$** long. A **$5\text{ m}$** section is cut off. Is the remaining piece shorter than the original tape?

- Whole tape ($12\text{ m}$) $=$ remaining piece ($7\text{ m}$) $+$ cut piece ($5\text{ m}$).
- The whole is greater than its part.
- Hence $12 > 7$, meaning the remaining piece is shorter than the whole tape. This uses **Axiom 5**.

### Example 5

Liam is **twice** as old as Maya, and Elena is also **twice** as old as Maya. What can you say about the ages of Liam and Elena?

- Both ages are double of the same quantity (Maya's age).
- Therefore, **Liam and Elena are the exact same age**. This uses **Axiom 6**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Trying to prove axioms or postulates.**
   Axioms and postulates are **assumed truths** at the very start of the system. You do not prove them — you use them to prove **theorems**.

2. **Mistake 2: Treating "axiom" and "postulate" as identical in Euclid's system.**
   For Euclid, an axiom applies **universally** (to numbers, weights, money, lengths), whereas a postulate applies **only to geometry**. "All right angles are equal" is a postulate, not an axiom.

3. **Mistake 3: Reversing Axiom 4.**
   Figures that coincide are always equal, yet figures with equal areas need not coincide. A $2 \times 8$ rectangle and a $4 \times 4$ square both have area $16$, yet they cannot be laid flat to match edge-for-edge.

4. **Mistake 4: Picking the wrong side in Postulate 5.**
   The two lines meet on the side where the interior angles add up to **less than $180^\circ$**, *not* the side where the sum is greater than $180^\circ$.

5. **Mistake 5: Claiming lines meet when the interior angle sum equals $180^\circ$.**
   Postulate 5 predicts an intersection only when the sum is **strictly less than $180^\circ$**. If the sum is exactly $180^\circ$, the lines are parallel and never meet.

6. **Mistake 6: Reading Postulate 1 as "exactly one line."**
   Postulate 1 only guarantees that a straight line *can be drawn* between two points. The uniqueness question ("how many lines?") is addressed separately in Topic 2 and Topic 3.

7. **Mistake 7: Thinking Euclid invented all of geometry.**
   Geometry thrived long before Euclid in Egypt, the Indus Valley, India, and Greece. Euclid's breakthrough was **organizing** that knowledge logically and **proving** every result from a minimal set of assumptions.

## Quick Recap

- **Geometry $=$ geo (earth) $+$ metron (measure)**, born from practical land surveying.
- Historical roots include **Egypt** (re-measuring Nile farms with rope-stretchers), the **Indus Valley** (right-angled streets, $4:2:1$ brick ratio), the **Sulbasutras** (geometric altars), and **Greece** (Thales, Pythagoras).
- **Euclid** (c. $300\text{ BCE}$, Alexandria) wrote the **Elements** ($13$ books) and is called the "Father of Geometry."
- Euclid's method: assume a few clear truths **without proof**, and **prove** everything else logically from them.
- **Axiom:** universal assumption across mathematics. **Postulate:** geometric assumption.
- **7 Axioms:** equal to same $\implies$ equal; equals $+$ equals; equals $-$ equals; coincide $\implies$ equal; whole $>$ part; doubles of same $\implies$ equal; halves of same $\implies$ equal.
- **5 Postulates:** join two points; extend a terminated line; draw a circle; all right angles equal; the parallel postulate.
- **Playfair's Axiom** is a simpler equivalent to Postulate 5: through a point off a line, there is **exactly one** parallel line.
