# Topic 1: Basic Terms and Angles (Types of Angles)

In *Introduction to Euclid's Geometry*, we laid down the foundation: points, lines, rays, and the axioms that hold everything together. Now we start building on that foundation. The central star of this chapter is the **angle** — the geometric figure formed whenever two rays shoot out from the same point. In this topic, we will learn how to spot an angle, name it properly, measure it, and classify all of its types.

---

## The Story of the Wall Clock

It is $9{:}00\text{ PM}$. **Maya** is studying at her desk and keeps glancing up at the analog clock on her studio wall.

- Earlier at **$3{:}00\text{ PM}$**, she noticed the two hands formed a crisp **"L"** corner.
- At **$6{:}00\text{ PM}$**, the two hands lined up in one **straight line**, pointing straight up and straight down.
- At **$1{:}00\text{ PM}$**, the two hands sat **close together**, like a pair of scissors barely opened.
- And at **$12{:}00$**, the two hands overlapped **right on top of each other**.

Maya realized something neat: *"Every single time, both hands start from the exact same pivot at the centre of the clock. The only thing changing is **how wide open** the space between them is."*

In geometry, that amount of "opening" between two rays is called an **angle**. Once you start looking, you see angles everywhere:
- Swinging a door slightly open or wide open changes the angle.
- Opening a pair of drafting dividers creates an angle between its two legs.
- Tilting a laptop screen adjusts the angle between screen and keyboard.
- Aiming a surveyor's telescope rotates it through an angle.

By the end of this topic, you will know how to **name** and **measure** every single one of these angles.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Angle** | The figure (and opening) formed when two rays start from the same endpoint | Space between two clock hands |
| **Arms** | The two rays that form the sides of an angle | The hour hand and minute hand |
| **Vertex** | The common endpoint where the two arms meet | The centre pivot of the clock |
| **Degree ($^\circ$)** | The unit used to measure angles; a full turn equals $360^\circ$ | A crisp "L" corner $= 90^\circ$ |
| **Protractor** | A semicircular drafting instrument used to measure angles | The D-shaped tool in a geometry kit |
| **Angle bisector** | A ray that splits an angle into two equal halves | Splitting $60^\circ$ into $30^\circ + 30^\circ$ |

## Part B: What Is an Angle?

> **Angle:** When **two rays originate from the same endpoint**, they form an angle.

```
            A
           /
          /
         /
        /
       B -------------- C
```

In this diagram:
- **$\overrightarrow{BA}$ and $\overrightarrow{BC}$** are the two rays — they are the **arms** of the angle.
- **$B$** is the common starting point where both arms meet — it is the **vertex** of the angle.
- The opening between the two arms is the **angle**.

### How to Name an Angle

We usually name an angle using three capital letters, and the **vertex letter always goes in the middle**.

The angle in the diagram above can be written as:
- **$\angle ABC$** (read as *"angle ABC"*)
- or **$\angle CBA$** (equally valid)

In both names, $B$ sits in the middle, so both refer to the exact same angle.

**Wrong names:** $\angle BAC$ or $\angle ACB$. Because $B$ is not in the middle, those describe completely different angles!

Memory trick: **the vertex is the filling of the sandwich — it always sits right in the middle.**

### When Is a Single Letter ($\angle B$) Enough?

When **only one angle** exists at a vertex, writing $\angle B$ is perfectly clear.

However, if **three or more rays** meet at the same vertex, multiple angles share that corner, so writing just $\angle B$ becomes ambiguous.

```
            A
           /
          /      D
         /    .
        /  .
       B -------------- C
```

Here, three angles share vertex $B$:
- $\angle ABD$ (between $BA$ and $BD$)
- $\angle DBC$ (between $BD$ and $BC$)
- $\angle ABC$ (the full angle between $BA$ and $BC$)

Whenever multiple angles share a vertex, **three letters are mandatory**. Sometimes diagrams also label angles with a small number or lowercase letter tucked inside the corner, such as $\angle 1$ or $\angle x$.

---

## Part C: Interior and Exterior of an Angle

An angle divides the flat plane into **three distinct regions**:

```
                A
     exterior  /
              /   interior
             /     •P
            /
           B ------------- C
               •Q
              exterior
```

1. **Interior:** the region **between** the two arms. Point $P$ lies in the interior of $\angle ABC$.
2. **Exterior:** the region **outside** the two arms. Point $Q$ lies in the exterior of $\angle ABC$.
3. **On the angle:** points lying directly on the arms $\overrightarrow{BA}$ or $\overrightarrow{BC}$ (including vertex $B$). They are neither in the interior nor in the exterior.

Picture an open door: the wedge-shaped space between the door and the wall is the **interior**; behind the door and across the rest of the hallway is the **exterior**.

## Part D: Measuring an Angle (Degrees and Protractor)

### What Is a Degree?

Angles are measured in **degrees**, written with the symbol **$^\circ$**.

> **One complete turn $= 360^\circ$**

If a ray pivots all the way around its vertex and returns to its exact starting position, it has turned through **$360^\circ$**.

### Why $360$?

Here is a neat piece of history: ancient **Babylonian** astronomers and mathematicians counted in base $60$ and estimated that a year had roughly $360$ days. On top of that, $360$ is extraordinarily convenient to divide — it splits evenly by $2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 12, 15, 18, 20, 24, 30, 36, 40, 45, 60, 72, 90, 120,$ and $180$!

Look how clean that makes everyday fractions of a turn:
- Half turn $= 360^\circ \div 2 = \mathbf{180^\circ}$
- Quarter turn $= 360^\circ \div 4 = \mathbf{90^\circ}$
- $12$ hours on a clock face: each hour gap $= 360^\circ \div 12 = \mathbf{30^\circ}$
- $60$ minutes around a clock face: each minute tick $= 360^\circ \div 60 = \mathbf{6^\circ}$

### Measuring an Angle with a Protractor

A **protractor** is the D-shaped tool in your geometry set, marked from $0^\circ$ to $180^\circ$.

**Steps:**
1. Place the **centre crossmark** of the protractor squarely on the **vertex** of the angle.
2. Align the protractor's **straight baseline** exactly along one arm of the angle.
3. Start counting from **$0^\circ$** on the scale that touches that first arm, and read where the second arm crosses the curved edge.
4. That number is the measure of the angle.

**Watch out:** A protractor has **two scales** — an inner scale and an outer scale running in opposite directions. Always **read the scale that starts at $0^\circ$ along your first arm!** Reading the wrong scale turns a $50^\circ$ angle into $130^\circ$.

Quick sanity check: if the angle looks **narrow (sharper than a square corner)**, your reading must be **less than $90^\circ$**; if it looks **wide (wider than a square corner)**, it must be **greater than $90^\circ$**. That instant visual check catches wrong-scale mistakes every time!

## Part E: Types of Angles

Angles are classified by their **size in degrees**.

### 1. Acute angle: greater than $0^\circ$, less than $90^\circ$

```
          /
         /
        /
       /______
```

**$0^\circ < x < 90^\circ$**

Examples:
- Slightly opened scissors.
- Clock hands at **$1{:}00$** ($30^\circ$) and **$2{:}00$** ($60^\circ$).
- One slice of an $8$-slice pizza ($360^\circ \div 8 = 45^\circ$).
- The sharp point of the letter **"V"**.

### 2. Right angle: exactly $90^\circ$

```
       |
       |
       |
       |_______
```

**$x = 90^\circ$**

In geometric diagrams, a right angle is marked with a **small square symbol** at the corner.

Examples:
- The corner of a textbook, a doorframe, or where a wall meets a level floor.
- Clock hands at **$3{:}00$** and **$9{:}00$**.
- The corner of the letter **"L"**.
- All four corners of a plus sign ($+$).

When two lines meet at a right angle ($90^\circ$), we say they are **perpendicular** to each other (written $l \perp m$).

### 3. Obtuse angle: greater than $90^\circ$, less than $180^\circ$

```
  \
   \
    \
     \_________
```

**$90^\circ < x < 180^\circ$**

Examples:
- A laptop screen tilted gently back for reading.
- Clock hands at **$4{:}00$** ($120^\circ$) and **$5{:}00$** ($150^\circ$).
- The angle between the seat and the tilted backrest of a reclining chair.

### 4. Straight angle: exactly $180^\circ$

```
  <---------•--------->
            B
```

**$x = 180^\circ$**

Both arms form a **single straight line**, pointing in opposite directions — exactly half of a full turn.

Examples:
- Clock hands at **$6{:}00$**.
- A hardcover book opened completely flat on a desk.
- Looking straight ahead and then turning to look directly behind along a straight avenue.

### 5. Reflex angle: greater than $180^\circ$, less than $360^\circ$

```
        /
       /
      /   <- not the small inner angle,
     /       the larger outer turn around the outside
     ‾‾‾‾‾‾‾
```

**$180^\circ < x < 360^\circ$**

A reflex angle is the **"long way around"** between two rays.

Examples:
- The outer body angle of a Pac-Man shape with an open mouth.
- Measuring the **larger** gap between the clock hands at $8{:}00$ ($240^\circ$).
- A swing-through café door opened $270^\circ$ past its closed position.

### 6. Complete angle: exactly $360^\circ$

**$x = 360^\circ$**

One ray sweeps through **one full revolution** and lands right back where it started.

Examples:
- The rotation of a clock's minute hand in **one full hour**.
- One full spin of a ceiling fan blade or wind turbine rotor.
- Doing a full $360^\circ$ turn in snowboarding or skateboarding.

### (Bonus) Zero angle: exactly $0^\circ$

When both arms lie **directly on top of each other** with zero opening between them, the angle is **$0^\circ$** — like the clock hands at **$12{:}00$**.

### All Types at a Glance

| Type | Range | Clock Example |
|---|---|---|
| Zero angle | $0^\circ$ | $12{:}00$ |
| Acute angle | $0^\circ < x < 90^\circ$ | $1{:}00$ ($30^\circ$), $2{:}00$ ($60^\circ$) |
| Right angle | $90^\circ$ | $3{:}00$, $9{:}00$ |
| Obtuse angle | $90^\circ < x < 180^\circ$ | $4{:}00$ ($120^\circ$), $5{:}00$ ($150^\circ$) |
| Straight angle | $180^\circ$ | $6{:}00$ |
| Reflex angle | $180^\circ < x < 360^\circ$ | Large angle at $8{:}00$ ($240^\circ$) |
| Complete angle | $360^\circ$ | $1$ full hour of the minute hand |

Memory trick: **$90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ are the two gateways.** Before $90^\circ$ is *acute* (sharp); between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ is *obtuse* (wide); past $180^\circ$ is *reflex* (wrapping around the back).

## Part F: Every Angle's Reflex Partner

Whenever two rays share a vertex, they actually create **two angles**: a **smaller** inner angle and a **larger** outer (reflex) angle. Together, they complete the full $360^\circ$ circle.

> **Reflex angle $= 360^\circ - (\text{smaller angle})$**

| Smaller Angle | Reflex Partner |
|---|---|
| $30^\circ$ | $360^\circ - 30^\circ = \mathbf{330^\circ}$ |
| $90^\circ$ | $360^\circ - 90^\circ = \mathbf{270^\circ}$ |
| $110^\circ$ | $360^\circ - 110^\circ = \mathbf{250^\circ}$ |
| $145^\circ$ | $360^\circ - 145^\circ = \mathbf{215^\circ}$ |
| $179^\circ$ | $360^\circ - 179^\circ = \mathbf{181^\circ}$ |

Check: $30^\circ + 330^\circ = 360^\circ$ ✅, $110^\circ + 250^\circ = 360^\circ$ ✅

By convention, writing simply "$\angle ABC$" refers to the **smaller** angle. Whenever we want the outer angle, we write explicitly: **"reflex $\angle ABC$."**

## Part G: Angle Bisector (Just the Idea)

To **"bisect"** means to **split into two equal parts**.

> **Angle bisector:** A ray that divides an angle into **two equal smaller angles**.

```
            A
           /
          /      D   (BD is the bisector)
         /    .
        /  .
       B -------------- C
```

If ray $\overrightarrow{BD}$ bisects $\angle ABC$, then:
$$\angle ABD = \angle DBC = \frac{1}{2}\angle ABC$$

Examples:
- The bisector of $60^\circ$ splits it into **$30^\circ + 30^\circ$**.
- The bisector of $90^\circ$ splits it into **$45^\circ + 45^\circ$**.
- The bisector of $140^\circ$ splits it into **$70^\circ + 70^\circ$**.

In carpentry, when joining two pieces of picture frame moulding at a $90^\circ$ corner, a carpenter cuts both ends at a **$45^\circ$ mitre** — right along the angle bisector!

*(We will learn how to construct an angle bisector with a compass and straightedge in Constructions; for now, knowing what it does is all you need.)*

## Part H: Finding Angles on a Clock

Clocks are one of the best training grounds for working with angles. Keep two exact speeds in mind:

- The **minute hand** sweeps $360^\circ$ in $60$ minutes $\implies$ **$6^\circ$ per minute**.
- The **hour hand** sweeps $360^\circ$ in $12$ hours $\implies$ **$30^\circ$ per hour**, which is **$0.5^\circ$ per minute** ($30 \div 60$).

### Whole Hours

At any exact hour (such as $4{:}00$), the minute hand sits on **$12$** and the hour hand points directly at the hour number. Each hour gap around the dial measures **$30^\circ$**.

$$\text{Angle} = (\text{number of hour gaps between the hands}) \times 30^\circ$$

| Time | Hour Gaps | Angle | Type |
|---|---|---|---|
| $1{:}00$ | $1$ | $30^\circ$ | Acute |
| $2{:}00$ | $2$ | $60^\circ$ | Acute |
| $3{:}00$ | $3$ | $90^\circ$ | Right |
| $4{:}00$ | $4$ | $120^\circ$ | Obtuse |
| $5{:}00$ | $5$ | $150^\circ$ | Obtuse |
| $6{:}00$ | $6$ | $180^\circ$ | Straight |

**What about $7{:}00, 8{:}00, 9{:}00, 10{:}00, 11{:}00$?** There are two gaps around the circle — a shorter way and a longer way. Unless stated otherwise, *"the angle between the hands"* means the **smaller** angle:
- At $8{:}00$: $8$ gaps one way ($240^\circ$), $4$ gaps the shorter way (**$120^\circ$**). Smaller angle $= \mathbf{120^\circ}$; reflex angle $= 240^\circ$.
- At $9{:}00$: $3$ gaps the shorter way (**$90^\circ$**).
- At $10{:}00$: $2$ gaps the shorter way (**$60^\circ$**).

### Half Hours: A Neat Twist

Think about **$3{:}30$**. The minute hand points straight down at **$6$**. Where is the hour hand? **Not on $3$!** Half an hour has passed, so the hour hand has crept **halfway between $3$ and $4$**.

Let us calculate both positions clockwise from $12$ ($0^\circ$):

**At $3{:}30$:**
- Minute hand: $30\text{ min} \times 6^\circ = \mathbf{180^\circ}$ (at $6$)
- Hour hand: $3\text{ hr} \times 30^\circ + 30\text{ min} \times 0.5^\circ = 90^\circ + 15^\circ = \mathbf{105^\circ}$
- Angle $= 180^\circ - 105^\circ = \mathbf{75^\circ}$ (Acute)

**At $6{:}30$:**
- Minute hand: **$180^\circ$**
- Hour hand: $6 \times 30^\circ + 15^\circ = 180^\circ + 15^\circ = \mathbf{195^\circ}$
- Angle $= 195^\circ - 180^\circ = \mathbf{15^\circ}$ (Acute)

Notice how at $6{:}30$ the two hands are almost overlapping, with the hour hand just slightly ahead of the minute hand!

**At $9{:}30$:**
- Minute hand: **$180^\circ$**
- Hour hand: $9 \times 30^\circ + 15^\circ = 270^\circ + 15^\circ = \mathbf{285^\circ}$
- Angle $= 285^\circ - 180^\circ = \mathbf{105^\circ}$ (Obtuse)

**At $12{:}30$:**
- Minute hand: **$180^\circ$**
- Hour hand: $0^\circ + 15^\circ = \mathbf{15^\circ}$
- Angle $= 180^\circ - 15^\circ = \mathbf{165^\circ}$ (Obtuse)

Check: at $12{:}30$, the minute hand is on $6$ and the hour hand is just past $12$ — nearly opposite, slightly shy of a straight line, so $165^\circ$ (just under $180^\circ$) makes complete sense ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Failing to put the vertex in the middle when naming an angle.**
   If the vertex is $B$, the angle is $\angle ABC$ or $\angle CBA$. Writing $\angle BAC$ names the angle at corner $A$, which is a totally different angle.

2. **Mistake 2: Writing just $\angle B$ when multiple angles share vertex $B$.**
   If three rays leave $B$, $\angle B$ is ambiguous — nobody knows which angle you mean. Always use three letters.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking longer arms make a bigger angle.**
   Whether the arms are $2\text{ cm}$ long or $2\text{ metres}$ long, **the angle stays identical**. An angle measures *opening (rotation)*, not length. A wristwatch and Big Ben in London both show $90^\circ$ at $3{:}00$.

4. **Mistake 4: Reading the wrong scale on a protractor.**
   The inner and outer scales run in opposite directions. Always start from **$0^\circ$** on the arm aligned with the baseline, then sanity-check visually (narrow $< 90^\circ$, wide $> 90^\circ$).

5. **Mistake 5: Calling $90^\circ$ acute or $180^\circ$ obtuse.**
   Acute means **strictly less than $90^\circ$**; obtuse means **strictly less than $180^\circ$**. Exactly $90^\circ$ is **right**, and exactly $180^\circ$ is **straight**.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking a reflex angle is "negative" or "backwards."**
   A reflex angle is a perfectly normal positive angle (between $180^\circ$ and $360^\circ$) — it simply measures the wide outer sweep between the arms.

7. **Mistake 7: Leaving the hour hand frozen on the hour mark at half past.**
   At $3{:}30$, the hour hand is **halfway between $3$ and $4$**, not on $3$. That is why the angle is **$75^\circ$**, not $90^\circ$.

## Quick Recap

- An **angle** is formed by two rays (**arms**) sharing a common endpoint (**vertex**).
- Always keep the **vertex in the middle** when naming ($\angle ABC$). Use a single letter ($\angle B$) only when one angle sits at that vertex.
- An angle splits the plane into its **interior**, **exterior**, and the angle itself.
- Angles are measured in **degrees ($^\circ$)**; **one full turn $= 360^\circ$**. On a protractor, always read from the scale starting at $0^\circ$.
- **Types:**
  - Acute: $0^\circ < x < 90^\circ$
  - Right: $90^\circ$
  - Obtuse: $90^\circ < x < 180^\circ$
  - Straight: $180^\circ$
  - Reflex: $180^\circ < x < 360^\circ$
  - Complete: $360^\circ$
- **Reflex partner $= 360^\circ - \text{smaller angle}$.**
- An **angle bisector** splits an angle into two equal halves.
- **Clocks:** each hour gap $= 30^\circ$; minute hand moves $6^\circ/\text{min}$; hour hand moves $0.5^\circ/\text{min}$.
