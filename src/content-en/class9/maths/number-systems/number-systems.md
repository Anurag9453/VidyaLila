# Topic 1: The Number Family: Natural, Whole, Integers, Rational Numbers, and the Number Line

By now, you have used many kinds of numbers: counting numbers, negative numbers, and fractions. Yet have you ever paused to notice that they all belong to a single **family**? Every new type of number was invented because people ran into a fresh real-world need. In this topic, we will meet the family from its earliest members, see how each group fits inside the next, and place every number in its exact spot on a straight line.

---

## The Story of How Numbers Grew

Long ago, in a quiet mountain valley, lived a young shepherdess named **Maya** whose family also ran a small village bakery with her neighbor, **Mr. Thorne**.

**First need: counting.**
Every morning, Maya led her flock of sheep out to graze, and every evening she had to make sure every single one had returned. So she counted: $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ These are the oldest numbers in human history. We call them **natural numbers**, because counting objects was the most "natural" thing for people to do.

**Second need: "nothing left."**
One stormy afternoon, the wind blew the pasture gate open and every sheep wandered away into the mist. The pen was completely empty. Now, what is the answer to "How many sheep are in the pen?" **None.** People needed a symbol for nothing: **$0$**. Add $0$ to the counting numbers, and you get the **whole numbers**: $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$

**Third need: debt and freezing temperatures.**
To rebuild her flock, Maya borrowed $5$ sheep from Mr. Thorne's farm. Now she did not own any sheep of her own — instead, she **owed** $5$. To record what was owed, "minus" numbers were born: **$-5$**. Likewise, on winter nights in the valley, the outdoor thermometer often dropped below $0^\circ\text{C}$, reading $-3^\circ\text{C}$. Together, positive numbers, $0$, and negative numbers form the **integers**: $\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$

**Fourth need: sharing equally.**
To celebrate finding the lost sheep, Mr. Thorne baked one large sourdough loaf at his bakery and gave it to Maya to share equally among $4$ friends. None of the four friends got a whole loaf; each received **$1/4$** of the loaf. Now people needed numbers capable of describing parts of a whole. These became the **rational numbers** (numbers expressed as ratios or fractions).

Notice what happened every single time: whenever the old numbers were not enough to solve a problem, a new member joined the family. Yet the older numbers never disappeared — they simply became part of the larger family. That is the central idea of this entire topic.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$)** | Counting numbers starting from $1$ | $1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ |
| **Whole numbers ($\mathbb{W}$)** | All natural numbers together with $0$ | $0, 1, 2, 3, \dots$ |
| **Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$)** | Whole numbers together with their negative counterparts | $\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots$ |
| **Rational numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$)** | Numbers that can be written in the form $p/q$, where $p$ and $q$ are integers and $q \neq 0$ | $3/4$, $-2/5$, $7$ ($= 7/1$) |
| **Numerator / Denominator** | The top number / bottom number of a fraction | In $3/4$, $3$ is the numerator and $4$ is the denominator |
| **Equivalent fractions** | Fractions that look different but represent the exact same value | $1/2 = 2/4 = 5/10$ |
| **Lowest form** | A fraction simplified so the numerator and denominator share no common factor other than $1$ | The lowest form of $18/24$ is $3/4$ |
| **Number line** | A straight line on which every number has one fixed position | $0$ in the middle, positives to the right, negatives to the left |

Where do those letters come from? **$\mathbb{N}$** stands for *Natural*, **$\mathbb{W}$** for *Whole*, **$\mathbb{Z}$** comes from the German word *Zahlen* (meaning "numbers"), and **$\mathbb{Q}$** stands for *Quotient* (the result of division), because every rational number is a ratio or division.

## Part A: Members of the Number Family

### Natural Numbers ($\mathbb{N}$)

$$\mathbb{N} = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, \dots\}$$

- They begin at $1$.
- They go on forever. No matter how huge a number you imagine, adding $1$ gives an even larger natural number.
- **$0$ is not a natural number.** Counting always starts at $1$.

### Whole Numbers ($\mathbb{W}$)

$$\mathbb{W} = \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots\}$$

- Just one new member joins the natural numbers: **$0$**.
- Therefore, every natural number is also a whole number. However, $0$ is a whole number that is *not* a natural number.

### Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$)

$$\mathbb{Z} = \{\dots, -3, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots\}$$

- This family contains three groups: positive integers ($1, 2, 3, \dots$), **zero**, and negative integers ($-1, -2, -3, \dots$).
- $0$ is neither positive nor negative; it stands right at the centre.

Integers in everyday life:
- Bank account balance: depositing $\$500$ is $+500$; owing $\$200$ is $-200$.
- Elevator buttons: ground level is $0$, basement parking is $-1$, first floor up is $+1$.
- Elevation and pacing: mountain peaks above sea level are $+$, ocean trenches below sea level are $-$; in marathon pacing, running $12$ seconds faster than target split can be logged as $-12\text{ s}$, while $8$ seconds slower is $+8\text{ s}$.

### Rational Numbers ($\mathbb{Q}$)

Any number that can be written in the form **$p/q$**, where:
- both $p$ and $q$ are **integers**, and
- **$q \neq 0$**,

is a **rational number**.

Examples:
- $3/4$ ($p = 3$, $q = 4$)
- $-2/5$ ($p = -2$, $q = 5$)
- **$7$**, because $7 = 7/1$
- **$0$**, because $0 = 0/1$
- **$-6$**, because $-6 = -6/1$

Look closely: **every integer is also a rational number**, because you can always write it over $1$.

### Why $q \neq 0$?

Division asks: "What number must we multiply the divisor by to get the dividend?"

- $6/2 = 3$, because $2 \times \mathbf{3} = 6$. That gives one clear answer.
- $6/0 = ?$ That asks: $0 \times \mathbf{?} = 6$. Yet multiplying $0$ by *any* number always gives $0$, never $6$. **There is no answer at all.**

Think back to the bakery story: sharing $1$ loaf of sourdough equally among **$0$ friends**. How much does each friend get? The question makes no sense. That is why the denominator can never be $0$.

## Part B: Equivalent Fractions and Lowest Form

### One Number, Many Disguises

Imagine taking a loaf of sourdough from Mr. Thorne's bakery and:
- slicing it into $2$ equal pieces and taking $1$ piece: **$1/2$**
- slicing it into $4$ equal pieces and taking $2$ pieces: **$2/4$**
- slicing it into $10$ equal pieces and taking $5$ pieces: **$5/10$**

In all three cases, you hold the **exact same amount of bread**. So $1/2$, $2/4$, and $5/10$ look different on paper, yet they represent the **same rational number** — just like one person wearing different outfits stays the exact same person.

**Rule:** Multiplying or dividing both the numerator and the denominator by the **same non-zero integer** leaves the value of the fraction unchanged.

$$\frac{3}{5} = \frac{3 \times 4}{5 \times 4} = \frac{12}{20}$$

### Lowest Form

When the numerator and denominator share **no common factor other than $1$**, the fraction is in its **lowest form** (or simplest form).

Method: divide both top and bottom by their **HCF** (highest common factor).

**Example 1:** $18/24$
$\text{HCF}(18, 24) = 6$
$$\frac{18}{24} = \frac{18 \div 6}{24 \div 6} = \frac{3}{4}$$

**Example 2:** $-15/35$
$\text{HCF}(15, 35) = 5$
$$-\frac{15}{35} = -\frac{3}{7}$$

**Example 3:** $45/(-60)$
First, move the minus sign to the numerator: $45/(-60) = -45/60$. (By convention, we keep the denominator positive.)
$\text{HCF}(45, 60) = 15$
$$-\frac{45}{60} = -\frac{3}{4}$$

Check: $-\frac{3}{4} = \frac{-3 \times 15}{4 \times 15} = -\frac{45}{60}$ ✅

## Part C: Who Belongs Where? ($N \subset W \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q}$)

In our story, each new number family swallowed all the older ones inside it. Picture it as a set of nested boxes:

```
 +----------------------------------------------------+
 |  Q: Rational numbers        3/4   -2/5   1/2       |
 |   +-------------------------------------------+    |
 |   |  Z: Integers        -1   -2   -3   -10    |    |
 |   |   +-----------------------------------+   |    |
 |   |   |  W: Whole numbers        0        |   |    |
 |   |   |   +---------------------------+   |   |    |
 |   |   |   |  N: Natural   1, 2, 3, ...|   |   |    |
 |   |   |   +---------------------------+   |   |    |
 |   |   +-----------------------------------+   |    |
 |   +-------------------------------------------+    |
 +----------------------------------------------------+
```

In mathematics, we write this relationship as: **$N \subset W \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q}$** (or $\mathbb{N} \subset \mathbb{W} \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q}$). The symbol "$\subset$" means "is contained inside" (is a subset of).

This tells us:
- Every natural number is a whole number.
- Every whole number is an integer.
- Every integer is a rational number.

However, the reverse is **not** always true:
- $0$ is a whole number, but not a natural number.
- $-4$ is an integer, but not a whole number.
- $2/3$ is a rational number, but not an integer.

### Who Belongs Where? (Summary Table)

| Number | Natural? | Whole? | Integer? | Rational? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $7$ | Yes | Yes | Yes | Yes ($7/1$) |
| $0$ | No | Yes | Yes | Yes ($0/1$) |
| $-5$ | No | No | Yes | Yes ($-5/1$) |
| $3/4$ | No | No | No | Yes |
| $-2/3$ | No | No | No | Yes |
| $9/3$ | **Yes** | **Yes** | **Yes** | Yes |
| $-12/4$ | No | No | **Yes** | Yes |

Look carefully at the last two rows. **$9/3$ looks like a fraction, yet $9/3 = 3$**, which is a natural number. Similarly, $-12/4 = -3$, which is an integer. Always **simplify a number first** before classifying it.

## Part D: Numbers on the Number Line

### How a Number Line Is Built

1. Draw a straight horizontal line extending in both directions.
2. Pick a point near the middle and label it **$0$**.
3. Mark equally spaced points to the right of $0$ as $1, 2, 3, \dots$
4. Mark points at the same equal spacing to the left of $0$ as $-1, -2, -3, \dots$

```
 <--+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-->
   -3    -2    -1     0     1     2     3     4
```

- Moving to the **right** makes numbers **larger**.
- Moving to the **left** makes numbers **smaller**.
- That is why $-3 < -1$, because $-3$ lies farther to the left. (Just as $-3^\circ\text{C}$ is colder than $-1^\circ\text{C}$.)

Natural numbers sit only on the positive ticks starting from $1$. Whole numbers add $0$. Integers cover all whole-number ticks in both directions.

### Plotting Rational Numbers on the Number Line

**Method:** First find which two consecutive integers the number lies between. Divide that interval into **as many equal parts as the denominator**, and then count out **as many steps as the numerator**.

**Example 1: $3/4$**

$3/4$ lies between $0$ and $1$ (since $3 < 4$). Divide the segment from $0$ to $1$ into **$4$ equal parts**, and count **$3$ steps to the right** from $0$.

```
     0    1/4   2/4   3/4    1
 ----+-----+-----+-----*-----+----
                       ^
                      3/4
```

**Example 2: $-3/4$**

This lies between $0$ and $-1$, to the **left of $0$**. Divide the segment from $0$ to $-1$ into $4$ equal parts, and count **$3$ steps to the left** from $0$.

```
    -1   -3/4  -2/4  -1/4    0
 ----+-----*-----+-----+-----+----
           ^
         -3/4
```

**Example 3: $7/3$**

First write it as a mixed number: $7 \div 3 = 2$ with remainder $1$, so **$7/3 = 2\frac{1}{3}$** ($2$ wholes plus $1/3$).
This tells us it lies between $2$ and $3$. Divide the segment between $2$ and $3$ into **$3$ equal parts**, and move **$1$ step to the right** from $2$.

```
     2    2⅓    2⅔    3
 ----+-----*-----+-----+----
           ^
          7/3
```

Check: $2\frac{1}{3} = \frac{2 \times 3 + 1}{3} = \frac{7}{3}$ ✅

**Example 4: $-5/2$**

$-5/2 = -2\frac{1}{2}$. It lies between $-2$ and $-3$. Divide the segment from $-2$ to $-3$ into $2$ equal parts, and move $1$ step to the **left** from $-2$.

```
    -3   -5/2   -2
 ----+-----*-----+----
           ^
         -5/2
```

## Part E: How Many Numbers Lie Between Two Rational Numbers?

Between two consecutive integers, such as $3$ and $4$, there is **no other integer**. Is the same true for rational numbers? Let us find out.

### Method 1: Finding the Midpoint (Average)

Between any two numbers $a$ and $b$, there is always another number right in the middle: their average **$(a + b)/2$**.

**Example 1:** Find one rational number between $1/2$ and $1$.

$$\frac{\frac{1}{2} + 1}{2} = \frac{\frac{3}{2}}{2} = \frac{3}{4}$$

Check: $1/2 = 2/4$ and $1 = 4/4$, so $2/4 < 3/4 < 4/4$ ✅

**Example 2:** Find **three** rational numbers between $1$ and $2$.

- Midpoint of $1$ and $2$: $\frac{1 + 2}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{3}{2}}$
- Midpoint of $1$ and $3/2$: $\frac{1 + \frac{3}{2}}{2} = \frac{\frac{5}{2}}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{5}{4}}$
- Midpoint of $3/2$ and $2$: $\frac{\frac{3}{2} + 2}{2} = \frac{\frac{7}{2}}{2} = \mathbf{\frac{7}{4}}$

So $1 < 5/4 < 3/2 < 7/4 < 2$.

Check: Rewrite with denominator $4$: $4/4 < 5/4 < 6/4 < 7/4 < 8/4$ ✅

### Method 2: Increasing the Denominator

When you need several rational numbers at once, this method is much faster.

**Example 3:** Find **$5$** rational numbers between $3/5$ and $4/5$.

At first glance, there seems to be nothing between $3$ and $4$. Yet remember: we are looking for numbers between the *values*, not just between the numerators!

Step 1: Since we need $5$ numbers, multiply both numerator and denominator by **$5 + 1 = 6$**:
- $\frac{3}{5} = \frac{18}{30}$
- $\frac{4}{5} = \frac{24}{30}$

Step 2: Now pick the numerators in between: $19, 20, 21, 22, 23$.

**Answer:** $19/30,\ 20/30,\ 21/30,\ 22/30,\ 23/30$

You can simplify them if you wish: $20/30 = 2/3$, $21/30 = 7/10$, $22/30 = 11/15$.

Check: $18/30 < 19/30 < 20/30 < 21/30 < 22/30 < 23/30 < 24/30$ ✅

If you needed $10$ numbers, you would multiply by $11$; for $100$ numbers, you would multiply by $101$. You can always create as much room as you want!

### How Many Numbers Are There? Infinitely Many!

Think about Method 1 again. Between any two rational numbers, we found a new rational number. Then between that new number and the first one, we can find *another* rational number. And between those two, yet *another*... This process never ends.

Picture it like zooming in with a camera lens:
- From far away, the space between $0$ and $1$ looks empty.
- Zoom in once, and $1/2$ appears.
- Zoom in closer, and $1/4$ and $3/4$ appear.
- Zoom in closer still, and $1/8, 3/8, 5/8, 7/8$ appear...

The closer you zoom, the more rational numbers you discover.

> **Between any two distinct rational numbers, there are infinitely many rational numbers.**

That is why mathematicians say the rational numbers are **dense** on the number line. Integers leave clear gaps between neighbors, whereas rational numbers never have a "next" number.

*(An intriguing question to carry into the next topics: even though rational numbers are packed so densely, do they fill **every single point** on the number line? The surprising answer awaits in Topic 3.)*

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Thinking $0$ is a natural number.**
   Natural numbers start at $1$. $0$ is a whole number, an integer, and a rational number, but **not** a natural number.

2. **Mistake 2: Allowing $0$ in the denominator.**
   $5/0$ is not a number — division by $0$ is undefined. A rational number $p/q$ strictly requires **$q \neq 0$**.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking integers are not rational numbers.**
   Saying "$-6$ isn't a fraction, so it isn't rational" is incorrect. Since $-6 = -6/1$, it fits $p/q$ with $q = 1 \neq 0$. Every integer is rational.

4. **Mistake 4: Classifying a fraction without simplifying it first.**
   Do not say "$-16/4$ is a fraction, not an integer." Because $-16/4 = -4$, it is indeed an integer (and rational).

5. **Mistake 5: Placing negative fractions on the wrong side of $0$ or past $-1$.**
   $-3/4$ goes to the **left** of $0$, not the right — and it lies between $-1$ and $0$, not to the left of $-1$.

6. **Mistake 6: Claiming there are "no numbers" between consecutive numerators.**
   In $3/5$ and $4/5$, the numerators $3$ and $4$ are consecutive, yet infinitely many rational numbers (such as $7/10$) lie between them. Increase the denominator to see them.

7. **Mistake 7: Placing an improper fraction near the wrong integer.**
   Do not place $7/3$ right next to $3$. Convert to a mixed number first: $7/3 = 2\frac{1}{3}$, so it lies between $2$ and $3$, closer to $2$.

## Quick Recap

- **$\mathbb{N}$** $= \{1, 2, 3, \dots\}$, **$\mathbb{W}$** $= \{0, 1, 2, \dots\}$, **$\mathbb{Z}$** $= \{\dots, -2, -1, 0, 1, 2, \dots\}$.
- **$\mathbb{Q}$** consists of all numbers $p/q$ where $p, q \in \mathbb{Z}$ and **$q \neq 0$**.
- **$N \subset W \subset \mathbb{Z} \subset \mathbb{Q}$**: every natural number is whole, every whole number is an integer, and every integer is rational.
- **Equivalent fractions** represent the same rational number ($1/2 = 2/4 = 5/10$).
- To reduce a fraction to its **lowest form**, divide numerator and denominator by their HCF.
- On the **number line**, numbers increase to the right and decrease to the left. To plot $p/q$, divide the interval between the two surrounding integers into $q$ equal parts.
- Between any two rational numbers $a$ and $b$, their midpoint **$(a + b)/2$** always lies between them.
- To find many rational numbers between two fractions quickly, **increase the denominator** using equivalent fractions.
- Between any two distinct rational numbers, there exist **infinitely many** rational numbers.

## Practice Questions

1. Classify each number as natural, whole, integer, and/or rational:
   (a) $15$ (b) $0$ (c) $-8$ (d) $2/7$ (e) $-16/4$ (f) $12/3$

2. State whether each statement is true or false. Give a reason.
   (a) Every natural number is a whole number.
   (b) Every integer is a rational number.
   (c) Every rational number is an integer.
   (d) $0$ is a natural number.
   (e) $0$ is a rational number.

3. Write each rational number in its lowest form:
   (a) $21/28$ (b) $-36/48$ (c) $45/(-60)$

4. Describe how you would represent $5/4$ and $-2/3$ on the number line.

5. Find one rational number between $2/3$ and $3/4$.

6. Find four rational numbers between $1/3$ and $1/2$.

7. Find three rational numbers between $-1$ and $0$.

8. Explain why $5/0$ is not a rational number.

9. Are there infinitely many rational numbers between $0$ and $1$? Explain in one or two lines.

### Answers

1. (a) $15$: natural, whole, integer, rational
   (b) $0$: whole, integer, rational (**not** natural)
   (c) $-8$: integer, rational
   (d) $2/7$: rational only
   (e) $-16/4 = -4$: integer, rational
   (f) $12/3 = 4$: natural, whole, integer, rational

2. (a) **True** ✅. Whole numbers contain every natural number along with $0$.
   (b) **True** ✅. Every integer $n$ can be written as $n/1$, which satisfies $p/q$ with $q = 1 \neq 0$.
   (c) **False** ❌. For example, $1/2$ is rational, but it is not an integer.
   (d) **False** ❌. Natural numbers begin at $1$.
   (e) **True** ✅. $0 = 0/1$, where $0$ and $1$ are integers and $1 \neq 0$.

3. (a) $\text{HCF}(21, 28) = 7$. $21/28 = \mathbf{3/4}$
   (b) $\text{HCF}(36, 48) = 12$. $-36/48 = \mathbf{-3/4}$
   (c) First write $45/(-60) = -45/60$. $\text{HCF}(45, 60) = 15$. Result: $\mathbf{-3/4}$

4. **$5/4 = 1\frac{1}{4}$.** It lies between $1$ and $2$. Divide the interval from $1$ to $2$ into $4$ equal parts, and move $1$ step to the right from $1$.
   **$-2/3$.** It lies between $-1$ and $0$. Divide the interval from $0$ to $-1$ into $3$ equal parts, and move $2$ steps to the left from $0$.

5. Take the average: $\frac{2/3 + 3/4}{2}$. First add: $2/3 + 3/4 = 8/12 + 9/12 = 17/12$. Divide by $2$: **$\mathbf{17/24}$**.
   Check: $2/3 = 16/24$ and $3/4 = 18/24$, so $16/24 < 17/24 < 18/24$ ✅

6. Use a common denominator: $1/3 = 2/6$ and $1/2 = 3/6$. Since we need $4$ numbers, multiply numerator and denominator by $4 + 1 = 5$: $10/30$ and $15/30$.
   Numbers in between: **$11/30,\ 12/30,\ 13/30,\ 14/30$** (which simplify to $11/30,\ 2/5,\ 13/30,\ 7/15$).

7. Write $-1 = -4/4$ and $0 = 0/4$. Three rational numbers between them are: **$-3/4,\ -2/4\ (= -1/2),\ -1/4$**.

8. $5/0$ asks: "Which number multiplied by $0$ equals $5$?" Multiplying any number by $0$ always gives $0$, never $5$. Therefore $5/0$ is undefined, and it also breaks the rational number requirement that **$q \neq 0$**.

9. **Yes.** Between $0$ and $1$ lies $1/2$. Between $0$ and $1/2$ lies $1/4$, between $0$ and $1/4$ lies $1/8$, and so on. You can always take the midpoint between two rational numbers, so the process never ends.
