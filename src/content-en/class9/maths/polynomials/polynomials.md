# Topic 1: Constants, Variables, and Algebraic Expressions

## A Ride-Share Fare Story

Imagine Maya finishes her evening track practice and books a ride-share cab home. The driver's fare meter displays a straightforward pricing rule:

"A fixed base fee of **\$30** as soon as the trip starts, plus **\$12** for every kilometre travelled."

Now the question is: how much will Maya have to pay?

- If she travels $1\text{ km}$: $30 + 12 \times 1 = \$42$
- If she travels $5\text{ km}$: $30 + 12 \times 5 = \$90$
- If she travels $10\text{ km}$: $30 + 12 \times 10 = \$150$

Notice something important: **\$30 stays the same every single time**, whether Maya travels $1\text{ km}$ or $10\text{ km}$. Meanwhile, **the number of kilometres keeps changing**, and the total fare changes along with it.

Now, if we use the letter **$x$** to represent the number of kilometres, we can write the fare in one compact formula:

$$\text{Fare} = 30 + 12x$$

Just like that, you have written your very first algebraic expression. In this topic, we will learn what $30$ is called, what $x$ is called, and what the whole expression $30 + 12x$ represents.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Constant** | A number whose value never changes | $5$, $-7$, $\frac{3}{4}$, $\pi$ |
| **Variable** | A letter whose value can change or vary | $x$, $y$, $a$, $t$ |
| **Algebraic expression** | A mathematical phrase formed by combining numbers and letters using $+$, $-$, $\times$, or $\div$ | $3x + 5$, $a^2 - 2b$ |
| **Term** | A single part of an expression separated by $+$ or $-$ | In $3x + 5$, the terms are $3x$ and $5$ |
| **Coefficient** | The number multiplying a variable in a term | In $7x$, the coefficient is $7$ |
| **Like terms** | Terms that have the exact same variables raised to the exact same powers | $3x$ and $8x$ |

Read through this table once; below, we will unpack every single term with stories and worked examples.

---

## Part A: What Is a Constant?

A **constant** is a quantity that **remains constant — meaning its value never changes**.

Examples from everyday life:

- There are always **7** days in a week. That number will never become 8.
- There are always **60** minutes in an hour.
- The three interior angles of a triangle always add up to **$180^\circ$**.
- There are always **12** items in a dozen.

In mathematics, constants are fixed numbers such as:

- Integers like $5$, $100$, $-3$, $0$
- Fractions like $\frac{3}{4}$, $-\frac{2}{7}$
- Decimals like $0.5$, $2.75$
- Special numbers like $\pi$ (approximately $3.14$) and $\sqrt{2}$

Their values are fixed. The number $5$ will always remain $5$, no matter where you write it.

### An Important Note

Sometimes letters are also used to stand for constants. For example, in $ax + b$, the letters $a$ and $b$ represent fixed numbers within a given problem (such as $a = 3$ and $b = 5$), so they are treated as constants while $x$ varies. Do not worry too much about that yet — for now, simply remember that **a constant is anything that does not change within a given problem**.

---

## Part B: What Is a Variable?

A **variable** is a quantity that **varies — meaning its value can change**.

Look at everyday quantities that change:

- Today's **temperature**: $22^\circ\text{C}$ in the morning, $35^\circ\text{C}$ at noon, $25^\circ\text{C}$ at night. It keeps changing.
- The **price of fuel**: it can shift from week to week.
- Your **age**: it increases by $1$ every year.
- **Sports telemetry** during a race or match: a runner's speed or a team's score changes from moment to moment.

We can represent any of these changing quantities with a letter — for instance, $t$ for temperature, $a$ for age, or $s$ for score.

Why do we use letters? There are two main reasons:

1. **The value changes**, so instead of rewriting a different number every time, we use one letter that can take any value.
2. **The value is not yet known**, so we place a letter there as a placeholder — like an empty box waiting to be filled.

Most often we use letters like $x, y, z, a, b, p, q, t$, though any letter can serve as a variable.

### Understanding Through an Example

At Leo's school sports complex, snack packs cost **\$10** each. The number of packs Leo and his teammates buy changes every day. Suppose the number of packs is **$n$**:

| Snack packs ($n$) | Total cost ($10 \times n$) |
|---|---|
| $1$ | \$10 |
| $3$ | \$30 |
| $7$ | \$70 |
| $12$ | \$120 |

Here:
- **$10$** is a constant (the unit price is fixed).
- **$n$** is a variable (the quantity changes).
- **$10n$** gives the total cost for $n$ snack packs.

## Part C: What Is an Algebraic Expression?

When we **combine constants and variables using the operations $+$, $-$, $\times$, and $\div$**, the result is called an **algebraic expression**.

Maya's ride-share fare **$30 + 12x$** is an algebraic expression. Here are several more:

| Expression | What it represents |
|---|---|
| $5x$ | Total cost of $5$ pens if each pen costs $x$ dollars |
| $2l + 2b$ | Perimeter of a rectangle with length $l$ and breadth $b$ |
| $3a - 7$ | $7$ subtracted from $3$ times a number $a$ |
| $x^2 + y^2$ | Sum of the squares of two numbers $x$ and $y$ |
| $\frac{a}{2}$ | Half of a quantity $a$ |
| $4m + 3n$ | Total cost of $4$ mangoes (at \$m each) and $3$ oranges (at \$n each) |

### Expression vs. Equation

This is a very common point of confusion. Think of the difference like this:

- An **expression is a phrase**, such as "Liam's $x$ points".
- An **equation is a complete sentence**, such as "Liam has $x$ points, and his total is $10$." An equation always contains an **equals sign ($=$)**.

| Statement | Expression or Equation? |
|---|---|
| $3x + 5$ | Expression (no $=$ sign) |
| $3x + 5 = 20$ | Equation (contains $=$, making a complete statement) |
| $a^2 - 4b$ | Expression |
| $a^2 - 4b = 0$ | Equation |

In this chapter, we will work mainly with expressions.

## Part D: Terms and Coefficients

### Terms

The parts of an expression that are **separated by $+$ or $-$ signs** are called **terms**.

**Example 1:** $3x + 5$
- Term 1: $3x$
- Term 2: $5$
- Total: $2$ terms.

**Example 2:** $4x^2 - 7x + 2$
- Term 1: $4x^2$
- Term 2: $-7x$ (notice that the minus sign stays attached to the term)
- Term 3: $2$
- Total: $3$ terms.

**Example 3:** $5xy$
- Only $1$ term. There is no $+$ or $-$ between $x$ and $y$, only multiplication.

> Remember: **$+$ and $-$ separate terms.** Multiplication ($\times$) and division ($\div$) happen inside a single term.

### Coefficient

The **number multiplying the variable(s) in a term** is called its **coefficient**. It tells us how many times that variable part appears.

- In $7x$, the coefficient of $x$ is **$7$** (meaning $7$ times $x$).
- In $-3y$, the coefficient of $y$ is **$-3$**.
- In $x$, the coefficient of $x$ is **$1$** (because $x = 1 \times x$).
- In $-a$, the coefficient of $a$ is **$-1$**.

Here is an interesting extra detail. In the term **$5xy$**:
- Looking only at the number, the numerical coefficient is **$5$**.
- The coefficient of $x$ is **$5y$** (because $5xy = 5y \times x$).
- The coefficient of $y$ is **$5x$**.

### Constant Term

A term that contains **no variable at all** is called a **constant term**.

In $4x^2 - 7x + 2$, the constant term is **$2$**.

## Part E: Like Terms and Unlike Terms

Imagine you have **$3$ apples** in a basket, and a friend gives you **$5$ more apples**. How many apples do you have? **$8$ apples.** Easy!

Now suppose you have **$3$ apples** and a friend hands you **$5$ bananas**. Can you combine them into "8 apple-bananas"? No! They are different items, so they cannot be merged into a single combined count.

Algebra works the exact same way.

**Like terms** are terms that have **the exact same variables with the exact same powers**. Only their numerical coefficients can differ.

| Terms | Like or Unlike? | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| $3x$ and $8x$ | Like | Both have $x$ to the power $1$ |
| $5y^2$ and $-2y^2$ | Like | Both have $y^2$ |
| $4ab$ and $7ba$ | Like | $ab$ and $ba$ are identical (multiplication order does not matter) |
| $3x$ and $3x^2$ | Unlike | One has $x$, the other has $x^2$ |
| $2x$ and $2y$ | Unlike | Different variables |
| $5xy$ and $5x$ | Unlike | One has both $x$ and $y$, the other has only $x$ |
| $6$ and $6x$ | Unlike | One is a constant, the other contains a variable |

### Adding Like Terms

When adding or subtracting like terms, **only combine the coefficients; the variable part stays unchanged**.

- $3x + 5x = \mathbf{8x}$ ($3\text{ apples} + 5\text{ apples} = 8\text{ apples}$)
- $9y - 4y = \mathbf{5y}$
- $7a^2 - 10a^2 = \mathbf{-3a^2}$
- $2xy + 6xy - xy = \mathbf{7xy}$

Unlike terms cannot be combined:

- $3x + 5y$ cannot be shortened any further; it stays $3x + 5y$.
- $2x + 3x^2$ also stays as it is.

### Simplifying Example

**Simplify: $5x^2 + 3x - 2x^2 + 4x + 1$**

Step 1: Group the like terms together.
- $x^2$ terms: $5x^2$ and $-2x^2$
- $x$ terms: $3x$ and $4x$
- Constant term: $1$

Step 2: Combine each group.
- $5x^2 - 2x^2 = 3x^2$
- $3x + 4x = 7x$
- $1$ is alone, so it stays $1$

**Answer: $3x^2 + 7x + 1$**

## Part F: Types of Expressions (Based on Number of Terms)

| Name | Number of terms | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Monomial** | $1$ term (*mono* = one) | $5x$, $7a^2b$, $-4$ |
| **Binomial** | $2$ terms (*bi* = two) | $x + 3$, $2a - 5b$ |
| **Trinomial** | $3$ terms (*tri* = three) | $x^2 + 2x + 1$, $a + b + c$ |
| **Multinomial** | More than $3$ terms | $x^3 + x^2 + x + 1$ |

An easy way to remember the prefixes: a **bi**cycle has $2$ wheels and a **tri**cycle has $3$ wheels — the exact same "bi" and "tri" appear here.

**Watch out:** Always simplify an expression before counting its terms. For example, $3x + 5x + 2$ looks like a trinomial at first glance, but combining like terms gives $8x + 2$, which is actually a **binomial**.

## Part G: Forming Expressions from Words

One of algebra's greatest strengths is turning word descriptions into compact mathematics. First, check these common English keywords:

| Words | Mathematical operation |
|---|---|
| more than, plus, increased by, sum | $+$ |
| less than, minus, decreased by, difference | $-$ |
| of, times, product, twice ($2 \times$), thrice ($3 \times$) | $\times$ |
| divided by, quotient, half of ($\div 2$) | $\div$ |

### Examples

1. **"$5$ times a number $x$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{5x}$
2. **"$7$ more than a number $x$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{x + 7}$
3. **"$3$ less than a number $y$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{y - 3}$
4. **"Half of a number $n$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{\frac{n}{2}}$
5. **"Add $5$ to twice a number $x$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{2x + 5}$
6. **"Subtract $3$ from $4$ times a number $y$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{4y - 3}$
7. **"Multiply the sum of $a$ and $b$ by $2$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{2(a + b)}$
8. **"The square of a number $x$, plus $3$ times $x$"** $\rightarrow \mathbf{x^2 + 3x}$

### Watch Out: The Reversed Order of "Less Than"

Both **"3 subtracted from $x$"** and **"$3$ less than $x$"** translate to **$x - 3$**.

However, **"$3$ minus $x$"** means **$3 - x$**, which is completely different. Whenever you see "less than" or "subtracted from", the amount being taken away goes **after** the minus sign.

### Real-Life Examples

- A rectangle has length $l$ and breadth $b$. Its perimeter is **$2(l + b)$** and its area is **$lb$**.
- A square has side length $s$. Its perimeter is **$4s$** and its area is **$s^2$**.
- Sophia is $x$ years old today. In $5$ years, her age will be **$x + 5$**. Three years ago, her age was **$x - 3$**. Her father is $25$ years older than her, so his age is **$x + 25$**.
- A notebook costs \$n and a pen costs \$p. The total cost of $4$ notebooks and $6$ pens is **$4n + 6p$**.

## Part H: Finding the Value of an Expression

Replacing the variable in an expression with a specific number is called **substituting**. The number you get after simplifying is the **value** of the expression.

Let's return to Maya's ride-share fare: $\text{Fare} = 30 + 12x$.

- When $x = 10$: $30 + 12(10) = 30 + 120 = \mathbf{\$150}$
- When $x = 3$: $30 + 12(3) = 30 + 36 = \mathbf{\$66}$

More worked examples:

**Example 1:** Find the value of $3x + 5$ when $x = 2$.
$$3(2) + 5 = 6 + 5 = \mathbf{11}$$

**Example 2:** Find the value of $x^2 - 4x + 7$ when $x = -1$.
$$(-1)^2 - 4(-1) + 7 = 1 + 4 + 7 = \mathbf{12}$$

**Example 3:** Find the value of $2a + 3b$ when $a = 2$ and $b = -3$.
$$2(2) + 3(-3) = 4 - 9 = \mathbf{-5}$$

**Example 4:** Find the value of $x^2 + y^2 - 2xy$ when $x = 3$ and $y = 2$.
$$3^2 + 2^2 - 2(3)(2) = 9 + 4 - 12 = \mathbf{1}$$

> **Tip:** Whenever you substitute a negative number, **always wrap it in brackets**. For $x = -1$, write $x^2$ as $(-1)^2$, not $-1^2$. Those two give different answers: $(-1)^2 = 1$, whereas $-1^2 = -1$.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Reading $2x$ as "2 and $x$ side by side" (like digits of a number).**
   $2x$ means $2 \times x$. If $x = 5$, then $2x = \mathbf{10}$, not $25$.

2. **Mistake 2: Adding unlike terms together.**
   $3x + 5 \neq 8x$. Here $3x$ is a variable term and $5$ is a constant. Check with numbers: at $x = 1$, $3x + 5 = 8$; at $x = 2$, $3x + 5 = 11$, whereas $8x = 16$. Since they give different values, they are not equal.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing $x^2$ and $2x$.**
   - $x^2 = x \times x$
   - $2x = x + x$
   When $x = 4$, $x^2 = 16$, whereas $2x = 8$.

4. **Mistake 4: Treating $xy$ as $x + y$.**
   When $x = 3$ and $y = 4$, $xy = 12$, whereas $x + y = 7$.

5. **Mistake 5: Forgetting the sign in front of a term.**
   In $x^2 - 5x + 6$, the second term is **$-5x$**, not $5x$. The sign always travels with the term.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking the coefficient of $x$ is $0$.**
   When a variable is written alone as $x$, its coefficient is **$1$**, not $0$.

---

## Quick Recap

- **Constant:** a number whose value never changes ($5, -3, \pi$).
- **Variable:** a letter whose value can change ($x, y, a$).
- **Algebraic expression:** a mathematical phrase combining constants and variables with $+$, $-$, $\times$, $\div$ (such as $3x + 5$). It has no equals ($=$) sign.
- **Term:** a part of an expression separated by $+$ or $-$.
- **Coefficient:** the numerical factor (or multiplier) attached to a variable in a term.
- **Like terms:** terms with the exact same variables and exponents. Only like terms can be added or subtracted.
- **Monomial, Binomial, Trinomial:** expressions with $1$, $2$, and $3$ terms respectively.
- **Evaluating an expression:** substitute the given number for each variable (always use brackets for negative numbers).

# Topic 2: Terms of an Algebraic Expression

In Topic 1, we saw that a term is a component of an expression separated by **+ or -**, and a coefficient is the numerical factor attached to a variable. Now, we take our understanding a step deeper. In this topic, we will examine what lies **inside** a term (its factors and coefficients) and master how to count terms when expressions involve brackets or fractions.

---

## A Meal Tray Story

Picture Elena sitting down in the school cafeteria with a divided lunch tray. In its separate compartments are soup, salad, steamed rice, and a bread roll.

- **The whole tray is the expression.**
- **Each separate compartment is a term.**
- The dividers keep the compartments separate — that is exactly what separates one term from another.

Now look inside a single compartment — say, the salad bowl. Inside it, lettuce, cucumbers, tomatoes, and dressing are tossed together. They all **combine to form one single compartment** (one term).

In algebra, the pieces **multiplied together** inside a single term are called the **factors** of that term.

Keep this one rule in mind:

> **Terms are separated by $+$ and $-$. Factors are joined by $\times$.**

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Factor** | One of the numbers or letters multiplied together inside a term | In $5xy$, the factors are $5, x, y$ |
| **Numerical factor** | The number among the factors of a term | In $5xy$, the numerical factor is **$5$** |
| **Literal factor** | The variable letters among the factors of a term (*literal* = letter-based) | In $5xy$, the literal factors are **$x$ and $y$** |
| **Numerical coefficient** | The numerical part of a term (same as the numerical factor) | In $5xy$, **$5$** |
| **Literal coefficient** | The variable part of a term | In $5xy$, **$xy$** |

---

## Part A: Factors Inside a Term

Any term can be broken down into **a product of its individual factors**.

**Example 1:** Term **$5xy$**
$$5xy = 5 \times x \times y$$
Factors: **$5, x, y$**

**Example 2:** Term **$4x^2y$**
Since $x^2 = x \times x$:
$$4x^2y = 4 \times x \times x \times y$$
Factors: **$4, x, x, y$**

**Example 3:** Term **$-7ab^2c$**
Since $b^2 = b \times b$:
$$-7ab^2c = (-7) \times a \times b \times b \times c$$
Factors: **$-7, a, b, b, c$**

Notice that the minus sign stays attached to the **number**. That is why the numerical factor is **$-7$**, not $7$.

**Example 4:** Term **$x$**
$$x = 1 \times x$$
Factors: **$1, x$**

### Separating Numerical and Literal Factors

| Term | Numerical factor | Literal factors |
|---|---|---|
| $6a$ | $6$ | $a$ |
| $-3pq$ | $-3$ | $p, q$ |
| $8x^2y$ | $8$ | $x, x, y$ |
| $-z$ | $-1$ | $z$ |
| $\frac{2}{5}mn$ | $\frac{2}{5}$ | $m, n$ |
| $9$ | $9$ | none |

Look at the last row: the constant term $9$ has no letters at all, so it only has a numerical factor.

---

## Part B: Understanding Coefficients in Depth

In Topic 1, we saw that in $5xy$, the coefficient of $x$ is $5y$. Now let's state the complete rule:

> **The coefficient of any factor = the product of all the remaining factors in the term.**

In other words, "cover up" the factor you are asked about — whatever remains is its coefficient.

### All Coefficients of the Term $9x^2yz$

| Coefficient of... | Cover up | Remaining product (coefficient) |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical coefficient | all letters | **$9$** |
| $x^2$ | $x^2$ (leaving $9, y, z$) | **$9yz$** |
| $y$ | $y$ (leaving $9, x^2, z$) | **$9x^2z$** |
| $z$ | $z$ (leaving $9, x^2, y$) | **$9x^2y$** |
| $yz$ | $yz$ (leaving $9, x^2$) | **$9x^2$** |
| $x^2y$ | $x^2y$ (leaving $9, z$) | **$9z$** |

As you can see, **a single term can have many different coefficients**, depending on which factor you are asking about.

### When a Term Contains a Fraction

- In **$\frac{3}{5}pq$**, the numerical coefficient is **$\frac{3}{5}$**.
- Rewrite **$-\frac{x^2y}{3}$** as $-\frac{1}{3} \times x^2y$, so its numerical coefficient is **$-\frac{1}{3}$**.
- Rewrite **$\frac{y}{4}$** as $\frac{1}{4} \times y$, so its numerical coefficient is **$\frac{1}{4}$**.

### When a Term Contains $\pi$

Remember that $\pi$ is a constant number (approximately $3.14$). Therefore, in **$2\pi r$**, the numerical coefficient is **$2\pi$** and the literal factor is **$r$**. The circumference of a circle takes this exact form: $C = 2\pi r$.

---

## Part C: Counting Terms with Brackets or Fractions

This is where students make the most mistakes, so read carefully.

### Rule 1: Signs ($+$ / $-$) Inside Brackets Do Not Create Separate Terms

As long as a bracket **has not been expanded**, the entire bracketed part counts as a single term.

**Example:** $2(x + y) - 5$

As written:
- Term 1: **$2(x + y)$**
- Term 2: **$-5$**
- Total: **$2$ terms**.

After expanding the brackets:
$$2(x + y) - 5 = 2x + 2y - 5$$
- Terms: $2x, 2y, -5$
- Total: **$3$ terms**.

So whenever a question asks you to count terms, check whether it means **as written** or **after simplifying/expanding**. Usually, we expand brackets first.

### Rule 2: Watch the $+$ / $-$ in Fraction Numerators

- **$\frac{x}{2} + \frac{y}{3}$**: This has **$2$ terms** ($\frac{x}{2}$ and $\frac{y}{3}$).
- **$\frac{x + y}{2}$**: As written, this is a single combined term. Split into $\frac{x}{2} + \frac{y}{2}$, it becomes **$2$ terms**.

### Rule 3: Do Not Count Terms Inside Multiplication or Powers

- **$3x^2y$** $\rightarrow$ $1$ term (only multiplication is present).
- **$(x + 1)^2$** $\rightarrow$ $1$ term as written. Expanding it produces $x^2 + 2x + 1$, which has $3$ terms. (We will learn how to expand expressions like this in later topics.)

### Quick Check for Counting Terms

| Expression | Terms | Count |
|---|---|---|
| $5x^2 - 3xy + 2y^2 - 7$ | $5x^2, -3xy, 2y^2, -7$ | $4$ |
| $\frac{a}{2} - \frac{b}{3}$ | $\frac{a}{2}, -\frac{b}{3}$ | $2$ |
| $6mn$ | $6mn$ | $1$ |
| $4p + 3(q - r)$ | $4p, 3(q - r)$ | $2$ (as written) |
| $4p + 3q - 3r$ | $4p, 3q, -3r$ | $3$ (after expanding) |

---

## Part D: A Term Always Carries Its Sign

We mentioned this in Topic 1, and it is important enough to verify with another quick example.

Expression: **$8a - 3b + 5$**

| Statement | Correct or Incorrect? |
|---|---|
| The terms are $8a, 3b, 5$ | Incorrect (there is a minus sign in front of $3b$) |
| The terms are $8a, \mathbf{-3b}, 5$ | Correct |

That is why the coefficient of $b$ is **$-3$**. This sign matters whenever you evaluate an expression or combine like terms.

**Example:** Take the $-3b$ term from $8a - 3b + 5$ and add $2b$.
$$-3b + 2b = \mathbf{-b}$$

If you mistakenly dropped the minus sign and used $+3b$, you would get $5b$, which is wrong.

## Part E: Arranging Terms

When an expression has several terms, writing them in a neat order makes it much easier to read. There are two standard ways to arrange terms:

- **Descending order:** highest power of $x$ first, stepping down to the lowest power. This is the most common order.
- **Ascending order:** lowest power of $x$ first, stepping up to the highest power.

**Example:** Expression **$5 + 2x^3 - x + 4x^2$**

First inspect each term: constant $5$, $x^3$ term $2x^3$, $x$ term $-x$, and $x^2$ term $4x^2$.

**Descending order (powers decreasing):**
$$2x^3 + 4x^2 - x + 5$$

**Ascending order (powers increasing):**
$$5 - x + 4x^2 + 2x^3$$

Remember that **each term keeps its sign when moved**. When moving $-x$, keep the minus sign attached to it.

## Part F: Terms and Factors in Real Life

Look at an equipment and stationery receipt for Liam's design project:

- $4$ sketchbooks at \$n each
- $3$ technical pens at \$p each
- $1$ backpack for \$250

Total bill $= \mathbf{4n + 3p + 250}$

| Term | What it represents | Factors |
|---|---|---|
| $4n$ | Cost of sketchbooks | $4$ (quantity), $n$ (unit price) |
| $3p$ | Cost of pens | $3$ (quantity), $p$ (unit price) |
| $250$ | Cost of backpack | numerical constant only |

Here, the cost of each category is a **term**, and inside each term, "quantity $\times$ unit price" gives its **factors**.

One more example: adding a rectangle's area and perimeter gives:
$$lb + 2l + 2b$$
- Terms: $lb, 2l, 2b$ ($3$ terms)
- Factors of $lb$: $l$ and $b$
- Factors of $2l$: $2$ and $l$

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing terms with factors.**
   In $3x + 5$, **$3x$** and **$5$** are **terms**. Inside $3x$, **$3$** and **$x$** are **factors**. Terms are separated by $+$ / $-$; factors are joined by $\times$.

2. **Mistake 2: Dropping the minus sign.**
   In $x^2 - 4x + 1$, the second term is **$-4x$**, not $4x$.

3. **Mistake 3: Counting terms inside unexpanded brackets separately.**
   As written, $3(a + b)$ is **one** term. Only after expanding does it split into two terms, $3a$ and $3b$.

4. **Mistake 4: Assuming a coefficient is always just a number.**
   For "the coefficient of $x$ in $5xy$", the answer is **$5y$**, not $5$. You answer $5$ only when asked specifically for the *numerical* coefficient.

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking the coefficient of a lone variable is $0$.**
   If a term is written simply as $x$, its coefficient is **$1$**. For $-x$, the coefficient is **$-1$**.

6. **Mistake 6: Thinking a constant term has no factors.**
   A constant term like $7$ still has a numerical factor of $7$ — it simply has no literal (letter) factors.

---

## Quick Recap

- **Terms** are separated by $+$ and $-$. **Factors** are multiplied ($\times$) together inside a term.
- The number inside a term is its **numerical factor** (or **numerical coefficient**); the letters are its **literal factors**.
- **Coefficient of a factor** $=$ the product of all remaining factors in the term.
- Every term carries its sign with it ($-3b$ is the term, not $3b$).
- Unexpanded brackets count as a single term; expanding them creates separate terms.
- $\frac{x}{2} + \frac{y}{3}$ has $2$ terms, whereas $\frac{x + y}{2}$ is $1$ term as written.
- Terms can be arranged in descending or ascending order of powers (always carrying their signs).
