# Topic 1: What Is a Polygon?

In the Quadrilaterals chapter, we studied $4$-sided figures—squares, rectangles, rhombuses, parallelograms, and trapeziums. Every one of them was formed by joining $4$ straight line segments into a closed loop. Now ask yourself: what if there are not $4$ sides, but $5$, $7$, or $20$ sides? In this chapter, we study **all** such closed, straight-edged shapes together as one unified family. First, let us learn what a polygon is and how we name polygons by counting their sides.

---

## The Mosaic Floor of the Grand Hall

On a museum field trip, a group of students stepped into a historic limestone gallery. The entire floor was laid in colourful mosaic stone tiles—three-cornered triangles, neat squares, six-sided star patterns in the centre, and eight-sided tiles bordering the walls.

The curator, Dr. Bennett, turned to Sophia and asked, "Can you name these tile shapes?"

Sophia knelt down and pointed along the floor: "These three-cornered pieces are triangles. These four-cornered ones are squares. And this one..." She traced each edge carefully with her finger: "one, two, three, four, five, six—six sides! What do we call it?"

"A **hexagon**," Dr. Bennett smiled. "And that eight-sided border tile?"

Sophia counted again: "...eight. It must have a special name too!"

"An **octagon**."

Then Sophia spotted an unusual stone medallion tucked in the corner and counted: "one, two, three, four, five, six, seven—seven sides! Dr. Bennett, what is this one called?"

Dr. Bennett grinned. "Great catch! That is a **heptagon**. Every one of these names comes straight from counting the sides. And notice something all these tiles have in common—can you spot it?"

Sophia thought for a moment. "Every edge is completely **straight**, and every shape is **closed**—no open gaps anywhere."

"Exactly right," said Dr. Bennett. "That is the definition of a **polygon**."

In this topic, we will see precisely what makes a shape a polygon, how to tell polygons apart from non-polygons, and what counting the sides immediately reveals.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Polygon** | A closed plane figure formed entirely from straight line segments | Triangle, square, hexagon |
| **Rectilinear** | *Rect* $=$ straight, *linear* $=$ of lines; made solely of straight segments | Polygons are also called rectilinear figures |
| **Side** | One of the straight line segments forming a polygon | A triangle has $3$ sides |
| **Vertex** *(plural: vertices)* | A corner point where two adjacent sides meet | A square has $4$ vertices |
| **Interior angle** | An angle formed inside the polygon between two adjacent sides at a vertex | Each interior angle of a square is $90^\circ$ |
| **Diagonal** | A line segment joining any two non-adjacent vertices | A square has $2$ diagonals |
| **Simple polygon** | A polygon whose sides never cross one another between vertices | Triangle, hexagon |
| **Convex polygon** | A polygon in which every interior angle is strictly less than $180^\circ$ | Square, rectangle |
| **Concave polygon** | A polygon with at least one interior angle greater than $180^\circ$ (a reflex angle) | An arrowhead (dart) shape |
| **Regular polygon** | A polygon with all sides equal and all interior angles equal | Square, equilateral triangle |
| **$n$-gon** | A polygon having $n$ sides | A $12$-sided polygon is a $12$-gon |

## Part A: What Is a Polygon?

A **polygon** is a plane figure that is:
1. made **exclusively of straight line segments** (no curves), and
2. **closed** (the path ends right where it started, leaving no gap).

That is why this chapter is titled *Rectilinear Figures*—figures built purely out of straight lines.

Each straight segment is a **side**, and each point where two sides meet is a **vertex**. In any polygon, **the number of sides always equals the number of vertices** (a triangle has $3$ sides and $3$ vertices; a hexagon has $6$ and $6$).

### Polygon or Not?

```
  Is a polygon                 Not a polygon

   A-------B                    ___
   |       |                   /   \      <- circle: no straight
   |       |                   \___/         sides at all
   D-------C

     /\                   A--B
    /  \                       \          <- open zigzag:
   /    \                      C--D          not closed
  /______\

                              ______
  Pentagon                   |      \     <- one edge is
     /\                      |       )       curved
    /  \                     |______/
   /    \
   \    /
    \__/
```

Notice the third non-example: three of its sides are straight, yet one side is curved. Even a **single curve** disqualifies a shape from being a polygon.

**The two-question polygon test:** Ask: *"Is every edge straight?"* and *"Is the figure completely closed?"* Only if both answers are **yes** is it a polygon.

- Circle: no straight edges $\implies$ Not a polygon ❌
- Open zigzag: not closed $\implies$ Not a polygon ❌
- Closed figure with all straight sides (like a triangle): Polygon ✅

## Part B: Naming Polygons by Side Count

A polygon is named according to how many sides it has:

| Sides | Name | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|
| $3$ | **Triangle** | Triangular truss brace, drafting set-square |
| $4$ | **Quadrilateral** | Square tile, rectangular window frame |
| $5$ | **Pentagon** | Classic house-front outline, the Pentagon building in Arlington, cross-section of a bellflower |
| $6$ | **Hexagon** | Honeycomb cell, hexagonal bolt head, cross-section of a standard wooden pencil |
| $7$ | **Heptagon** | Rare in daily objects; $50\text{p}$ and $20\text{p}$ curved-edge coins are inspired by $7$-gons, Sophia's museum tile |
| $8$ | **Octagon** | Red octagonal STOP street sign |
| $9$ | **Nonagon** | $9$-sided architectural dome or decorative rosette |
| $10$ | **Decagon** | Decorative star-and-tile geometric inlay |

For polygons with more than $10$ sides, we usually just call them an **"$n$-gon"**: a $12$-sided figure is a **$12$-gon**, a $20$-sided figure is a **$20$-gon**, and a polygon with an unknown number $n$ of sides is an **$n$-gon**.

**Memory trick for the names:** The prefixes come from Greek/Latin roots: *tri* $= 3$, *quad* $= 4$, *penta* $= 5$, *hexa* $= 6$, *hepta* $= 7$, *octa* $= 8$, *nona* $= 9$, *deca* $= 10$, and *-gon* means "angle/corner." So a pentagon literally means "five corners," and Sophia's $7$-sided tile is a heptagon.

Notice too: **an $n$-gon has $n$ sides, $n$ vertices, and $n$ interior angles.**

**Check:** For an octagon: $8$ sides, $8$ vertices, $8$ interior angles ✅

## Part C: Simple and Non-Simple Polygons

Some closed figures made of straight segments have sides that **cross over each other** in the middle—like a bow-tie:

```
 A--------B
  \      /
   \    /
    \  /
     \/        <- two sides cross
     /\           each other here
    /  \
   /    \
  /      \
 D--------C
```

Tracing $A \to B \to D \to C \to A$, segments $BD$ and $CA$ intersect right in the centre. This is **not** a simple polygon.

> **Simple polygon:** A polygon whose sides meet *only* at their endpoints (vertices) and never cross one another anywhere else.

**Throughout this chapter, whenever we say "polygon," we mean a simple polygon.** Self-intersecting bow-tie shapes are excluded.

---

## Part D: Convex and Concave Polygons

Simple polygons fall into two classes:

**Convex polygon:** Every interior angle is **strictly less than $180^\circ$**. Equivalently, if you pick *any* two points inside the polygon and join them with a straight segment, the entire segment stays **completely inside** the polygon.

**Concave polygon:** At least one interior angle is **greater than $180^\circ$** (a **reflex angle**). A concave polygon looks as if one corner has been "caved in."

```
  Convex (pentagon)              Concave (arrowhead)

       /\                              A
      /  \                            / \
     /    \                          /   \
    |      |                        /     \
    |______|                       /   C   \
                                  /  _/ \_  \
                                 B_/       \_D
```

In the arrowhead, the interior angle at vertex $C$ exceeds $180^\circ$ (a reflex angle), making the polygon concave.

An easy visual test: pick one point inside tip $B$ and another inside tip $D$ and connect them with a straight line. That segment passes **outside** the polygon across the indented notch at $C$! That never happens in a convex polygon.

| Test Question | Convex | Concave |
|---|---|---|
| Every interior angle? | Strictly less than $180^\circ$ | At least one exceeds $180^\circ$ |
| Segment joining any two interior points? | Always stays inside | Can step outside the boundary |
| Examples | Square, triangle, regular hexagon | Arrowhead (dart), L-shaped floor plan |

Every triangle is automatically convex (none of its angles can ever reach $180^\circ$, as we know from the angle sum of $180^\circ$).

## Part E: Regular and Irregular Polygons

> **Regular polygon:** A polygon in which **all sides are equal** **and** **all interior angles are equal**.

Any polygon that is not regular is called **irregular**.

Classic regular polygons include the equilateral triangle, square, regular pentagon, regular hexagon (honeycomb cell), and regular octagon (STOP sign).

### Equal Sides Alone Are Not Enough

A **rhombus** has all $4$ sides equal, yet its angles need not be equal (for example, $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$). Therefore, a rhombus is **not regular**.

```
      ____
     /   /       <- Rhombus: equal sides,
    /   /           but unequal angles
   /___/
```

### Equal Angles Alone Are Not Enough

A **rectangle** has all $4$ angles equal ($90^\circ$), yet its adjacent sides have different lengths. Therefore, a rectangle is **not regular** either.

```
   ________
  |        |      <- Rectangle: equal angles (90°),
  |________|         but unequal sides
```

### So Which $4$-Sided Polygon Is Regular?

Only the shape that satisfies **both** conditions at once—all sides equal **and** all angles equal—namely the **square**. Likewise, a triangle is regular only when it is **equilateral** (all sides equal and all angles $60^\circ$).

**Quick check:**

| Figure | All Sides Equal? | All Angles Equal? | Regular? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Square | Yes | Yes | Yes ✅ |
| Rhombus | Yes | No | No ❌ |
| Rectangle | No | Yes | No ❌ |

Regular polygons will play a huge role in Topics 2, 3, and 4—for now, lock in this two-part definition.

## Part F: Counting Diagonals

A **diagonal** is a line segment connecting two **non-adjacent** vertices of a polygon. Connecting two adjacent vertices simply traces a side of the polygon, not a diagonal.

### Count Small Polygons First

**Quadrilateral ($n = 4$):** From vertex $A$, vertices $B$ and $D$ are neighbours (sides $AB$ and $AD$). Only $C$ remains, giving **$1$ diagonal** ($AC$) from $A$, and **$2$ diagonals in total** ($AC$ and $BD$).

**Pentagon ($n = 5$):** From vertex $A$, skip $A$ itself and its two neighbours, leaving $2$ non-adjacent vertices. Across all $5$ vertices, there are **$5$ diagonals in total** (forming a five-pointed star inside!).

### Where Does the Formula Come From?

Consider a general $n$-gon (with $n$ vertices).

**Step 1: How many diagonals start from ONE vertex?**
From any single vertex, you cannot draw a diagonal to **itself** ($1$ vertex excluded) or to its **$2$ adjacent neighbours** ($2$ vertices excluded, as those form sides). That leaves **$n - 3$** vertices:

> **From one vertex, you can draw $(n - 3)$ diagonals.**

**Step 2: How many diagonals across ALL vertices?**
There are $n$ vertices, and each sends out $(n - 3)$ diagonals, giving $n(n - 3)$ endpoints. However, every diagonal has two ends, so we counted each diagonal **twice**—once from $A$ to $C$, and once from $C$ to $A$. Divide by $2$:

$$\text{Total diagonals} = \frac{n(n - 3)}{2}$$

### Testing the Formula

| Polygon | $n$ | $n - 3$ | $n(n - 3)$ | $n(n - 3) \div 2$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Triangle | $3$ | $0$ | $0$ | **$0$** |
| Quadrilateral | $4$ | $1$ | $4$ | **$2$** |
| Pentagon | $5$ | $2$ | $10$ | **$5$** |
| Hexagon | $6$ | $3$ | $18$ | **$9$** |
| Heptagon | $7$ | $4$ | $28$ | **$14$** |
| Octagon | $8$ | $5$ | $40$ | **$20$** |
| Decagon | $10$ | $7$ | $70$ | **$35$** |

A triangle has $0$ diagonals (every vertex is adjacent to the other two), and the formula gives $0$ ✅.

**List a hexagon's diagonals by hand (should be $9$):** With vertices $A, B, C, D, E, F$, the diagonals are $AC, AD, AE, BD, BE, BF, CE, CF, DF$. Count $= 9$ ✅, matching the formula!

## Part G: Solved Examples

### Example 1: Name the Polygon

A polygon has $9$ vertices. What is its name?
$9$ vertices means $n = 9$ sides, so it is a **nonagon**. Check: $9$ vertices $\implies 9$ sides ✅

### Example 2: Convex or Concave?

A pentagon has interior angles $70^\circ, 80^\circ, 60^\circ, 90^\circ$, and $240^\circ$. Is it convex or concave?
Because $240^\circ$ is **greater than $180^\circ$** (a reflex angle), the pentagon is **concave**.

### Example 3: Diagonals of a $12$-gon

Here $n = 12$.
- From one vertex: $n - 3 = 12 - 3 = \mathbf{9}$ diagonals
- Total diagonals: $\dfrac{12 \times 9}{2} = \dfrac{108}{2} = \mathbf{54}$ diagonals

**Check:** $12 \times 9 = 108$, and $108 \div 2 = 54$ ✅

### Example 4: Finding the Number of Sides from Diagonals

A polygon has $27$ diagonals in total. How many sides does it have?

$$\frac{n(n - 3)}{2} = 27 \implies n(n - 3) = 54$$
Look for two whole numbers differing by $3$ whose product is $54$: $9 \times 6 = 54$ ($9 - 3 = 6$).
Therefore, $n = \mathbf{9}$ (a nonagon).

**Check:** $9 \times 6 \div 2 = 54 \div 2 = 27$ ✅

### Example 5: Given the Diagonals from One Vertex

Exactly $7$ diagonals can be drawn from one vertex of a polygon. How many sides and how many total diagonals does it have?

$n - 3 = 7 \implies n = \mathbf{10}$ (a decagon).
Total diagonals $= \dfrac{10 \times 7}{2} = \mathbf{35}$.

**Check:** Matches $35$ for a decagon in the table above ✅

### Example 6: Can Such a Polygon Exist?

Can a polygon have *exactly* $10$ diagonals?
$\dfrac{n(n - 3)}{2} = 10 \implies n(n - 3) = 20$.
Test consecutive integers: for $n = 5$, $5 \times 2 = 10$; for $n = 6$, $6 \times 3 = 18$; for $n = 7$, $7 \times 4 = 28$. It skips right past $20$!
**No**, no polygon has exactly $10$ diagonals.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Counting sides as diagonals.**
   In pentagon $ABCDE$, connecting $A$ to $B$ gives side $AB$, not a diagonal. Diagonals from $A$ go only to non-adjacent vertices ($C$ and $D$). That is why there are $n - 3$ per vertex, not $n - 1$.

2. **Mistake 2: Forgetting to divide by $2$ when counting total diagonals.**
   In a hexagon, $6$ vertices $\times$ $3$ diagonals each gives $18$, because each diagonal ($AD$ and $DA$) was counted twice. Always **divide by $2$**: $18 \div 2 = \mathbf{9}$ ✅

3. **Mistake 3: Calling a bow-tie shape a polygon.**
   A bow-tie's sides cross in the middle, so it is not a simple polygon. In this chapter, "polygon" always means a simple polygon.

4. **Mistake 4: Thinking "regular" only requires equal sides.**
   A rhombus has equal sides, yet it is not regular because its angles are unequal. "Regular" requires **both** equal sides **and** equal angles.

5. **Mistake 5: Counting shapes with curved edges as polygons.**
   If even a single edge is curved, the figure is not a polygon.

## Quick Recap

- **Polygon** $=$ a closed plane figure formed exclusively by straight line segments (hence *rectilinear*).
- The number of sides, vertices, and interior angles is always **equal** ($n$ each in an $n$-gon).
- Names: $3$ triangle, $4$ quadrilateral, $5$ pentagon, $6$ hexagon, $7$ heptagon, $8$ octagon, $9$ nonagon, $10$ decagon, and $n$-gon beyond $10$.
- **Simple polygon:** sides never cross between vertices (no bow-ties).
- **Convex:** every interior angle $< 180^\circ$. **Concave:** at least one interior angle $> 180^\circ$ (reflex).
- **Regular:** all sides equal **and** all angles equal. Neither a rhombus nor a rectangle is regular.
- **Diagonal:** segment connecting two non-adjacent vertices.
- Diagonals from one vertex $= \mathbf{n - 3}$. Total diagonals $= \mathbf{\dfrac{n(n - 3)}{2}}$.

## Practice Questions

1. Which of the following are polygons? Give a reason for each: (a) a triangle, (b) a circle, (c) a figure made of $5$ straight segments that does not close, (d) a figure with $4$ straight sides and one curved side.

2. Name the polygon with (a) $5$ sides, (b) $8$ sides, (c) $10$ sides. What do we call a polygon with $15$ sides?

3. Draw (or describe) a concave quadrilateral. How many reflex angles can a quadrilateral have at most? Explain with a sketch idea.

4. A student says: "A rhombus is a regular polygon because all its sides are equal." Is she right? Explain. Give a similar example for the "equal angles" case.

5. Find the number of diagonals of (a) a heptagon, (b) a $15$-gon.

6. A polygon has $44$ diagonals. How many sides does it have?

7. From one vertex of a polygon, $10$ diagonals can be drawn. Find the number of sides and the total number of diagonals.

8. Is there a polygon with exactly $12$ diagonals? Justify.

9. A student counts the diagonals of a hexagon as $6 \times 3 = 18$. Where is the mistake?

10. State True or False:
    (a) Every polygon has the same number of sides and vertices.
    (b) A bow-tie shaped figure is a simple polygon.
    (c) A triangle has no diagonals.
    (d) A rectangle is a regular polygon.

### Answers

1. (a) **Polygon:** $3$ straight sides, closed figure.
   (b) **Not a polygon:** no straight sides; entirely curved.
   (c) **Not a polygon:** the figure is not closed.
   (d) **Not a polygon:** one side is curved.

2. (a) Pentagon (b) Octagon (c) Decagon. A $15$-sided polygon is called a **$15$-gon**.

3. An arrowhead (dart) shape is a concave quadrilateral (see Part D). A quadrilateral can have **at most $1$ reflex angle** (its total angle sum is $360^\circ$, and two reflex angles alone would already exceed $360^\circ$; sketching it shows that trying to push two corners inward forces the sides to cross!).

4. No, she is wrong. A rhombus has equal sides, yet its angles need not be equal (for example, $60^\circ$ and $120^\circ$), so it is not regular. Similarly, a rectangle has all angles equal ($90^\circ$) yet unequal sides, so it is not regular either. A regular polygon needs both conditions.

5. (a) $n = 7$: $\dfrac{7 \times 4}{2} = \dfrac{28}{2} = \mathbf{14}$.
   (b) $n = 15$: $\dfrac{15 \times 12}{2} = \dfrac{180}{2} = \mathbf{90}$.
   Check: $7 \times 4 = 28, \; 28 \div 2 = 14$ ✅; $15 \times 12 = 180, \; 180 \div 2 = 90$ ✅

6. $\dfrac{n(n - 3)}{2} = 44 \implies n(n - 3) = 88$. At $n = 11$, $11 \times 8 = 88$ ✅. Number of sides $= \mathbf{11}$ ($11$-gon).

7. $n - 3 = 10 \implies n = \mathbf{13}$ ($13$-gon). Total diagonals $= \dfrac{13 \times 10}{2} = \dfrac{130}{2} = \mathbf{65}$.
   Check: $13 \times 10 = 130, \; 130 \div 2 = 65$ ✅

8. $\dfrac{n(n - 3)}{2} = 12 \implies n(n - 3) = 24$. Testing: $n = 6$ gives $18$, $n = 7$ gives $28$. $24$ never appears. **No**, no such polygon exists.

9. Each diagonal was counted twice (e.g., $AD$ and $DA$). She needed to **divide $18$ by $2$**: $18 \div 2 = \mathbf{9}$ diagonals.

10. (a) **True** ✅. An $n$-gon always has $n$ sides and $n$ vertices.
    (b) **False** ❌. Two sides cross each other, so it is not simple.
    (c) **True** ✅. Every vertex of a triangle is adjacent to both others, and the formula gives $3 \times 0 \div 2 = 0$.
    (d) **False** ❌. A rectangle's sides need not all be equal.
