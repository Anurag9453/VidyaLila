# Topic 1: Electric Charge and Static Electricity

In the previous chapter, we explored light—an energy we can visibly track as it travels in straight lines, bends across interfaces, and forms sharp images through lenses. Now, we encounter another fundamental form of energy humming quietly behind every wall switch: **electricity**. Ceiling fans, LED lights, smartphone chargers, refrigerators, and high-speed trains all rely on it. But what exactly is electricity? Is it an invisible fluid coursing through metal wires? Across the topics in this chapter, we will build this understanding step by step, beginning with the fundamental cornerstone of all electrical phenomena: **electric charge**.

In this first topic, charges do not travel along continuous circuits. Instead, they **remain at rest** on an object's surface—such as on a woollen sweater or an inflated balloon. For this reason, this study is known as **static electricity** (or electrostatics).

---

## A Cold Winter Evening, a Dark Room, and Sparks

It was a dry, chilly winter evening. Mr. Davis arrived home and sat down in the living room with his daughter Maya. Earlier that afternoon, Maya had discovered a curious parlor trick: she rubbed an inflated latex balloon vigorously against her dry hair and pressed it against the painted wall. To her amazement, the balloon **clung firmly to the wall**, seemingly defying gravity without any tape or glue! Next, she ran a plastic comb briskly through her dry hair and held it just above tiny scraps of paper on her desk. The paper scraps leapt off the desk and danced upward, sticking to the teeth of the comb.

Maya was bewildered: "Dad, why are these paper pieces jumping on their own? Is there a hidden magnet inside this plastic comb?"

Mr. Davis smiled. "There is no magnet at work here, Maya. But wait—let me show you something even more startling. Switch off the overhead lamp."

With the room cast in pitch darkness, Mr. Davis pulled off his heavy **woollen sweater**. As the fabric separated from his cotton shirt, distinct **crackling sounds** echoed through the room, accompanied by **faint blue sparks** flashing in the dark!

Maya gasped, stepping back: "Dad! Your sweater is sparking! Is that real electricity? Could we get an electric shock from it?"

Mr. Davis chuckled reassuringly. "Don't worry, Maya. This is not the dangerous current running through our home wall sockets. What you just witnessed is **static electricity**. By the end of this topic, you will understand how your hair, the balloon, the comb, the sweater, and even lightning in thunderclouds are all connected by the exact same physical principle." Let us unravel this puzzle together.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Electric charge** | An intrinsic physical property of matter that causes it to experience an electrostatic force (attraction or repulsion). Exists as positive (+) and negative (−) | A rubbed balloon acquires a negative charge |
| **Static electricity** | An accumulation of stationary electric charges residing on the surface of an object | A plastic comb lifting bits of paper |
| **Proton, neutron, electron** | Subatomic particles: protons carry positive charge, neutrons carry no charge (neutral), and electrons carry negative charge | Charge of an electron = $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$ |
| **Coulomb (C)** | The SI unit of electric charge | $1\text{ C} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{18}\text{ electrons}$ |
| **Conductor** | A material containing abundant mobile charges (free electrons) that allow electric charge to flow freely through it | Copper, aluminium, the human body |
| **Insulator** | A material whose electrons are tightly bound, preventing the free flow of electric charge | Plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood |
| **Earthing (Grounding)** | The process of sharing or discharging excess charge to the Earth through a conducting path | The third (top) pin in a three-prong household plug |
| **Electrostatic induction** | The temporary redistribution of electrical charges in an object caused by the proximity of a nearby charged body without physical contact | Holding a charged rod near a neutral metal sphere |
| **Gold-leaf electroscope** | A sensitive instrument used to detect the presence and sign of an electric charge | A glass-encased jar with two delicate gold leaves |

## Part A: The Origin of Charge: From Amber to Balloons

**A brief glimpse into history.** Around 600 BCE, the ancient Greek philosopher **Thales of Miletus** recorded that when a piece of **amber** (fossilized tree resin) was rubbed with wool or animal fur, it acquired the curious ability to attract lightweight particles such as feathers, straw, and lint. The ancient Greek word for amber was **"elektron"**, which is the etymological root of modern words such as **"electron"** and **"electricity"**.

The exact same phenomenon happens today when Maya rubs a plastic comb through dry hair or rubs a party balloon against a woollen jumper.

### Two Types of Charge

When different materials are rubbed together and brought near one another, scientists observed that some pairs attract each other while other pairs repel each other. This led American scientist and statesman **Benjamin Franklin** to conclude that there are strictly **two types of electric charge**, which he designated as **positive (+)** and **negative (−)**.

The fundamental law of electrostatics states:

> **Like charges repel each other (positive repels positive; negative repels negative).**  
> **Unlike charges attract each other (positive attracts negative).**

Furthermore, **a charged object can also attract a neutral (uncharged) object**. This explains how Maya's comb attracts uncharged paper scraps—a phenomenon driven by electrostatic induction, which we explore in Part F.

```
   Like charges: repel                     Unlike charges: attract

     (+) <──────  ──────> (+)                 (+) ──────>  <────── (−)

     (−) <──────  ──────> (−)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Like charges push apart (repulsion); opposite charges pull together (attraction).

**Try it yourself (completely safe):** Suspend two inflated balloons from light threads side by side. Rub both balloons thoroughly against woollen cloth or dry hair. When released, the two balloons **swing apart and repel each other**, because both have acquired the same type of (negative) charge. Now, rub one balloon and bring your bare hand close to it: the balloon swings toward your hand, demonstrating attraction between a charged body and an induced neutral body.

## Part B: Where Does Charge Come From? A Brief Model of the Atom

All physical matter is built from **atoms**. While atomic structure is studied in depth in chemistry, a simple physical picture suffices to understand electric charge:

- At the center lies a compact, dense **nucleus** containing **protons (positive charge)** and **neutrons (electrically neutral)**.
- Surrounding the nucleus, tiny **electrons (negative charge)** orbit in continuous shells.

```
              e⁻
        .  .  .  .  .
      .               .
    .      ( p+ n )     .  e⁻
    e⁻     ( n  p+ )      .
      .                 .
        .  .  .  .  .
              e⁻

   p+ = proton (positive),  n = neutron (neutral),  e⁻ = electron (negative)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Simplified model of an atom: central nucleus with protons and neutrons, orbited by outer electrons.

Three critical principles must be remembered:
1. The magnitude of positive charge on a proton is **identical** to the magnitude of negative charge on an electron ($1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$).
2. In an uncharged, neutral atom, the **number of protons equals the number of electrons**. The positive and negative charges precisely balance to yield a net charge of zero. **Neutral does not mean an object has no charges;** it means it contains billions of positive and negative charges in exact numerical balance.
3. **In solid matter, only electrons can move from one atom or material to another.** Protons are locked immovably inside the nucleus by powerful nuclear forces. Therefore:
   - When a body **loses electrons**, it has an excess of protons and becomes **positively charged**.
   - When a body **gains electrons**, it has an excess of electrons and becomes **negatively charged**.

> **Conservation of Electric Charge:** Electric charge can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only be transferred from one body to another via the movement of electrons. What one material loses, the other inevitably gains.

## Part C: How Does Rubbing (Friction) Create Charge?

When two distinct insulating materials are rubbed together, mechanical friction brings their surface atoms into intimate contact. The friction supplies energy that dislodges loosely bound outer electrons from one material, transferring them to the other. The material losing electrons becomes **positively charged**, while the material acquiring those electrons becomes **negatively charged**. Both bodies acquire charges of **equal magnitude but opposite sign**.

| Rubbed Pair | Becomes Positively Charged | Becomes Negatively Charged |
|---|---|---|
| Glass rod rubbed with silk | Glass rod (loses electrons) | Silk cloth (gains electrons) |
| Ebonite / Plastic rod rubbed with fur or wool | Fur or wool (loses electrons) | Ebonite / Plastic rod (gains electrons) |
| Inflated balloon rubbed with dry hair | Human hair (loses electrons) | Balloon (gains electrons) |

Whether a material becomes positive or negative depends on its relative electron affinity in the specific pair (known historically as the triboelectric series). Glass rubbed with silk becomes positive, but glass rubbed with certain other materials can become negative. Always examine the **pair** of interacting materials.

### The Secret Behind the Sweater, Balloon, and Comb

Now we can resolve Maya's three observations:

- **Balloon and hair:** The rubber balloon has a greater affinity for electrons than human hair. As Maya rubs the balloon over her head, electrons migrate from her hair onto the balloon. The balloon acquires a **net negative charge**, while her hair is left with a **net positive charge**. When pressed against the wall, the negative balloon repels the electrons in the wall's surface atoms slightly backward, leaving the near surface with an induced positive layer that attracts the balloon.
- **Comb and paper scraps:** Friction with dry hair transfers electrons onto the plastic comb, giving it a negative charge. When held near neutral paper scraps, it attracts them by induction. Because paper scraps are exceedingly light, this electrostatic attraction easily overcomes gravity.
- **Woollen sweater in the dark:** Pulling off a woollen sweater creates intense friction against undergarments. In dry winter air, humidity is exceptionally low, preventing accumulated charge from leaking into the atmosphere. Significant static charges build up. When the sweater is pulled away, the intense electric field ionizes nearby air molecules, causing micro-avalanches of discharging electrons. This produces tiny **sparks (miniature lightning)** and snapping crackles.

Maya asked whether her sweater was "giving an electric shock like a mains socket." The answer is **no**. While the voltage in static discharge can reach several thousand volts, the total quantity of transferred charge is minuscule (microcoulombs), and the discharge lasts only a fraction of a microsecond. It is completely harmless, unlike dangerous continuous electric currents from wall outlets.

## Part D: Measuring Charge: The Coulomb (C)

In the International System of Units (SI), electric charge is measured in **coulombs (symbol: C)**, named in honor of French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb.

The elementary charge carried by a single proton or electron is extraordinarily tiny:

> **$e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$**  (for an electron: $-1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$; for a proton: $+1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$)

Consequently, a full charge of **$1\text{ C}$** requires an enormous collection of elementary charges:

$$\text{Number of electrons in } 1\text{ C} = \frac{1\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{18}\text{ electrons}$$

Because $1\text{ C}$ represents a massive amount of charge for everyday electrostatics, practical laboratory charges are expressed in smaller metric sub-multiples:

| Unit | Name | Conversion to Coulomb |
|---|---|---|
| **$1\text{ }\mu\text{C}$** | Microcoulomb ($10^{-6}\text{ C}$) | $1\text{ }\mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}\text{ C}$ |
| **$1\text{ nC}$** | Nanocoulomb ($10^{-9}\text{ C}$) | $1\text{ nC} = 10^{-9}\text{ C}$ |
| **$1\text{ pC}$** | Picocoulomb ($10^{-12}\text{ C}$) | $1\text{ pC} = 10^{-12}\text{ C}$ |

A rubbed balloon or comb typically carries static charges ranging from a few nanocoulombs ($\text{nC}$) to several microcoulombs ($\mu\text{C}$).

### Charge is Quantized: Q = n × e

Electrons cannot be sliced into fractions during physical interactions; they are transferred as whole, indivisible particles. Therefore, the net charge $Q$ on any body must always be an **integer multiple of the elementary charge $e$**:

> **$Q = n \times e$**  
> Where $Q$ is the net charge in coulombs ($\text{C}$), $n$ is an integer ($1, 2, 3, \dots$) representing the number of electrons gained or lost, and $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.

If an object gains $n$ electrons, its net charge is $Q = -n e$. If it loses $n$ electrons, its net charge is $Q = +n e$.

## Part E: Conductors and Insulators

Not all materials allow electric charges to travel through them with equal ease:

- **Conductors:** Materials containing a sea of loosely bound "free electrons" that move easily throughout the metallic lattice. Electric charge spreads instantly across a conductor's surface.
- **Insulators:** Materials whose electrons are tightly bound to their parent nuclei, with virtually no free electrons available to move. Charges deposited on an insulator remain locked at that exact location.

| Conductors | Insulators |
|---|---|
| Metals (copper, aluminium, iron, silver, gold) | Plastics (polythene, PVC, nylon) |
| Human body | Rubber |
| Tap water (containing dissolved mineral salts) | Glass |
| Graphite (allotrope of carbon in pencil lead) | Dry wood |
| The Earth (ground) | Dry cloth / wool |
| Molten salts and ionic solutions | Dry air |
| | Ebonite |

A third intermediate category exists: **semiconductors** (such as silicon and germanium). Their electrical conductivity lies between conductors and insulators, forming the technological foundation for modern computer chips and solar cells.

### Why Does a Handheld Metal Rod Not Remain Charged?

If you hold a copper rod in your bare hand and rub it with wool, the rod **shows no sign of electric charge**. However, if you repeat the identical procedure with a plastic rod, it becomes noticeably charged. Why?

- Copper is an excellent conductor, and the **human body is also an electrical conductor**. Any charge generated on the copper rod immediately flows through your hand and body down into the Earth.
- Plastic is an insulator. Charges deposited on the plastic remain stationary right where they were generated, unable to flow through your body.

To charge a metal rod by friction, you must mount it on an **insulating handle** (made of plastic, acrylic, or dry wood).

### Earthing (Grounding)

When a charged conducting object is connected to the Earth via a conducting wire or human touch, its excess charge flows instantly into the ground, restoring the object to electrical neutrality. This process is called **earthing** (or **grounding**). Because the Earth is immense and an excellent electrical conductor, it can absorb or donate massive quantities of electrons without altering its overall potential.

Everyday applications of earthing include:
- Lightning protection rods atop tall buildings.
- Metal safety drag chains on fuel delivery tankers (see Part I).
- The dedicated **third (grounding) pin** on household three-prong electrical plugs (covered in Topic 7).

## Part F: Three Methods of Charging

An object can be electrically charged in three distinct ways:

### 1. By Friction (Rubbing)
As discussed in Part C, mechanical rubbing between two dissimilar insulators forces electrons from one material to the other, creating equal and opposite charges.

### 2. By Conduction (Direct Contact)
When a **charged body** physically touches an **uncharged conductor**, charge transfers directly between them. The uncharged conductor acquires a charge of the **same sign** as the charging body. For example, touching a positively charged glass rod to an insulated metal sphere transfers positive charge (by drawing electrons away from the sphere), leaving the sphere positively charged.

### 3. By Induction (Without Contact)
Electrostatic induction allows a conductor to be charged **without physical contact**, resulting in a charge of the **opposite sign**. 

Consider charging a metal sphere positively using a negatively charged rod:

```
 Step 1: Neutral sphere on an            Step 2: Bring negative rod close
         insulating stand.                       (electrons repelled to far side).

        ┌───┐                              rod (−)      ┌───┐
        │ ± │                              (− − −)      │ + │ ← near side positive
        └─┬─┘                                           │ − │ ← far side negative
        stand                                           └─┬─┘

 Step 3: Ground the sphere with a finger Step 4: Disconnect ground first,
         or wire. Free electrons flee           then remove the charged rod.
         into the Earth.

   rod (−)      ┌───┐                         rod removed      ┌───┐
   (− − −)      │ + │                                          │ + │  ← sphere remains
                └─┬─┘──── wire ──── Earth                      └─┬─┘    positively charged
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Four steps of electrostatic induction: a negative rod repels electrons; grounding carries them into the Earth; removing the ground and then the rod leaves a uniform positive net charge.

**Comparison:**
- **Conduction** requires physical contact and imparts the **same sign** of charge.
- **Induction** requires no contact from the charging rod and imparts the **opposite sign** of charge (utilizing temporary grounding).

## Part G: The Gold-Leaf Electroscope

The **gold-leaf electroscope** is a classic, sensitive physical instrument used to detect the presence, relative magnitude, and sign of electric charge.

**Construction:**
- A vertical **brass rod** passes through an insulating stopper fitted into a transparent **glass jar**.
- The upper end of the rod terminates in an external **metal brass disc or knob**.
- The lower end of the rod inside the jar holds **two extremely thin, flexible leaves of gold foil** (or lightweight aluminium foil) hanging parallel to each other.
- The glass jar shields the delicate leaves from air drafts and dust.

```
            ( ) ← metal brass knob
             │
        ┌────┴────┐
        │    │    │   ← brass conducting rod
        │    │    │
        │   ╱ ╲   │   ← two delicate gold leaves
        │  ╱   ╲  │
        └─────────┘
          glass jar
```

> **Figure 1.4** – The Gold-leaf electroscope: brass knob, central conducting rod, and diverging gold leaves within a protective glass jar.

**Working Principle:**
When a charged object touches the top knob, charge flows down the conducting rod to both gold leaves (conduction). Because both leaves receive the **same sign of charge**, they experience electrostatic repulsion and **diverge (spread apart)**. The angle of divergence indicates the relative quantity of charge: greater charge produces wider divergence. (Induction also causes divergence if the charged rod is held close without touching.)

**Uses:**
1. **Detecting charge:** If the leaves diverge, the testing body is charged; if they remain collapsed, it is uncharged.
2. **Identifying the sign of an unknown charge:** First give the electroscope a known charge (e.g., positive), causing the leaves to diverge. Now bring the unknown charged object near the knob:
   - If the leaves **diverge further**, the unknown object carries the **same sign** (positive).
   - If the leaves **converge (collapse partially)**, the unknown object carries the **opposite sign** (negative).
   *(Note: A neutral conducting object brought close can also cause leaves to collapse slightly due to induction. Thus, further divergence is the only foolproof test of sign.)*

**Limitation:** An electroscope reveals whether an object is charged and indicates its sign, but it cannot measure the exact numerical charge in coulombs.

## Part H: Atmospheric Electricity: Lightning

The crackling blue spark from Maya's sweater is a miniature desktop version of nature's most spectacular electrostatic event: **lightning**.

Inside towering storm clouds (cumulonimbus), violent updrafts force ice crystals, hail pellets, and water droplets into turbulent collisions. The friction strips electrons, polarizing the cloud: lighter positive ice crystals rise to the cloud top, while heavier negative hail pellets gather at the cloud base. The massive negative charge at the cloud base repels electrons in the ground below, inducing a colossal positive charge on the Earth's surface. 

When the electric field exceeds the dielectric breakdown strength of air (roughly $3 \times 10^6\text{ V/m}$), the insulating air ionizes into conducting plasma. A massive electrostatic discharge jumps between cloud and ground: **a lightning strike**. The superheated air expands explosively at supersonic speeds, creating the shockwave we hear as **thunder**.

### Safety Measures During Lightning
- **Seek shelter inside a substantial building** or an enclosed metal vehicle (a car acts as a protective Faraday cage).
- **Avoid open fields, hilltops, and isolated tall trees**, as lightning naturally strikes the highest conducting point.
- **Stay away from water bodies** (lakes, pools) and avoid handling corded electrical appliances or plumbing fixtures.

### Lightning Conductors
Invented by Benjamin Franklin, a **lightning conductor (lightning rod)** is a thick copper rod with pointed spikes mounted at the highest apex of a building. Its lower end connects to a heavy copper grounding strip buried deep in damp soil. If lightning strikes the structure, the immense current is safely routed through the copper conductor directly into the Earth, protecting the building from structural and fire damage.

## Part I: Static Electricity in Everyday Life

- **Car door and door handle shocks:** Sliding across synthetic car seats in dry weather transfers electrons to your clothing. Touching the metal door frame produces a harmless snap and spark as charge discharges.
- **Static cling in laundry:** Synthetic fabrics tumbling against each other in a dry clothes dryer exchange electrons and cling tightly together until separated.
- **Dust on television and monitor screens:** Operating display screens acquire static surface charge, attracting airborne dust particles.
- **Photocopiers and laser printers:** These machines project an electrostatic charge pattern onto a photoconductive drum, attracting charged black toner powder before fusing it onto paper with heat.
- **Fuel tanker safety drag chains:** Liquid fuel sloshing against metal tank walls during road transit generates significant static charge. To prevent a dangerous spark that could ignite flammable fuel vapors, tankers use conductive rubber tires or trailing copper chains touching the road to continuously ground the vehicle.

## Part J: Solved Examples

*(Throughout these calculations, $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$.)*

**Example 1 (Calculating charge from electrons):** A glass rod loses $5 \times 10^{12}$ electrons when rubbed with silk. Calculate the net charge on the rod.

- $n = 5 \times 10^{12}$, $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$
- Magnitude: $Q = n \times e = (5 \times 10^{12}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = 8 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C}$
- Since the rod lost electrons, its charge is **positive**:  
  $$Q = +8 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C} = +0.8\text{ }\mu\text{C}$$  
  *(Recall: $1\text{ }\mu\text{C} = 10^{-6}\text{ C}$, so $8 \times 10^{-7} / 10^{-6} = 0.8\text{ }\mu\text{C}$)*

**Check:** $\frac{0.8 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} = 5 \times 10^{12}\text{ electrons}$ ✅

---

**Example 2 (Finding number of electrons from charge):** How many excess electrons constitute a negative charge of $-3.2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}$?

- $n = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} = \mathbf{2 \times 10^{13}\text{ electrons}}$

**Check:** $(2 \times 10^{13}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = 3.2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}$ ✅

---

**Example 3 (Electrons on a balloon):** A latex balloon is charged to $-2\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ by rubbing with hair. How many electrons were transferred, and which body gained them?

- $Q = 2\text{ }\mu\text{C} = 2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}$
- $n = \frac{2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} = \mathbf{1.25 \times 10^{13}\text{ electrons}}$
- The balloon gained $1.25 \times 10^{13}$ electrons from the hair. The hair was left with an equal positive charge of $+2\text{ }\mu\text{C}$.

**Check:** $(1.25 \times 10^{13}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = 2.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C} = 2\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ ✅

---

**Example 4 (Net charge upon losing electrons):** A neutral plastic object loses $4 \times 10^{12}$ electrons to a cloth. What is the net charge on the object and on the cloth?

- The object lost electrons, so it is **positive**:  
  $$Q_{\text{object}} = +n e = +(4 \times 10^{12}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = \mathbf{+6.4 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C} = +0.64\text{ }\mu\text{C}}$$
- By conservation of charge, the cloth gains the identical number of electrons:  
  $$Q_{\text{cloth}} = \mathbf{-0.64\text{ }\mu\text{C}}$$

**Check:** $(+0.64\text{ }\mu\text{C}) + (-0.64\text{ }\mu\text{C}) = 0\text{ C}$ (total charge conserved) ✅

---

**Example 5 (Charge sharing between identical conductors):** Two identical isolated metal spheres, $A$ and $B$, carry charges of $+6\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ and $0\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ (neutral), respectively. They are brought into contact and then separated. What is the final charge on each sphere?

- Identical conducting spheres share total charge equally upon contact:
- Total charge: $Q_{\text{total}} = +6\text{ }\mu\text{C} + 0 = +6\text{ }\mu\text{C}$
- Charge on each sphere: $Q_A = Q_B = \frac{+6\text{ }\mu\text{C}}{2} = \mathbf{+3\text{ }\mu\text{C}}$

**Check:** $+3\text{ }\mu\text{C} + (+3\text{ }\mu\text{C}) = +6\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ ✅

**Example 8 (Electroscope leaf behavior):** An electroscope is initially charged positive, and its leaves are diverged. When an unknown charged rod is brought near the brass knob, the leaves diverge even further. What is the sign of the charge on the rod?

- Leaf divergence increases when additional like charge is repelled down into the leaves.
- Increased divergence indicates the unknown rod has the **same sign** as the electroscope.
- Therefore, the rod carries a **positive charge**.

**Check:** If the rod were negative, it would pull positive charges upward toward the knob, causing the leaves to partially collapse ✅

---

**Example 9 (Testing quantization of charge):** Can a body carry a net charge of: (a) $4.8 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$, (b) $2.4 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$?

- (a) $n = \frac{4.8 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}} = \mathbf{3}$ (integer). **Yes, this charge is possible** (loss or gain of 3 electrons).
- (b) $n = \frac{2.4 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}} = \mathbf{1.5}$ (non-integer). **No, this charge is impossible**, as fractional electrons cannot exist independently in physical transfers.

**Check:** Quantization condition $Q = n e$ requires $n \in \mathbb{Z}$ ✅

---

**Example 10 (Converting nanocoulombs to electrons):** A plastic comb has a static charge of $-250\text{ nC}$. Express this in coulombs and calculate how many excess electrons are present on the comb.

- $Q = -250\text{ nC} = -250 \times 10^{-9}\text{ C} = \mathbf{-2.5 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C}}$
- $n = \frac{2.5 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} = \mathbf{1.5625 \times 10^{12}\text{ electrons}} \approx 1.56 \times 10^{12}\text{ electrons}$

**Check:** $(1.5625 \times 10^{12}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = 2.5 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C} = 250\text{ nC}$ ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming protons move during frictional charging.**
   *Correction:* In solid materials, protons are bound immovably inside atomic nuclei. **Only electrons move.** When an object becomes positive, it has lost electrons; it did not gain protons.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking a neutral body contains no electric charge.**
   *Correction:* A neutral object contains vast numbers of protons and electrons—they are simply present in exact equal quantities, resulting in zero net charge. This explains why a charged comb can attract a neutral scrap of paper via induction.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing like and unlike charges.**
   *Correction:* Remember the universal rule: **Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.** Two positive rods or two negative balloons will always push away from one another.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing the human body is an electrical insulator.**
   *Correction:* The human body consists of saline water and electrolytes, making it a good conductor. This is why a handheld copper rod cannot be charged by rubbing—any accumulated charge drains through your body to the ground.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating static electricity and socket current as the same phenomenon.**
   *Correction:* Both involve electric charge, but static electricity consists of stationary surface charges of tiny quantity (microcoulombs) with brief discharges. Socket electricity provides continuous, powerful streams of moving charges that can cause severe injury.

6. **Mistake 6: Assigning a positive sign to electrons.**
   *Correction:* Electrons carry negative charge ($-1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$), while protons carry positive charge ($+1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$). Gaining electrons makes an object negative, not positive.

## Quick Recap

- Electricity derives its name from the Greek word *elektron* (amber), first observed by Thales when rubbed amber attracted light chaff.
- There are two kinds of electric charge: **positive (+)** and **negative (−)**, named by Benjamin Franklin. **Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.** A charged body can also attract neutral matter.
- Atoms consist of a positive nucleus (protons and neutrons) orbited by negative electrons. In neutral matter, number of protons = number of electrons.
- In solids, **only electrons are mobile**: gaining electrons creates negative charge; losing electrons creates positive charge.
- In charging by friction, the signs depend on the material pair: glass rubbed with silk becomes positive; plastic rubbed with wool becomes negative.
- The SI unit of charge is the **coulomb (C)**. Elementary charge $e = 1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}$. $1\text{ C} \approx 6.25 \times 10^{18}\text{ electrons}$. Common sub-multiples are $\mu\text{C}$ ($10^{-6}\text{ C}$) and $\text{nC}$ ($10^{-9}\text{ C}$).
- Charge is quantized: **$Q = n \times e$**, where $n$ is an integer.
- **Conductors** (metals, graphite, human body, tap water, Earth) permit charges to move freely. **Insulators** (plastics, rubber, glass, dry wood, dry air) keep charges localized.
- **Earthing (grounding)** safely neutralizes charged bodies by transferring excess charges to the Earth.
- Three charging methods: **friction** (rubbing), **conduction** (direct contact, same sign), and **induction** (no contact, opposite sign with grounding).
- The **gold-leaf electroscope** detects charge and identifies charge sign through leaf divergence, but does not provide quantitative measurement.
- **Lightning** is a gigantic natural electrostatic spark discharging between storm clouds and the ground. Lightning conductors protect structures by channeling current safely to ground.
- Practical static phenomena include dry-weather door shocks, static cling in tumble dryers, photocopier toner adhesion, and fuel tanker grounding chains.

### Answers

1. The glass rod becomes **positively charged** and the silk cloth becomes **negatively charged**. **Electrons** moved from the glass rod to the silk cloth; protons remained stationary inside the nuclei.  
   *Check:* Glass lost electrons (positive); silk gained electrons (negative) ✅

2. $n = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{4.8 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} = \mathbf{3 \times 10^{13}\text{ electrons}}$.  
   *Check:* $(3 \times 10^{13}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 4.8 \times 10^{-6}\text{ C}$ ✅

3. $Q = n e = (3 \times 10^{12}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = 4.8 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C} = 0.48\text{ }\mu\text{C}$.  
   Since electrons were gained, the charge is **negative**: $Q = \mathbf{-4.8 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C} = -0.48\text{ }\mu\text{C}}$.  
   *Check:* $0.48 \times 10^{-6} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 3 \times 10^{12}$ ✅

4. Electrons were lost, so the charge is **positive**:  
   $Q = +n e = +(2.5 \times 10^{12}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C}) = \mathbf{+4.0 \times 10^{-7}\text{ C} = +0.4\text{ }\mu\text{C}}$.  
   *Check:* $0.4 \times 10^{-6} / 1.6 \times 10^{-19} = 2.5 \times 10^{12}$ ✅

5. $n = \frac{1\text{ C}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}\text{ C/electron}} = \mathbf{6.25 \times 10^{18}\text{ electrons}}$.  
   *Check:* $(6.25 \times 10^{18}) \times (1.6 \times 10^{-19}) = 1.0\text{ C}$ ✅

6. For (a): $n = \frac{5.6 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 3.5$. Since $n$ is not a whole number, this charge **cannot exist**.  
   For (b): $n = \frac{3.2 \times 10^{-19}}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = 2$. Since $n$ is an integer, this charge **can exist** (representing 2 elementary charges).  
   *Check:* $2 \times 1.6 = 3.2$ ✅

7. Initial total charge: $Q_{\text{total}} = (+8\text{ }\mu\text{C}) + (-2\text{ }\mu\text{C}) = +6\text{ }\mu\text{C}$.  
   Upon contact, identical spheres divide total charge equally:  
   $Q_{\text{each}} = \frac{+6\text{ }\mu\text{C}}{2} = \mathbf{+3\text{ }\mu\text{C}}$.  
   *Check:* $+3\text{ }\mu\text{C} + (+3\text{ }\mu\text{C}) = +6\text{ }\mu\text{C}$ ✅

8. **Conductors:** steel spoon, graphite pencil lead, tap water, aluminium foil, human body.  
   **Insulators:** rubber glove, dry timber, glass bottle, plastic pen, ebonite bar.  
   *Check:* 5 conductors + 5 insulators = 10 items correctly classified ✅

9. Both balloons carry the **same sign of charge** (either both negative or both positive), which causes electrostatic repulsion. Bringing an oppositely charged rod between them will **attract** both balloons toward the rod.

10. Both copper and the human body are electrical conductors. When copper is rubbed, transferred charges immediately leak through the experimenter's hand and body into the Earth. In contrast, plastic is an electrical insulator, so transferred charges remain immobilized at the rubbed surface.

11. Since the electroscope leaves partially close (decreased divergence), the rod carries a charge **opposite** to the electroscope, meaning the rod is **positively charged**. If the rod is brought much closer, the leaves will collapse completely and then diverge again, now carrying an induced positive charge.

12. (a) **False** ❌. A neutral body possesses equal numbers of positive and negative charges.  
    (b) **False** ❌. Only negative electrons transfer during rubbing.  
    (c) **True** ✅. A charged object induces opposite charge on the near surface of neutral matter, causing attraction.  
    (d) **False** ❌. The human body is a conductor.  
    (e) **False** ❌. Static electricity involves stationary surface charges of microcoulomb magnitude, whereas socket electricity delivers large, continuous currents.

13. The three methods are **friction, conduction, and induction**. Charging by **induction** produces an opposite sign of charge. It requires bringing a charged rod close without physical contact, temporarily grounding the object, disconnecting the ground, and finally removing the charging rod.

14. Liquid fuel sloshing against the tanker's inner walls builds up static charge through friction. A trailing metal chain grounds the tanker continuously, preventing high-voltage sparks from igniting flammable fuel fumes. During thunderstorms, isolated tall trees provide the easiest conducting path for lightning strikes; standing beneath them places an individual directly in the path of deadly ground currents and side flashes.

15. Removing a wool sweater causes friction between wool and undergarments, transferring electrons and creating localized static charge. In dry winter air, low humidity prevents charges from dissipating gradually. As the fabric separates, the intense local voltage ionizes air molecules, producing tiny harmless sparks (microscopic lightning) and acoustic snapping sounds. This is electrostatic discharge, not continuous mains electrical current.
