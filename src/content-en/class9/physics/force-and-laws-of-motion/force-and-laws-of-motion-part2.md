# Topic 2: Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

In Topic 1, we learned that a force is a **push or a pull**, measured in **newtons (N)**, and represented with **vector arrows** where length signifies magnitude. We also noted a lingering puzzle: why did the heavy cart fail to move when Mr. Clark and Maya first pushed it? In the real world, objects rarely experience just a single isolated force. A textbook sitting on a desk is pulled downward by Earth's gravity and pushed upward by the desk surface. When you ride a bicycle, your legs push the pedals forward while wind resistance and road friction pull backward. What happens when **several forces act simultaneously on the same object**? In this topic, we will discover the answer.

## The Tug-of-War: When the Rope Stood Still

It was Sports Day at Oakridge High, and the most anticipated event was the annual inter-house tug-of-war championship: **Falcon House** versus **Eagle House**. The physical education teacher, **Coach Davies**, tied a bright red ribbon at the exact center of a heavy hemp rope and marked a reference line in the chalk on the turf. His student assistant, **Sophia**, held two heavy-duty digital spring balances (force meters) spliced directly into the rope links on either side to measure the pulling tension exerted by each team.

The whistle blew. Both squads gripped the rope and pulled with everything they had. Muscles strained, cleats dug deep into the turf, and faces flushed red with exertion. Yet, the **rope did not budge by a single millimeter**. The red marker remained locked directly over the white chalk line.

Sophia checked the force meter readouts: **500 N on Falcon's side, 500 N on Eagle's side**. Bewildered, she looked at Coach Davies and asked, "Coach, both teams are pulling with enormous effort! If massive forces are acting on the rope, why isn't it moving at all? Shouldn't a force cause motion?"

Coach Davies smiled knowingly. "That is the essential puzzle of mechanics, Sophia. Watch closely."

Just then, two substitute pullers from Eagle House stepped up, gripped the tail end of the rope, and each contributed an additional **60 N** of pull. Sophia glanced at the digital monitors: Eagle House was now pulling with **620 N**, while Falcon House remained at **500 N**. Almost instantly, the red marker began to slide toward Eagle House. Once the rope started moving, it accelerated rapidly across the line.

Both situations involved strong pulling forces. Why did the rope stay perfectly still during the first phase, but accelerate rapidly during the second? We will unpack the exact numbers and physics behind this contest.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain English) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Net Force (Resultant Force)** | The single combined vector sum of all individual forces acting on an object | $620\text{ N} - 500\text{ N} = 120\text{ N}$ |
| **Balanced Forces** | A system of forces whose vector sum equals **zero** | $500\text{ N}$ left and $500\text{ N}$ right |
| **Unbalanced Forces** | A system of forces whose vector sum is **non-zero** | $620\text{ N}$ right and $500\text{ N}$ left |
| **Equilibrium** | The physical state of an object when the net force acting on it is zero | A textbook resting motionless on a table |
| **Normal Force** | The perpendicular supporting force exerted by a surface on an object in contact | A table supporting a book against gravity |
| **Direction** | The orientation or line along which a force acts | East, West, forward, backward |

## Part A: Multiple Forces on an Object: Net Force

When multiple forces act on a body at the same time, the body does not respond to each force separately. Instead, it responds to the single combined effect of all forces acting together. This total combined force is known as the **net force** (or **resultant force**).

In Class 9, we focus primarily on **collinear forces**—forces that act along the same straight line (such as along a horizontal rope or a straight highway).

### Rule 1: Forces in the Same Direction Add Up

When two or more forces act along the same straight line in the **same direction**, they assist one another.  
$$\text{Net Force} = F_1 + F_2$$  
The direction of the net force is the same as that of the individual forces.

```
Same Direction:  F1 = 30 N,  F2 = 20 N

   [ Box ]  ======> 30 N
            ====>   20 N

   Net force = 30 N + 20 N = 50 N  (to the right)

   [ Box ]  ==========> 50 N
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Two collinear forces acting in the same direction (30 N and 20 N) combine to produce a net force of 50 N to the right. (Scale: each "=" represents 5 N.)

### Rule 2: Forces in Opposite Directions Subtract

When two forces act along the same straight line in **opposite directions**, they oppose one another.  
$$\text{Net Force} = F_{\text{larger}} - F_{\text{smaller}}$$  
The direction of the net force is along the **larger force**.

```
Opposite Directions:  600 N right,  450 N left

   450 N  <=========   [ Rope ]   ============>  600 N
          (9 "=")                  (12 "=")

   Net force = 600 N - 450 N = 150 N  (to the right, along the larger force)
```

> **Figure 2.2** – A 600 N force acting to the right opposing a 450 N force acting to the left yields a net force of 150 N to the right. (Scale: each "=" represents 50 N.)

### The Sign Convention Method (Alternative Approach)

You can assign positive ($+$) to one direction (e.g., right or East) and negative ($-$) to the opposite direction (e.g., left or West). Adding the signed numbers gives both the magnitude and sign of the net force:
$$\Sigma F = (+600\text{ N}) + (-450\text{ N}) = +150\text{ N}\quad (\text{to the right})$$

Let us work through several foundational examples:

**Example 1 (Initial Tug-of-War):** Eagle House pulls with 500 N to the right, and Falcon House pulls with 500 N to the left. What is the net force on the rope?
- Force to the right = $+500\text{ N}$
- Force to the left = $-500\text{ N}$
- Net force = $500\text{ N} - 500\text{ N} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$
- **Check:** Equal and opposite forces cancel completely: $500\text{ N} - 500\text{ N} = 0\text{ N}$ ✅

**Example 2 (Unequal Pulls):** In another match, Team A pulls with 600 N to the right and Team B pulls with 450 N to the left. Find the magnitude and direction of the net force.
- Net force = $600\text{ N} - 450\text{ N} = \mathbf{150\text{ N}}$ to the **right** (direction of the larger force).
- **Check:** $450\text{ N} + 150\text{ N} = 600\text{ N}$ ✅

**Example 3 (Three Collinear Forces):** A wooden crate is acted upon by three horizontal forces: $30\text{ N}$ right, $20\text{ N}$ right, and $40\text{ N}$ left. Find the net force.
- Combine forces to the right: $30\text{ N} + 20\text{ N} = 50\text{ N}$ (right)
- Force to the left: $40\text{ N}$ (left)
- Net force = $50\text{ N} - 40\text{ N} = \mathbf{10\text{ N to the right}}$
- **Check with signs:** $(+30) + (+20) + (-40) = +10\text{ N}$ (right) ✅

**Example 4 (Push and Friction):** Sophia pushes a packing box with a horizontal force of $40\text{ N}$ toward the East. The floor surface exerts a frictional resistance of $25\text{ N}$ toward the West. What is the net force?
- Force East = $40\text{ N}$
- Force West = $25\text{ N}$
- Net force = $40\text{ N} - 25\text{ N} = \mathbf{15\text{ N toward the East}}$

```
  Friction 25 N              Applied Push 40 N
  <===        [ Box ]       =====>

  Net force = 40 N - 25 N = 15 N  ->  East
```

> **Figure 2.3** – A 40 N applied push East against a 25 N frictional resistance West yields a net driving force of 15 N East. (Scale: each "=" is approx 8 N.)

- **Check:** $25\text{ N} + 15\text{ N} = 40\text{ N}$ ✅

## Part B: Balanced Forces (Net Force = 0)

When the vector sum of all forces acting on a body equals zero ($\Sigma F = 0$), the forces are said to be **balanced**. The object is in a state of **mechanical equilibrium**.

> **Balanced forces do not change an object's state of motion.**
> - If an object is **at rest**, it **remains at rest**.
> - If an object is **moving**, it **continues moving at constant velocity** (constant speed in a straight line).

Notice an essential distinction: balanced forces do **not** mean that zero forces are present. Multiple powerful forces may be acting, but they cancel each other out completely.

### Quick Look: A Book Resting on a Table

Consider a heavy physics textbook lying stationary on a laboratory table. Two vertical forces act upon it:
1. **Weight ($W$):** The gravitational pull of the Earth pulling downward ($12\text{ N}$).
2. **Normal force ($N$):** The solid tabletop pressing upward against the book ($12\text{ N}$).

```
          ^  Table support (Normal force) = 12 N
          |
       [ Book ]
          |
          v  Gravitational pull (Weight) = 12 N
    ==================  Table surface
```

> **Figure 2.4** – A textbook resting on a table: 12 N downward weight balanced by 12 N upward normal force. Net force is zero, so the book remains at rest.

**Example 5 (Book on Table):** Downward weight = 12 N, upward normal force = 12 N. Net force?
- Net force = $12\text{ N} - 12\text{ N} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$
- The forces are **balanced**, so the book remains motionless.
- **Check:** If the normal force were smaller (e.g., 5 N), the net force would be $12\text{ N} - 5\text{ N} = 7\text{ N}$ downward, causing the book to break through the table. Because the tabletop supports it completely, the upward force equals the downward force ✅

### A Car Traveling at Constant Velocity on a Straight Highway

Now consider an object in motion: a passenger car cruising at a steady $90\text{ km/h}$ along a flat, straight highway. The engine applies a forward driving force through the wheels, while air resistance and tire friction exert an opposing backward force.

**Example 6 (Cruising Car):** Forward engine thrust = 2000 N, total opposing drag and friction = 2000 N backward. What is the net force?
- Net force = $2000\text{ N} - 2000\text{ N} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$
- Because the net force is zero, the car neither speeds up nor slows down: it maintains a **constant velocity**.
- **Check:** If the engine generated 2300 N while resistance remained 2000 N, an unbalanced forward force of 300 N would cause the car to accelerate. Because velocity is uniform, the forces must be balanced ✅

This reinforces the kinematic insight from Chapter 8: **zero acceleration corresponds directly to zero net force**.

### Equal Pushes on a Stationary Object from Opposite Sides

**Example 7 (Wardrobe):** Two students push on a heavy storage locker from opposite sides. Leo pushes to the right with $80\text{ N}$, and Liam pushes to the left with $80\text{ N}$. Will the locker move?
- Net force = $80\text{ N} - 80\text{ N} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$
- Because the opposing pushes are balanced, the locker **remains at rest**.
- **Check:** $80\text{ N} - 80\text{ N} = 0\text{ N}$ ✅

## Part C: Unbalanced Forces (Net Force ≠ 0)

When the vector sum of all forces acting on an object is not zero ($\Sigma F \neq 0$), the forces are **unbalanced**.

> **Unbalanced forces produce a change in the state of motion (acceleration).**
> - A stationary object **begins to move**.
> - A moving object **speeds up** (if net force is in the direction of motion).
> - A moving object **slows down** (if net force opposes motion).
> - A moving object **changes its direction of travel**.
> 
> The resulting acceleration always occurs in the **direction of the net force**.

### Larger Net Force Produces Greater Acceleration

Qualitatively, for a given object:
> **The larger the net force, the greater the rate of change of motion (acceleration).**

Exerting a 10 N net force on a wooden box accelerates it gently. Exerting a 100 N net force on the exact same box accelerates it much more violently. The quantitative law connecting net force and acceleration ($F = ma$) will be formulated in **Topic 6**.

```
Same box, different net forces:

   Case P:  [ Box ] --->          Small net force = 10 N  -> modest acceleration
   Case Q:  [ Box ] ========>     Large net force = 100 N -> rapid acceleration
```

> **Figure 2.5** – For identical mass, a larger net force produces a proportionally greater acceleration.

### Changing Direction is Also an Acceleration

When a tennis player hits an incoming ball with a racket, the racket applies an unbalanced force. The ball reverses direction and flies across the court. Because velocity is a vector, changing direction constitutes acceleration, which always requires an unbalanced force.

### Solving the Tug-of-War Puzzle with Numbers

Let us revisit Sophia's tug-of-war puzzle using our net force principles.

**Example 8 (Tug-of-War Phase 1):** Falcon House pulls with 500 N left, and Eagle House pulls with 500 N right.
- Net force = $500\text{ N} - 500\text{ N} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$
- The forces are **balanced**, so the rope remains in equilibrium and does not move.
- This resolves Sophia's initial confusion: large forces were present, but their net vector sum was zero.

**Example 9 (Tug-of-War Phase 2):** Two extra pullers join Eagle House, each adding 60 N of pull.
- Additional pull = $2 \times 60\text{ N} = 120\text{ N}$
- Total pull by Eagle House = $500\text{ N} + 120\text{ N} = \mathbf{620\text{ N}}$ (right)
- Total pull by Falcon House = $500\text{ N}$ (left)
- Net force = $620\text{ N} - 500\text{ N} = \mathbf{120\text{ N toward Eagle House}}$

```
  Falcon House (500 N)                  Eagle House (620 N)
  <==========   --- Red Marker ---   ============>
  (10 "=")                             (12 "=")

  Net force = 620 N - 500 N = 120 N  ->  toward Eagle House
```

> **Figure 2.6** – After Eagle House adds two pullers: 620 N right versus 500 N left. The net force of 120 N to the right accelerates the rope toward Eagle House. (Scale: each "=" represents 50 N.)

- **Check:** $500\text{ N} + 120\text{ N} = 620\text{ N}$ ✅; $2 \times 60\text{ N} = 120\text{ N}$ ✅
- **Conclusion:** As soon as the net force became non-zero (120 N), the forces became unbalanced, accelerating the rope in the direction of the net force.

## Part D: Reading Forces from Vector Diagrams

In vector diagrams, the length of an arrow reflects the force magnitude based on an assigned scale.

**Example 10 (Reading a Diagram):** Three horizontal forces act on a storage chest. Scale: each "=" represents **10 N**.

```
  Force A (left, 4 "=")       Chest       Force B (right, 7 "=")
  <====                      [ CHEST ]    =======>

                                          Force C (right, 2 "=")
                                          ==>
```

> **Figure 2.7** – Force A = 40 N left, Force B = 70 N right, Force C = 20 N right.

- Force A = $4 \times 10\text{ N} = 40\text{ N}$ (left)
- Force B = $7 \times 10\text{ N} = 70\text{ N}$ (right)
- Force C = $2 \times 10\text{ N} = 20\text{ N}$ (right)
- Total force to the right = $70\text{ N} + 20\text{ N} = 90\text{ N}$
- Net force = $90\text{ N} - 40\text{ N} = \mathbf{50\text{ N to the right}}$
- The chest will accelerate to the **right**.
- **Check with signs:** $(-40) + (+70) + (+20) = +50\text{ N}$ ✅

### The Balancing Force (Equilibrant)

Sometimes a problem asks: *What single force must be added to bring an existing system of forces into balance?* The answer is a force with the **exact same magnitude as the net force, acting in the exact opposite direction**.

**Example 11 (Finding the Balancing Force):** A stationary crate experiences three horizontal forces: $70\text{ N East}$, $30\text{ N East}$, and $50\text{ N West}$. What additional force must be applied to keep the crate in equilibrium?
- Sum of forces to the East = $70\text{ N} + 30\text{ N} = 100\text{ N}$
- Force to the West = $50\text{ N}$
- Current net force = $100\text{ N} - 50\text{ N} = 50\text{ N East}$
- To balance this net force, we must apply **50 N toward the West**.
- **Check:** Total West = $50\text{ N} + 50\text{ N} = 100\text{ N}$; Total East = $100\text{ N}$; $\text{Net} = 100\text{ N} - 100\text{ N} = 0\text{ N}$ ✅

## Part E: Will an Object Accelerate? A Three-Step Decision Process

Whenever solving a force problem:
1. **Choose reference directions** (e.g., assign $+$ to right/East and $-$ to left/West).
2. **Compute the net force** (add forces in the same direction, subtract forces in opposite directions).
3. **Determine the outcome:**
   - If $\text{Net Force} = 0$: Forces are **balanced**; velocity remains constant (stationary remains stationary, moving stays moving).
   - If $\text{Net Force} \neq 0$: Forces are **unbalanced**; object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

| Physical Scenario | Net Force | Dynamic Outcome |
|---|---|---|
| $500\text{ N}$ vs $500\text{ N}$ on a rope | $0\text{ N}$ | Rope remains motionless |
| $620\text{ N}$ vs $500\text{ N}$ on a rope | $120\text{ N}$ to Eagle House | Rope accelerates toward Eagle House |
| Book on desk ($12\text{ N}$ down, $12\text{ N}$ up) | $0\text{ N}$ | Book remains at rest |
| Cruising car ($2000\text{ N}$ forward, $2000\text{ N}$ drag) | $0\text{ N}$ | Car maintains constant cruising velocity |
| Box with $40\text{ N East}$ push, $25\text{ N West}$ friction | $15\text{ N East}$ | Box accelerates toward the East |
| Equal $80\text{ N}$ pushes from both sides | $0\text{ N}$ | Locker remains at rest |

**Example 12 (Putting it All Together):** A delivery cart is pushed by Leo from the left with $90\text{ N}$ and by Liam from the right with $90\text{ N}$. Later, Maya steps in beside Leo, adding an extra $30\text{ N}$ from the left. What is the net force initially and finally?
- **Initially:** $90\text{ N} - 90\text{ N} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$ (balanced; cart stays at rest).
- **Finally:** Total push from left = $90\text{ N} + 30\text{ N} = 120\text{ N}$; push from right = $90\text{ N}$.  
  $$\text{Net Force} = 120\text{ N} - 90\text{ N} = \mathbf{30\text{ N to the right}}$$  
  The cart accelerates toward the right.
- **Check:** $120\text{ N} - 90\text{ N} = 30\text{ N}$ ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming zero motion means zero forces are acting.**  
   During the first stage of the tug-of-war, each team pulled with $500\text{ N}$. Heavy forces were present, but they cancelled out ($\text{net force} = 0$).

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming that all moving objects must have an unbalanced force.**  
   A car traveling at a constant speed of $90\text{ km/h}$ on a straight highway has balanced forces ($\text{engine thrust} = \text{resistive drag}$; $\text{net force} = 0$). Unbalanced forces are required to *change* velocity, not to maintain a uniform velocity.

3. **Mistake 3: Adding forces that act in opposite directions.**  
   A $500\text{ N}$ force to the right and a $300\text{ N}$ force to the left do NOT add to $800\text{ N}$. Because they oppose each other, you must subtract: $500\text{ N} - 300\text{ N} = 200\text{ N}$ to the right.

4. **Mistake 4: Omitting the direction of the net force.**  
   Force is a vector. Stating "the net force is 150 N" is an incomplete answer. You must specify: "150 N to the East."

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking "balanced forces" means forces vanish.**  
   Sophia's spring balances read 500 N each. The forces were very real and exerted substantial tension on the rope fibers; their vector sum was zero.

6. **Mistake 6: Dropping units in intermediate calculations.**  
   Always carry the unit **N** through every step: $620\text{ N} - 500\text{ N} = 120\text{ N}$.

## Quick Recap

- Multiple forces acting on a single body combine into one single vector effect called the **net force** (or resultant force).
- For collinear forces: **add** forces pointing in the same direction; **subtract** forces pointing in opposite directions. The net force points along the larger force.
- **Balanced forces:** Net force equals zero ($\Sigma F = 0$). The object's motion does not change: stationary objects stay at rest, and moving objects maintain constant velocity.
- **Unbalanced forces:** Net force is non-zero ($\Sigma F \neq 0$). The object accelerates (speeds up, slows down, or changes direction) in the direction of the net force.
- **Magnitude rule:** For a given body, a larger net force produces a greater rate of acceleration.
- A resting book experiences balanced forces: downward gravity (weight) and upward contact support (normal force) cancel out.
- **Tug-of-war solution:** $500\text{ N}$ vs $500\text{ N}$ resulted in zero net force (no movement). Adding $2 \times 60\text{ N} = 120\text{ N}$ produced an unbalanced net force of $120\text{ N}$, causing acceleration toward Eagle House.
