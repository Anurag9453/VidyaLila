# Topic 7: Newton's Third Law: Action and Reaction

In Topic 6, we formulated Newton's Second Law ($F = ma$): when an unbalanced net force acts on a body, that body accelerates. In that analysis, we focused on a single isolated object and asked: *"What force acts upon this object, and what acceleration does it produce?"* Now we take a deeper step and ask: **where does this force come from in the first place? When you push an object, what does that object do to you?** The answer is revealed in **Newton's Third Law of Motion**.

---

## Maya's First Swimming Lesson at the Aquatic Center

It was Maya's first day in the competitive swimming lane at the community pool. Her instructor, **Coach Davies**, stood at the pool deck and called out, "Alright Maya, let's learn how to propel yourself across the lane."

Maya assumed that to travel forward through the water, she should push the water *forward* with her hands. She cupped her palms and shoved water ahead of herself—yet her body remained stubbornly stationary, splashing water uselessly in place.

Coach Davies laughed warmly. "You have the direction backward, Maya! If you want to surge **forward**, you must sweep the water **backward** behind you with your hands and kick backward with your feet."

Maya tried again. She scooped her arms through the water and shoved firmly backward. Instantly, her body glided forward across the surface! "Wow, the water pushed me forward!" she exclaimed.

Next, Coach Davies had Maya climb onto a lightweight floating foam platform anchored loosely in the middle of the pool. Maya prepared to leap onto the concrete pool deck. As she **jumped forward**, she was shocked to see the floating platform **shoot violently backward** into the pool beneath her heels!

Brushing the water from her goggles, Maya asked, "Coach Davies, I didn't try to kick the platform backward—I was trying to leap forward onto the deck! Why did the platform shoot in reverse?"

Coach Davies smiled. "The explanation lies at the very heart of mechanics: Newton's Third Law of Motion. We will solve your platform puzzle with exact numbers at the end of this topic."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain English) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Action** | The force exerted by body A on body B | Maya's hands pushing water backward |
| **Reaction** | The simultaneous, equal, and oppositely directed force exerted by body B back on body A | Water pushing Maya forward |
| **Action-Reaction Pair** | The two reciprocal forces that always accompany any physical interaction | The hand-water interaction pair |
| **Newton's Third Law** | To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction | $F_{AB} = -F_{BA}$ |
| **Different Bodies** | Action and reaction act on two distinctly different objects and therefore never cancel each other | Action acts on the water; reaction acts on the swimmer |
| **Normal Force** | The perpendicular contact force exerted by a solid surface on an object resting upon it | A chair pushing upward on a seated student |

## Part A: What the Law States

Sir Isaac Newton expressed his Third Law in a famous, elegant sentence:

> **To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction.**

In rigorous modern physics terms:

> **Whenever body A exerts a force on body B, body B simultaneously exerts an equal and opposite force on body A.**  
> $$\vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}$$

Three essential truths define this law:
1. **Forces always occur in matched pairs.** An isolated, single force cannot exist in nature. Whenever a push or pull occurs, both participating objects experience force.
2. **The two forces have identical magnitude** (exact same number of newtons) but point in **directly opposite directions**.
3. **The two forces occur simultaneously.** Action and reaction arise at the exact same instant and cease at the exact same instant; there is zero time delay between them.

### Maya's Hands and the Water

```
   Maya's hand  ---- 30 N backward ---->  Water
   (Action: force exerted ON the water)

   Maya  <---- 30 N forward ----  Water
   (Reaction: force exerted ON Maya)

   Forward Direction:  --->
```

> **Figure 7.1** – Maya exerts a 30 N backward push on the water (action on water). Simultaneously, the water exerts a 30 N forward push on Maya (reaction on Maya). The forces are equal in size and opposite in direction.

Who propelled Maya forward across the pool? **The water did!** Maya's muscles pushed the water backward; the reactive force of the water pushed Maya forward. Every fish swimming in an ocean, every rowing crew pulling oars, and every competitive swimmer relies on this identical principle.

### Two Names for the Same Interaction

There is no physical hierarchy between action and reaction. Neither force is the "cause" nor the "effect"—they are two simultaneous aspects of a single interaction. You may label Maya's push on the water as "action" and the water's push on Maya as "reaction," or vice versa. Both labels describe the exact same mechanical reality.

## Part B: The Crucial Principle: Forces Act on Different Bodies

This is the single most important concept in this topic:

> **Action and reaction forces act on two completely different bodies. Therefore, they can NEVER cancel each other out!**

Forces can cancel each other out (yielding a net force of zero, as in Topic 2) **only when they act on the exact same single object**. In an action-reaction pair:
- The **action** acts on **Object B**.
- The **reaction** acts on **Object A**.

Because each object experiences only the force applied to it, each object accelerates independently according to its own net force ($a = F_{\text{net}} / m$).

### Illustration 1: Pushing Against a Wall

Suppose you plant your palms against a solid concrete wall and push with an applied force of $80\text{ N}$.

- **Action:** Your hands exert $80\text{ N}$ forward on the wall.
- **Reaction:** The wall exerts $80\text{ N}$ backward against your hands.

```
   Person -------> |  Wall
           80 N        (Force acting ON the wall, forward)

   Person <------- |  Wall
           80 N        (Force acting ON the person, backward)
```

> **Figure 7.2** – Pushing a wall: your hands exert 80 N forward on the wall; the wall exerts 80 N backward on your hands. The forces act on different bodies.

Why doesn't the wall move? Because the wall is anchored into deep concrete foundations that supply large opposing forces. But notice: the $80\text{ N}$ forward force on the wall and the $80\text{ N}$ backward force on you **do not cancel**, because one acts on the masonry while the other acts on your body. If you were wearing roller skates, that $80\text{ N}$ reaction from the wall would send you rolling backward across the room!

### Illustration 2: Pulling a Crate with a Rope

Suppose you pull a crate across a floor with a rope tension of $60\text{ N}$.
- You exert a forward pulling force of **60 N on the crate**.
- Simultaneously, the crate exerts a backward pulling force of **60 N on you**.

Now consider the crate alone. The forces acting **on the crate** are:
1. Your forward pull: $60\text{ N}$
2. Opposing ground friction: $20\text{ N}$ backward

The net force acting on the crate is:  
$$F_{\text{net}} = 60\text{ N} - 20\text{ N} = 40\text{ N}\quad (\text{forward})$$

The crate accelerates forward under this $40\text{ N}$ net force! The $60\text{ N}$ reaction force does not act on the crate; it acts on your hands.

### The Free-Body Golden Rule

Whenever applying $F = ma$, focus on **one body at a time**. List only the forces acting **upon that specific body**. Never include the reaction force that the body exerts back onto the outside world.

## Part C: Everyday Manifestations of Action and Reaction

### Swimming
To swim forward, you push the water backward with your arms and feet (action on water). The water pushes you forward (reaction on swimmer).

### Walking and Running
When walking, your shoes push **backward and downward against the pavement** (action on Earth). Ground friction pushes **forward on your shoes** (reaction on human), propelling you forward. Without friction (e.g., on frictionless ice), your feet cannot push backward, no forward reaction can arise, and walking becomes impossible.

### Rocket Propulsion
A rocket engine burns fuel to blast exhaust gases out of its nozzle at high speed **downward** (action on gas). The expelled exhaust gas pushes the rocket chassis **upward** (reaction on rocket). A rocket does not need air or a launchpad to push against; it accelerates purely by expelling its own propellant.

### Releasing an Inflated Balloon
Blow up a latex balloon and release the neck without tying it. Compressed air rushes out **backward** (action on air). The escaping air pushes the balloon **forward** (reaction on balloon), sending it darting erratically around the room.

### Recoil of a Rifle or Cannon
When a rifle fires, expanding gunpowder gases drive the bullet **forward** at high velocity (action on bullet). The bullet exerts an equal and opposite force that kicks the rifle **backward** against the shooter's shoulder (reaction on rifle).

### Leaping from a Boat or Floating Dock
When an athlete leaps forward from a rowboat toward the shore, their legs shove the rowboat **backward** (action on boat). The boat surface pushes the athlete **forward** (reaction on athlete). Because the boat is free to move in water, it recoils backward noticeably.

### Hammer Striking a Nail
A hammer strikes a nail downward with $200\text{ N}$ (action on nail). The nail exerts an equal upward force of $200\text{ N}$ on the hammer (reaction on hammer), bringing the heavy hammerhead to an immediate halt.

### Sitting on a Chair: A Common Misconception

When you sit on a chair, two vertical forces act upon your body:
1. Downward gravitational pull (your **weight**).
2. Upward support force from the seat (the **normal force**).

Because these two forces are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, beginners frequently assume they constitute an action-reaction pair.

**This is a widespread error!** Both forces act on the **exact same body** (you). True action-reaction pairs must act on **two different bodies**:
- The true reaction to Earth's gravitational pull on you is **your gravitational pull on the Earth** (you pull the entire Earth upward with equal force!).
- The upward normal force of the chair is the reaction to **your buttocks pressing downward against the chair cushion**.

## Part D: Identifying Action-Reaction Pairs in 8 Everyday Scenarios

**Example 1:** Identify the action and reaction forces for each scenario:

| Scenario | Action (Acting ON, Exerted BY) | Reaction (Acting ON, Exerted BY) |
|---|---|---|
| Swimmer moving forward | Swimmer pushes water backward | Water pushes swimmer forward |
| Walking across a room | Foot pushes ground backward | Ground pushes foot forward |
| Rocket launch | Rocket expels exhaust gases downward | Exhaust gases push rocket upward |
| Firing a hunting rifle | Rifle accelerates bullet forward | Bullet accelerates rifle backward (recoil) |
| Hammering a steel nail | Hammer strikes nail downward | Nail pushes hammer upward |
| Jumping from a rowboat | Person shoves boat backward | Boat shoves person forward |
| Batter hitting a baseball | Bat strikes ball forward | Ball exerts backward impact on bat |
| Person sitting on a chair | Person presses downward on seat | Seat pushes upward on person |

Notice the pattern: in every row, the participating objects swap roles, and the directions are strictly reversed.

**Example 2 (Finding Reaction Force):** A teenager shoves against a heavy gym door with a horizontal force of $50\text{ N}$. What force does the door exert back on the teenager?
- Action: Teenager exerts $50\text{ N}$ forward on the door.
- Reaction (by Newton's Third Law): The door exerts **50 N backward on the teenager**.
- **Check:** Magnitude is identical ($50\text{ N} = 50\text{ N}$), direction is opposite, and forces act on different bodies ✅

**Example 3 (Hammer and Nail Magnitudes):** A carpenter strikes a nail with a hammer force of $200\text{ N}$. What is the magnitude of the force exerted by the nail on the hammer? Does the hammer experience more force than the nail?
- By Newton's Third Law, the nail exerts an upward force of **200 N** on the hammer.
- Neither object experiences greater force; **the forces are strictly identical** ($200\text{ N} = 200\text{ N}$).
- The nail penetrates wood while the hammer halts because of their differing masses and the structural resistance of the timber, not because of unequal forces!

## Part E: Equal Forces, Unequal Accelerations

If action and reaction forces are strictly equal in magnitude, do the two interacting objects always experience equal accelerations? **No!**

By Newton's Second Law:  
$$a = \frac{F}{m}$$

Even though the force $F$ is identical for both bodies, their **masses ($m$) are usually different**. Consequently:
> **The less massive body experiences a much larger acceleration than the more massive body.**

### Jumper and Floating Platform

**Example 4 (Leaping from a Floating Platform):** A student of mass $60\text{ kg}$ leaps forward from a floating platform of mass $120\text{ kg}$. During the leap, the student and platform push against each other with an interaction force of $300\text{ N}$. Calculate the acceleration of each.
- **Student:**  
  $$a_{\text{student}} = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{300\text{ N}}{60\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{5\text{ m/s}^2}\quad (\text{forward})$$
- **Platform:**  
  $$a_{\text{platform}} = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{300\text{ N}}{120\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ m/s}^2}\quad (\text{backward})$$
- **Check:** $60\text{ kg} \times 5\text{ m/s}^2 = 300\text{ N}$ ✅; $120\text{ kg} \times 2.5\text{ m/s}^2 = 300\text{ N}$ ✅
- Notice: The platform has double the mass of the student ($120 / 60 = 2$), so its acceleration is exactly half ($2.5 / 5 = 0.5$). Greater mass yields smaller acceleration!

### Leaping from a Rowboat

**Example 5:** A boy of mass $50\text{ kg}$ leaps from a stationary rowboat of mass $100\text{ kg}$ toward the shore. The mutual push between feet and boat is $200\text{ N}$. Find the acceleration of each.
- Boy: $a = \frac{200\text{ N}}{50\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s}^2}$ toward shore.
- Rowboat: $a = \frac{200\text{ N}}{100\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{2\text{ m/s}^2}$ away from shore.
- **Check:** $50 \times 4 = 200$ ✅; $100 \times 2 = 200$ ✅

### Two Ice Skaters Pushing Apart

**Example 6:** Two figure skaters, Leo (mass $50\text{ kg}$) and Liam (mass $75\text{ kg}$), stand face-to-face on frictionless ice. Leo pushes Liam with a force of $50\text{ N}$. Find the acceleration and direction of motion for each skater.
- By Newton's Third Law, Liam simultaneously exerts an equal force of $50\text{ N}$ back on Leo in the opposite direction.
- Leo ($50\text{ kg}$):  
  $$a_{\text{Leo}} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{50\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{1\text{ m/s}^2}\quad (\text{backward})$$
- Liam ($75\text{ kg}$):  
  $$a_{\text{Liam}} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{75\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{0.67\text{ m/s}^2}\quad \left(\frac{2}{3}\text{ m/s}^2,\text{ forward}\right)$$

```
   <---- 1 m/s²   [ Leo: 50 kg ]  <-- 50 N | 50 N -->  [ Liam: 75 kg ]   0.67 m/s² ---->
```

> **Figure 7.3** – Two skaters on frictionless ice pushing apart: both experience identical 50 N forces. The lighter skater (50 kg) accelerates faster ($1\text{ m/s}^2$) than the heavier skater ($0.67\text{ m/s}^2$).

- **Check:** $50 \times 1 = 50\text{ N}$ ✅; $75 \times \frac{2}{3} = 50\text{ N}$ ✅
- Liam did not push "harder" because he is heavier; both forces are strictly $50\text{ N}$. Liam simply accelerated less because of his greater inertia!

### Total Momentum of the Interacting System (A Preview)

Notice that in Example 6, after $1\text{ s}$:
- Leo's momentum = $50\text{ kg} \times (-1\text{ m/s}) = -50\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
- Liam's momentum = $75\text{ kg} \times (+0.67\text{ m/s}) = +50\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m/s}$
- Total combined momentum = $-50 + 50 = \mathbf{0\text{ kg}\cdot m/s}$!

The equal and opposite forces generated by Newton's Third Law ensure that the total momentum of an isolated system remains perfectly conserved. We will explore this law of conservation in Topic 8!

## Part F: The Classic Horse-and-Cart Paradox

A classic physics paradox is framed as follows:  
*A horse pulls forward on a cart with force $F$. By Newton's Third Law, the cart pulls backward on the horse with an equal force $F$. Because these two forces are equal and opposite, how can the cart ever move forward?*

The solution lies directly in Part B: **the forward pull acts on the cart, while the backward pull acts on the horse!** They act on different bodies and therefore do not cancel.

**Example 7 (Horse and Cart with Quantitative Analysis):**  
A horse pulls forward on a cart with a harness tension of $500\text{ N}$. The cart (mass $200\text{ kg}$) experiences a ground friction of $300\text{ N}$ opposing its wheels. The horse has a mass of $400\text{ kg}$. To move forward, the horse's hooves push backward against the ground, and the ground pushes forward on the horse with a horizontal static friction force $F_{\text{ground}}$. If the cart accelerates at $1\text{ m/s}^2$, find the net force on the cart and the forward push of the ground on the horse.

**Step 1: Analyze the Cart Alone (Isolate the cart).**
- Forward force on cart (harness pull) = $500\text{ N}$
- Opposing friction on cart = $300\text{ N}$
- Net force on cart:  
  $$F_{\text{net, cart}} = 500\text{ N} - 300\text{ N} = \mathbf{200\text{ N forward}}$$
- Cart acceleration:  
  $$a = \frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m} = \frac{200\text{ N}}{200\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{1\text{ m/s}^2 forward}$$

**Step 2: Analyze the Horse Alone (Isolate the horse).**
- Backward force on horse (cart pulling back) = $500\text{ N}$ (the reaction)
- Forward force on horse (ground pushing hooves) = $F_{\text{ground}}$
- Because horse and cart move together, the horse accelerates at $a = 1\text{ m/s}^2$.
- Net force on horse:  
  $$F_{\text{net, horse}} = m_{\text{horse}} \times a = 400\text{ kg} \times 1\text{ m/s}^2 = 400\text{ N forward}$$
- Therefore:  
  $$F_{\text{ground}} - 500\text{ N} = 400\text{ N} \implies F_{\text{ground}} = \mathbf{900\text{ N forward}}$$

```
   On Horse:   <-- 500 N (Cart pull)       900 N (Ground push) -->    Net: 400 N forward
   On Cart:    <-- 300 N (Wheel drag)      500 N (Horse pull)  -->    Net: 200 N forward
```

> **Figure 7.4** – Force breakdown for horse and cart. The cart accelerates forward because the horse's pull (500 N) exceeds wheel friction (300 N). The horse accelerates forward because the ground's forward thrust (900 N) exceeds the backward pull of the cart (500 N).

**Verification:**  
- Treat horse and cart as one combined system of mass $400 + 200 = 600\text{ kg}$.  
  External forces on system = $900\text{ N forward}$ (ground on hooves) minus $300\text{ N backward}$ (road friction on cart) = $600\text{ N forward}$.  
  System acceleration = $600\text{ N} / 600\text{ kg} = 1\text{ m/s}^2$ ✅  
  The internal $500\text{ N}$ action-reaction tension forces cancel within the combined system, confirming our single-body calculations!

## Part G: Solution to Maya's Poolside Dilemma

Now let us revisit Maya leaping from the floating platform:
- Maya's mass: $40\text{ kg}$
- Platform's mass: $120\text{ kg}$
- Maya shoves backward on the platform with $120\text{ N}$ for a duration of $0.5\text{ s}$ during her launch.

**Example 8 (The Platform Leap):** Calculate the acceleration and velocity of both Maya and the platform at the instant of departure ($0.5\text{ s}$).
- Force on Maya: $120\text{ N forward}$.  
  $$a_{\text{Maya}} = \frac{120\text{ N}}{40\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{3\text{ m/s}^2 forward}$$  
  $$v_{\text{Maya}} = u + at = 0 + (3\text{ m/s}^2 \times 0.5\text{ s}) = \mathbf{1.5\text{ m/s forward}}$$
- Force on Platform: $120\text{ N backward}$.  
  $$a_{\text{platform}} = \frac{120\text{ N}}{120\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{1\text{ m/s}^2 backward}$$  
  $$v_{\text{platform}} = u + at = 0 + (1\text{ m/s}^2 \times 0.5\text{ s}) = \mathbf{0.5\text{ m/s backward}}$$

**Check:**  
$40 \times 3 = 120\text{ N}$ ✅; $120 \times 1 = 120\text{ N}$ ✅.  
Because the platform was 3 times more massive than Maya ($120 / 40 = 3$), its acceleration and resulting recoil velocity were exactly one-third of Maya's ($1\text{ m/s}^2$ vs $3\text{ m/s}^2$; $0.5\text{ m/s}$ vs $1.5\text{ m/s}$).

Maya's puzzle is completely resolved: the platform moved backward because Maya's feet pushed it backward to launch herself forward!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Claiming action and reaction cancel each other out.**  
   Forces can cancel only if they act on the same body. Action acts on body B; reaction acts on body A. They can never cancel each other.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming reaction occurs after action.**  
   Action and reaction are completely simultaneous. There is zero time delay.

3. **Mistake 3: Believing heavier objects exert larger forces than lighter objects.**  
   Action and reaction are strictly equal in magnitude ($F_{AB} = F_{BA}$). When a massive truck hits a small mosquito, the force exerted by the truck on the mosquito equals the force exerted by the mosquito on the truck! The mosquito is crushed because its tiny mass experiences astronomical acceleration and stress.

4. **Mistake 4: Mistaking weight and normal force for an action-reaction pair.**  
   For a person resting on a chair, weight (downward) and normal force (upward) both act on the person. They are balanced forces acting on a single body, not an action-reaction pair.

5. **Mistake 5: Adding reaction forces onto the acting body's free-body diagram.**  
   When calculating the cart's acceleration, never include the force the cart exerts on the horse. Include only the forces acting directly on the cart.

---

## Quick Recap

- **Newton's Third Law:** To every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction ($\vec{F}_{AB} = -\vec{F}_{BA}$).
- Action and reaction forces act on **two different bodies**, meaning they **never cancel each other**.
- Both forces are strictly **simultaneous**, have **identical magnitude**, and act in **opposite directions**.
- Practical examples: Swimming (shoving water backward), walking (shoving ground backward), rocket propulsion, balloon thrust, rifle recoil, leaping from boats.
- While forces are equal, accelerations differ according to mass ($a = F/m$). The lighter body experiences proportionally greater acceleration.
- In the horse-and-cart paradox, the cart moves because harness tension exceeds road friction; the horse moves because ground friction pushes its hooves forward.
