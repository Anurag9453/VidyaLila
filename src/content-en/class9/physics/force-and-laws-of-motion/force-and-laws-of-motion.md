# Topic 1: What is Force?

In our study of motion, we examined **how** objects move: how fast they travel, in which direction, and with what acceleration. However, one fundamental question remained unanswered: **why do objects start moving, speed up, slow down, or turn in the first place?** Kinematics explained the "how"; now dynamics explains the **"why"**. The answer to this "why" lies in one foundational concept: **force**.

---

## The Stuck Cart on the Incline

On an avenue leading up to the neighborhood market, there was a gentle incline. At the foot of the slope stood a heavy wooden handcart piled high with crates of fresh apples, potatoes, and pumpkins. The local grocer, **Mr. Clark**, was trying to wheel his cart up the hill to his stall.

Planting his feet firmly on the pavement, Mr. Clark pushed against the cart with all his might. The cart did not budge by even a millimeter. He took a deep breath and shoved again. Still, nothing happened.

Just then, a Class 9 student named **Maya** was walking home from school. Seeing Mr. Clark struggling, she set down her backpack and offered, "Here, Mr. Clark, let me help!" Together, they pushed with shared effort. The cart's wooden wheels groaned slightly, but the cart remained stubbornly frozen in place.

Moments later, three neighbors passing by ran over to lend a hand. Now, all five of them lined up side by side and shoved together on the count of three. First, the cart gave a small shudder. Then, it began to creep forward. As they continued pushing, its speed increased, and within seconds the heavy cart was rolling steadily up the incline.

Maya wiped her brow and smiled, "Mr. Clark, when you pushed alone, nothing moved at all. But when all five of us joined in, it accelerated up the slope! What is actually happening with all these pushes?" We will analyze this story at the end of the topic using numbers, units, and vector arrows.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning (Plain English) | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Force** | A **push or a pull** exerted on an object resulting from its interaction with another object | Pushing a shopping cart |
| **Newton (N)** | The SI unit of force | 1 N, 50 N, 550 N |
| **Spring balance** | A device containing a coiled spring used to measure the magnitude of a force | A hanging spring scale in a science lab |
| **Magnitude** | The numerical size or amount of a force (number + unit) | 150 N |
| **Direction** | The line or path along which a force acts | Upward along the incline |
| **Contact force** | A force that acts only when two interacting bodies are physically touching | Muscular push, tension in a rope |
| **Non-contact force** | A force that acts across a distance without physical contact | Gravity, magnetic attraction |
| **Muscular force** | Force exerted by the muscles of the human or animal body | Pushing a stalled cart |
| **Tension** | The pulling force transmitted through a string, rope, or cable | Hauling a bucket from a well with a rope |
| **Friction** | An opposing force that resists relative motion between surfaces in contact | A box sliding across a rough floor |

## Part A: What is Force: A Push or a Pull

In the simplest terms: **a force is a push or a pull.**

- Shoving a stalled car, punching a boxing bag, kicking a soccer ball: **push**.
- Opening a refrigerator door, towing a trailer with a cable, pulling open a drawer: **pull**.

Notice an essential fact: force never exists in isolation. When Mr. Clark pushes the cart, **two entities** are involved: **Mr. Clark** (the agent exerting the force) and the **cart** (the object experiencing the force). Therefore, physicists state that force is an **interaction** between two objects.

### What Force is NOT

Force is not a tangible material or substance residing inside an object. Nor is force identical to energy. You cannot "store" a bucket of force inside a closet. A force exists **only during the interaction** when one object is pushing or pulling another. The moment Mr. Clark removes his hands from the cart, his muscular force on the cart ceases instantly.

---

## Part B: The Unit of Force: The Newton (N)

Just as length is measured in meters and time in seconds, the standard international (SI) unit of force is the **newton**, abbreviated as **N**.

> **SI unit of force = newton (N)**

We will establish the rigorous mathematical definition of 1 newton in **Topic 6** through Newton's Second Law ($F = ma$). For now, let us develop an intuitive physical feel:

> When you place a small apple of mass roughly **100 g** (0.1 kg) in the palm of your hand, the downward gravitational pull of the Earth presses against your palm with a force of approximately **1 N**.

This provides an everyday benchmark:

| Situation | Approximate Force (Intuitive Scale) |
|---|---|
| Downward gravitational pull on a 100 g apple | $\approx 1\text{ N}$ |
| Downward pull on a standard 300 g textbook | $\approx 3\text{ N}$ |
| Downward pull on a 500 g bag of sugar | $\approx 5\text{ N}$ |
| Mr. Clark pushing alone on the cart (in our story) | $150\text{ N}$ |

### Do Not Confuse N with kg

A common beginner mistake is confusing **kilograms (kg)** with **newtons (N)**.
- **Kilogram (kg)** measures **mass**—the quantity of matter contained in an object.
- **Newton (N)** measures **force**—the intensity of a push or pull.

Saying "I exerted a force of 5 kg" is scientifically incorrect; the correct statement is "I exerted a force of 50 N" (or whatever number of newtons applies). Mass and force are distinct physical quantities.

## Part C: Measuring Force with a Spring Balance

To measure force quantitatively in a laboratory, we use a **spring balance** (or force meter). Inside the casing is a coiled steel spring attached to a hook. When a force pulls on the hook, the spring stretches proportionally, moving an indicator pointer along a calibrated scale.

```
      [ ring ]
         ||
   +-----++-----+
   | 0 -- ----  |
   | 1 -- ----  |
   | 2 -- ----  |
   | 3 -- ----  |      <-- pointer here, reading 3 N
   | 4 -- ----  |
   | 5 -- ----  |
   +-----++-----+
         ||   (internal spring)
       [hook]
         ||
       [ bag ]
```

> **Figure 1.1** – A spring balance calibrated in increments of 1 N. The suspended bag pulls the spring downward, holding the pointer at 3 N.

**How to Read a Spring Balance:**
1. Check zero error: Ensure the pointer rests precisely on **0 N** when no load is attached.
2. Suspend the object from the hook or pull the hook along the line of action.
3. Observe the line where the indicator stops; record the magnitude followed by the unit **N**.

**Example 1:** A spring balance has graduation marks every 1 N. The pointer stops directly on the 4th mark.
- Reading = **4 N**

**Example 2:** The pointer rests exactly halfway between the 2nd and 3rd marks.
- Reading = $2\text{ N} + 0.5\text{ N} = \mathbf{2.5\text{ N}}$

**Example 3:** Hanging one small pouch from the hook stretches the balance to 5 N. A second, identical pouch is hooked alongside it.
- Total load doubles: $5\text{ N} \times 2 = \mathbf{10\text{ N}}$
- **Check:** $5\text{ N} + 5\text{ N} = 10\text{ N}$ ✅

The working principle of the spring balance is simple: greater force produces a proportionally greater extension of the spring.

## Part D: Force Has Both Magnitude and Direction

If someone asks you, "Apply a force of 100 N to this desk," your immediate question must be, "In which direction?" 
- Pushing the desk forward moves it away from you.
- Pulling the desk backward brings it closer to you.

Because the physical effect depends entirely on both how hard you push and where you push, force requires two pieces of information:

> **Force = Magnitude (how much) + Direction (which way)**

Any physical quantity possessing both magnitude and direction, and obeying vector laws of addition, is called a **vector quantity**. (Recall from our study of Motion that displacement and velocity are also vectors.) Force is a vector.

### Representing Force with Arrows

In physics diagrams, we represent forces using **directed line segments (arrows)**:
- The **arrowhead (tip)** points in the **direction** of the force.
- The **length of the arrow** represents the **magnitude**: a longer arrow signifies a larger force.
- The **tail** of the arrow indicates the point of application where the force acts.

To draw them accurately, we choose a convenient scale, such as "1 box = 50 N":

```
Scale: 1 box [ ] = 50 N

 Force A on cart:   --[ ][ ][ ]-->        (3 boxes = 150 N, forward)
 Force B on cart:   --[ ]-->              (1 box = 50 N, forward)
 Force C on cart:   <--[ ][ ]--           (2 boxes = 100 N, backward)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Three forces represented by arrows: Force A is longest (150 N forward); Force B is shortest (50 N forward); Force C points in the opposite direction (100 N backward).

**Example 4:** On a scale where 1 box = 50 N, an arrow is drawn 4 boxes long.
- Force magnitude = $4 \times 50\text{ N} = \mathbf{200\text{ N}}$
- **Check:** $200\text{ N} \div 50\text{ N} = 4\text{ boxes}$ ✅

**Example 5:** Arrow P measures 2 boxes, while Arrow Q measures 5 boxes. Which represents the larger force, and by how much?
- $P = 2 \times 50\text{ N} = 100\text{ N}$
- $Q = 5 \times 50\text{ N} = 250\text{ N}$
- Arrow Q is larger by $250\text{ N} - 100\text{ N} = \mathbf{150\text{ N}}$.
- **Check:** $100\text{ N} + 150\text{ N} = 250\text{ N}$ ✅

Remember: the absolute length of an arrow on paper has meaning only with respect to the chosen scale.

## Part E: What Can a Force Do?

We cannot see a force directly, but we readily witness its **effects**. A force can produce the following changes:

| Effect of Force | Everyday Example |
|---|---|
| **Set a stationary object in motion** | A stalled cart begins to roll when pushed |
| **Stop a moving object** | A goalkeeper catches and holds a soccer ball |
| **Increase speed (accelerate)** | Pedaling harder on a bicycle along a straight path |
| **Decrease speed (decelerate)** | Applying the handbrakes on a moving bicycle |
| **Change direction of motion** | A baseball batter hitting an incoming pitch toward the outfield |
| **Change shape or size (deformation)** | Squeezing a lump of clay, stretching a spring or rubber band |

Additional illustrations:
- **Lump of modeling clay:** Pressing it between your thumbs flattens it into a disc (change of shape).
- **Steel spring:** Pulling both ends lengthens it; compressing it shortens it (change of size).
- **Rubber band:** Stretching it expands its loop; releasing it allows it to snap back.

A single force application can produce more than one effect simultaneously. For instance, when a tennis racket strikes a ball, it momentary compresses the ball (change of shape), reverses its direction, and increases its speed.

## Part F: Two Families of Forces: Contact and Non-Contact

Forces in nature are broadly classified into two categories:

**1. Contact Forces (require direct physical touch):**

| Force | Description | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Muscular force** | Force exerted by biological muscles | Shoving a heavy crate, lifting a backpack |
| **Friction** | Resistive force between surfaces sliding or attempting to slide against each other | Cart wheels grinding against the rough road |
| **Tension** | Force transmitted through a taut rope, cable, or string | Pulling a bucket out of a well |

**2. Non-Contact Forces (act over a distance without physical contact):**

| Force | Description | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Gravitational force** | Universal attraction between masses | An apple falling from a tree to the ground |
| **Magnetic force** | Attraction or repulsion exerted by magnets on magnetic materials or poles | A magnet sticking to a steel refrigerator door |
| **Electrostatic force** | Attraction or repulsion between electric charges | A rubbed plastic ruler attracting small paper scraps |

When Mr. Clark's hands pushed the cart, his hands were in physical contact with the wood—this was a **contact force** (muscular force). Meanwhile, the Earth's gravity pulled downward on the cart continuously across empty space—a **non-contact force**.

## Part G: When Force is Applied but Nothing Moves

Recall the curious dilemma in our opening story: Mr. Clark pushed hard on the cart, yet it did not move. Maya joined in, pushing with combined effort, and still the cart did not budge. **Does applying a force guarantee that an object must move? No.**

An object can experience significant applied forces and yet remain completely motionless. Why does this happen? The complete answer will unfold in the next topic. For now, remember this crucial principle: **an object rarely experiences only a single force in isolation; multiple forces act simultaneously.** If opposing forces balance each other out, motion will not occur.

## Story Solution: Mr. Clark's Cart

Now let us revisit our opening story with quantitative rigor. Let the scale be: **1 box = 50 N**.

| Person | Applied Force (Push) | Arrow Representation (Boxes) |
|---|---|---|
| Mr. Clark | 150 N | 3 |
| Maya | 100 N | 2 |
| Neighbor 1 | 100 N | 2 |
| Neighbor 2 | 100 N | 2 |
| Neighbor 3 | 100 N | 2 |

```
Uphill Direction -->

Mr. Clark:   -->[ ][ ][ ]-->
Maya:        -->[ ][ ]-->
Neighbor 1:  -->[ ][ ]-->
Neighbor 2:  -->[ ][ ]-->
Neighbor 3:  -->[ ][ ]-->
```

> **Figure 1.3** – All five forces act in the same direction (up the incline). Mr. Clark's vector arrow is longest (150 N, 3 boxes); each of the others is 100 N (2 boxes).

Because all five individuals push in the exact same direction, their forces add together directly:
- Mr. Clark + Maya = $150\text{ N} + 100\text{ N} = \mathbf{250\text{ N}}$
- The three neighbors = $100\text{ N} + 100\text{ N} + 100\text{ N} = \mathbf{300\text{ N}}$
- Combined effort of all five = $250\text{ N} + 300\text{ N} = \mathbf{550\text{ N}}$

**Check with scale boxes:** $3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 11\text{ boxes}$; $11 \times 50\text{ N} = 550\text{ N}$ ✅

This real-world scenario demonstrates the multiple effects of forces:
1. When the combined push became large enough, the cart transitioned from rest into motion (**started moving**).
2. As they maintained this combined effort, the cart accelerated (**sped up**).
3. Once reaching the level market square, if Mr. Clark pulled back on the handles, opposing force would bring it to rest (**stopped motion**).

Maya's initial puzzle is resolved: a push is a force, and forces govern whether objects remain stationary, accelerate, or come to a halt.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing force is an internal property stored inside an object.**  
   Force is not a substance or an energy reserve. It is an **interaction** (a push or a pull) between two distinct bodies. Once physical interaction ends, the force ceases to exist.

2. **Mistake 2: Believing a continuous force is always required just to keep an object moving.**  
   This ancient misconception feels intuitive because of everyday friction. However, if all opposing forces are eliminated, a moving body keeps moving without any applied force. We will explore this thoroughly in **Topic 3** (Newton's First Law).

3. **Mistake 3: Treating force as a scalar with magnitude only.**  
   Force has **direction**. A 100 N push forward produces the exact opposite mechanical effect of a 100 N push backward, even though both have identical magnitudes.

4. **Mistake 4: Equating newtons (N) with kilograms (kg).**  
   The newton is the unit of force; the kilogram is the unit of mass. Never write "a force of 5 kg."

5. **Mistake 5: Drawing vector arrows of arbitrary lengths.**  
   In physics diagrams, vector lengths must correspond to an explicit scale. A 200 N force arrow must be drawn twice as long as a 100 N force arrow pointing in the same diagram.

## Quick Recap

- A **force** is an interaction between two objects manifested as a **push or a pull**.
- The SI unit of force is the **newton (N)**. A 100 g mass exerts roughly **1 N** of downward gravitational pull.
- A **spring balance** measures force magnitude by recording the extension of an internal spring.
- Force is a **vector quantity** possessing both **magnitude and direction**.
- We represent forces using **arrows**: the length represents magnitude, and the arrowhead indicates direction.
- Forces can **start motion, stop motion, speed up, slow down, change direction, or deform shape and size**.
- Forces divide into **contact forces** (muscular, friction, tension) and **non-contact forces** (gravitational, magnetic, electrostatic).
- An applied force does not guarantee motion; other opposing forces can cancel its effect.
