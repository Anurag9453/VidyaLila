# Topic 5: Mass and Weight

In the previous topic, we saw how freely falling bodies accelerate under gravity at rate $g$, and how kinematic equations predict their velocities, flight times, and altitudes. Now we confront an everyday concept that is frequently misunderstood: **if the Earth is constantly pulling every object downward, how do we measure this pull, and what does "weight" truly mean?** In daily conversation, people routinely declare "my weight is $55\text{ kg}$." In the language of physics, that single casual remark conflates two fundamentally different physical quantities: **mass** and **weight**.

---

## Maya's "Weight" at the Fitness Centre

Maya recently joined the school fitness centre. During her initial fitness assessment, her instructor, Mr. Clark, had her step onto a digital bathroom scale. The digital readout flashed: **$55.0$**.

Mr. Clark stated: "Maya, your reading is $55\text{ kilograms}$."

Maya noticed a mechanical **spring balance** hanging from a hook on the training wall. She asked, "Mr. Clark, if I were to suspend myself from that spring balance, would it also display $55$?"

Mr. Clark smiled. "No, it would not read $55$. A spring balance measures force directly in **newtons**, so the dial would indicate a completely different number."

Maya's curiosity deepened: "I read that gravity on the Moon is only a fraction of Earth's gravity. If I were to take this digital scale to the Moon and step on it, what would it display? Would I become thinner on the Moon?"

Mr. Clark replied, "A wonderful question! The answer is not a simple yes or no, because the everyday word 'kilo' masks two distinct physical realities: mass and weight. Let us unpack both concepts with precision."

| Term | Meaning | Example |

| **Weight (W)** | The downward **gravitational force** with which a celestial body attracts an object | $539\text{ N}$ |
| **Kilogram (kg)** | The base SI unit of mass | $55\text{ kg}$ |

| **Weightlessness** | The state where a scale reads zero because the object and scale are in mutual free fall | Astronauts in low-Earth orbit |

## Part A: What is Mass?

The **mass** of an object is the measure of the **quantity of matter** it contains. Whether we consider an iron sphere, a cotton towel, or a human body, all consist of matter, and each possesses mass.

- **SI Unit:** kilogram (**$\text{kg}$**).
- Mass is a **scalar quantity**: it has magnitude but no directional orientation.
- **Mass is invariant:** It remains strictly identical regardless of location—on Earth, on the Moon, on Mars, or floating in deep interstellar space. Transporting an object does not alter the number of atoms composing it.
- **Inertia:** Mass is also the quantitative measure of an object's inertia (as studied in [force-and-laws-of-motion-part2.md](../force-and-laws-of-motion/force-and-laws-of-motion-part2.md)). More mass means greater resistance to changes in motion.

**Measuring Mass:** Mass is measured using balances (such as a two-pan beam balance or an electronic mass comparator) that balance the unknown object against a **known reference mass** (reviewed in [measurement-and-units-part3.md](../measurement-and-units/measurement-and-units-part3.md)). Because gravity pulls equally on both pans, a beam balance that balances on Earth will balance identically on the Moon!

---

## Part B: What is Weight?

In Topics 1 and 2, we learned that Earth attracts every nearby mass inward towards its centre. This downward **gravitational force** exerted on an object is defined as its **weight**.

> **Weight = the gravitational force with which a celestial body (such as Earth or the Moon) attracts an object towards its centre.**

Recall Newton's Second Law: $F = ma$ ([force-and-laws-of-motion-part6.md](../force-and-laws-of-motion/force-and-laws-of-motion-part6.md)). During free fall near a planet's surface, gravity accelerates an object at rate $g$ (Topic 3). Substituting $a = g$ into $F = ma$:

> **$W = m \times g$**

Where:
- **W** = weight (measured in newtons, $\text{N}$)
- **m** = mass (measured in kilograms, $\text{kg}$)
- **g** = local acceleration due to gravity (measured in $\text{m/s}^2$ or $\text{N/kg}$)

**Unit Check:** $\text{kg} \times \text{m/s}^2 = \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2 = \mathbf{\text{N}}$ (newtons). Hence, the SI unit of weight is the **newton**.

**Weight is a Vector:** Weight possesses both magnitude ($W = mg$) and a definite direction: **directed radially inward towards the centre of the attracting body** (i.e., straight downward toward Earth's centre).

```
          ( Object of mass m )
                 [ m ]
                   |
                   |  W = m g
                   v
          Downward, towards Earth's centre

   ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~ Earth's Surface ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
```

> **Figure 5.1** – The weight of an object ($W = mg$) acts as a downward force directed toward the Earth's centre. Thus, weight is a vector quantity.

### Weight Changes with Location, Mass Does Not

In the formula $W = mg$, the local gravitational acceleration $g$ **varies from one location to another** (on Earth $g \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$; on the Moon $g \approx 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$). Consequently:

- Location changes $\rightarrow$ local $g$ changes $\rightarrow$ **weight changes**.
- Object remains intact $\rightarrow$ matter is conserved $\rightarrow$ **mass remains unchanged**.

## Part C: Differences Between Mass and Weight

| Parameter | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | The quantity of matter contained in an object | The gravitational force acting on an object |
| **Type of Quantity** | Scalar (magnitude only) | Vector (magnitude and direction toward planetary centre) |
| **SI Unit** | kilogram ($\text{kg}$) | newton ($\text{N}$) |
| **Formula** | Measured directly by comparison against known standards | $W = mg$ |
| **Variation with Location** | **Constant everywhere** | **Varies** with local gravitational field strength ($g$) |
| **Can it ever be zero?** | Never zero for physical matter | Zero far from celestial bodies, or apparent reading zero during free fall |
| **Measuring Instrument** | Beam balance, pan balance | Spring balance |

### How a Spring Balance Works

A spring balance consists of a calibrated helical spring enclosed within a graduated casing. When an object is suspended from its lower hook, the downward gravitational force (weight) stretches the spring according to Hooke's Law. Greater weight produces greater elongation, moving an indicator down the calibrated scale. Therefore, a spring balance measures **force (weight)** directly, with its scale graduated in **newtons**.

```
               O        <- Suspended from a fixed support
               |
           +---+---+
           |   {   |    <- Calibrated helical spring
           |   }   |
      0 N -|   {   |
     10 N -|   }   |
     20 N -|  =+=  |    <- Indicator needle (reads 20 N)
           +---+---+
               |
              [ ]       <- Suspended load (weight pulling downward)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – A spring balance: the gravitational force (weight) of the load elongates the spring, moving an indicator across a scale calibrated in newtons.

## Part D: Solved Examples

Throughout this section: **Earth's $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$** and **Moon's $g = 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$**, unless an example specifies $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ for simpler calculation. Never mix different values of $g$ within the same example.

### Example 1: Calculating Weight from Mass

Determine the weight of a $60\text{ kg}$ person on Earth: (a) taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, (b) taking $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$.

- (a) $W = mg = (60\text{ kg})(10\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{600\text{ N}}$
- (b) $W = mg = (60\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{588\text{ N}}$

**Check:** $\frac{588\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} = 60\text{ kg}$ ✅; $\frac{600\text{ N}}{10\text{ m/s}^2} = 60\text{ kg}$ ✅. The mass is strictly $60\text{ kg}$ in both cases.

### Example 2: Determining Mass from Weight

An object weighs $490\text{ N}$ on Earth ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). Find its mass.

Rearranging $W = mg \implies m = \frac{W}{g}$:
$$m = \frac{490\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} = \mathbf{50\text{ kg}}$$

**Check:** $(50\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = 490\text{ N}$ ✅.

### Example 3: Weight on the Moon

A person has a mass of $60\text{ kg}$. What is their mass and weight on the Moon ($g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$)?

- Mass on the Moon: **$60\text{ kg}$** (remains completely unchanged).
- Weight on the Moon:  
  $W_{\text{Moon}} = m g_{\text{Moon}} = (60\text{ kg})(1.6\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{96\text{ N}}$

**Check:** $\frac{96\text{ N}}{1.6\text{ m/s}^2} = 60\text{ kg}$ ✅.  
Comparing Moon weight to Earth weight ($W_{\text{Earth}} = 588\text{ N}$):
$$\frac{W_{\text{Moon}}}{W_{\text{Earth}}} = \frac{96\text{ N}}{588\text{ N}} \approx 0.163 \approx \frac{1}{6}$$
Because $g_{\text{Moon}} \approx \frac{1}{6} g_{\text{Earth}}$, an object on the Moon weighs approximately **one-sixth** of its Earth weight.

### Example 4: Maya's Scale (Solving the Fitness Centre Scenario)

Maya's digital scale displays a reading of $55\text{ kilograms}$. What is her weight on Earth in newtons? What would her mass and weight be on the Moon?

The scale's readout corresponds to a **mass $m = 55\text{ kg}$**.

**On Earth:**
- Taking $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$: $W = mg = (55\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{539\text{ N}}$
- (Or taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$: $W = (55)(10) = \mathbf{550\text{ N}}$)

**On the Moon:**
- Mass remains: **$55\text{ kg}$**
- Weight: $W_{\text{Moon}} = (55\text{ kg})(1.6\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{88\text{ N}}$

**Check:** $\frac{539}{9.8} = 55\text{ kg}$ ✅; $\frac{88}{1.6} = 55\text{ kg}$ ✅; $\frac{88}{539} \approx 0.163 \approx \frac{1}{6}$ ✅.  
**Mr. Clark's explanation to Maya:** "On a spring balance on Earth, you would register approximately $539\text{ N}$. On the Moon, that same spring balance would display only $88\text{ N}$. You have not become thinner—your **mass is still $55\text{ kg}$**. However, because the Moon pulls with only one-sixth the gravitational force, you would feel light and effortlessly leap high into the lunar sky!"

### Example 5: Weight on an Exoplanet

A newly discovered planet has a surface gravity of $g = 25\text{ m/s}^2$. What is the weight of a $10\text{ kg}$ sphere on this planet? What would it weigh on Earth (using $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$)? What is the ratio?

- Weight on the planet: $W = (10\text{ kg})(25\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{250\text{ N}}$
- Weight on Earth: $W = (10\text{ kg})(10\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{100\text{ N}}$
- Ratio: $\frac{250\text{ N}}{100\text{ N}} = \mathbf{2.5}$

**Check:** Ratio of weights matches the ratio of gravities: $\frac{25}{10} = 2.5$ ✅.

### Example 6: Finding Earth Weight when Lunar Weight is Given

An instrument weighs $80\text{ N}$ on the Moon ($g_{\text{Moon}} = 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$). Find its mass and its weight on Earth ($g_{\text{Earth}} = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$).

- Mass: $m = \frac{W_{\text{Moon}}}{g_{\text{Moon}}} = \frac{80\text{ N}}{1.6\text{ m/s}^2} = \mathbf{50\text{ kg}}$
- Earth Weight: $W_{\text{Earth}} = mg = (50\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{490\text{ N}}$

**Check:** $(50)(1.6) = 80\text{ N}$ ✅; $\frac{490}{9.8} = 50\text{ kg}$ ✅. Mass ($50\text{ kg}$) is the bridge between celestial bodies.

### Example 7: Reading from a Spring Balance

A spring balance records a load of $49\text{ N}$ near Earth's surface ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). Find the mass of the suspended object.

$$m = \frac{W}{g} = \frac{49\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} = \mathbf{5\text{ kg}}$$

**Check:** $(5\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = 49\text{ N}$ ✅.

### Example 8: Connecting Weight to Newton's Second Law

A $5\text{ kg}$ stone is dropped in free fall (neglect air resistance, $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). What is the net force acting on the stone, and what is its acceleration?

In free fall, the only force acting is **gravity (the stone's weight)**:
- Net force: $F = W = mg = (5\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{49\text{ N}}$ (downward)
- Acceleration: $a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{49\text{ N}}{5\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{9.8\text{ m/s}^2}$ ($= g$)

**Check:** $F = ma = (5)(9.8) = 49\text{ N}$ ✅.

### Example 9: Comparing Weights of Two Masses

On Earth, Object A has mass $2\text{ kg}$ and Object B has mass $6\text{ kg}$ ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). Find their weights and compare their ratio.

- $W_A = (2\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{19.6\text{ N}}$
- $W_B = (6\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{58.8\text{ N}}$
- $\frac{W_B}{W_A} = \frac{58.8\text{ N}}{19.6\text{ N}} = \mathbf{3}$

**Check:** Mass ratio is $\frac{6\text{ kg}}{2\text{ kg}} = 3$ ✅. Because $g$ is identical for both objects, the weight ratio strictly equals the mass ratio.

### Example 10: Converting Kilogram-Force to Newtons

A crate experiences a gravitational pull of $686\text{ N}$. Express its weight in kilogram-force ($\text{kgf}$), and identify its mass ($1\text{ kgf} = 9.8\text{ N}$).

- Weight in $\text{kgf} = \frac{686\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ N/kgf}} = \mathbf{70\text{ kgf}}$
- Mass $m = \frac{686\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} = \mathbf{70\text{ kg}}$

**Check:** $(70\text{ kgf})(9.8\text{ N/kgf}) = 686\text{ N}$ ✅.

## Part E: "Kilograms" and "Kilogram-Force": Everyday Confusion Clarified

When Maya stood on the bathroom scale, the digital display indicated "$55$". How did it arrive at that number?

Inside a digital bathroom scale is an electronic force sensor (a load cell). When you step on the scale, it detects the **downward force (weight)** you exert: $539\text{ N}$. The internal microcontroller is pre-programmed with Earth's surface gravity ($g \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). It automatically divides the measured force by $9.8$ to compute your **mass in kilograms**:
$$\text{Mass} = \frac{539\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} = \mathbf{55\text{ kg}}$$

In non-scientific settings, people often describe this downward force as **$55\text{ kgf}$ (kilogram-force)**: the force with which Earth attracts a $1\text{ kg}$ mass at sea level:
$$1\text{ kgf} = 9.8\text{ N} \implies 55\text{ kgf} = 55 \times 9.8\text{ N} = 539\text{ N}$$

**Why do everyday people confuse mass and weight?** Because all human life takes place on Earth's surface, where $g \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ is nearly uniform. As a result, weight and mass are always strictly proportional. Doubling mass doubles weight, so colloquially, people use "weight" and "mass" interchangeably. In physics, however, we must maintain precision: **mass is measured in $\text{kg}$, while weight is measured in $\text{N}$**.

**A Thought Experiment:** If Maya took that exact Earth-calibrated scale to the Moon and stepped on it, the scale would detect her lunar weight of $88\text{ N}$. Dividing by its hardcoded Earth value of $9.8$, it would display:
$$\frac{88\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} \approx 8.98 \approx \mathbf{9\text{ kg}}$$
This $9\text{ kg}$ is simply an artifact of an incorrectly calibrated device! Maya's real mass remains $55\text{ kg}$. Only her weight has diminished.

## Part F: How Weight Varies at Other Locations (Conceptual Overview)

Even on Earth, $g$ varies slightly across different environments:

- **At High Altitudes:** On high mountain summits or in passenger airplanes, distance from Earth's centre increases ($r = R + h$). As a result, $g$ decreases slightly, making weight slightly smaller.
- **At the Poles vs the Equator:** Because Earth bulges at the equator, sea level at the poles is closer to Earth's centre than at the equator ($R_{\text{pole}} < R_{\text{equator}}$). Consequently, $g$ is slightly larger at the poles ($\approx 9.83\text{ m/s}^2$) and slightly smaller at the equator ($\approx 9.78\text{ m/s}^2$). An object weighs slightly more at the North Pole than in Singapore!
- **On Other Planetary Bodies:** As seen with the Moon ($g \approx 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$), weight changes dramatically while mass remains constant.

### Weightlessness: A Brief Look

Inside an orbiting spacecraft, astronauts appear to float freely, and a bathroom scale placed beneath them reads **zero**. 

Does this occur because gravity has ceased to exist? Absolutely not! Earth's gravity is strong at orbital altitudes (roughly $89\%$ of surface gravity). The apparent floating occurs because **the astronaut, the scale, and the entire spacecraft are falling around Earth together at the exact same rate in continuous free fall**. Because the scale falls away beneath the astronaut's feet as fast as gravity pulls them down, they cannot compress the scale, producing a reading of zero. We will study orbital mechanics and satellite paths in detail in **Topic 6**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Stating "My weight is 60 kg."**  
   *Correction:* In physics, the kilogram is the SI unit of **mass**. Weight is a force, so its correct unit is the **newton**. The accurate statement is: "My mass is $60\text{ kg}$, and my weight on Earth is approximately $588\text{ N}$."

2. **Mistake 2: Believing an object's mass decreases on the Moon.**  
   *Correction:* Mass is the quantity of matter and never changes ($55\text{ kg}$ on Earth is still $55\text{ kg}$ on the Moon). Only the downward gravitational force (weight) changes.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing units: writing weight in kg.**  
   *Correction:* Never write $W = mg = (60)(9.8) = 588\text{ kg}$. The unit must be newtons ($\text{N}$).

4. **Mistake 4: Mixing values of g within the same problem.**  
   *Correction:* If you compute weight with $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ ($55\text{ kg} \rightarrow 550\text{ N}$), do not invert using $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$. Stick to one value consistently throughout.

5. **Mistake 5: Claiming that astronauts float because there is no gravity in space.**  
   *Correction:* Gravity is very present in low-Earth orbit. Astronauts feel weightless because they are in continuous free fall alongside their spacecraft.

6. **Mistake 6: Treating weight as a scalar quantity.**  
   *Correction:* Weight is a force and therefore a **vector** pointing radially toward the centre of the attracting body.

## Quick Recap

- **Mass ($m$):** The amount of matter in an object. Base SI unit is the **kilogram ($\text{kg}$)**. Scalar quantity. Constant everywhere in the universe. Measured using a beam balance or pan balance.
- **Weight ($W$):** The gravitational force pulling an object toward a celestial body's centre. **$W = mg$**. SI unit is the **newton ($\text{N}$)**. Vector quantity directed downward toward the body's centre. Measured using a spring balance.
- **Variation with Location:** Weight changes whenever local gravity $g$ changes ($W_{\text{Moon}} \approx \frac{1}{6} W_{\text{Earth}}$); mass remains unchanged.
- A $60\text{ kg}$ person has a mass of $60\text{ kg}$ everywhere; they weigh $588\text{ N}$ on Earth ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$) and $96\text{ N}$ on the Moon ($g = 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$).
- **$1\text{ kgf} = 9.8\text{ N}$:** Bathroom scales measure downward force and convert it to display mass in kilograms based on Earth's gravity.
- **Free-Fall Weightlessness:** An apparent zero-scale reading occurs when an observer and scale fall together at the same acceleration $g$, not because gravity is absent.

An Earth-calibrated scale dividing $88\text{ N}$ by $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ yields approximately $9\text{ kg}$, but that reading is simply a calibration error: the scale assumes Earth's gravity. Mass represents the conserved amount of physical matter, so Maya's mass remains exactly $55\text{ kg}$.
