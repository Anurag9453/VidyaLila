# Topic 6: Introduction to Satellites and Tides

In the previous topic, we examined the vital distinction between mass and weight: mass is the fundamental amount of matter in an object and remains invariant everywhere, whereas weight ($W = mg$) is the gravitational force acting on that mass, varying with local gravity $g$ (becoming approximately one-sixth on the Moon). We also introduced the concept of weightlessness—noting that feeling weightless and having true zero gravitational force are entirely different phenomena.

Now, we extend gravity from the Earth's surface outward into the heavens. In this concluding topic of the chapter, we explore profound questions: Why does the Moon not fall down to Earth? How do artificial satellites stay aloft in orbit? Why do astronauts appear to float weightlessly inside a space station? And why do ocean waters rise and fall in a rhythmic cycle twice every day? As this is the final topic, we will also provide a comprehensive wrap-up of the entire Gravitation chapter.

## An Evening by the Ocean Shore

Dusk was settling over a quiet coastal fishing village. Mr. Pereira, a seasoned local fisherman, was hauling his wooden fishing skiff high up onto the dry sand and hanging his nets up to dry. Standing nearby on the beach was his curious young granddaughter, Sophia.

Earlier that afternoon, Sophia had walked along this exact shoreline: the water had receded hundreds of metres away, exposing a wide, damp expanse of sand and tide pools. High in the twilight sky, the bright Moon shone directly above the calm waters.

"Grandpa, this afternoon the sea was so far back, and now the waves are nearly reaching the road! Does the Moon have something to do with the water rising? It is shining right above us."

Mr. Pereira smiled warmly. "You observed astutely, Sophia. For generations, fishermen have looked to the Moon to understand the moods and rhythms of the sea."

Sophia asked another clever question: "If Earth's gravity pulls on everything, why doesn't the Moon crash straight down into the Earth like a dropped pebble?"

Mr. Pereira chuckled. "Both questions are woven from the exact same thread of physics. Let us first understand why the Moon never crashes into us."

## Part A: Why Does the Moon Not Fall to Earth?

### Debunking a Common Misconception

Many people mistakenly believe that the Moon orbits in space because it is "beyond Earth's gravity," or that Earth exerts no pull on it. **Both ideas are completely false.** 

Earth exerts a colossal gravitational pull of approximately $2 \times 10^{20}\text{ N}$ on the Moon (as calculated in Topic 2, Example 4), and by Newton's Third Law, the Moon pulls back on Earth with the exact same magnitude. The true puzzle is: if Earth pulls the Moon with such immense force, why does the Moon not fall straight down into Earth's surface?

The answer is both surprising and poetic: **The Moon *is* constantly falling towards Earth every second! However, it possesses such enormous sideways (tangential) orbital velocity that as it falls, the curved surface of the Earth bends away beneath it at the exact same rate!** Consequently, the Moon continuously falls *around* the Earth, never touching the surface.

Isaac Newton illustrated this brilliant concept with a famous thought experiment known as **Newton's Cannon**.

### Newton's Cannon Thought Experiment

Imagine placing a powerful cannon atop an impossibly high mountain summit reaching above Earth's atmosphere (so air drag is absent). The cannon fires projectiles horizontally (parallel to the ground):

- **Case 1: Low muzzle velocity.** The cannonball travels forward while gravity pulls it downward in a parabolic arc (Topic 3 free fall). It strikes the ground a short distance from the mountain.
- **Case 2: Higher muzzle velocity.** The cannonball travels much further horizontally before striking the ground.
- **Case 3: Orbital velocity.** Notice that Earth is a sphere; its surface curves downward beneath the horizontal. If the horizontal launch speed is fast enough, the rate at which the projectile drops due to gravity matches the rate at which Earth's spherical surface curves away beneath it. The projectile falls continuously, but its distance above the surface remains constant!
- **Case 4: Closed circular orbit.** The projectile sweeps completely around the globe and returns to its starting point at the mountain peak, continuing in an eternal circular loop. It has become an **artificial satellite**!

```
                  M  (Cannon on mountain peak)
                  *----->  .  .  .
                  |'.  '.          .   (3) The cannonball falls around Earth
            .-----+--x----x--.       .     in a continuous closed orbit
          .'     (1)  (2)     '.      .
         /                      \      .
        |         Earth          |      .
         \                      /      .
          '.                  .'      .
            '----------------'      .
               .  .  .  .  .  .  .

    (1) Low speed: lands nearby    (2) Higher speed: lands further away
    (x = impact points on the ground)
    (3) Orbital speed: continually curves around Earth without striking the ground
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Newton's Cannon thought experiment. As horizontal launch velocity increases from summit M, the projectile lands further away. At orbital velocity, the projectile's downward fall matches Earth's curvature, keeping it in perpetual free-fall orbit.

This is precisely what occurs with the Moon. The Moon was not fired from a cannon, but since the formation of the Solar System, it has possessed a high sideways velocity ($\approx 1.02\text{ km/s}$). Gravity pulls the Moon continuously towards Earth's centre, bending what would have been a straight path into a stable closed orbit.

### Gravity as the Centripetal Force

In our study of kinematics ([../motion/motion-part7.md](../motion/motion-part7.md)), we learned that any object moving in a circular path experiences a **centripetal acceleration** directed towards the centre of the circle ($a_c = v^2/r$). Newton's Second Law dictates that an inward net force—a **centripetal force**—must provide this acceleration.

For a stone whirled on a string, the tension in the string provides the centripetal force. For the Moon orbiting Earth, there is no physical tether: **Earth's invisible gravitational pull provides the required centripetal force**.

If Earth's gravity were to suddenly vanish, the Moon would fly off instantaneously along a straight tangent into deep interstellar space (Newton's First Law). Earth's gravity continuously curves the Moon's velocity vector, maintaining its near-circular path. While the Moon's orbital speed remains roughly uniform, its direction of travel changes continuously, meaning it undergoes continuous inward acceleration.

## Part B: What is a Satellite?

**A satellite is any body that orbits around a more massive primary body in a gravitationally bound path (orbit).** Satellites fall into two categories:

1. **Natural Satellites:** Naturally occurring celestial bodies. The Moon is Earth's natural satellite. Other planets possess numerous natural satellites (for example, Jupiter has over 90 moons).
2. **Artificial Satellites:** Human-engineered machines launched into orbit by multi-stage rockets. Simply lifting a satellite to space is not enough; the launch vehicle must steer horizontally and accelerate the spacecraft to an extreme sideways speed ($\approx 7.9\text{ km/s}$ for low orbits). Once achieved, the rocket engines shut down, and Earth's gravity gracefully maintains the satellite in stable free fall around the planet for years or decades.

### Applications of Artificial Satellites

| Domain | Primary Function | Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Telecommunications** | Relaying television broadcasts, phone calls, and high-speed broadband data across continents | Satellite television and transoceanic live broadcasts |
| **Meteorology** | Monitoring cloud movements, ocean temperatures, and tracking severe cyclonic storms | Early warning systems for tropical hurricanes and cyclones |
| **Navigation** | Providing precise real-time geolocation and timing signals to ground receivers | GPS, Galileo, and smartphone mapping apps |
| **Earth Observation & Remote Sensing** | High-resolution optical and radar imaging of agricultural crops, forests, water bodies, and urban growth | Assessing flood damage and monitoring polar ice melt |

## Part C: Orbits: Low Orbits vs Distant Orbits

The altitude of an orbit governs both the orbital speed of the satellite and the time required to complete one full revolution (the **orbital period, $T$**):

- **Low-Earth Orbit (LEO):** Satellites orbiting relatively close to Earth's surface (altitudes between $300\text{ km}$ and $1000\text{ km}$) travel at very high orbital velocities ($\approx 7.8\text{ km/s}$ to $7.9\text{ km/s}$) and complete a revolution in only **$90\text{ minutes}$** (approximately $1.5\text{ hours}$). They complete about $16$ full orbits every single day!
- **Distant Orbits:** Satellites located further from Earth experience weaker gravity and travel more slowly, covering much larger orbital perimeters. The Moon is located roughly $384,000\text{ km}$ away and takes approximately **$27.3\text{ days}$** to complete one revolution.

Remember the general orbital principle: **The closer a satellite is to Earth, the faster it must travel and the shorter its orbital period.**

```
          Distant Orbit (Moon: slow speed, 27.3 days)
        . - - - - - - - - - - - .
      .'   . - - - - - - - - .   '.
     /   .'     Low Orbit       '.   \
    |   /    (LEO: fast, 90 min)  \   |
    |  |         . - - .          |  |
    |  |        ( Earth )         |  |
    |  |         ' - - '          |  |
     \  \                        /  /
      '.  ' - - - - - - - - - '  .'
        ' - - - - - - - - - - - '
```

> **Figure 6.2** – Comparison of orbits around Earth. Low-Earth orbits are close to Earth, requiring high orbital speeds and yielding short periods ($\approx 90\text{ min}$). High orbits are further out, with slower orbital velocities and much longer orbital periods.

### Geostationary Satellites

At an altitude of approximately $35,786\text{ km}$, a circular orbit has an orbital period of exactly **$24\text{ hours}$**—matching Earth's own rotation period on its axis. A satellite placed in this equatorial orbit travels eastward at the exact rate Earth rotates, making it appear **completely stationary** relative to an observer on the ground. Such a craft is called a **geostationary satellite**. Because the satellite stays permanently fixed over one geographic spot, ground receiving dishes (such as residential satellite TV dishes) can point permanently at a fixed direction in the sky without needing to track across the heavens.

## Part D: Why Do Astronauts Float in Space?

Video footage from the International Space Station (ISS) shows astronauts floating gracefully in mid-air, leading many people to assume: "There must be zero gravity in space!"

This is a widespread **misconception**.

The ISS orbits at an altitude of approximately $400\text{ km}$ above sea level. Given Earth's mean radius of $6400\text{ km}$, the distance from Earth's centre is $r = 6800\text{ km}$. Using the inverse-square law, the gravitational field strength at ISS altitude is still **nearly $90\%$ of its surface value** ($\approx 8.7\text{ m/s}^2$ compared to $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$)! Gravity in low-Earth orbit is exceptionally strong.

Why then do astronauts float?

Astronauts float because **the astronaut, their tools, and the entire spacecraft are falling around Earth together in continuous, uninterrupted free fall**.

Recall from Topic 3 that in free fall, all objects accelerate at the exact same rate regardless of their mass. Because the floor of the space station accelerates towards Earth at the exact same rate as the astronaut's feet, the astronaut exerts no contact force on the floor, and the floor exerts no upward normal support force on the astronaut. Without a supporting normal force, the human sensation of weight vanishes!

Think of a hypothetical elevator whose support cable snaps: the elevator cab and an occupant inside fall with the identical downward acceleration $g$. The passenger floats freely inside the cab during the plunge. Orbital motion is simply a continuous, sideways-directed version of this state.

### Why Does a Weighing Scale Read Zero?

As studied in Topic 5, a bathroom scale measures the **contact normal force** pressing down upon its surface. On the ground, you press against the scale with a force equal to your weight ($mg$), so the scale reads your true weight. In orbit, both you and the scale are in simultaneous free fall. Because you do not compress the scale, it indicates **$0\text{ N}$**.

This condition is termed **apparent weightlessness**. Earth's gravitational force ($F = mg$) continues to act on the astronaut at all times—indeed, it is what prevents them from flying off into deep space—and the astronaut's **mass is strictly unchanged**.

## Part E: Ocean Tides: The Rising and Falling of the Seas

Now let us address Sophia's observation at the beach: why ocean water rhythmically rises and falls along the coast.

- **High Tide:** The point in the tidal cycle where ocean water rises to its highest elevation, covering the upper shore.
- **Low Tide:** The point where the water recedes to its lowest level, exposing wide sandy shallows.

Tides are not ordinary ocean waves. Ordinary waves are surface ripples created by blowing wind. Tides represent a **gradual, cosmic rise and fall of the entire ocean surface** occurring over hours, driven by the gravitational pulls of the Moon and the Sun.

### Gravitational Pull of the Moon

The Moon's gravitational field reaches across space to Earth. Because gravitational force obeys the inverse-square law ($F \propto 1/r^2$), the Moon pulls more strongly on the side of Earth facing it (which is closer) than on Earth's centre, and pulls least on the far side of Earth (which is furthest away).

This difference in gravitational attraction across Earth's diameter creates a **differential tidal force**:
- The ocean waters on the near side are pulled toward the Moon more strongly than the solid body of Earth, causing the water to stretch outward in a **tidal bulge (high tide)**.
- The solid Earth is pulled toward the Moon more strongly than the waters on the far side, effectively pulling Earth away from the far ocean water. This leaves behind a second **matching tidal bulge on the opposite side (high tide)**!

```
                         Low Tide
                   . - - - - - - - - .
             . '       .---------.       ' .
    High    (         (   Earth   )         )    High          Moon
    Tide     ' .       '---------'       . '     Tide           ( )
                   ' - - - - - - - - '
                         Low Tide

  Outer ellipse = Ocean water layer. Water bulges on the side facing the Moon
  and simultaneously on the opposite side: two high tide bulges.
  The intermediate regions experience low tide.
```

> **Figure 6.3** – Tidal bulges created by the Moon's differential gravitational pull. Earth rotates within this elongated envelope of ocean water, causing coastal regions to experience two high tides and two low tides each day.

The Sun also exerts a gravitational pull on Earth's oceans. However, because the Sun is approximately $390\text{ times}$ further away than the Moon, its differential tidal effect is **less than half** that of the Moon. The Moon is the primary driver of terrestrial ocean tides.

### The Timing of Semi-Diurnal Tides

As Earth rotates once every $24\text{ hours}$ through these two oceanic bulges, a coastline passes through both bulges, experiencing **two high tides and two low tides every day**.

Because the Moon is also orbiting Earth in the same direction, a given location on Earth must rotate slightly more than $360^\circ$ to realign with the Moon. The cycle between successive high tides is approximately **$12\text{ hours and } 25\text{ minutes}$**. Consequently, the time of high tide shifts forward by approximately $50\text{ minutes}$ each day ($2 \times 25\text{ min} = 50\text{ min}$).

### Spring Tides and Neap Tides

The height of high and low tides varies systematically throughout the monthly lunar cycle:

- **Spring Tides:** Occur during the **Full Moon** and **New Moon**. At these phases, the Sun, Earth, and Moon align in a straight line (syzygy). The gravitational tidal forces of the Sun and Moon reinforce one another, producing **exceptionally high high tides and exceptionally low low tides** (the tidal range is maximized). The name has nothing to do with the spring season; it comes from the idea of the water "springing up."
- **Neap Tides:** Occur during the **First Quarter** and **Third Quarter** half-moons. Here, the Sun and Moon lie at right angles ($90^\circ$) relative to Earth. The Sun's gravitational pull partially counteracts the Moon's pull, resulting in **milder tides: lower high tides and higher low tides** (the tidal range is minimized).

```
   Spring Tide (Linear Alignment)          Neap Tide (Right-Angle Alignment)

    Sun ---- Earth ---- Moon                    Moon
    (or Moon ---- Earth ---- Sun)                |
                                                 |
    Gravitational pulls combine                 Earth ------- Sun
    -> Extra-high tidal range                   Pulls partially cancel
                                                -> Moderate tidal range
```

> **Figure 6.4** – Spring tides occur when Sun, Earth, and Moon align linearly (new and full moon). Neap tides occur when the Sun and Moon act at right angles to each other (quarter moons).

### Fishermen and Tide Tables

Coastal mariners, dockworkers, and fishermen like Mr. Pereira consult published **tide tables** daily. These tables provide the exact forecasted clock times and predicted heights of high and low water for local harbours, allowing boats to enter or depart safely without running aground on exposed sandbars.

## Part F: Quantitative Examples

(Values used are realistic astronomical approximations. Take $\pi = 3.14$.)

### Example 1: Orbital Period and Frequency of a Satellite

A low-Earth orbit satellite completes $16$ full revolutions around Earth in one day ($24\text{ hours}$). Determine its orbital period $T$ in hours and seconds, and its frequency $f$ in hertz ($\text{Hz}$).

- Total time in one day: $24\text{ h} = 24 \times 3600\text{ s} = 86,400\text{ s}$
- Orbital period:  
  $T = \frac{24\text{ h}}{16} = \mathbf{1.5\text{ h}} = 1.5 \times 3600\text{ s} = \mathbf{5400\text{ s}}$
- Orbital frequency:  
  $f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{5400\text{ s}} \approx \mathbf{1.85 \times 10^{-4}\text{ Hz}}$

**Check:** $(5400\text{ s})(16) = 86,400\text{ s} = 1\text{ day}$ ✅; $(1.85 \times 10^{-4}\text{ s}^{-1})(5400\text{ s}) = 1.0$ ✅.

### Example 2: Distance and Orbital Speed in One Orbit

A satellite moves in a circular orbit of radius $r = 6.8 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ with an orbital period of $T = 5400\text{ s}$. Calculate the distance traveled in one orbit and the satellite's orbital speed.

- Circumference (distance in one orbit):  
  $s = 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times (6.8 \times 10^6\text{ m}) = \mathbf{4.27 \times 10^7\text{ m}}$
- Orbital speed:  
  $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = \frac{4.2704 \times 10^7\text{ m}}{5400\text{ s}} \approx \mathbf{7908\text{ m/s} \approx 7.9\text{ km/s}}$

**Check:** $(7908\text{ m/s})(5400\text{ s}) \approx 4.27 \times 10^7\text{ m}$ ✅. This corresponds to approximately $28,500\text{ km/h}$!

### Example 3: Orbital Speed of the Moon

The Moon orbits Earth at a mean radius of $r = 3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m}$ with an orbital period of $T = 27.3\text{ days}$. Compute the orbital speed of the Moon.

- Time in seconds:  
  $T = 27.3 \times 24 \times 3600\text{ s} = 27.3 \times 86,400\text{ s} = 2.3587 \times 10^6\text{ s}$
- Circumference of orbit:  
  $2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times (3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m}) = 2.4115 \times 10^9\text{ m}$
- Orbital speed:  
  $v = \frac{2\pi r}{T} = \frac{2.4115 \times 10^9\text{ m}}{2.3587 \times 10^6\text{ s}} \approx \mathbf{1022\text{ m/s} \approx 1.02\text{ km/s}}$

**Check:** $(1022\text{ m/s})(2.3587 \times 10^6\text{ s}) \approx 2.41 \times 10^9\text{ m}$ ✅.  
Notice the contrast: The distant Moon travels at $1.02\text{ km/s}$ taking $27.3\text{ days}$, while the close-in satellite travels at $7.9\text{ km/s}$ taking just $1.5\text{ hours}$.

### Example 4: Number of High Tides per Day

Successive high tides at a port occur every $12\text{ h } 25\text{ min}$. How many high tides occur within an exact $24\text{ hour}$ window?

- Interval between tides: $12\text{ h } 25\text{ min} = (12 \times 60) + 25 = 745\text{ minutes}$
- Total minutes in a day: $24 \times 60 = 1440\text{ minutes}$
- Number of high tides $= \frac{1440}{745} \approx \mathbf{1.93}$

Thus, there are approximately two high tides per solar day, but slightly less than two full cycles fit into 24 hours. This explains why tide times advance forward by roughly $50\text{ minutes}$ every day.

**Check:** $745\text{ min} \times 2 = 1490\text{ min} = 24\text{ h } 50\text{ min}$ ✅.

### Example 5: Tide Timetable Predictions

A high tide occurs at a coastal harbour at 6:00 am. At what times will the next two high tides occur, assuming an interval of $12\text{ h } 25\text{ min}$?

- Second high tide: $6:00\text{ am} + 12\text{ h } 25\text{ min} = \mathbf{6:25\text{ pm (18:25)}}$
- Third high tide: $18:25 + 12\text{ h } 25\text{ min} = \mathbf{6:50\text{ am (next morning)}}$

**Check:** Total elapsed time from first to third tide $= 24\text{ h } 50\text{ min} = 2 \times (12\text{ h } 25\text{ min})$ ✅.

### Example 6: Magnitude of Gravity in Low Earth Orbit

Earth has a radius of $R = 6.4 \times 10^6\text{ m}$. A satellite orbits at a distance of $r = 6.8 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ from Earth's centre (an altitude of $400\text{ km}$). What fraction of surface gravity ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$) exists at this orbit?

By the inverse-square law ($g \propto 1/r^2$):
$$\frac{g_{\text{orbit}}}{g_{\text{surface}}} = \left(\frac{R}{r}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{6.4 \times 10^6}{6.8 \times 10^6}\right)^2 = (0.9412)^2 \approx \mathbf{0.886 \text{ (approx. } 89\%)}$$
$$g_{\text{orbit}} = 0.886 \times 9.8\text{ m/s}^2 \approx \mathbf{8.68\text{ m/s}^2}$$

**Check:** $(6.8 / 6.4)^2 = (1.0625)^2 = 1.1289$; $1 / 1.1289 = 0.886$ ✅. Earth's gravity in low-Earth orbit remains substantial ($\approx 8.7\text{ m/s}^2$).

### Example 7: Gravitational Force on an Astronaut vs Scale Reading

An astronaut has a mass of $60\text{ kg}$.  
(a) What is their weight on Earth's surface ($g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$)?  
(b) What is the gravitational force acting on them in the orbit of Example 6 ($g = 8.68\text{ m/s}^2$)?  
(c) What does a weighing scale read if placed beneath them in orbit?

- (a) Surface weight: $W = mg = (60\text{ kg})(9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{588\text{ N}}$
- (b) Gravitational force in orbit: $F = mg_{\text{orbit}} = (60\text{ kg})(8.68\text{ m/s}^2) \approx \mathbf{521\text{ N}}$
- (c) Scale reading: **$0\text{ N}$**

**Check:** The true gravitational force is $521\text{ N}$ (retaining $89\%$ of surface weight). The scale reading is zero because the astronaut and the scale are falling together in free fall.

### Example 8: Gravity at Twice Earth's Radius (2R)

Determine $g$ at a point located at distance $2R$ from Earth's centre ($R = 6.4 \times 10^6\text{ m}$). What is the altitude above Earth's surface?

- Ratio: $\frac{g_{2R}}{g_R} = \frac{1}{2^2} = \mathbf{\frac{1}{4} = 0.25}$
- Gravitational acceleration: $g_{2R} = \frac{9.8\text{ m/s}^2}{4} = \mathbf{2.45\text{ m/s}^2}$
- Altitude above surface: $h = 2R - R = \mathbf{R = 6.4 \times 10^6\text{ m} \text{ (6400 km)}}$

**Check:** A $60\text{ kg}$ astronaut at this distance experiences $F = (60)(2.45) = 147\text{ N} = \frac{588\text{ N}}{4}$ ✅.

"Indeed, Sophia," Mr. Pereira confirmed. "It travels so swiftly that even as Earth's gravity constantly pulls it downward, the curvature of space and Earth ensures it never hits the ground. It is like the cannonball from Newton's mountain, falling endlessly around us."

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing gravity is zero inside an orbiting spacecraft.**  
   *Correction:* In low-Earth orbit, gravity retains nearly $90\%$ of its surface strength. Astronauts float because they and the craft are in simultaneous free fall.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming the Moon is not attracted by Earth's gravity.**  
   *Correction:* Earth exerts an immense attractive pull on the Moon ($F \approx 2 \times 10^{20}\text{ N}$), providing the centripetal force that maintains its orbit.

3. **Mistake 3: Believing tides are caused primarily by the Sun.**  
   *Correction:* Although the Sun is much more massive, it is nearly $400\text{ times}$ further away. The Moon's tidal influence is more than twice as strong as the Sun's.

4. **Mistake 4: Confusing tides with wind waves.**  
   *Correction:* Wind waves are localized surface disturbances. Tides are planetary-scale fluctuations of entire ocean basins driven by celestial gravity.

5. **Mistake 5: Thinking a distance of 2R means an altitude of 2R above the surface.**  
   *Correction:* Distance $r$ in gravitational formulas is measured from Earth's **centre**. A distance of $2R$ from the centre corresponds to an altitude of $h = 2R - R = R$ above the surface.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming high tides occur at the exact same hour every day.**  
   *Correction:* Because the Moon orbits Earth as Earth spins, the tidal cycle is $24\text{ hours and } 50\text{ minutes}$, shifting high tide forward by about $50\text{ minutes}$ each day.

## Quick Recap

- **Why the Moon does not fall:** The Moon has a high tangential velocity ($\approx 1.02\text{ km/s}$). As it falls toward Earth under gravity, Earth's surface curves away beneath it at the same rate. It is in perpetual free fall around Earth (Newton's Cannon).
- **Centripetal Force:** Earth's gravitational force provides the inward acceleration keeping satellites and the Moon in orbit.
- **Satellites:** Natural (e.g., the Moon) or artificial (launched by rockets). Low-Earth orbit satellites travel at $\approx 7.9\text{ km/s}$ with periods of $\approx 90\text{ minutes}$. **Geostationary satellites** orbit at $\approx 35,786\text{ km}$ with a period of $24\text{ hours}$, remaining fixed over one spot on Earth.
- **Weightlessness in Orbit:** Gravity is not zero ($\approx 8.7\text{ m/s}^2$ in LEO). Astronauts float because they and their spacecraft are in mutual free fall.
- **Ocean Tides:** Caused primarily by the differential gravitational attraction of the Moon across Earth's diameter, creating two high-tide bulges.
- **Spring Tides vs Neap Tides:** Spring tides occur at new and full moons (Sun and Moon aligned $\rightarrow$ maximum tidal range). Neap tides occur at quarter moons (Sun and Moon at $90^\circ \rightarrow$ minimum tidal range).
- **Tide Tables:** Coastal mariners use published schedules of high and low tides to plan safe navigation.

## Practice Questions

1. A satellite completes $12$ orbits in one day ($24\text{ hours}$). Find its orbital period in hours and seconds, and calculate its frequency in hertz (to three significant figures).

2. A satellite travels in a circular orbit of radius $7.0 \times 10^6\text{ m}$ with an orbital period of $5800\text{ s}$. Find the distance traveled in one revolution and the orbital speed in $\text{km/s}$ (take $\pi = 3.14$).

3. Using the Moon's orbital speed of $1.02\text{ km/s}$, calculate the total distance (in kilometres) traversed by the Moon in one full day ($86,400\text{ s}$).

4. At a seaport, a high tide occurs at 4:00 am. At what clock times will the next two high tides occur if the tidal interval is $12\text{ h } 25\text{ min}$? Approximately when will the intervening low tide occur (assume it falls midway)?

5. Find the value of $g$ at a point located at a distance of $3R$ from the centre of the Earth (where $R$ is Earth's radius and surface $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). What is the altitude of this point above the surface?

6. A $75\text{ kg}$ astronaut is in orbit where $g = 8.68\text{ m/s}^2$. What is the gravitational force acting on the astronaut, and what does a weighing scale read inside the spacecraft? Explain why these two values differ.

7. State whether each statement is True or False, providing a brief scientific justification:  
   (a) There is zero gravity inside an orbiting space station.  
   (b) Earth exerts no gravitational force on the Moon.  
   (c) A satellite in low-Earth orbit takes less time to complete a revolution than the Moon.  
   (d) Ocean tides are physically identical to wind-driven waves.

8. Using the concept of Newton's mountain cannon thought experiment, explain in your own words why an artificial satellite in orbit does not fall down to the ground.

9. What is the fundamental physical difference between a spring tide and a neap tide? On which lunar phases do each occur?

10. Name two practical modern applications of artificial satellites and define what is meant by a geostationary orbit.

### Answers

1. $T = \frac{24\text{ h}}{12} = \mathbf{2\text{ h}} = 2 \times 3600\text{ s} = \mathbf{7200\text{ s}}$.  
   Frequency: $f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{7200\text{ s}} \approx \mathbf{1.39 \times 10^{-4}\text{ Hz}}$.  
   *Check:* $(7200\text{ s})(12) = 86,400\text{ s} = 1\text{ day}$ ✅

2. Distance $= 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times (7.0 \times 10^6\text{ m}) = \mathbf{4.396 \times 10^7\text{ m}}$.  
   Speed $= \frac{4.396 \times 10^7\text{ m}}{5800\text{ s}} \approx 7579\text{ m/s} \approx \mathbf{7.58\text{ km/s}}$.  
   *Check:* $(7579)(5800) \approx 4.396 \times 10^7\text{ m}$ ✅

3. Distance $= \text{speed} \times \text{time} = (1.02\text{ km/s})(86,400\text{ s}) = \mathbf{88,128\text{ km}}$ (approximately $8.8 \times 10^4\text{ km}$).  
   *Check:* $\frac{88,128\text{ km}}{86,400\text{ s}} = 1.02\text{ km/s}$ ✅

4. Next high tide: $4:00\text{ am} + 12\text{ h } 25\text{ min} = \mathbf{4:25\text{ pm}}$.  
   Subsequent high tide: $4:25\text{ pm} + 12\text{ h } 25\text{ min} = \mathbf{4:50\text{ am (following day)}}$.  
   Midway low tide: $4:00\text{ am} + 6\text{ h } 12.5\text{ min} \approx \mathbf{10:12\text{ am}}$.  
   *Check:* $4:00\text{ am}$ to $4:50\text{ am}$ next day $= 24\text{ h } 50\text{ min} = 2 \times 745\text{ min}$ ✅

5. $g(3R) = \frac{g}{(3)^2} = \frac{9.8\text{ m/s}^2}{9} \approx \mathbf{1.09\text{ m/s}^2}$.  
   Altitude above surface: $h = 3R - R = 2R = 2 \times (6.4 \times 10^6\text{ m}) = \mathbf{1.28 \times 10^7\text{ m} \text{ (12,800 km)}}$.  
   *Check:* $(1.09)(9) = 9.81 \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ ✅

6. Gravitational force: $F = mg = (75\text{ kg})(8.68\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{651\text{ N}}$.  
   Scale reading: **$0\text{ N}$**.  
   *Explanation:* The true gravitational force continues to act, keeping the astronaut in orbit. However, because the astronaut and the scale are in mutual free fall around Earth, the astronaut exerts no compressive contact force on the scale.  
   *Check:* $651 / 75 = 8.68\text{ m/s}^2$ ✅

7. (a) **False** ❌. Gravity retains roughly $90\%$ of surface strength; weightlessness is caused by mutual free fall.  
   (b) **False** ❌. Earth exerts a mutual gravitational force of $\approx 2 \times 10^{20}\text{ N}$ that bends the Moon's path into an orbit.  
   (c) **True** ✅. A low-Earth satellite takes $\approx 90\text{ minutes}$, whereas the Moon takes $\approx 27.3\text{ days}$.  
   (d) **False** ❌. Waves are local wind-driven surface ripples; tides are vast ocean-wide bulges driven by lunar and solar gravity.

8. When launched horizontally at sufficiently high speed from a high summit, an object falls downward under gravity at the exact same rate that Earth's spherical surface curves away beneath it. Because the projectile constantly falls toward Earth but continuously misses the ground due to its high forward speed, it enters a perpetual free-fall circular orbit.

9. In a spring tide, the Sun, Earth, and Moon align linearly (during Full Moon and New Moon), causing their gravitational pulls to reinforce and creating maximum tidal range. In a neap tide, the Sun and Moon act at right angles to each other relative to Earth (during Quarter Moons), causing their gravitational pulls to partially cancel and producing minimum tidal range.

10. Applications include global telecommunications (satellite television, transoceanic data), meteorological forecasting (storm tracking), and global satellite navigation (GPS). A geostationary orbit is an equatorial orbit with a $24\text{ hour}$ orbital period, allowing the satellite to remain permanently positioned over one fixed spot on Earth's equator.

# Chapter Wrap-up: Gravitation

| Topic | Core Concepts Learned | Key Takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| **1. What is Gravity?** | Gravity is the mutual attractive force between all masses; Earth pulls objects toward its centre; non-contact force; legend of Newton's apple | Gravity exists between all pairs of masses, not just near Earth |
| **2. Universal Law of Gravitation** | $F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$; $G = 6.67 \times 10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$; inverse-square law: doubling distance quarters the force | Distance $r$ is always measured centre-to-centre; forces are equal and opposite (Newton's Third Law) |
| **3. Free Fall and g** | In free fall, only gravity acts; $g = \frac{GM}{R^2} \approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ on Earth; independent of falling body's mass | In a vacuum, all objects fall with the exact same acceleration |
| **4. Equations of Free Fall** | Dropped from rest: $v = gt, h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2, v^2 = 2gh$; Thrown upward: $H = \frac{u^2}{2g}$, $T = \frac{2u}{g}$ | Establish sign conventions first ($a = \pm g$); at the peak $v = 0$ while $a = -g$ |
| **5. Mass and Weight** | Mass is quantity of matter ($\text{kg}$, invariant); Weight is gravitational force ($W = mg$, measured in $\text{N}$, varies with $g$); $W_{\text{Moon}} \approx \frac{1}{6} W_{\text{Earth}}$ | Mass and weight are fundamentally different physical quantities |
| **6. Satellites and Tides** | The Moon falls continuously while missing Earth (Newton's Cannon); orbital centripetal force; free-fall weightlessness; ocean tides caused by Moon's gravity; spring and neap tides | Gravity remains active in orbit; tidal forces arise from differential gravitational attraction |

### Key Definitions and Units

| Quantity / Concept | Physical Meaning | SI Unit |
|---|---|---|
| **Gravity** | The mutual attractive force acting between any two masses; Earth pulls objects toward its centre | newton ($\text{N}$) |
| **G (Universal Gravitational Constant)** | The universal proportionality constant in Newton's Law of Gravitation: $6.67 \times 10^{-11}$ | $\text{N m}^2/\text{kg}^2$ |
| **g (Acceleration due to gravity)** | The acceleration experienced by an object in free fall ($\approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ on Earth) | $\text{m/s}^2$ or $\text{N/kg}$ |
| **Free fall** | Motion occurring solely under the influence of gravitational force | (state of motion) |
| **Mass (m)** | The amount of matter contained in an object; measure of inertia | kilogram ($\text{kg}$) |
| **Weight (W)** | The gravitational force exerted on an object by a celestial body ($W = mg$) | newton ($\text{N}$) |
| **Satellite** | A celestial or artificial body orbiting a more massive primary body | (physical entity) |
| **Tide** | The rhythmic, periodic rise and fall of ocean surface levels driven primarily by lunar gravity | (physical phenomenon) |

### Summary of Core Formulas

| Physical Relation | Formula |
|---|---|
| **Newton's Universal Law of Gravitation** | $F = G \frac{m_1 m_2}{r^2}$ |
| **Acceleration Due to Gravity** | $g = \frac{GM}{R^2}$ |
| **Weight** | $W = mg$ |
| **Free Fall Speed from Rest** | $v = gt$ |
| **Free Fall Distance from Rest** | $h = \frac{1}{2}gt^2$ |
| **Free Fall Velocity-Distance Relation** | $v^2 = 2gh$ |

### Mass vs Weight at a Glance

| Property | Mass | Weight |
|---|---|---|
| **Definition** | Measure of the quantity of matter | Gravitational force on that matter |
| **SI Unit** | kilogram ($\text{kg}$) | newton ($\text{N}$) |
| **Variation with Location** | Invariant (constant everywhere) | Varies with local gravity $g$ (approx. $1/6$ on Moon) |
