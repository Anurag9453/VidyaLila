# Topic 5: Change of State and Latent Heat

In all the preceding topics, whenever thermal energy entered or left a substance, its **temperature changed**: warming water raised its temperature, cooling an iron block lowered its temperature, and in calorimetry, thermal exchange continued until both bodies converged at an intermediate temperature. In every case, the governing formula was $Q = m c \Delta T$.

Does supplying heat energy **always** cause a rise in temperature? We now investigate a fascinating physical scenario where heat energy pours continuously into an object, yet a thermometer embedded inside refuses to move by even a fraction of a degree.

Throughout this topic, we use standard empirical values:
$$c_{\text{water}} = 4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)}, \quad c_{\text{ice}} = 2100\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)}, \quad c_{\text{steam}} \approx 2000\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)}$$
$$L_f (\text{specific latent heat of fusion of ice}) = 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg} = 336\text{ kJ/kg}$$
$$L_v (\text{specific latent heat of vaporisation of water}) = 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg} = 2260\text{ kJ/kg}$$

## The Giant Block of Ice in the Sunlight

At a commercial ice production facility, the facility manager, **Mr. Vance**, was supervising morning logistics. Outside on the loading dock under direct, intense morning sunlight stood a solid **$50\text{-kg}$ block of ice** awaiting transport. Visiting the facility for a school physics assignment was Class 9 student **Maya**. Mr. Vance had inserted a dial laboratory thermometer deep into a drilled borehole in the center of the ice block.

The morning sun beat down relentlessly. Rivulets of meltwater poured down the sides of the block, puddling on the concrete. The sharp edges softened, and the slab was visibly shrinking in size. Maya checked the thermometer: it read **$0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**.

Ten minutes later, with water streaming steadily, she checked again: **$0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**.
Half an hour later, with nearly a third of the block converted into liquid water, she checked once more: stubbornly, the needle remained locked at **$0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**!

Maya turned to Mr. Vance in disbelief. "Mr. Vance, intense radiant heat from the sun has been pouring into this ice block for over thirty minutes! Why hasn't its temperature risen even slightly? **Where is all that absorbed solar energy going?**"

Mr. Vance smiled. "That question puzzled the greatest natural philosophers for centuries, Maya. The energy is not disappearing; it is performing intense microscopic work beneath the surface. It is becoming **latent heat**—hidden thermal energy. Let us examine what happens to the atoms during a phase change." We will calculate the energy budget of Mr. Vance's $50\text{-kg}$ ice block in Part F.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **State of matter (phase)** | The distinct physical form taken by matter: solid, liquid, or gas | Ice, liquid water, and steam |
| **Melting (fusion)** | The phase transition from solid to liquid upon absorbing latent heat | Ice melting into liquid water at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ |
| **Freezing (solidification)** | The phase transition from liquid to solid upon releasing latent heat | Water solidifying into ice in a freezer |
| **Vaporisation** | The phase transition from liquid to gas (via boiling or surface evaporation) | Water boiling into steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ |
| **Condensation** | The phase transition from gas to liquid upon releasing latent heat | Droplets forming on the cold exterior of a chilled drinking glass |
| **Sublimation** | Direct phase change from solid to gas without entering an intermediate liquid state | Dry ice ($\text{CO}_2$), mothballs (naphthalene), and camphor |
| **Melting point** | The constant temperature at which a pure crystalline solid melts at standard atmospheric pressure | $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ for pure water ice |
| **Boiling point** | The constant temperature at which a liquid boils vigorously throughout its volume | $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ for pure water at standard pressure |
| **Latent heat** | Heat absorbed or released during an isothermal phase transition without any change in temperature | Heat absorbed by melting ice |
| **Specific latent heat of fusion ($L_f$)** | Heat required to convert $1\text{ kg}$ of a solid into liquid at its melting point without temperature change | For ice: $L_f = 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}$ |
| **Specific latent heat of vaporisation ($L_v$)** | Heat required to convert $1\text{ kg}$ of a liquid into vapor at its boiling point without temperature change | For water: $L_v = 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$ |
| **Evaporation** | Spontaneous conversion of liquid into vapor occurring exclusively at the exposed surface at all temperatures | Cooling of skin as perspiration evaporates |

## Part A: The Three States of Matter

All matter exists primarily in three physical states: **solid**, **liquid**, and **gas**. A single chemical substance—such as water ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$)—can adopt all three forms: ice (solid), water (liquid), and water vapor / steam (gas). 

The differences between these states arise from how their **constituent particles** (atoms or molecules) are arranged and the strength of the intermolecular forces holding them together:

- **Solid:** Particles are closely packed in a rigid, fixed geometric lattice. They cannot migrate from their fixed sites; they only vibrate back and forth about mean equilibrium positions. Consequently, a solid maintains a definite shape and a definite volume.
- **Liquid:** Particles remain closely packed, but possess enough thermal kinetic energy to break out of rigid lattice positions. They slide and roll past one another freely. Thus, a liquid has a definite volume, but flows to conform to the shape of its container.
- **Gas:** Particles possess substantial kinetic energy and are separated by enormous distances compared to their own sizes. Intermolecular attractive forces are negligible. Particles fly about in rapid, random, chaotic straight-line motion, colliding with one another and the container walls. A gas has neither a fixed shape nor a fixed volume, expanding to fill any vessel.

```
      SOLID                    LIQUID                     GAS

    o o o o o                o   o    o                 o          o
    o o o o o                 o    o   o                     o
    o o o o o                o   o   o  o             o             o
    o o o o o                 o   o    o                   o   o
                                                        o        o
   Fixed positions,         Closely packed,          Vast separations,
   vibrate in place.        slide past each other.   free random motion.
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Microscopic particle models of solids, liquids, and gases showing packing density and particle freedom.

Crucially, strong **intermolecular attractive forces** bind particles together. These cohesive forces are strongest in solids, moderate in liquids, and negligible in gases. Overcoming these cohesive bonds is the central mechanism of latent heat.

## Part B: Phase Transitions: How Matter Changes State

By adding or extracting heat energy, matter can transition between states. Each transition has a designated scientific term:

| Phase Transition | Name of Process | Thermal Energy Flow |
|---|---|---|
| **Solid $\rightarrow$ Liquid** | **Melting (Fusion)** | Heat is **absorbed** by the substance |
| **Liquid $\rightarrow$ Solid** | **Freezing (Solidification)** | Heat is **released** by the substance |
| **Liquid $\rightarrow$ Gas** | **Vaporisation** (Boiling or Evaporation) | Heat is **absorbed** by the substance |
| **Gas $\rightarrow$ Liquid** | **Condensation** | Heat is **released** by the substance |
| **Solid $\rightarrow$ Gas** | **Sublimation** | Heat is **absorbed** by the substance |
| **Gas $\rightarrow$ Solid** | **Deposition** (Reverse Sublimation) | Heat is **released** by the substance |

**Sublimation in Everyday Life:** Certain solids pass directly into the gaseous phase without melting into a liquid. Examples include **dry ice** (solid carbon dioxide at $-78.5\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), **camphor**, and **naphthalene balls** (used to protect wool clothing in closets, gradually shrinking and disappearing over weeks without leaving damp spots).

```
                      Sublimation (Heat Absorbed)
         ┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
         │                                                             ▼
       SOLID ───── Melting ────▶  LIQUID  ──── Vaporisation ────▶     GAS
       (Ice) ◀──── Freezing ────  (Water) ◀─── Condensation ────    (Steam)
         ▲                                                             │
         └─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                       Deposition (Heat Released)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Phase transition cycle: Forward transitions (solid to liquid to gas) absorb latent heat; reverse transitions release latent heat.

### Melting Point and Boiling Point

Every pure crystalline substance has a characteristic **melting point** at which solid and liquid coexist in equilibrium at standard atmospheric pressure ($0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ for pure ice). 

Similarly, every pure liquid has a characteristic **boiling point** at which its vapor pressure equals standard atmospheric pressure ($100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ for pure water). 

For a pure substance, the freezing point is identical to its melting point: water freezes into ice at the exact same temperature ($0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) at which ice melts into water.

## Part C: Latent Heat: The "Hidden" Energy

Let us return to Maya's question: **where did all the solar energy go as the ice block melted at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$?**

Temperature is a macroscopic measure of the **average translational kinetic energy** of constituent particles. When heat enters ice below $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, particle vibrations intensify, and the temperature rises.

However, once the ice reaches its melting point ($0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), the absorbed thermal energy is directed toward a different physical task: **breaking the rigid intermolecular hydrogen bonds holding the crystal lattice together**. 

The energy does not increase particle speeds (kinetic energy); instead, it increases the **potential energy** of the molecules as they separate from their locked crystalline positions to become mobile liquid particles. Because particle kinetic energy does not increase, **the thermometer registers no change in temperature whatsoever**!

> **During a phase change, the temperature remains strictly constant until the entire mass has completely changed state, regardless of how much heat is supplied.**

Because this absorbed heat energy does not manifest as a temperature rise on a thermometer, eighteenth-century scientists named it **latent heat** (from the Latin *latere*, meaning "to lie hidden"). 

The energy is not destroyed; it is stored as intermolecular potential energy in the liquid water. When that water freezes back into ice, this exact quantity of stored latent heat is **released back into the surroundings**, still occurring at a constant $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

The same principle applies during boiling: at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, heat supplied to water does not raise its temperature; rather, it completely tears the molecules away from liquid cohesive bonds into widely separated gas particles.

**Essential Distinction:**
- Use **$Q = m c \Delta T$** when temperature changes **within a single phase** (solid, liquid, or gas).
- Use **$Q = m L$** during a **phase transition** at constant temperature ($\Delta T = 0$).

## Part D: Specific Latent Heat and the Formula $Q = m L$

Different substances require different amounts of energy to change state, and larger masses require proportionally more heat. To standardize this property per unit mass:

- **Specific Latent Heat of Fusion ($L_f$):** The quantity of heat energy required to convert **$1\text{ kg}$** of a substance from solid to liquid at its melting point without any change in temperature.
  For water ice: **$L_f = 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg} = 336\text{ kJ/kg}$**.
- **Specific Latent Heat of Vaporisation ($L_v$):** The quantity of heat energy required to convert **$1\text{ kg}$** of a substance from liquid to gas at its boiling point without any change in temperature.
  For water: **$L_v = 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg} = 2260\text{ kJ/kg}$**.

The SI unit of specific latent heat is **joule per kilogram ($\text{J/kg}$)**.

To calculate the heat absorbed or released when a mass $m$ undergoes a phase transition:

$$\mathbf{Q = m \times L}$$

Where:
- $Q$ = Latent heat absorbed or released (in joules, $\text{J}$)
- $m$ = Mass of substance undergoing phase change (in kilograms, $\text{kg}$)
- $L$ = Specific latent heat ($L_f$ for melting/freezing; $L_v$ for boiling/condensing, in $\text{J/kg}$)

*Notice that $\Delta T$ is absent from this formula, reflecting the isothermal nature of phase changes.*

**Why is $L_v$ so much larger than $L_f$?**
$$\frac{L_v}{L_f} = \frac{2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}}{3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}} \approx \mathbf{6.7}$$
Converting liquid water into steam requires nearly **seven times more energy** than melting an equal mass of ice! In melting, molecules only need their rigid bonds loosened so they can slide past one another while remaining in close contact. In boiling, molecules must be completely torn apart against strong attractive forces and separated by immense distances into the gaseous state.

### Comparison with Traditional Calorie Units

In historical and culinary contexts, latent heats were expressed in $\text{cal/g}$:
- $L_f(\text{ice}) = \mathbf{80\text{ cal/g}}$
- $L_v(\text{water}) = \mathbf{540\text{ cal/g}}$

Converting using $1\text{ cal} \approx 4.2\text{ J}$ and $1\text{ kg} = 1000\text{ g}$:
$$80\text{ cal/g} \times 4.2\text{ J/cal} = 336\text{ J/g} = \mathbf{336{,}000\text{ J/kg}} = 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}$$
$$540\text{ cal/g} \times 4.2\text{ J/cal} = 2268\text{ J/g} \approx \mathbf{2{,}260{,}000\text{ J/kg}} = 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$$

Both unit systems agree perfectly.

## Part E: The Journey from Ice to Steam: The Heating Curve

Consider taking $1\text{ kg}$ of pure ice at $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and supplying heat energy continuously at a steady rate until it transforms into superheated steam at $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. 

Tracking temperature against total heat supplied generates the classic five-segment **heating curve**:

```
 Temperature (°C)
  110 ┤                                                 ╱  Segment E: Heating steam
      │                                               ╱
  100 ┤                               ╱───────────────   Segment D: Boiling (latent heat Lv)
      │                             ╱
      │                           ╱   Segment C: Heating liquid water
      │                         ╱
    0 ┤             ╱───────────      Segment B: Melting ice (latent heat Lf)
      │           ╱
  -10 ┤         ╱   Segment A: Warming cold ice
      │
      └──────────────────────────────────────────────────────▶ Heat Supplied Q (J)
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Heating curve for $1\text{ kg}$ of water from $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ (not to linear scale). Sloped segments A, C, and E represent temperature changes ($Q = m c \Delta T$). Horizontal plateaus B ($0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and D ($100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) represent phase transitions ($Q = m L$).

Let us calculate the heat budget for each of the five segments for $m = 1\text{ kg}$:

| Segment | Physical Process | Formula Applied | Heat Energy Required ($1\text{ kg}$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Warming ice from $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $Q_1 = m c_{\text{ice}} \Delta T$ | $1 \times 2100 \times 10 = \mathbf{21{,}000\text{ J}}$ |
| **B** | Melting ice to water at constant $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $Q_2 = m L_f$ | $1 \times 336{,}000 = \mathbf{336{,}000\text{ J}}$ |
| **C** | Heating water from $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $Q_3 = m c_{\text{water}} \Delta T$ | $1 \times 4200 \times 100 = \mathbf{420{,}000\text{ J}}$ |
| **D** | Vaporising water into steam at constant $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $Q_4 = m L_v$ | $1 \times 2{,}260{,}000 = \mathbf{2{,}260{,}000\text{ J}}$ |
| **E** | Heating steam from $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $Q_5 = m c_{\text{steam}} \Delta T$ | $1 \times 2000 \times 10 = \mathbf{20{,}000\text{ J}}$ |

**Total Heat Energy Supplied:**
$$Q_{\text{total}} = 21{,}000 + 336{,}000 + 420{,}000 + 2{,}260{,}000 + 20{,}000 = \mathbf{3{,}057{,}000\text{ J}} \approx \mathbf{3.06 \times 10^6\text{ J}}$$

Cumulative thermal progression:

| Cumulative Heat Added | Temperature | Physical State of Matter |
|---|---|---|
| $0\text{ J}$ | $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | Solid ice |
| $21{,}000\text{ J}$ | $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | Ice at the threshold of melting |
| $357{,}000\text{ J}$ | $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | Fully melted into liquid water at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ |
| $777{,}000\text{ J}$ | $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | Liquid water at the threshold of boiling |
| $3{,}037{,}000\text{ J}$ | $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | Fully vaporised into steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ |
| $3{,}057{,}000\text{ J}$ | $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | Superheated steam at $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ |

*Key Insight:* Segment D (vaporisation) alone accounts for nearly **$74\%$ of the total energy** ($2{,}260{,}000 \div 3{,}057{,}000 \approx 0.74$). The horizontal plateaus at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ are graphical proof that temperature does not change during phase transitions.

## Part F: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Melting Pure Ice):** Calculate the thermal energy needed to melt $2\text{ kg}$ of ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ into water at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

- Phase change at constant temperature: $Q = m L_f$
- $Q = 2\text{ kg} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg} = \mathbf{672{,}000\text{ J}}$ ($672\text{ kJ}$)

---

**Example 2 (Vaporising Boiling Water):** Calculate the heat required to convert $0.5\text{ kg}$ of water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ into steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

- $Q = m L_v = 0.5\text{ kg} \times 2{,}260{,}000\text{ J/kg} = \mathbf{1{,}130{,}000\text{ J}}$ ($1.13 \times 10^6\text{ J}$)

---

**Example 3 (Multi-Stage Process: Sub-Zero Ice to Warm Water):** Calculate the total heat energy required to transform $200\text{ g}$ of ice at $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ into warm water at $20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

Convert mass to kilograms: $m = 200\text{ g} = 0.2\text{ kg}$. This process consists of three consecutive stages:
1. **Warming ice from $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:**
   $$Q_1 = m c_{\text{ice}} \Delta T = (0.2\text{ kg}) \times (2100\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)}) \times (10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}) = \mathbf{4200\text{ J}}$$
2. **Melting ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:**
   $$Q_2 = m L_f = (0.2\text{ kg}) \times (336{,}000\text{ J/kg}) = \mathbf{67{,}200\text{ J}}$$
3. **Heating water from $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:**
   $$Q_3 = m c_{\text{water}} \Delta T = (0.2\text{ kg}) \times (4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)}) \times (20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}) = \mathbf{16{,}800\text{ J}}$$

**Total Heat Required:**
$$Q_{\text{total}} = Q_1 + Q_2 + Q_3 = 4200 + 67{,}200 + 16{,}800 = \mathbf{88{,}200\text{ J}}$$

*Notice:* The latent heat of melting ($67{,}200\text{ J}$) constitutes over $76\%$ of the entire energy budget!

---

**Example 4 (Heat Released During Condensation):** How much thermal energy is released to the surroundings when $100\text{ g}$ of steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ condenses into liquid water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$?

- $m = 100\text{ g} = 0.1\text{ kg}$
- Condensation releases the exact latent heat of vaporisation absorbed during boiling:
  $$Q = m L_v = 0.1\text{ kg} \times 2{,}260{,}000\text{ J/kg} = \mathbf{226{,}000\text{ J}} \quad (\text{released})$$

---

**Example 5 (Cooling Steam Down to Room Temperature Water):** Calculate the total heat energy released when $1\text{ kg}$ of steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ condenses and cools to water at $20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

1. **Condensation at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:**
   $$Q_1 = m L_v = 1\text{ kg} \times 2{,}260{,}000\text{ J/kg} = 2{,}260{,}000\text{ J}$$
2. **Cooling water from $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T = 80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$):**
   $$Q_2 = m c_{\text{water}} \Delta T = 1\text{ kg} \times 4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)} \times 80\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 336{,}000\text{ J}$$

**Total Heat Released:**
$$Q_{\text{total}} = 2{,}260{,}000 + 336{,}000 = \mathbf{2{,}596{,}000\text{ J}} \approx \mathbf{2.60 \times 10^6\text{ J}}$$

---

**Example 6 (Determining Mass of Melted Ice from Heat Input):** An electric heater delivers $100{,}800\text{ J}$ of thermal energy to a container of ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Assuming all energy is absorbed by the ice, what mass of ice melts?

- $Q = m L_f \implies m = \frac{Q}{L_f}$
- $m = \frac{100{,}800\text{ J}}{336{,}000\text{ J/kg}} = \mathbf{0.3\text{ kg}}$ ($300\text{ g}$)

---

**Example 7 (Calorimetry with Ice: Cooling Warm Water):** A glass contains $0.5\text{ kg}$ of water at $30\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. What mass of ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ must be added to chill the entire water volume down to $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$? (Assume all ice melts and no heat is lost to the environment).

$$\text{Heat Lost by Warm Water} = \text{Heat Absorbed by Melting Ice}$$
- Heat lost by water cooling from $30\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:
  $$Q_{\text{lost}} = m_{\text{water}} c_{\text{water}} \Delta T = (0.5\text{ kg}) \times (4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)}) \times (30\text{ }^\circ\text{C}) = \mathbf{63{,}000\text{ J}}$$
- Heat absorbed by ice melting at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:
  $$Q_{\text{absorbed}} = m_{\text{ice}} L_f = m_{\text{ice}} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg}$$
- Equating the two:
  $$m_{\text{ice}} \times 336{,}000 = 63{,}000$$
  $$m_{\text{ice}} = \frac{63{,}000}{336{,}000} = \mathbf{0.1875\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{187.5\text{ g}}$$

---

**Example 8 (Operating Time of an Immersion Heater):** A $2000\text{-W}$ electric heater is used to melt $2\text{ kg}$ of ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Assuming $100\%$ efficiency, how long will the melting process take?

- Total heat required: $Q = m L_f = 2\text{ kg} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg} = 672{,}000\text{ J}$
- Power $P = 2000\text{ W} = 2000\text{ J/s}$
- Time $t = \frac{Q}{P} = \frac{672{,}000\text{ J}}{2000\text{ J/s}} = \mathbf{336\text{ seconds}} = \frac{336}{60}\text{ min} = \mathbf{5.6\text{ minutes}}$

---

**Example 9 (Experimental Determination of $L_f$):** In an experiment, supplying $33{,}600\text{ J}$ of heat melts exactly $0.1\text{ kg}$ of ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Determine the experimental specific latent heat of fusion.

$$L_f = \frac{Q}{m} = \frac{33{,}600\text{ J}}{0.1\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{336{,}000\text{ J/kg}} = \mathbf{3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}}$$

---

**Example 10 (Cooling Power of Evaporating Sweat):** On a hot summer day, a student's body perspires and evaporates $10\text{ g}$ of sweat. How much thermal energy is extracted from the body? (Take $L_v \approx 2260\text{ J/g}$).

- $m = 10\text{ g}$
- $Q = m L_v = 10\text{ g} \times 2260\text{ J/g} = \mathbf{22{,}600\text{ J}}$ ($22.6\text{ kJ}$)
- This extracted energy is enough to raise the temperature of $1\text{ kg}$ of water by over $5.4\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$! This demonstrates why perspiration is such an exceptionally effective biological cooling mechanism.

---

**Example 11 (Quantitative Proof: Steam Burns vs. Boiling Water Burns):** Compare the energy released to human skin by $1\text{ g}$ of boiling water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ cooling to $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ with that released by $1\text{ g}$ of steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ merely condensing into water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. ($c_{\text{water}} = 4.2\text{ J/(g }^\circ\text{C)}$, $L_v = 2260\text{ J/g}$).

- $1\text{ g}$ of **boiling water** ($100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} \rightarrow 0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$):
  $$Q = m c \Delta T = 1\text{ g} \times 4.2\text{ J/(g }^\circ\text{C)} \times 100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = \mathbf{420\text{ J}}$$
- $1\text{ g}$ of **steam** at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ merely condensing at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:
  $$Q = m L_v = 1\text{ g} \times 2260\text{ J/g} = \mathbf{2260\text{ J}}$$
- Ratio: $\frac{2260\text{ J}}{420\text{ J}} \approx \mathbf{5.4}$

Merely condensing into liquid delivers over **5.4 times more heat energy** than boiling water cooling through a full $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ span! After condensing, the boiling hot liquid then continues to scald the tissue as it cools, releasing an additional $420\text{ J}$.

---

**Example 12 (Resolving the $50\text{-kg}$ Ice Block Puzzle):** Calculate the total solar heat absorbed by Mr. Vance's $50\text{-kg}$ ice block to melt completely at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. If sunlight delivers heat at a net rate of $5000\text{ W}$ ($5000\text{ J/s}$), how long does total melting take?

- (a) $Q = m L_f = 50\text{ kg} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg} = \mathbf{16{,}800{,}000\text{ J}} = \mathbf{1.68 \times 10^7\text{ J}}$
- (b) $t = \frac{Q}{P} = \frac{16{,}800{,}000\text{ J}}{5000\text{ J/s}} = \mathbf{3360\text{ seconds}} = \frac{3360}{60}\text{ min} = \mathbf{56\text{ minutes}}$

*Conclusion:* Over $16.8\text{ million joules}$ of solar energy entered the ice block, yet the thermometer registered $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ throughout those 56 minutes because every joule was consumed in breaking the crystal lattice.

## Part G: Real-World Manifestations of Latent Heat

### 1. Why Steam Burns Are Far More Severe Than Boiling Water Burns
Steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and liquid water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ possess the identical temperature. However, every kilogram of steam contains an extra **$2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J}$ of latent heat of vaporisation**. When steam contacts skin, it condenses instantly, transferring this vast latent heat directly into epidermal tissue, resulting in severe, deep-tissue thermal burns.

### 2. Why Ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ Cools Drinks Vastly Better Than Water at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$
Suppose you add $100\text{ g}$ ($0.1\text{ kg}$) of liquid water at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ versus $100\text{ g}$ of solid ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to a warm beverage, allowing both to warm up to $10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:
- The $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ liquid water absorbs:
  $$Q_{\text{water}} = 0.1\text{ kg} \times 4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)} \times 10\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = \mathbf{4200\text{ J}}$$
- The $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ice must first melt ($Q = m L_f$) before warming:
  $$Q_{\text{ice}} = (0.1 \times 336{,}000) + (0.1 \times 4200 \times 10) = 33{,}600 + 4200 = \mathbf{37{,}800\text{ J}}$$
- Ratio: $\frac{37{,}800}{4200} = \mathbf{9}$

The ice extracts **nine times more heat energy** from the beverage than an equal mass of ice-cold water!

### 3. Evaporation vs. Boiling: Key Differences

| Feature | Evaporation | Boiling |
|---|---|---|
| **Temperature** | Occurs spontaneously at **all temperatures** | Occurs only at one **fixed temperature** (boiling point) |
| **Location** | Confined strictly to the **exposed liquid surface** | Occurs **throughout the entire bulk volume** (bubbles form) |
| **Speed** | Slow, silent, gradual process | Rapid, vigorous, turbulent process |
| **Thermal Effect** | Causes noticeable **cooling** of remaining liquid | Temperature remains **strictly constant** during heating |
| **Examples** | Drying laundry, perspiration evaporating | Water boiling furiously in a kettle on a stove |

**Cooling Caused by Evaporation:**
Molecules in a liquid possess a distribution of kinetic energies. Energetic molecules at the surface overcome surface tension and escape into the air. Because the most energetic particles depart, the **average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid drops**, cooling the liquid:
- **Perspiration:** Sweating regulates body temperature as evaporating moisture extracts latent heat from skin.
- **Damp Cloth on a Feverish Forehead:** Evaporation draws heat from the patient's head, lowering core discomfort.
- **Earthen Pitchers (Matkas):** Microscopic pores in unglazed clay seep tiny amounts of water to the outer wall. Evaporating water extracts latent heat from the pot, chilling the drinking water inside.

**Factors Governing the Rate of Evaporation:**
1. **Surface Area:** Larger surface areas allow more surface molecules to escape (water in a wide tray evaporates faster than in a cup).
2. **Temperature:** Warmer liquids give more molecules sufficient energy to break free.
3. **Humidity:** High ambient air humidity slows evaporation (laundry dries very slowly on humid, rainy days).
4. **Air Flow (Wind):** Breezes blow away accumulated vapor molecules above the surface, accelerating evaporation.

### 4. Effect of Pressure on Boiling Point
The boiling point of a liquid is the temperature at which its saturated vapor pressure equals external atmospheric pressure:
- **Pressure Cookers:** Sealing steam raises internal pressure to roughly twice atmospheric pressure ($\approx 2\text{ atm}$), elevating water's boiling point to approximately **$120\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**. Cooking in $120\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ water cooks lentils, grains, and meats in a fraction of the normal time.
- **High Altitude Cooking:** In mountainous regions, atmospheric pressure is substantially lower. Water boils at $90\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ or lower. Because boiling water cannot exceed this reduced temperature, cooking food takes much longer.

### 5. Impurities and Depression of Freezing Point
Dissolving non-volatile solutes (such as common salt, $\text{NaCl}$) in pure water **depresses its freezing point below $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**:
- **Salting Icy Roads:** In sub-zero winters, highway crews scatter rock salt on frozen roads. The salt lowers the freezing point of water to $-5\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ or lower, causing the ice sheet to melt spontaneously.
- **Freezing Mixtures:** Traditional ice-cream makers surround cream canisters with ice mixed with coarse salt, creating a chilling brine that drops below $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, rapidly freezing the dessert.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing temperature rises while ice melts**
   *Correction:* During a phase change, temperature remains strictly clamped at the melting or boiling point until the entire mass changes state.

2. **Mistake 2: Forgetting the latent heat step in multi-stage heating problems**
   *Correction:* When warming ice from $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $+20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, calculating only the $m c \Delta T$ steps misses the largest component ($m L_f$). Always break the problem into distinct segments: warm solid $\rightarrow$ melt solid $\rightarrow$ warm liquid.

3. **Mistake 3: Using $Q = m c \Delta T$ during a phase transition**
   *Correction:* Because $\Delta T = 0$ during a phase change, substituting into $m c \Delta T$ erroneously yields $0\text{ J}$. Always use $\mathbf{Q = m L}$ for phase changes.

4. **Mistake 4: Swapping $L_f$ and $L_v$**
   *Correction:* Use $L_f = 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}$ strictly for melting and freezing; use $L_v = 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$ strictly for vaporisation and condensation.

5. **Mistake 5: Failing to convert grams to kilograms**
   *Correction:* Since specific latent heat is in $\text{J/kg}$, a mass of $200\text{ g}$ must be expressed as $0.2\text{ kg}$. Multiplying $200$ directly by $336{,}000$ overstates the answer by a factor of 1000!

6. **Mistake 6: Treating evaporation and boiling as identical phenomena**
   *Correction:* Evaporation occurs quietly at all temperatures exclusively from the surface, cooling the liquid. Boiling occurs turbulently throughout the liquid volume only at the fixed boiling point.

## Quick Recap

- Matter exists in three states: solid (rigid lattice), liquid (close, sliding particles), and gas (widely separated, free particles).
- Phase changes: Melting / Fusion (solid $\rightarrow$ liquid), Freezing (liquid $\rightarrow$ solid), Vaporisation (liquid $\rightarrow$ gas), Condensation (gas $\rightarrow$ liquid), Sublimation (solid $\rightarrow$ gas).
- During phase transitions, **temperature remains strictly constant**. Absorbed heat energy breaks intermolecular cohesive bonds and is stored as **latent heat**.
- Master formula for phase changes:
  $$\mathbf{Q = m \times L}$$
  Where $L_f = 3.36 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}$ ($336\text{ kJ/kg}$) and $L_v = 2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$ ($2260\text{ kJ/kg}$). Traditional values: $80\text{ cal/g}$ and $540\text{ cal/g}$.
- On a heating curve, horizontal plateaus represent phase changes where $Q = m L$, while sloped lines represent single-phase warming where $Q = m c \Delta T$.
- Steam burns at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ are far more severe than boiling water burns because steam releases an extra $2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$ upon condensing.
- Ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ cools drinks 9 times more effectively than water at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ because it absorbs latent heat ($L_f$) while melting.
- Evaporation occurs at any temperature from the liquid surface, causing cooling. Factors increasing rate: higher surface area, higher temperature, lower humidity, higher wind speed.
- Increased pressure raises the boiling point (pressure cookers); reduced pressure lowers the boiling point (high altitude). Solutes depress the freezing point (salting icy roads).

9. What mass of ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ is required to cool $1\text{ kg}$ of water from $20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ down to $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$? Assume zero heat loss to surroundings.

10. State whether the following statements are True or False, correcting the false ones:
    (a) The temperature of melting ice increases steadily as heat is absorbed from the surroundings.
    (b) Evaporation occurs strictly at the boiling point of a liquid.
    (c) Steam at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ produces more destructive burns than boiling water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ because it releases latent heat of vaporisation upon condensing.
    (d) During an isothermal phase transition, the heat absorbed is calculated using $Q = m c \Delta T$.

---

### Answers

1. (a) **Melting (Fusion)**.
   (b) **Condensation**.
   (c) **Sublimation**.
   (d) **Freezing (Solidification)**.

2. $Q = m L_f = 3\text{ kg} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg} = \mathbf{1{,}008{,}000\text{ J}} = \mathbf{1.008 \times 10^6\text{ J}}$ ($1008\text{ kJ}$).

3. $Q = m L_v = 0.4\text{ kg} \times 2{,}260{,}000\text{ J/kg} = \mathbf{904{,}000\text{ J}}$ ($904\text{ kJ}$).

4. $m = 500\text{ g} = 0.5\text{ kg}$.
   - Melting ice at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $Q_1 = 0.5\text{ kg} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg} = 168{,}000\text{ J}$.
   - Warming water from $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $30\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $Q_2 = 0.5\text{ kg} \times 4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)} \times 30\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 63{,}000\text{ J}$.
   - Total $Q = 168{,}000 + 63{,}000 = \mathbf{231{,}000\text{ J}}$ ($231\text{ kJ}$).

5. - Condensation at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $Q_1 = 0.2\text{ kg} \times 2{,}260{,}000\text{ J/kg} = 452{,}000\text{ J}$.
   - Cooling water from $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T = 50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$): $Q_2 = 0.2\text{ kg} \times 4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)} \times 50\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 42{,}000\text{ J}$.
   - Total heat released $= 452{,}000 + 42{,}000 = \mathbf{494{,}000\text{ J}}$ ($494\text{ kJ}$).

6. $m = \frac{Q}{L_f} = \frac{168{,}000\text{ J}}{336{,}000\text{ J/kg}} = \mathbf{0.5\text{ kg}}$ ($500\text{ g}$).

7. Heat required: $Q = 0.25\text{ kg} \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg} = 84{,}000\text{ J}$.
   Time $t = \frac{Q}{P} = \frac{84{,}000\text{ J}}{500\text{ J/s}} = \mathbf{168\text{ seconds}} = \mathbf{2.8\text{ minutes}}$.

8. $L_f = \frac{Q}{m} = \frac{15{,}000\text{ J}}{0.05\text{ kg}} = \mathbf{300{,}000\text{ J/kg}} = \mathbf{3.0 \times 10^5\text{ J/kg}}$.

9. Heat lost by water:
   $$Q_{\text{lost}} = 1\text{ kg} \times 4200\text{ J/(kg }^\circ\text{C)} \times 20\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 84{,}000\text{ J}$$
   Heat absorbed by melting ice:
   $$Q_{\text{absorbed}} = m \times 336{,}000\text{ J/kg}$$
   Equating: $m = \frac{84{,}000}{336{,}000} = \mathbf{0.25\text{ kg}}$ ($250\text{ g}$).

10. (a) **False**. The temperature of pure melting ice remains strictly clamped at $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ until melting is complete.
    (b) **False**. Evaporation occurs spontaneously at all temperatures from the liquid surface; boiling occurs only at the fixed boiling point.
    (c) **True**. Steam releases its enormous latent heat of vaporisation ($2.26 \times 10^6\text{ J/kg}$) upon condensing on skin.
    (d) **False**. During a phase transition, temperature does not change ($\Delta T = 0$); the heat absorbed or released is calculated using $\mathbf{Q = m L}$.
