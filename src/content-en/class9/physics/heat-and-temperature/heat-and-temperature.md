# Topic 1: Heat and Temperature: The Difference and the Thermometer

At the conclusion of our study on machines, we noted that work done against friction turns into **"heat"**: the screw of a mechanical jack feels warm after lifting a load, and bicycle chains and automobile engines heat up during operation. In our earlier study of energy, **heat** was already listed among its diverse forms—alongside kinetic, potential, sound, and light energy. Therefore, **heat is a form of energy**. In this chapter, we will investigate heat systematically: what it is, how we measure it, how it flows from one body to another, and the dramatic effects it produces on matter.

We begin by drawing a sharp distinction between two terms that everyday language frequently blurs: **heat** and **temperature**. We then examine the primary instrument designed to measure temperature: the **thermometer**.

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Heat** | A form of energy that transfers between bodies solely due to a temperature difference | Thermal energy transferring from hot soup to a metal spoon |

## Part A: What is Heat and What is Temperature?

### Heat: Energy in Transit

**Heat** is defined as **energy in transit**. It is the energy that spontaneously transfers from an object at a higher temperature to an object at a lower temperature across their boundary.

- Because heat is a form of energy, its **SI unit is the joule (J)**, identical to the unit of mechanical work and kinetic energy.
- An older unit historically used in calorimetry and still widely cited in nutrition is the **calorie (cal)**. By definition:
  $$1\text{ cal} \approx 4.186\text{ J} \approx 4.2\text{ J}$$
- **1 kilocalorie (kcal)** $= 1000\text{ cal} \approx 4200\text{ J}$. The "Calories" printed on food nutrition labels are actually dietary Calories, which correspond to kilocalories.

An important physical distinction: in rigorous physics, "heat" refers strictly to energy while it is **flowing** from one system to another. Once absorbed, it becomes part of the internal thermal energy (the kinetic and potential energies of the constituent atoms and molecules). A body does not "contain heat"; rather, it absorbs or releases heat, altering its internal energy.

### Temperature: How Hot or How Cold

**Temperature** is a physical quantity that measures the **degree of hotness or coldness** of a body. In microscopic terms, it represents the average kinetic energy of the particles within that substance. Macroscopically, temperature serves two vital roles:

1. It provides a numerical scale for hotness (e.g., $38.5\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$).
2. It dictates the **direction of spontaneous heat flow**: heat always transfers spontaneously from a body of higher temperature to one of lower temperature.

### The Difference Between Heat and Temperature: A Comparison

To conceptualize the relationship:
- **Temperature** resembles the **level** or "pressure" of thermal energy (analogous to water level in a reservoir).
- **Heat** represents the **quantity** of energy transferred (analogous to the total volume of water flowing through a channel).

Let us revisit Leo's query: **a small cup of tea at $80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$** versus **a large bucket of water at $40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**.

- The tea has a higher temperature ($80\text{ }^\circ\text{C} > 40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$)—its molecules possess a higher average kinetic energy, making it much hotter to the touch.
- However, the bucket contains an enormous mass of water ($15\text{ kg}$ to $20\text{ kg}$ compared to barely $0.2\text{ kg}$ in the teacup). Because total thermal energy depends on both temperature and total mass, the large bucket stores far more total thermal energy than the tiny teacup!

> **Temperature indicates "how hot" a body is. Heat measures the "quantity of thermal energy transferred". A hotter body does not necessarily possess more total thermal energy than a cooler, massive body.**

### Heat Flows Spontaneously Only from Hot to Cold

When a hot mug of coffee sits on a dining table, it gradually cools down as heat transfers into the surrounding cooler room air. Thermal energy never gathers spontaneously from the cooler tabletop into the mug to make the hot coffee boil. 

> **Heat spontaneously flows exclusively from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature, never in reverse.**

(To force heat to move from a cooler space to a warmer space—such as inside a domestic refrigerator—external electrical work must be supplied to drive a mechanical compressor.)

### Thermal Equilibrium: When Flow Ceases

If you pour boiling soup into a cold ceramic bowl, heat flows rapidly from the soup into the bowl. The soup cools while the bowl warms. Eventually, both reach the exact same intermediate temperature. At that moment, because there is no temperature difference between them, **net heat transfer ceases completely**. This state is known as **thermal equilibrium**.

```
 Initial State                     Final State (Thermal Equilibrium)

   Hot Soup                           Soup and Bowl
   ┌────────┐                         ┌────────┐
   │  80 °C │ ~~~ Heat flow ~~~>      │  60 °C │   Net heat flow ceases
   └────────┘                         └────────┘
   Cold Bowl (20 °C)                  Bowl at 60 °C
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Heat transfers spontaneously from the hotter body to the cooler body until both attain the same temperature (thermal equilibrium). Illustrative values shown.

## Part B: Why the Sense of Touch is Unreliable

Leo observed: "Nurse Thompson, you initially touched my forehead with your fingers before using the thermometer. Why cannot doctors simply diagnose fevers by hand?"

Nurse Thompson decided to demonstrate a classic experiment. She placed **three bowls** on the laboratory counter:
- **Bowl A:** filled with uncomfortably warm water.
- **Bowl B:** filled with lukewarm, room-temperature water.
- **Bowl C:** filled with ice-cold water.

She smiled at Leo. "Place your right hand in Bowl A (warm) and your left hand in Bowl C (cold) for one full minute. Then, lift both hands simultaneously and plunge them into Bowl B (lukewarm)."

Leo performed the steps and gashed in surprise: "This feels bizarre! My right hand—which was in the warm water—reports that the lukewarm water is **cold**. But my left hand—which was in ice water—insists that the exact same lukewarm water is **warm**!"

```

 (Warm Water)    (Lukewarm Water)    (Cold Water)
  ┌────────┐        ┌────────┐        ┌────────┐
  │~~~~~~~~│        │~~~~~~~~│        │~~~~~~~~│
  └────────┘        └────────┘        └────────┘
```

## Part C: The Thermometer

### How a Thermometer Works

A **thermometer** is an instrument designed to measure temperature quantitatively. The traditional **liquid-in-glass thermometer** operates on a simple, reliable thermometric principle: **liquids expand when heated and contract when cooled**. As the temperature rises, the liquid volume increases, forcing a narrow column upward along a calibrated glass tube.

A standard liquid-in-glass thermometer consists of three essential components:
- **Bulb:** A thin-walled glass reservoir at the base holding the bulk of the thermometric liquid. Its thin glass allows rapid thermal conduction from the substance being measured.
- **Stem:** A thick-walled glass capillary tube with a very narrow, uniform bore. Because the bore is extremely fine, a tiny expansion in the bulb results in a noticeable, easily readable movement of the liquid column.
- **Scale:** Engraved gradations along the stem marking temperature values in degrees.

```
          ┌─ Glass stem (narrow, uniform capillary bore)
          │
   ...    ││   <- Calibrated scale (°C)
          ││
   20 ──  ││
          ││
   10 ──  ││   <- Thermometric liquid (mercury or dyed alcohol)
          ││
    0 ──  ││
         ┌──┐
         │  │  <- Thin-walled glass bulb
         └──┘
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Anatomy of a liquid-in-glass thermometer: thin bulb reservoir, uniform capillary stem, and etched numerical scale.

### Why Mercury?

Mercury is widely used as a thermometric liquid in laboratory and industrial thermometers because of several distinct physical properties:
1. **Uniform thermal expansion:** Its coefficient of volume expansion remains remarkably constant over a wide temperature range.
2. **Does not wet glass:** It has strong cohesive forces, leaving no clinging droplets on the capillary walls to distort readings.
3. **Opaque and silvery:** The shiny meniscus is effortlessly visible and readable against an etched scale.
4. **Good thermal conductor:** It reaches thermal equilibrium rapidly with the measured object.
5. **Wide liquid range:** It freezes at $-39\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and boils at $357\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, making it versatile for standard laboratory procedures.

**Limitations:** Mercury is highly toxic and hazardous if the glass envelope shatters. Modern clinical thermometers frequently use safe electronic digital thermistors or non-toxic gallium-indium alloys (Galinstan). Furthermore, in polar regions where ambient air temperatures drop below $-40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, mercury freezes solid; for such environments, **alcohol thermometers** (colored with red dye) are employed, as ethyl alcohol remains liquid down to $-114\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

### Fixed Points and Calibration: Constructing a Scale

To calibrate any thermometer scale, two reproducible physical states of pure water at standard atmospheric pressure ($1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$) are established as **fixed points**:

1. **Lower Fixed Point (Ice Point):** The temperature at which pure ice melts or pure water freezes at standard atmospheric pressure. On the Celsius scale, this is designated as **$0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**.
2. **Upper Fixed Point (Steam Point):** The temperature of steam above pure boiling water at standard atmospheric pressure. On the Celsius scale, this is designated as **$100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**.

The interval between these two marks is termed the **fundamental interval**. Dividing this fundamental interval into 100 equal divisions produces steps of **$1\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$** each.

### Three Temperature Scales: Celsius, Fahrenheit, Kelvin

| Scale | Symbol | Lower Fixed Point (Ice) | Upper Fixed Point (Steam) | Fundamental Interval |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Celsius** | $^\circ\text{C}$ | $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ | $100$ equal degrees |
| **Fahrenheit** | $^\circ\text{F}$ | $32\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ | $212\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ | $180$ equal degrees ($212 - 32$) |
| **Kelvin** | $\text{K}$ | $273\text{ K}$ | $373\text{ K}$ | $100$ equal kelvins ($373 - 273$) |

Essential observations:
- **Kelvin ($\text{K}$)** is the **SI base unit** of thermodynamic temperature. It does not take a degree symbol: we write "$300\text{ K}$", never "$300\text{ }^\circ\text{K}$".
- A change of $1\text{ K}$ is identical in magnitude to a change of $1\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($\Delta T = 1\text{ K} \equiv 1\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$).
- Each Fahrenheit degree is smaller than a Celsius degree: $1\text{ }^\circ\text{F} = \frac{100}{180}\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = \frac{5}{9}\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

### Converting Between Scales

Any temperature reading represents a fixed physical state. Therefore, the fractional progress from the ice point to the steam point along the fundamental interval must be equal across all three scales:

$$\frac{C - 0}{100} = \frac{F - 32}{180} = \frac{K - 273}{100}$$

Simplifying pairwise yields our three standard operational conversion formulas:

1. **Celsius to Fahrenheit:**
   $$\frac{C}{5} = \frac{F - 32}{9} \implies F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$$

2. **Fahrenheit to Celsius:**
   $$C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)$$

3. **Celsius to Kelvin:**
   $$K = C + 273$$
   *(The precise value is $273.15$, but $273$ is standard for Class 9).*

### Comparing the Three Scales

```
    °C            °F             K
   100 ───────── 212 ────────── 373   <- Steam Point (Boiling water)
    :             :              :
    :             :              :
    37 ───────── 98.6 ───────── 310   <- Normal Human Body Temperature
    :             :              :
     0 ────────── 32 ────────── 273   <- Ice Point (Freezing water)
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Comparison of Celsius, Fahrenheit, and Kelvin scales showing corresponding ice points, normal body temperatures, and steam points.

## Part D: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Converting from $^\circ\text{C}$ to $^\circ\text{F}$):** Convert $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, $40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, and $25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to the Fahrenheit scale.

*Formula:* $F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$

- At $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(0) + 32 = 0 + 32 = \mathbf{32\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$
- At $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(37) + 32 = 66.6 + 32 = \mathbf{98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$
- At $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(100) + 32 = 180 + 32 = \mathbf{212\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$
- At $40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(40) + 32 = 72 + 32 = \mathbf{104\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$
- At $25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(25) + 32 = 45 + 32 = \mathbf{77\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$

*Verification:* $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 32\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ and $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 212\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ match our standard lower and upper fixed points. $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ matches normal human body temperature.

---

**Example 2 (Converting from $^\circ\text{F}$ to $^\circ\text{C}$):** Convert $212\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$, $98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$, $50\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$, and $14\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ to the Celsius scale.

*Formula:* $C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)$

- At $212\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$: $C = \frac{5}{9}(212 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(180) = \mathbf{100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$
- At $98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$: $C = \frac{5}{9}(98.6 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(66.6) = \mathbf{37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$
- At $50\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$: $C = \frac{5}{9}(50 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(18) = \mathbf{10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$
- At $14\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$: $C = \frac{5}{9}(14 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(-18) = \mathbf{-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$

*Verification:* Converting $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ back to Fahrenheit: $\frac{9}{5}(-10) + 32 = -18 + 32 = 14\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$.

---

**Example 3 (Converting between $^\circ\text{C}$ and $\text{K}$):** Convert $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin; convert $300\text{ K}$ and $373\text{ K}$ to Celsius.

- $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C} \implies K = 0 + 273 = \mathbf{273\text{ K}}$
- $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} \implies K = 100 + 273 = \mathbf{373\text{ K}}$
- $300\text{ K} \implies C = 300 - 273 = \mathbf{27\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$
- $373\text{ K} \implies C = 373 - 273 = \mathbf{100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$

*Verification:* $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 373\text{ K}$ matches the steam point perfectly.

---

**Example 4 (Where Celsius and Fahrenheit Numerical Readings Coincide):** At what numerical temperature do the Celsius and Fahrenheit scales read the exact same value?

Let the common numerical reading be $x$. Thus, $F = C = x$.
Substituting into the conversion equation:
$$x = \frac{9}{5}x + 32$$
$$x - \frac{9}{5}x = 32$$
$$-\frac{4}{5}x = 32$$
$$x = 32 \times \left(-\frac{5}{4}\right) = \mathbf{-40}$$

Therefore, **$-40\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = -40\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$**.

*Verification:* $F = \frac{9}{5}(-40) + 32 = -72 + 32 = -40\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$.

---

**Example 5 (Faulty Thermometer Calibration):** A faulty thermometer records the ice point as $2\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ and the steam point as $98\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, though its capillary bore is completely uniform. What is the true temperature when this faulty thermometer reads $50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$? What is the true temperature when it reads $26\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$?

*Method:* The faulty thermometer divides its fundamental interval into $98 - 2 = 96$ divisions, representing the 100 true Celsius degrees:
$$\text{True Temperature } C = \frac{\text{Reading} - \text{Lower Fixed Point}}{\text{Upper Fixed Point} - \text{Lower Fixed Point}} \times 100$$
$$C = \frac{\text{Reading} - 2}{98 - 2} \times 100 = \frac{\text{Reading} - 2}{96} \times 100$$

- When reading $= 50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:
  $$C = \frac{50 - 2}{96} \times 100 = \frac{48}{96} \times 100 = 0.5 \times 100 = \mathbf{50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$$
- When reading $= 26\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$:
  $$C = \frac{26 - 2}{96} \times 100 = \frac{24}{96} \times 100 = 0.25 \times 100 = \mathbf{25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$$

*Verification:* Because 48 is exactly halfway between 2 and 98, its true value is precisely $50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$; 24 is one-quarter of 96, corresponding accurately to $25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

---

**Example 6 (Length of Mercury Column):** In an uncalibrated mercury thermometer, the length of the mercury thread is $5\text{ cm}$ at the ice point ($0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$) and $25\text{ cm}$ at the steam point ($100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$). Determine the length of the mercury thread at $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

- Change in length over $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 25\text{ cm} - 5\text{ cm} = 20\text{ cm}$.
- Expansion per $1\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = \frac{20\text{ cm}}{100} = 0.2\text{ cm/}^\circ\text{C}$.
- Expansion for $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 35 \times 0.2\text{ cm} = 7\text{ cm}$.
- Total length at $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 5\text{ cm} + 7\text{ cm} = \mathbf{12\text{ cm}}$.

*Verification:* Proportionality check: $\frac{35}{100} \times 20\text{ cm} = 7\text{ cm}$; $5\text{ cm} + 7\text{ cm} = 12\text{ cm}$, which sits logically between $5\text{ cm}$ and $25\text{ cm}$.

---

**Example 7 (Comparing $300\text{ K}$, $300\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, and $300\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$):** Which of the three temperatures is the hottest? Convert all three to $^\circ\text{C}$ to compare.

- $300\text{ K} = 300 - 273 = \mathbf{27\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$
- $300\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = \mathbf{300\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$
- $300\text{ }^\circ\text{F} = \frac{5}{9}(300 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(268) \approx \mathbf{148.9\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$

*Order (from hottest to coldest):*
$$\mathbf{300\text{ }^\circ\text{C} > 300\text{ }^\circ\text{F} \;(\approx 148.9\text{ }^\circ\text{C}) > 300\text{ K} \;(27\text{ }^\circ\text{C})}$$

---

**Example 8 (Clinical Thermometer Reading at $39\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$):** A patient has a fever of $39\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. What would this register on a Fahrenheit clinical thermometer?

$$F = \frac{9}{5}(39) + 32 = 70.2 + 32 = \mathbf{102.2\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$$

*Verification:* Converting back: $\frac{5}{9}(102.2 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(70.2) = 39\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

---

**Example 9 (Range of a Clinical Thermometer):** A clinical thermometer has a Celsius range from $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $42\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$. Express this operating range in degrees Fahrenheit.

- At $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(35) + 32 = 63 + 32 = 95\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$
- At $42\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$: $F = \frac{9}{5}(42) + 32 = 75.6 + 32 = 107.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$

Thus, the range spans approximately **$95\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ to $108\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$** (often labeled $94\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ to $108\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$).

## Part E: Clinical and Laboratory Thermometers

The **clinical thermometer** is specialized to measure human body temperature.

- **Range:** $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $42\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ (or $94\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ to $108\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$), since human core temperature cannot survive outside these limits.
- **Normal Body Temperature:** **$37\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$**.
- **Constriction (Kink):** The stem features a sharp narrow constriction directly above the bulb. As the mercury warms, thermal expansion forces it past this kink. When removed from the patient's body, the mercury in the bulb contracts, but cohesive forces break the thread at the kink. The mercury column in the stem remains stationary, allowing the nurse or doctor to read the temperature accurately at leisure.
- **Resetting the Instrument:** Because the constriction prevents the mercury from returning on its own, the thermometer must be **firmly shaken or flicked downward** before its next use to force the liquid back into the bulb below the $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ mark.

In contrast, a **laboratory thermometer**:
- Has a broad range typically spanning **$-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**, allowing it to measure ice baths, boiling water, and chemical reactions.
- **Has no constriction**: the mercury column rises and falls freely, meaning it must be read **while the bulb remains fully immersed** in the liquid being measured.

Other modern thermometer types include:
- **Digital thermometers:** Rely on heat-sensitive resistors (thermistors) and display numerical readouts without mercury.
- **Infrared thermometers:** Measure thermal radiation emitted by the forehead or ear canal, providing non-contact readings within seconds.
- **Maximum-minimum thermometers:** Feature dual indicator markers to record the daily highest and lowest atmospheric temperatures for meteorological records.

## Part F: Solving Leo's Questions

Let us provide comprehensive answers to the three questions Leo posed to Nurse Thompson:

**1. Leo's fever:** The thermometer registered **$38.5\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**.
- In Fahrenheit: $F = \frac{9}{5}(38.5) + 32 = 69.3 + 32 = \mathbf{101.3\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$.
- Since normal body temperature is $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$), Leo's temperature is elevated by $38.5 - 37.0 = \mathbf{1.5\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$ ($2.7\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$). He does indeed have a fever.

**2. "What does temperature actually measure?"**
Temperature measures the **degree of hotness or coldness** of a substance, reflecting the average translational kinetic energy of its molecules. It determines the direction in which thermal energy will spontaneously flow upon contact.

**3. "Does a hot cup of tea hold more heat than a bucket of water?"**
- **Temperature:** The cup of tea at $80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ has a significantly higher temperature than the bathwater at $40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
- **Stored Thermal Energy:** The bucket contains thousands of grams of water compared to barely 200 grams in the cup. Because total thermal energy is proportional to mass, the bucket possesses vastly more internal thermal energy than the teacup.

If you touch the tea, it scalds your finger because heat transfers intensely across the sharp temperature gradient. However, if you placed both the $80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ cup and the $40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ bucket on large slabs of ice, the bucket would melt significantly more ice because its total thermal energy reservoir is substantially larger.

Nurse Thompson smiled, handed Leo a glass of water, and advised: "Drink plenty of fluids and rest in the quiet room while I contact your parents."

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Treating heat and temperature as synonyms**
   *Correction:* **Heat** is an energy quantity in transit measured in joules ($\text{J}$). **Temperature** is a thermodynamic state variable indicating the degree of hotness measured in degrees Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$) or kelvins ($\text{K}$).

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming a hotter object necessarily contains more thermal energy**
   *Correction:* Stored thermal energy depends on both temperature and total mass. A warm swimming pool at $28\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ contains vastly more thermal energy than a boiling drop of water at $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

3. **Mistake 3: Writing degrees on the Kelvin scale**
   *Correction:* Kelvin is an absolute scale. Write **$300\text{ K}$**, never "$300\text{ }^\circ\text{K}$".

4. **Mistake 4: Omitting the additive constant 32 during Fahrenheit conversions**
   *Correction:* When converting $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $^\circ\text{F}$, calculating only $\frac{9}{5} \times 37 = 66.6$ is incomplete. You must add $32$: $66.6 + 32 = \mathbf{98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$.

5. **Mistake 5: Performing arithmetic operations out of order**
   *Correction:* 
   - From $^\circ\text{F}$ to $^\circ\text{C}$: **subtract 32 first**, then multiply by $\frac{5}{9}$: $C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32)$.
   - From $^\circ\text{C}$ to $^\circ\text{F}$: **multiply by $\frac{9}{5}$ first**, then add 32: $F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32$.

6. **Mistake 6: Relying on sensory touch as an objective gauge**
   *Correction:* The three-bowl demonstration proves that sensory perception is strictly relative and prone to adaptation error. Reliable quantification requires a calibrated thermometer.

7. **Mistake 7: Shaking a clinical thermometer after taking a reading**
   *Correction:* The constriction locks the reading precisely so you can read it leisurely *after* removal. The thermometer should be shaken *before* taking the measurement to reset the mercury below $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

## Quick Recap

- **Heat** is a form of energy that transfers spontaneously from a body at higher temperature to one at lower temperature. SI unit: **joule (J)**. Traditional unit: **calorie (cal)**, where $1\text{ cal} \approx 4.186\text{ J} \approx 4.2\text{ J}$.
- **Temperature** indicates how hot or cold an object is and dictates the direction of spontaneous heat transfer. It measures the average kinetic energy of constituent particles.
- A small body at high temperature has a higher thermal "level", but a massive body at moderate temperature stores a greater total quantity of thermal energy.
- When two bodies reach identical temperatures, net heat transfer stops: they have reached **thermal equilibrium**.
- **Liquid-in-glass thermometers** rely on the uniform thermal expansion of a liquid. Mercury is preferred due to uniform expansion, non-wetting meniscus, high boiling point ($357\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), and low freezing point ($-39\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$).
- Calibration requires two fixed reference marks: **ice point ($0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$)** and **steam point ($100\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$)** at standard atmospheric pressure.
- Conversion relations:
  $$\frac{C}{100} = \frac{F - 32}{180} = \frac{K - 273}{100}$$
  $$F = \frac{9}{5}C + 32, \quad C = \frac{5}{9}(F - 32), \quad K = C + 273$$
- **Clinical thermometer:** Range $35\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $42\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ ($95\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$ to $108\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$); features a constriction (kink) that retains the maximum reading until shaken down. Normal human body temperature is $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 98.6\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$.
- **Laboratory thermometer:** Range $-10\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $110\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$; lacks a constriction and must be read while immersed.

### Answers

1. $F = \frac{9}{5}(60) + 32 = 108 + 32 = \mathbf{140\text{ }^\circ\text{F}}$.
   *Verification:* $C = \frac{5}{9}(140 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(108) = 60\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.

2. $C = \frac{5}{9}(68 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(36) = \mathbf{20\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$.
   *Verification:* $F = \frac{9}{5}(20) + 32 = 36 + 32 = 68\text{ }^\circ\text{F}$.

3. $K = 27 + 273 = \mathbf{300\text{ K}}$.
   $C = 310 - 273 = \mathbf{37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$.

4. Number of divisions in fundamental interval $= 95 - 5 = 90$.
   True temperature $C = \frac{41 - 5}{95 - 5} \times 100 = \frac{36}{90} \times 100 = 0.4 \times 100 = \mathbf{40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$.

5. Change in length from $0\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to $100\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 24\text{ cm} - 4\text{ cm} = 20\text{ cm}$.
   Rate of expansion $= \frac{20\text{ cm}}{100} = 0.2\text{ cm/}^\circ\text{C}$.
   - Length at $60\text{ }^\circ\text{C} = 4\text{ cm} + (60 \times 0.2\text{ cm}) = 4 + 12 = \mathbf{16\text{ cm}}$.
   - When length $= 20\text{ cm}$, expansion $= 20 - 4 = 16\text{ cm}$.
     Temperature $= \frac{16\text{ cm}}{0.2\text{ cm/}^\circ\text{C}} = \mathbf{80\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$.

6. $C = \frac{5}{9}(104 - 32) = \frac{5}{9}(72) = \mathbf{40\text{ }^\circ\text{C}}$.
   Since normal body temperature is $37\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$, the patient has a **high fever**.

7. Convert $50\text{ K}$ to Celsius: $C = 50 - 273 = -223\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$.
   Converting $50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ to Kelvin: $K = 50 + 273 = 323\text{ K}$.
   Clearly, **$50\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ is vastly hotter** than $50\text{ K}$ (which is near absolute zero).

8. The constriction prevents the mercury thread from contracting back into the bulb when the thermometer is removed from the warm body, holding the reading steady. Shaking the thermometer before use exerts an inertial force that drives the mercury back down into the bulb past the constriction.

9. (a) **False**. Because the bucket has much larger mass, its total thermal energy can greatly exceed that of the teacup.
   (b) **False**. Spontaneous heat transfer occurs exclusively from higher to lower temperatures.
   (c) **False**. The Kelvin scale does not use the degree symbol ($^\circ$).
   (d) **True**. Thermal equilibrium is defined by equality of temperature.

10. The hand previously placed in hot water loses heat rapidly to the lukewarm water, sensing cold; the hand from cold water gains heat, sensing warmth. Human skin detects the direction of thermal flux relative to skin temperature. A calibrated **thermometer** provides an objective, absolute measurement.
