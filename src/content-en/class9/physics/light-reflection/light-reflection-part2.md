# Topic 2: Laws of Reflection

In Topic 1, we learned that light travels in straight lines through a uniform medium, giving rise to sharp shadows, astronomical eclipses, and pinhole camera projections. However, light does not travel unimpeded forever. Sooner or later, a light beam encounters a physical interface: a concrete wall, a printed page, the surface of a pond, or a polished glass mirror.

What happens at that boundary? When light strikes an obstacle, a major portion of it **bounces back into the original medium**. In this topic, we master the **two fundamental Laws of Reflection** that govern every bouncing ray of light in the universe—whether reflecting from a dentist's precision mirror or a rough asphalt road.

## The Glare on the Balcony

On a clear, bright afternoon, Elena and her science teacher, Mr. Evans, were standing on the school's second-floor science balcony. Mr. Evans held a compact, flat pocket mirror in his palm.

Angling the mirror toward the sun, Mr. Evans caught a beam of direct sunlight and reflected it across the open courtyard onto the brick wall of the opposite building. A brilliant, circular patch of sunlight danced across the red bricks.

Elena exclaimed, "Look, Mr. Evans! There is a miniature second sun dancing on the library wall!"

Mr. Evans tilted the mirror by just a fraction of a degree. Instantly, the patch of light bounded across several windows. Tilting it in the other direction sent the patch leaping upward toward the roofline.

Elena watched with keen interest and asked, "Can I steer that reflected patch of sunlight directly onto the small arched window above the library door on my very first try, without guessing?"

Mr. Evans chuckled. "Absolutely—not by random trial and error, but by **geometric precision**. When light bounces off a mirror, it follows an exact physical law. Once you understand how angles are measured relative to the normal, you can guide a reflected beam to any target with mathematical certainty." We will resolve Elena's targeting challenge at the end of this topic.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Reflection** | The phenomenon wherein light striking an interface rebounds into the same medium with a changed direction | Sunlight bouncing off a pocket mirror onto a wall |
| **Incident ray** | The incoming ray of light that approaches and strikes the reflecting surface | A beam of sunlight traveling from the sky toward the mirror |
| **Reflected ray** | The outgoing ray of light that rebounds away from the reflecting surface | The beam leaving the mirror and traveling toward the wall |
| **Point of incidence ($O$)** | The exact point on the surface where the incident ray strikes | The pinpoint spot on the mirror glass where the ray lands |
| **Normal ($N$)** | An imaginary reference line drawn perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the surface at the point of incidence | A perpendicular line projecting straight out from the glass |
| **Angle of incidence ($i$)** | The angle formed between the incident ray and the **normal** | $i = 30^\circ$ |
| **Angle of reflection ($r$)** | The angle formed between the reflected ray and the **normal** | $r = 30^\circ$ |
| **Plane of incidence** | The flat geometrical plane containing both the incident ray and the normal | The flat sheet of paper on which the rays are traced |
| **Regular reflection** | Reflection from a smooth, polished surface where parallel incident rays rebound as parallel reflected rays | Reflection from a plane mirror or calm water surface |
| **Diffused reflection** | Reflection from a microscopically rough surface where parallel incident rays scatter in random directions | Reflection from paper, wood, clothing, or a wall |

## Part A: What Reflection Is and How Angles Are Measured

### Reflection

> **Reflection of light** is the phenomenon whereby a ray of light, upon encountering the boundary between two different media, **rebounds back into the original medium**, changing its direction of propagation while preserving its frequency, wavelength, and speed.

When you bounce a tennis ball against a smooth gymnasium floor, it rebounds back up into the air. Light behaves similarly, but with an uncompromising mathematical regularity.

### Understanding the Reflection Diagram

To analyze reflection quantitatively, we represent the optical surface in profile as a straight horizontal line, with short hatching on the back to represent the non-reflective silvered coating.

```
                     N (Normal)
                     :
   Incident ray      :      Reflected ray
             \       :       /
              \      :      /
               \     :     /
                \  i : r  /
                 ↘   :   ↗
                  \  :  /
                   \ : /
                    \:/
  ===================O===================  Mirror surface
                     ^
         O = Point of incidence
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Key geometric terms of reflection. The incident ray strikes point $O$ on the mirror, and the reflected ray departs from $O$. The normal line $N$ is erected perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the mirror surface at $O$. Angle $i$ is between the incident ray and the normal; angle $r$ is between the reflected ray and the normal.

Let us define every geometric element rigorously:
- **Incident ray:** The incoming straight-line trajectory along which light approaches the surface.
- **Reflected ray:** The outgoing straight-line trajectory along which light departs from the surface.
- **Point of incidence ($O$):** The specific spatial coordinate where the incoming ray meets the reflective plane.
- **Normal ($N$):** An imaginary perpendicular line ($90^\circ$) erected to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence. It serves as our universal angular benchmark.
- **Angle of incidence ($i$):** The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
- **Angle of reflection ($r$):** The angle between the reflected ray and the normal.
- **Plane of incidence:** The two-dimensional geometric plane containing the incident ray and the normal.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **A Vital Rule for Problem Solving:** In physics, the angle of incidence ($i$) and angle of reflection ($r$) are **always measured from the normal**, never from the mirror surface. Misidentifying the surface glancing angle as the angle of incidence is the most frequent student error.

## Part B: The Two Laws of Reflection

Regardless of whether the reflective boundary is flat, curved, polished, or rough, every ray of light obeys the **Two Laws of Reflection**:

> **First Law of Reflection:**
> The angle of incidence is strictly equal to the angle of reflection:
> $$ngle i = ngle r$$

> **Second Law of Reflection:**
> The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence all lie in the **same geometric plane**.

The First Law dictates the **exact angular direction** of the outgoing ray: light rebounds at the mirror image of its arrival angle relative to the perpendicular normal. The Second Law dictates the **spatial orientation**: the outgoing ray cannot twist or deflect out of the two-dimensional plane defined by the incoming ray and the normal.

### Activity: Verifying the Laws of Reflection with Pins

This classic laboratory experiment can be performed using an optics board, pins, a plane mirror strip, a protractor, and a sheet of white paper.

**Experimental Procedure:**
1. Fix a sheet of white drawing paper onto a flat wooden board with drawing pins. Draw a straight reference line $AB$ across the middle of the sheet.
2. Erect a small plane mirror vertically along line $AB$, using a wooden support block or plasticine so that its reflecting silver line coincides precisely with line $AB$.
3. Mark a point $O$ roughly in the centre of line $AB$. Using a protractor, draw a line $ON$ perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to $AB$. This is the normal.
4. Draw an incident line $PO$ making an angle of incidence $i = 30^\circ$ with the normal $ON$.
5. Fix two optical pins, $P_1$ and $P_2$, vertically along line $PO$, separated by at least $5\text{ cm}$.
6. Looking into the mirror from the opposite side of the normal, align your line of sight with the virtual images of pins $P_1$ and $P_2$. Fix two more pins, $P_3$ and $P_4$, into the board such that their bases appear to lie in a perfectly straight line with the images of $P_1$ and $P_2$.
7. Remove the pins and mirror. Mark the pinpricks of $P_3$ and $P_4$, and draw a straight line through them meeting line $AB$ at $O$. This represents the reflected ray $OQ$.
8. Measure the angle of reflection $r = \angle NOQ$ with your protractor. Repeat the procedure for angles of incidence of $20^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$.

```
          P       N       Q
            \     :     /
             \    :    /
              \   :   /
               \  :  /
                \ : /
                 \:/
         A========O========B

  In the plane of the paper: PO (incident), ON (normal), OQ (reflected)
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Top view of the pin verification experiment. The incident path $PO$, normal $ON$, and reflected path $OQ$ all lie coplanar upon the flat drawing paper.

**Sample Experimental Observations:**

| Trial | Angle of Incidence $i$ ($^\circ$) | Angle of Reflection $r$ ($^\circ$) | Difference ($|i - r|$) |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| 1 | $20^\circ$ | $20^\circ$ | $0^\circ$ |
| 2 | $30^\circ$ | $31^\circ$ | $1^\circ$ |
| 3 | $45^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $0^\circ$ |
| 4 | $60^\circ$ | $59^\circ$ | $1^\circ$ |

Within standard experimental reading uncertainty ($\pm 1^\circ$), the angle of incidence consistently equals the angle of reflection ($ngle i = ngle r$).

**Verifying the Second Law (Coplanarity):**
If the section of paper containing the reflected ray $OQ$ is creased and folded downward over the edge of the board, the reflected ray trace vanishes from that section of paper. Light continues traveling through space in its original plane; it cannot bend down into the folded paper. Smoothing the paper flat restores the visible trace immediately, confirming that the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal are strictly coplanar.

## Part C: Two Special Properties: Reversibility and Normal Incidence

### 1. Principle of Reversibility of Light

Consider a light ray traveling along path $AO$, striking the mirror at $O$, and reflecting along path $OB$. If a laser is placed at $B$ and aimed backward along line $BO$, the ray strikes point $O$ at an angle of incidence equal to the original $r$. Because $ngle i = ngle r$, the ray reflects along line $OA$, retracing its incoming trajectory in the exact opposite direction.

```
               N
        A      :      B
         \     :     /
          \    :    /
           ↘   :   ↗
            \  :  /
             \ : /
  =============O=============  Mirror
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Principle of Reversibility of Light. Light traveling from $A$ reflects toward $B$; light directed backward from $B$ retraces the identical spatial path to arrive at $A$.

> **The Principle of Reversibility of Light:**
> If the direction of a ray of light is reversed, it retraces its exact path in the opposite direction through any optical system.

### 2. Normal Incidence (Direct Perpendicular Impact)

When a ray of light strikes a reflecting surface perpendicularly (along the normal line itself), the incident ray coincides with the normal:

$$ngle i = 0^\circ$$

Applying the First Law of Reflection:

$$ngle r = ngle i = 0^\circ$$

The reflected ray rebounds directly along the normal, retracing its incoming path back toward the source.

```
          N (Normal)
     ↓    :    ↑
     ↓    :    ↑
     ↓    :    ↑
     ↓    :    ↑
     ↓    :    ↑
==========O==========  Mirror
```

> **Figure 2.4** – Normal incidence. When a light ray strikes perpendicular to the mirror surface, $i = 0^\circ$ and $r = 0^\circ$. The ray rebounds directly along its own path.

> [!NOTE]
> An angle of incidence of $0^\circ$ does **not** mean the light missed the mirror. It means there is zero angular separation between the ray and the surface normal. The ray strikes the mirror head-on at $90^\circ$ to the surface.

## Part D: Regular vs. Diffused Reflection

Why does a polished silver mirror produce a crisp, mirror-image reflection of your face, while a smooth sheet of white printer paper under the same overhead lighting produces no image at all? The answer lies in the microscopic topography of the reflecting surface.

### Regular (Specular) Reflection

When a beam of light strikes a surface that is **optically smooth and highly polished** (such as a silvered glass mirror, polished stainless steel, or the still surface of a calm pond):
- Every point on the macroscopic surface shares an identical, parallel normal.
- A bundle of parallel incident rays strikes the surface at the identical angle of incidence ($i_1 = i_2 = i_3$).
- Consequently, all reflected rays emerge parallel to one another ($r_1 = r_2 = r_3$).
- This preserved wavefront forms a distinct, high-contrast **optical image**.

```
   \       /   \       /   \       /
    ↘     ↗     ↘     ↗     ↘     ↗
     \   /       \   /       \   /
      \ /         \ /         \ /
=======*===========*===========*========
Smooth, polished surface
```

> **Figure 2.5** – Regular (specular) reflection. Parallel incoming rays rebound as parallel outgoing rays from a smooth surface.

### Diffused (Irregular) Reflection

When light strikes a surface that is **microscopically rough and irregular** (such as paper, unpolished wood, clothing, stone, or human skin):
- Although the surface may feel smooth to the touch, microscopic ridges, valleys, and pits cover the material.
- The normal at each individual microscopic facet points in a different, random direction.
- Parallel incident rays strike facets tilted at different angles, resulting in wildly different angles of incidence.
- Each individual ray strictly obeys $ngle i = ngle r$ at its local point of contact, but the outgoing rays **scatter diffusely in all directions**.
- The coherence of the beam is shattered; no optical image is formed.

```
   \       /   \   :       \ \
    ↘     ↗     ↘  ↑        ↘ ↖
     \   /       \ :         \ \
      \ /         \:          \ \
/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\/\
Rough, irregular surface
```

> **Figure 2.6** – Diffused reflection. Parallel incident rays strike microscopic facets with different normals, scattering light in diverse directions. The law $ngle i = ngle r$ holds true at every individual microscopic point.

### Why Diffused Reflection Enables Us to See Everyday Objects

If all objects in nature exhibited only specular (regular) reflection, the world would be an optical nightmare. You would see objects only if your eye happened to be positioned directly along the path of the reflected beam—every other viewpoint would appear pitch black.

Because most everyday objects (tables, walls, books, chalkboards, clothing) produce **diffused reflection**, they scatter ambient light uniformly in all directions. Consequently, students seated in every corner of a classroom can read the blackboard and see their teacher clearly.

### Classifying 8 Surfaces: Regular or Diffused?

| Surface | Reflection Type | Physical Topography |
|---|---|---|
| Silvered glass mirror | **Regular** | Polished flat down to molecular tolerances |
| Calm surface of a pond | **Regular** | Surface tension creates an optically flat liquid plane |
| Polished stainless steel tray | **Regular** | Machined and buffed to specular smoothness |
| Sheet of white printer paper | **Diffused** | Randomly oriented cellulose fibers scatter light |
| Painted plaster wall | **Diffused** | Microscopic surface pits scatter rays in all directions |
| Cotton fabric | **Diffused** | Woven threads present curved, irregular surfaces |
| Asphalt roadway | **Diffused** | Coarse aggregate particles create extreme roughness |
| Rippled, windy lake surface | **Diffused** | Turbulent waves tilt local normals randomly |

## Part E: Calculating Angles Correctly

To master reflection problems, always distinguish between three key angles:

```
                    N (Normal)
            \       :
             \      :
              \     :
               \ i  :
                \   :
                 \  :
                  \ :
  90° - i          \:
  ==================O==================  Mirror
```

> **Figure 2.7** – Geometric relationship between the angle of incidence ($i$) and the glancing angle ($90^\circ - i$). Because the normal is perpendicular to the mirror, the two angles sum to $90^\circ$.

| Angle Descriptor | Geometric Definition | Mathematical Relation |
|---|---|---|
| **Angle of incidence ($i$)** | Angle between the incident ray and the **normal** | $i = 90^\circ - (\text{glancing angle})$ |
| **Angle of reflection ($r$)** | Angle between the reflected ray and the **normal** | $r = i$ |
| **Glancing angle** | Angle between the ray and the **mirror surface** | $\alpha = 90^\circ - i$ |
| **Angle between rays ($\theta$)** | Total angular separation between incident and reflected paths | $\theta = i + r = 2i$ |

> [!NOTE]
> The angle between the two rays is $\theta = 2i$. Note that the angle through which the ray has been deviated from its original straight path is the angle of deviation: $\delta = 180^\circ - 2i$.

## Part F: Solved Examples

In every example, angles $i$ and $r$ are measured strictly from the normal.

### Example 1: Given Angle of Incidence, Find Angle of Reflection and Glancing Angle
A light ray strikes a flat mirror at an angle of incidence $i = 35^\circ$. Determine:
1. The angle of reflection ($r$).
2. The total angle between the incident and reflected rays ($\theta$).
3. The glancing angle between the reflected ray and the mirror surface.

**Solution:**
1. By the First Law of Reflection:
   $$r = i = 35^\circ$$
2. Total angle between the rays:
   $$\theta = i + r = 35^\circ + 35^\circ = 2 \times 35^\circ = 70^\circ$$
3. Glancing angle with the surface:
   $$\alpha = 90^\circ - r = 90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$$
   *Verification:* $35^\circ + 55^\circ = 90^\circ$ (perpendicular to normal) and $\theta = 70^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 2: Ray Striking at an Angle with the Mirror Surface
A light ray strikes a plane mirror making an angle of $30^\circ$ with the surface of the mirror. Find $i$, $r$, and the angle between the rays $\theta$.

**Solution:**
The given $30^\circ$ angle is the glancing angle with the surface, not $i$.
$$i = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$$
$$r = i = 60^\circ$$
$$\theta = i + r = 60^\circ + 60^\circ = 120^\circ$$
*Verification:* $60^\circ + 30^\circ = 90^\circ$ and $\theta = 2 \times 60^\circ = 120^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 3: Angle Between Incident Ray and Reflected Ray Given
The total angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray is measured to be $80^\circ$. Calculate the angle of incidence and the angle of reflection.

**Solution:**
$$\theta = i + r = 2i = 80^\circ$$
$$i = \frac{80^\circ}{2} = 40^\circ$$
$$r = i = 40^\circ$$
*Verification:* $40^\circ + 40^\circ = 80^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 4: Ray Falling Normally on a Mirror
A laser beam strikes a mirror along the normal line. Find $i$, $r$, and describe the path of the reflected beam.

**Solution:**
Because the incident ray lies directly along the normal, its angular separation from the normal is zero:
$$i = 0^\circ$$
$$r = i = 0^\circ$$
The reflected ray travels directly back along the normal, retracing its arrival path.
Angle between incident and reflected rays: $\theta = 2 \times 0^\circ = 0^\circ$.
Glancing angle with mirror surface: $90^\circ - 0^\circ = 90^\circ$. $\checkmark$

### Example 5: Ray Striking at 25° to the Mirror Surface
A ray of light strikes a flat mirror at a glancing angle of $25^\circ$ to the mirror plane. Find $i$, $r$, and $\theta$.

**Solution:**
$$i = 90^\circ - 25^\circ = 65^\circ$$
$$r = 65^\circ$$
$$\theta = 2 \times 65^\circ = 130^\circ$$
*Verification:* $65^\circ + 25^\circ = 90^\circ$ and $65^\circ + 65^\circ = 130^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 6: Two Consecutive Rays
Two distinct light rays strike a mirror: Ray 1 has an angle of incidence of $20^\circ$, and Ray 2 has an angle of incidence of $50^\circ$. For each ray, find $r$, $\theta$, and the glancing angle with the surface.

| Ray | $i$ | $r$ | $\theta = 2i$ | Glancing angle ($90^\circ - i$) |
|---|:---:|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Ray 1 | $20^\circ$ | $20^\circ$ | $40^\circ$ | $70^\circ$ |
| Ray 2 | $50^\circ$ | $50^\circ$ | $100^\circ$ | $40^\circ$ |

*Observation:* As the angle of incidence increases, the ray sweeps closer to the mirror surface (glancing angle decreases) and the divergence between the incident and reflected beams widens. $\checkmark$

### Example 7: Ray Incident at 55° to the Normal
A ray of light is incident upon a mirror at an angle of $55^\circ$ to the normal. Describe the direction of the reflected ray and calculate its glancing angle with the mirror.

**Solution:**
$$i = 55^\circ$$
$$r = 55^\circ$$
The reflected ray departs on the opposite side of the normal, making an angle of $55^\circ$ with the normal.
$$\theta = 2 \times 55^\circ = 110^\circ$$
$$\text{Glancing angle} = 90^\circ - 55^\circ = 35^\circ$$
*Verification:* $55^\circ + 35^\circ = 90^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 8: Flashlight Beam at 50° to the Mirror Surface
A narrow beam from a flashlight strikes a mirror, making an angle of $50^\circ$ with the mirror's reflecting surface. Determine $i$, $r$, and $\theta$.

**Solution:**
$$i = 90^\circ - 50^\circ = 40^\circ$$
$$r = 40^\circ$$
$$\theta = 2 \times 40^\circ = 80^\circ$$
*Verification:* $40^\circ + 50^\circ = 90^\circ$ and $40^\circ + 40^\circ = 80^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 9: Tracing Backward from Reflected Ray to Incident Ray
The reflected ray from a mirror is observed to make a glancing angle of $70^\circ$ with the mirror plane. Find $r$, $i$, and $\theta$.

**Solution:**
$$r = 90^\circ - 70^\circ = 20^\circ$$
By the First Law of Reflection:
$$i = r = 20^\circ$$
$$\theta = 20^\circ + 20^\circ = 40^\circ$$
*Verification:* $20^\circ + 70^\circ = 90^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 10: Given Angle of Deviation, Find Glancing Angle
The total angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray is $110^\circ$. Determine the angle of incidence and the angle made by the incident ray with the mirror surface.

**Solution:**
$$\theta = 2i = 110^\circ \implies i = \frac{110^\circ}{2} = 55^\circ$$
$$\text{Glancing angle} = 90^\circ - 55^\circ = 35^\circ$$
*Verification:* $55^\circ + 55^\circ = 110^\circ$ and $55^\circ + 35^\circ = 90^\circ \; \checkmark$

### Example 11 (Extension): What Happens When a Mirror Rotates?
*(Class 10 Curriculum Extension)*
A stationary incident ray strikes a mirror initially at $i = 30^\circ$. The mirror is then rotated by an angle of $10^\circ$ such that the angle of incidence increases. By what angle does the reflected ray turn?

**Solution:**
1. **Initial state:**
   $$i_1 = 30^\circ \implies r_1 = 30^\circ$$
   The angle between incident and reflected ray is $\theta_1 = 2 \times 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.
2. **Rotated state:**
   When the mirror rotates by $10^\circ$, its normal rotates by the exact same $10^\circ$.
   $$i_2 = 30^\circ + 10^\circ = 40^\circ \implies r_2 = 40^\circ$$
   The new angle between rays is $\theta_2 = 2 \times 40^\circ = 80^\circ$.
3. **Angular shift of the reflected ray:**
   $$\Delta \theta = \theta_2 - \theta_1 = 80^\circ - 60^\circ = 20^\circ$$

> **The Mirror Rotation Rule:**
> If a plane mirror rotates through an angle $\theta_m$ while the incident ray remains fixed, the reflected ray rotates through twice that angle:
> $$\Delta \theta_{\text{reflected}} = 2\theta_m$$

This explains why tiny vibrations or tilts in Elena's mirror caused the reflected sunlight patch on the courtyard wall to leap so dramatically.

### Example 12: Resolving the Balcony Glare Mystery
Now let us resolve Elena's challenge: how to hit the library window target with mathematical certainty.

Mr. Evans explained the geometric condition:
"To reflect sunlight onto the library window, the mirror must be oriented so that its **normal line bisects the angle** between the incoming sunlight and the direction of the library window."

Because $\angle i = \angle r$, the normal must lie exactly halfway between the incoming incident ray and the desired outgoing target ray.

**Numerical Demonstration:**
Suppose the angle measured between the incoming sunlight vector and the line of sight to the library window is $100^\circ$. How must the mirror be positioned?
1. The normal must bisect this $100^\circ$ angle:
   $$i = \frac{100^\circ}{2} = 50^\circ \quad \text{and} \quad r = 50^\circ$$
2. Because the mirror face is perpendicular to its normal, the glancing angle of the mirror relative to the incoming sunlight must be:
   $$\alpha = 90^\circ - 50^\circ = 40^\circ$$

Elena carefully tilted the mirror until its face made an angle of $40^\circ$ with the sunbeam. Instantly, the golden patch of sunlight centered precisely onto the library's arched window.

Elena cheered, "It works like pure geometry!"
"Not just geometry," Mr. Evans smiled, "but the fundamental law of physical optics in action."

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Measuring angles from the mirror surface instead of the normal.** ❌ If a problem states "the ray strikes the mirror at $30^\circ$", writing $i = 30^\circ$ is incorrect. $30^\circ$ is the glancing angle with the surface; the true angle of incidence is $i = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.
2. **Mistake 2: Believing the laws of reflection do not apply to diffused reflection.** ❌ The law $\angle i = \angle r$ holds strictly for every single light ray in the universe. In diffused reflection, the rays scatter only because the microscopic surface normals point in random directions.
3. **Mistake 3: Confusing incoming incident rays with outgoing reflected rays.** ❌ Always inspect the arrowhead on the ray diagram. Rays directed toward the surface are incident; rays directed away from the surface are reflected.
4. **Mistake 4: Believing only shiny mirrors reflect light.** ❌ All non-black visible objects reflect light. If an object did not reflect light diffusely into your eyes, it would be utterly invisible. Polished mirrors simply reflect specularly (regularly) to preserve image wavefronts.
5. **Mistake 5: Drawing the normal parallel to the mirror surface.** ❌ The normal is, by definition, perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the reflecting boundary at the point of incidence.

## Quick Recap

- **Reflection** is the rebounding of light into the same medium upon striking an interface, accompanied by a change in propagation direction.
- Key terms: **incident ray, reflected ray, point of incidence ($O$), normal ($N$), plane of incidence**.
- $i$ and $r$ are **always measured relative to the normal**, never the mirror surface.
- **First Law of Reflection:** $\angle i = \angle r$.
- **Second Law of Reflection:** The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the **same plane**.
- **Principle of Reversibility:** If a light ray's trajectory is reversed, it retraces the exact same path.
- **Normal incidence:** When a ray strikes along the normal ($i = 0^\circ$), it reflects back along the normal ($r = 0^\circ$).
- **Regular reflection** occurs on optically smooth surfaces, preserving parallel rays to form clear images. **Diffused reflection** occurs on microscopically rough surfaces, scattering rays in all directions while still obeying $\angle i = \angle r$ locally.
- The total angle between incident and reflected rays is $\theta = 2i$. The glancing angle with the surface is $90^\circ - i$.
- Rotating a mirror by $\theta_m$ while keeping the incident ray fixed rotates the reflected ray by **$2\theta_m$**.
- To direct a reflected ray toward a specific target, orient the mirror so its normal **bisects the angle** between the source and target.

8. A light ray strikes a mirror such that the reflected ray makes an angle of $35^\circ$ with the mirror surface. Find $i$, $r$, and the total angle between the incident and reflected rays.
9. Explain in two or three sentences why a painted classroom wall can be seen comfortably from any desk in the room, whereas a flat mirror reflecting direct sunlight casts a blinding glare in only one specific direction.
10. A ray of light is incident normally on a flat mirror. State the values of $i$, $r$, the total angle between the rays, and the angle made by the ray with the mirror surface.

### Answers

1. (a) By the First Law: $r = i = \mathbf{48^\circ}$.
   (b) $\theta = 2i = 2 \times 48^\circ = \mathbf{96^\circ}$.
   (c) Glancing angle $= 90^\circ - 48^\circ = \mathbf{42^\circ}$.
   *Verification:* $48^\circ + 48^\circ = 96^\circ$ and $48^\circ + 42^\circ = 90^\circ \; \checkmark$

2. $i = 90^\circ - 20^\circ = \mathbf{70^\circ}$. $r = \mathbf{70^\circ}$. $\theta = 2 \times 70^\circ = \mathbf{140^\circ}$.
   *Verification:* $70^\circ + 20^\circ = 90^\circ$ and $70^\circ + 70^\circ = 140^\circ \; \checkmark$

3. $i = \theta / 2 = 90^\circ / 2 = \mathbf{45^\circ}$. Glancing angle with surface $= 90^\circ - 45^\circ = \mathbf{45^\circ}$.
   *Verification:* $45^\circ + 45^\circ = 90^\circ \; \checkmark$

4. (a) **False** ❌. The angle of incidence is always measured between the incident ray and the surface normal.
   (b) **False** ❌. Every individual ray obeys $\angle i = \angle r$; scattering occurs solely due to randomly oriented microscopic facet normals.
   (c) **True** ✅. A perpendicular ray coincides with the normal, yielding $i = 0^\circ$ and $r = 0^\circ$.
   (d) **False** ❌. All non-luminous visible objects reflect light diffusely; otherwise, they would be invisible to human sight.

5. Initial ray angle: $\theta_1 = 2 \times 20^\circ = \mathbf{40^\circ}$.
   New ray angle: $\theta_2 = 2 \times 35^\circ = \mathbf{70^\circ}$.
   Rotation of reflected ray: $\Delta \theta = 70^\circ - 40^\circ = \mathbf{30^\circ}$, which is exactly twice the mirror rotation angle ($2 \times 15^\circ = 30^\circ$).
   *Verification:* $2 \times 15^\circ = 30^\circ \; \checkmark$

6. (a) Steel spoon: **Regular reflection** (optically smooth, polished metallic surface).
   (b) Cotton shirt: **Diffused reflection** (irregular, fibrous interwoven surface).
   (c) Still pond: **Regular reflection** (planar, optically flat liquid boundary).
   (d) Granite wall: **Diffused reflection** (coarse crystalline mineral grains).

7. By the Principle of Reversibility of Light, the ray directed from $B$ to $O$ will reflect along the path $OA$ toward point $A$. The angle of incidence will be $\mathbf{30^\circ}$, because the incident and reflected angles simply exchange roles.
   *Verification:* Initially $i = r = 30^\circ$; on reversal, $i = 30^\circ$ and $r = 30^\circ \; \checkmark$

8. $r = 90^\circ - 35^\circ = \mathbf{55^\circ}$.
   $i = r = \mathbf{55^\circ}$.
   $\theta = 2 \times 55^\circ = \mathbf{110^\circ}$.
   *Verification:* $55^\circ + 35^\circ = 90^\circ$ and $55^\circ + 55^\circ = 110^\circ \; \checkmark$

9. A painted wall is microscopically rough, causing incoming light to undergo diffused reflection and scatter evenly in all spatial directions, making it visible from every angle. In contrast, a flat mirror is optically smooth and produces regular reflection, concentrating all reflected rays into a single coherent, parallel beam that creates glare only when viewed directly along that reflected direction.

10. For normal incidence: $i = \mathbf{0^\circ}$, $r = \mathbf{0^\circ}$, total angle between rays $\theta = 2 \times 0^\circ = \mathbf{0^\circ}$ (rays coincide on the same line), and angle made with the mirror surface $= 90^\circ - 0^\circ = \mathbf{90^\circ}$.
    *Verification:* $0^\circ + 90^\circ = 90^\circ \; \checkmark$
