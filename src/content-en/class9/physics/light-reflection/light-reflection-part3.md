# Topic 3: Plane Mirrors

In Topic 2, we established the fundamental Laws of Reflection: the incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same geometric plane, and the angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection ($ngle i = ngle r$), with both angles measured strictly from the normal.

Now, we apply these laws to the most ubiquitous optical device in modern civilization: the **plane mirror** (a flat, silvered reflecting surface). We will discover how and where virtual images form behind a mirror, why your bathroom mirror seems to swap your left and right sides without turning you upside down, how many multiple reflections form between inclined mirrors, and why the bold red lettering on an emergency vehicle is deliberately painted backward.

---

## The Reversed Lettering on the Ambulance

Outside the city clinic, an emergency ambulance sat parked beside the drop-off lane. Liam was waiting outside with his grandmother after her routine health check-up.

Glancing at the hood of the ambulance, Liam noticed something puzzling about the bold red lettering displayed across the vehicle's front:

$$\text{ECNALUBMA}$$

Liam stopped and turned to the ambulance driver, Mr. Carter. "Excuse me, Mr. Carter, but why is the word **AMBULANCE** painted backward across your hood? The letters are mirrored from right to left, and letters like **E**, **C**, and **B** are completely reversed!"

Mr. Carter smiled. "It looks backward to someone standing on the pavement, Liam, but it was painted that way with deliberate scientific intent. When a motorist driving ahead of me glances into their rearview mirror, the plane mirror in their car reverses the letters once again. To that driver, the word appears perfectly upright and readable: **AMBULANCE**."

Liam marveled, "So a plane mirror inverts words laterally? But why does a mirror reverse left and right while leaving up and down completely undisturbed?"

Mr. Carter replied, "That exact question touches on the physics of **lateral inversion** and image formation in plane mirrors. Let us examine the optical geometry first, and then we will test the lettering ourselves!" We will resolve this puzzle completely in Part C.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Plane mirror** | A flat, optically smooth reflecting surface that produces an undistorted virtual image | A household bathroom or dressing mirror |
| **Object** | Any luminous or illuminated physical entity placed in front of an optical system | A person standing before a mirror |
| **Image** | The optical representation of an object formed by converging or diverging light rays | Your reflection visible in the mirror |
| **Real image** | An image formed when reflected rays physically intersect at a point; it can be captured on a screen | An image projected onto a cinema screen |
| **Virtual image** | An image formed when reflected rays only appear to diverge from a point behind the mirror; it cannot be captured on a screen | An image seen inside a plane mirror |
| **Erect** | An image that has the same vertical orientation as the object (upright, not upside down) | Your head appears at the top in a mirror |
| **Lateral inversion** | The apparent reversal of left and right in a mirror image while maintaining vertical orientation | Raising your right hand makes the image raise its left hand |
| **Periscope** | An optical instrument utilizing two parallel mirrors inclined at $45^\circ$ to view objects over barriers | A periscope on a submarine |

## Part A: What Plane Mirrors and Optical Images Are

A **plane mirror** consists of an optically flat sheet of glass whose rear surface is coated with a thin layer of reflective metal (typically vacuum-deposited silver or aluminum) protected by a durable layer of opaque red lead paint. Because the front glass boundary and the rear reflective coating are flat planes, the normal at every single point across the mirror points in the exact same spatial direction.

When we observe an illuminated object, our brain interprets the light entering our eyes as having traveled along straight lines. An **image** is formed wherever the reflected rays intersect, or appear to intersect.

### Real Images vs. Virtual Images

Optical images fall into two mutually exclusive physical categories:

> **Real Image:** Formed when reflected or refracted light rays **physically intersect** at a focal point in space. Because rays actually converge at the image coordinates, a real image **can be caught on a physical screen** (such as a sheet of paper or film).
>
> **Virtual Image:** Formed when reflected rays **diverge away from the mirror** and never physically intersect. Instead, when traced straight backward behind the mirror, they appear to originate from a common point. A virtual image **cannot be caught on a screen**, because no physical light rays exist behind the reflective surface.

The image produced by a plane mirror is always **virtual**. The light rays from your face bounce off the front glass and enter your eyes; zero light penetrates behind the silvered backing. If you place a photographic film or white card behind the mirror, no image appears on it. The image exists solely as an optical perception constructed by your brain from the diverging wavefronts.

## Part B: How an Image Is Formed in a Plane Mirror

Consider an idealized **point object $O$** positioned in front of a flat plane mirror. Light radiates from $O$ in all directions. To locate its image geometrically, we trace just **two representative rays**:

1. **Ray 1 (Normal incidence):** Radiates from $O$ perpendicular to the mirror surface. For this ray, $ngle i = 0^\circ$. By the First Law of Reflection, $ngle r = 0^\circ$. The ray rebounds directly backward along its incoming path.
2. **Ray 2 (Oblique incidence):** Radiates from $O$ and strikes the mirror at an oblique angle. A normal is erected at the point of incidence. The ray reflects on the opposite side of the normal such that $ngle r = ngle i$.

```
                              #/
                              #/
                              #/
      O-----►---------◄-------#/......................I
         \                    #/                 .
             \                #/             .
                 \            #/         .
                     \        #/     .
                         \    #/ .
                             \#/
                           /  #/
                       /      #/
                   /          #/
```

> **Figure 3.1** – Ray diagram for image formation of a point object $O$ in a plane mirror (`#` denotes the mirror surface and `/` denotes the opaque backing). Ray 1 strikes normally and reflects straight back. Ray 2 strikes obliquely and reflects with $ngle i = ngle r$. Tracing both reflected rays backward (dotted lines `....`) reveals that they intersect at virtual image point $I$, located symmetrically behind the mirror.

When an observer's eye intercepts these diverging reflected rays, the brain traces them backward along straight lines. The virtual intersection point is **$I$**.
By congruent triangles ($\Delta OMN \cong \Delta IMN$), the perpendicular distance from object $O$ to the mirror plane is strictly equal to the perpendicular distance from image $I$ to the mirror plane.

## Part C: Properties of the Image Formed by a Plane Mirror

Every image formed by a plane mirror exhibits six universal physical properties:

> 1. The image is **virtual** (it cannot be projected onto a screen).
> 2. The image is **erect** (upright; vertically oriented in the same direction as the object).
> 3. The image is of the **exact same size** as the object (magnification $m = +1$).
> 4. The image distance behind the mirror equals the object distance in front:
>    $$\text{Image distance } (v) = \text{Object distance } (u)$$
> 5. The image is **laterally inverted** (left and right are reversed).
> 6. The line joining any object point to its corresponding image point is **strictly perpendicular ($90^\circ$)** to the mirror plane.

### Object and Image Distance

The fact that the image forms as far behind the mirror as the object stands in front is a foundational rule. If an object is located at a distance $d$ in front of the mirror:
- Distance from mirror to image $= d$.
- Total distance between the physical object and its virtual image:

$$\text{Distance from Object to Image} = d + d = 2d$$

```
    Object                 Mirror                  Image
      O ◄────── 1.5 m ──────► # ◄────── 1.5 m ──────► I
                              #
      ◄──────────────── 3.0 m ────────────────►
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Symmetrical object-image spacing. A person standing $1.5\text{ m}$ in front of a mirror sees their virtual image located $1.5\text{ m}$ behind the glass. The total separation between the person and their virtual image is $1.5\text{ m} + 1.5\text{ m} = 3.0\text{ m}$.

### Motion and Relative Velocity

Suppose a student walks directly toward a plane mirror at a constant walking speed of $v = 1.0\text{ m/s}$.
- Every second, the student's distance to the mirror shrinks by $1.0\text{ m}$.
- Because $v_{\text{image}} = v_{\text{object}}$ relative to the mirror, the virtual image also approaches the mirror at $1.0\text{ m/s}$ from behind.
- **Relative to the student:** Because both object and image are closing the gap simultaneously, the distance between them shrinks by $1.0\text{ m} + 1.0\text{ m} = 2.0\text{ m}$ every second:

$$v_{\text{relative}} = v_{\text{object}} + v_{\text{image}} = 1.0\text{ m/s} + 1.0\text{ m/s} = 2.0\text{ m/s}$$

### Lateral Inversion: The Reversal of Left and Right

Stand before a bathroom mirror and raise your **right hand**. In the mirror, the figure facing you raises its **left hand**. If you wink with your left eye, the mirror figure winks with its right eye. This apparent horizontal reversal is called **lateral inversion**.

> [!NOTE]
> **Why doesn't a mirror flip you upside down?**
> A mirror does not actually swap left and right; it reverses **front and back along the perpendicular axis**! Every point on your body projects straight forward onto the mirror and appears directly opposite at the same depth behind the glass. Because your front and back are inverted relative to your line of sight, your brain interprets this front-to-back flip as a left-to-right swap.

Because of lateral inversion, printed text held before a mirror appears reversed:
- Vertically symmetrical letters such as **A**, **H**, **I**, **M**, **O**, **T**, **U**, **V**, **W**, **X**, and **Y** look unchanged when laterally inverted.
- Asymmetrical letters such as **B**, **C**, **D**, **E**, **F**, **G**, **J**, **K**, **L**, **N**, **P**, **Q**, **R**, **S**, and **Z** appear distinctly backward.

## Back to the Ambulance: Resolving the Mystery

Mr. Carter pulled a marker and a clipboard from the ambulance cabin. On the white sheet, he wrote the word **AMBULANCE** in large standard block letters. Holding it before the side mirror, Liam inspected the reflection:
- The word was reversed from right to left: the initial letter **A** appeared at the far right, and the final letter **E** appeared at the far left.
- Asymmetrical letters like **B**, **C**, and **E** were flipped horizontally into unfamiliar shapes.

Next, Mr. Carter wrote the pre-reversed lettering on a fresh sheet:

$$\text{ECNALUBMA}$$

When Liam held this pre-inverted sheet up to the mirror, the reflection instantly resolved into:

$$\text{AMBULANCE}$$

Liam grinned in realization: "A double inversion restores the original! The lettering is painted pre-inverted so that when a driver ahead looks through their rearview mirror, lateral inversion flips it a second time, presenting a perfectly readable word."

Mr. Carter nodded. "In a medical emergency, seconds matter. If the word were printed normally, a driver checking their mirror would need several seconds to decode the reversed text. With pre-inversion, they recognize the emergency vehicle immediately and yield the lane."

## Part D: Minimum Mirror Height for Full-Length Viewing

To view your entire body—from the top of your head to the soles of your shoes—in a vertical wall mirror, do you require a full-length mirror equal to your height?

The answer is **no**. You require a mirror whose vertical height is **only half of your total height**:

$$\text{Minimum Mirror Height} = \frac{\text{Height of Person}}{2} = \frac{H}{2}$$

```
    Eye/Head O ------►-------------◄-------------#   <- Top edge of mirror (Height H)
             :         \                         #
             :                  \                #
             :                           \       #
             :                                  /#   <- Bottom edge of mirror (Height H/2)
             :                          /
             :                 /
             :        /
             _   <- Feet (Height 0)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Minimum mirror height geometry. A ray from the observer's feet reflects at the midpoint between the eyes and the ground to enter the eye. A ray from the top of the head reflects at eye level. Thus, a mirror measuring exactly half the observer's physical height is sufficient to view the full body.

**Mathematical Proof:**
Let a student have height $H$, with their eyes located at height $E$ above the floor.
1. **Ray from the top of the head:** Travels horizontally to the mirror and reflects straight back into the eyes. The top edge of the mirror must be positioned at:
   $$\text{Top edge} = E + \frac{H - E}{2} = \frac{H + E}{2}$$
2. **Ray from the feet:** Originates at height $0$, travels upward to the mirror, and reflects into the eyes at height $E$. Because $\angle i = \angle r$, the ray strikes the mirror exactly halfway between the floor and the eyes:
   $$\text{Bottom edge} = \frac{E}{2}$$
3. **Required length of the mirror:**
   $$\text{Length} = \text{Top edge} - \text{Bottom edge} = \frac{H + E}{2} - \frac{E}{2} = \frac{H}{2}$$

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The required mirror height $H/2$ is **completely independent of how far you stand from the mirror**. Whether you stand $1\text{ metre}$ or $5\text{ metres}$ away, as you walk backward, your image shrinks in visual perspective at the exact same rate as the mirror frame shrinks, preserving full-body visibility throughout.

## Part E: Multiple Images in Inclined Mirrors

When an object is placed between two plane mirrors tilted toward each other at an angle $\theta$, light reflects repeatedly between the two reflective surfaces, producing **multiple virtual images**.

The total number of images $n$ formed by two plane mirrors inclined at an angle $\theta$ is given by:

$$n = \left(\frac{360^\circ}{\theta}\right) - 1 \quad \text{(when } 360^\circ/\theta \text{ is an even integer)}$$

*(If $360^\circ/\theta$ is an odd integer, $n = (360^\circ/\theta) - 1$ when the object lies symmetrically on the angle bisector, and $n = 360^\circ/\theta$ when placed asymmetrically.)*

| Angle $\theta$ ($^\circ$) | Ratio $360^\circ / \theta$ | Number of Images $n = (360^\circ / \theta) - 1$ |
|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| $90^\circ$ | $4$ | $3$ |
| $60^\circ$ | $6$ | $5$ |
| $45^\circ$ | $8$ | $7$ |
| $30^\circ$ | $12$ | $11$ |
| $72^\circ$ | $5$ (odd) | $4$ (for symmetrical placement) |

```
                        # Mirror M1 (#)
                        #
                        #
            I1          #           O
                        #
                        #
                        #                   Mirror M2 (=)
  ......................+===========================
                        :
                        :
                        :
            I3          :           I2
                        :
                        :
                        :
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Two plane mirrors oriented at $90^\circ$. The object $O$ produces image $I_1$ in $M_1$, image $I_2$ in $M_2$, and a third combined image $I_3$ resulting from sequential reflections, giving $n = (360/90) - 1 = 3$ images.

**Parallel Mirrors ($	heta = 0^\circ$): Infinite Reflections**

When two plane mirrors face each other in perfect parallel alignment ($\theta = 0^\circ$), the ratio $360^\circ / 0^\circ \to \infty$. Light bounces infinitely back and forth between the two mirrors, creating an **infinite corridor of virtual images**.
In a barber shop or dressing room with facing mirrors, you see this endless queue of receding images. Each successive image appears dimmer than the last because glass and silver coatings absorb roughly $4\%$ to $10\%$ of light energy on each bounce.

**The Kaleidoscope:** A **kaleidoscope** consists of two or three long rectangular plane mirrors fitted inside a tube at angles of $60^\circ$. Loose beads and coloured glass pieces placed at the far end reflect repeatedly between the mirrors, producing dazzling, perfectly symmetrical hexagonal geometric patterns that shift with every rotation of the cylinder.

## Part F: The Periscope

A **periscope** is an optical viewing instrument designed to observe objects over barriers or around corners where direct line of sight is obstructed.

A basic periscope consists of a long, rigid tube with two plane mirrors mounted at each turn:
- The two mirrors are arranged **parallel to each other**.
- Each mirror is mounted at an angle of **$45^\circ$** to the tube's longitudinal axis.

```
                ┌─────────────┐
                │   Mirror 1  │
                │             │
Object  ----►----------\      │
                │      ↓      │
                │      ↓      │
                │      ↓      │
                │      ↓      │
                │      ↓      │
                │      ↓      │
                │      \------------►----- Observer's Eye
                │   Mirror 2  │
                │             │
                └─────────────┘
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Ray trajectory through a periscope. Parallel light rays from a distant object strike Mirror 1 at $i = 45^\circ$, reflect downward through $90^\circ$, travel down the tube, strike Mirror 2 at $i = 45^\circ$, and reflect horizontally into the observer's eye.

Because light undergoes **two successive reflections**, the lateral inversion introduced by the first mirror is canceled by the second mirror. The resulting image seen by the observer is **upright (erect) and true-to-life**. Periscopes are essential tools in submarines (to view sea surface ships while remaining submerged) and military observation bunkers.

## Part G: Practical Applications of Plane Mirrors

1. **Domestic dressing mirrors:** For daily grooming, dressing, and personal hygiene.
2. **Barber shops and salons:** Paired facing mirrors allow patrons to inspect haircuts from the rear.
3. **Dentist's inspection mirrors:** Small, angled plane (or concave) mirrors mounted on slender rods allow dentists to inspect the hidden rear surfaces of teeth.
4. **Submarine periscopes:** Provide safe reconnaissance above the waterline.
5. **Kaleidoscopes:** Create aesthetic kaleidoscopic art and pattern designs.
6. **Solar cookers:** Flat adjustable mirror flaps reflect additional sunlight into solar cooking ovens to boost internal temperature.
7. **Scientific mirror writing:** Renowned Renaissance polymath Leonardo da Vinci wrote his scientific journals in mirrored script (from right to left), which can be read easily by holding a plane mirror beside the manuscript.

## Part H: Solved Examples

### Example 1: Object and Image Distance
A student stands $2.0\text{ m}$ directly in front of a flat plane mirror. Determine:
1. The distance of the virtual image from the mirror.
2. The total distance between the student and their virtual image.

**Solution:**
1. In a plane mirror, image distance equals object distance:
   $$\text{Distance of image from mirror} = 2.0\text{ m}$$
2. Total distance between student and image:
   $$\text{Total separation} = 2.0\text{ m} + 2.0\text{ m} = 2 \times 2.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.0\text{ m}}$$
   *Verification:* $2 \times 2.0\text{ m} = 4.0\text{ m} \; \checkmark$

### Example 2: Moving Closer to the Mirror
A person standing $3.0\text{ m}$ from a plane mirror walks $1.0\text{ m}$ closer to the glass. Calculate the initial and final separation between the person and their virtual image.

**Solution:**
- **Initial state:** $d_1 = 3.0\text{ m} \implies \text{Separation}_1 = 2 \times 3.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{6.0\text{ m}}$.
- **Final state:** $d_2 = 3.0\text{ m} - 1.0\text{ m} = 2.0\text{ m} \implies \text{Separation}_2 = 2 \times 2.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.0\text{ m}}$.
- The gap between person and image decreases by $6.0\text{ m} - 4.0\text{ m} = 2.0\text{ m}$, which is exactly $2 \times 1.0\text{ m}$. $\checkmark$

### Example 3: Speed of Image and Relative Speed
A girl walks directly toward a plane mirror at $1.0\text{ m/s}$. Find:
1. The velocity of her image relative to the mirror.
2. The velocity of her image relative to the girl herself.

**Solution:**
1. Relative to the mirror, the image moves forward at the same speed as the girl:
   $$v_{\text{image, mirror}} = \mathbf{1.0\text{ m/s}}$$
2. Relative to the girl, both she and the image approach each other at $1.0\text{ m/s}$:
   $$v_{\text{relative}} = 1.0\text{ m/s} + 1.0\text{ m/s} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ m/s}}$$
   *Verification:* Since total distance is $2d$, its time derivative is $2 \times (dd/dt) = 2 \times 1.0 = 2.0\text{ m/s} \; \checkmark$

### Example 4: Walking at 1.5 m/s for 2 Seconds
A man initially stands $5.0\text{ m}$ from a plane mirror. He walks directly toward the mirror at a speed of $1.5\text{ m/s}$ for $2.0\text{ seconds}$. What is his final separation from his virtual image?

**Solution:**
$$\text{Distance walked} = 1.5\text{ m/s} \times 2.0\text{ s} = 3.0\text{ m}$$
$$\text{New distance to mirror } d_2 = 5.0\text{ m} - 3.0\text{ m} = 2.0\text{ m}$$
$$\text{Final separation} = 2 \times 2.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.0\text{ m}}$$
*Verification:* Initial separation was $2 \times 5.0\text{ m} = 10.0\text{ m}$. Distance closed $= 10.0\text{ m} - 4.0\text{ m} = 6.0\text{ m}$. Speed $= 6.0\text{ m} / 2.0\text{ s} = 3.0\text{ m/s} = 2 \times 1.5\text{ m/s} \; \checkmark$

### Example 5: Minimum Mirror for a 1.6 m Tall Person
Calculate the minimum vertical height of a plane mirror required for a person $1.6\text{ m}$ tall to see their full reflection.

**Solution:**
$$\text{Minimum height} = \frac{H}{2} = \frac{1.6\text{ m}}{2} = \mathbf{0.8\text{ m}}$$
*Geometric check:* Assuming eyes are at $1.5\text{ m}$, the top edge is at $(1.6 + 1.5)/2 = 1.55\text{ m}$ and bottom edge is at $1.5/2 = 0.75\text{ m}$. Height $= 1.55 - 0.75 = 0.8\text{ m} \; \checkmark$

### Example 6: Minimum Mirror for a 1.8 m Tall Person
Calculate the minimum mirror height for an athlete $1.8\text{ m}$ tall. Does this requirement change if the athlete steps backward by $2\text{ m}$?

**Solution:**
$$\text{Minimum height} = \frac{1.8\text{ m}}{2} = \mathbf{0.9\text{ m}}$$
The requirement does **not** change. The required mirror height is mathematically independent of the observer's distance from the mirror. $\checkmark$

### Example 7: Calculating the Number of Multiple Images
Find the number of images formed by two plane mirrors inclined at the following angles:
1. $\theta = 90^\circ$
2. $\theta = 60^\circ$
3. $\theta = 45^\circ$
4. $\theta = 30^\circ$

**Solution:**
Using $n = (360^\circ / \theta) - 1$:
1. $\theta = 90^\circ \implies n = (360/90) - 1 = 4 - 1 = \mathbf{3\text{ images}}$
2. $\theta = 60^\circ \implies n = (360/60) - 1 = 6 - 1 = \mathbf{5\text{ images}}$
3. $\theta = 45^\circ \implies n = (360/45) - 1 = 8 - 1 = \mathbf{7\text{ images}}$
4. $\theta = 30^\circ \implies n = (360/30) - 1 = 12 - 1 = \mathbf{11\text{ images}}$
*Verification:* Smaller angles yield an increasing number of images. $\checkmark$

### Example 8: Determining the Inclination Angle
Two plane mirrors produce exactly $5$ virtual images of an illuminated object placed between them. Find the angle of inclination between the two mirrors.

**Solution:**
$$n = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} - 1 \implies 5 = \frac{360^\circ}{\theta} - 1$$
$$\frac{360^\circ}{\theta} = 6 \implies \theta = \frac{360^\circ}{6} = \mathbf{60^\circ}$$
*Verification:* $(360/60) - 1 = 6 - 1 = 5 \; \checkmark$

### Example 9: Two Facing Parallel Mirrors
Two parallel plane mirrors $A$ and $B$ are separated by a distance of $4.0\text{ m}$. A candle is placed between them, $1.0\text{ m}$ from mirror $A$ (and thus $3.0\text{ m}$ from mirror $B$). Find the distance of the first image in mirror $A$ and the first image in mirror $B$ from the candle.

**Solution:**
Set up a one-dimensional coordinate axis with Mirror $A$ at $x = 0$, Candle $O$ at $x = +1.0\text{ m}$, and Mirror $B$ at $x = +4.0\text{ m}$.
1. **First image in Mirror $A$ ($I_{A1}$):**
   Located $1.0\text{ m}$ behind Mirror $A$ at coordinate $x = -1.0\text{ m}$.
   $$\text{Distance from candle} = 1.0 - (-1.0) = \mathbf{2.0\text{ m}}$$
2. **First image in Mirror $B$ ($I_{B1}$):**
   Located $3.0\text{ m}$ behind Mirror $B$ at coordinate $x = 4.0 + 3.0 = +7.0\text{ m}$.
   $$\text{Distance from candle} = 7.0 - 1.0 = \mathbf{6.0\text{ m}}$$

```
                  A       B
                  #       #
    I           I # O     #     I   I
                  #       #
   -7          -1 0 1     4     7   9   (x in metres)
```

> **Figure 3.6** – Successive images between parallel mirrors. The candle at $x = 1$ forms first images at $x = -1$ and $x = 7$, which subsequently act as virtual objects for further reflections.

*Verification:* $2 \times 1.0\text{ m} = 2.0\text{ m}$ and $2 \times 3.0\text{ m} = 6.0\text{ m} \; \checkmark$

### Example 10: Analog Clock Seen in a Mirror
When an analog clock is viewed in a plane mirror, its hands appear to read $3:00$. What is the true physical time?

**Solution:**
Due to lateral inversion across the vertical $12-6$ axis:
$$\text{Actual time} = 12:00 - \text{Mirrored reading} = 12:00 - 3:00 = \mathbf{9:00}$$
*Verification:* At 9:00, the hour hand points left to 9 and minute hand points straight up to 12. In a mirror, the hour hand flips horizontally to point right to 3, appearing as 3:00. $\checkmark$

### Example 11: Mirror Clock Reading 8:20
A clock viewed in a plane mirror appears to show $8:20$. Determine the true time.

**Solution:**
Using the standard 11-hour, 60-minute reflection formula:
$$\text{Actual time} = 11:60 - 8:20 = \mathbf{3:40}$$
*Verification:* $11:60 - 3:40 = 8:20 \; \checkmark$

### Example 12: Shifting the Mirror
A person stands $2.0\text{ m}$ from a plane mirror. While the person remains stationary, the mirror is shifted $1.0\text{ m}$ farther away. By what distance does the virtual image shift?

**Solution:**
1. **Initial image position:**
   Distance from person to mirror $= 2.0\text{ m}$. Image is $2.0\text{ m}$ behind mirror ($4.0\text{ m}$ from person).
2. **New image position:**
   Distance from person to mirror $= 2.0 + 1.0 = 3.0\text{ m}$. Image is $3.0\text{ m}$ behind mirror ($6.0\text{ m}$ from person).
3. **Shift of the image:**
   $$\Delta x_{\text{image}} = 6.0\text{ m} - 4.0\text{ m} = \mathbf{2.0\text{ m}}$$
   *Rule:* When a plane mirror moves by distance $\Delta d$, the image moves by $2\Delta d = 2 \times 1.0\text{ m} = 2.0\text{ m}$. $\checkmark$

### Example 13: The AMBULANCE Lettering Inspection
Analyze the letter sequence of the word **AMBULANCE** when reflected in a plane mirror.

**Solution:**
The 9-letter word is `A - M - B - U - L - A - N - C - E`.
Under lateral inversion:
1. **Sequence order:** Reverses from left-to-right to right-to-left: the letters appear in the order `E - C - N - A - L - U - B - M - A`.
2. **Individual letter symmetry:**
   - Letters **A**, **M**, and **U** possess vertical axes of symmetry and retain their upright shapes.
   - Letters **B**, **L**, **N**, **C**, and **E** lack vertical symmetry and are individually flipped horizontally.
   Because both the word order and letter shapes invert, the word must be painted pre-inverted as $\text{ECNALUBMA}$ for drivers ahead to read it correctly. $\checkmark$

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing plane mirror images are smaller when you stand farther away.** ❌ The image is always physically identical in height to the object ($m = 1$). A distant image appears smaller merely due to angular perspective, exactly as a real person walking away appears visually smaller.
2. **Mistake 2: Assuming a virtual image can be focused onto a paper screen.** ❌ A virtual image has no physical rays converging behind the mirror; it exists purely as perceived straight-line extensions of diverging rays.
3. **Mistake 3: Confusing lateral inversion with vertical inversion.** ❌ Plane mirrors do not flip objects upside down. The image is strictly upright (erect). Only front-to-back coordinates are reversed relative to the mirror plane.
4. **Mistake 4: Forgetting to double the distance when calculating object-to-image separation.** ❌ The distance between object and image is $2d$, not $d$.
5. **Mistake 5: Assuming the minimum mirror height depends on observer distance.** ❌ The required mirror height for full-length viewing is always $H/2$, regardless of whether you stand $1\text{ m}$ or $10\text{ m}$ from the wall.

---

## Quick Recap

- A **plane mirror** produces an image that is **virtual, erect, the exact same size ($m = +1$)**, and located at an **equal distance behind the mirror ($v = u$)**.
- Total distance between object and image is **$2d$**.
- If an object approaches a mirror at speed $v$, its image approaches the mirror at $v$, closing the gap at a relative speed of **$2v$**.
- **Lateral inversion** is the front-to-back reversal that manifests as a left-to-right swap, which is why emergency vehicles paint $\text{ECNALUBMA}$ on their hoods.
- The minimum vertical height of a plane mirror for full-body viewing is **$H/2$**, and this height is independent of observer-to-mirror distance.
- Two plane mirrors inclined at angle $\theta$ produce **$n = (360^\circ / \theta) - 1$** images (for even $360^\circ / \theta$). Parallel mirrors ($\theta = 0^\circ$) generate an infinite corridor of reflections.
- A **periscope** uses two parallel mirrors inclined at $45^\circ$ to look over barriers; double reflection produces an erect final image.
- For analog clock reflections: $\text{Actual time} = 11:60 - \text{Mirrored time}$.

## Practice Questions

1. A person stands $3.5\text{ m}$ directly in front of a flat plane mirror. Determine:
   - (a) The distance of the image from the mirror surface.
   - (b) The total separation between the person and their virtual image.
2. A student walks directly toward a plane mirror at $0.8\text{ m/s}$.
   - (a) What is the speed of his image relative to the mirror?
   - (b) What is the speed of the image relative to the student?
   - (c) If he starts $4.0\text{ m}$ from the mirror, how far will he be from his image after $2.0\text{ seconds}$?
3. What is the minimum height of a flat wall mirror required for a girl $1.5\text{ m}$ tall to see her entire body? Does this required height change if she steps backward by $2.0\text{ m}$?
4. Calculate the number of virtual images formed by two plane mirrors inclined at:
   - (a) $\theta = 20^\circ$
   - (b) $\theta = 36^\circ$
5. Two plane mirrors produce $11$ images of a candle flame. What is the inclination angle between them?
6. Two parallel plane mirrors $A$ and $B$ face each other across a gap of $6.0\text{ m}$. An object is placed $2.0\text{ m}$ from mirror $A$ (between the mirrors). Find the distance from the object to the first image formed in mirror $A$ and the first image formed in mirror $B$.
7. An analog wall clock viewed in a plane mirror appears to display $4:25$. What is the actual physical time?
8. State whether each statement is True or False, providing a one-sentence scientific justification:
   - (a) A plane mirror image physically shrinks as an observer walks farther away.
   - (b) The image formed in a plane mirror can be captured on a piece of paper placed behind the glass.
   - (c) Lateral inversion causes an image to appear upside down.
   - (d) The minimum mirror height needed to see one's full body increases as the person steps farther back.
9. Explain why the word **AMBULANCE** is painted in laterally inverted form on the front hood of an emergency vehicle. Which letters in this word appear identical before and after lateral inversion?
10. Describe the arrangement of the two plane mirrors inside a standard periscope. Is the final observed image erect or inverted? Name one practical application of a periscope.

### Answers

1. (a) Image distance from mirror $= \mathbf{3.5\text{ m}}$ ($v = u$).
   (b) Separation from person $= 3.5\text{ m} + 3.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{7.0\text{ m}}$.
   *Verification:* $2 \times 3.5\text{ m} = 7.0\text{ m} \; \checkmark$

2. (a) Speed relative to mirror $= \mathbf{0.8\text{ m/s}}$.
   (b) Speed relative to student $= 0.8\text{ m/s} + 0.8\text{ m/s} = \mathbf{1.6\text{ m/s}}$.
   (c) Distance covered in $2\text{ s} = 0.8 \times 2 = 1.6\text{ m}$. New distance to mirror $= 4.0 - 1.6 = 2.4\text{ m}$. Distance to image $= 2 \times 2.4\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.8\text{ m}}$.
   *Verification:* Initial separation $= 8.0\text{ m}$. Distance closed $= 8.0 - 4.8 = 3.2\text{ m}$. Speed $= 3.2 / 2 = 1.6\text{ m/s} \; \checkmark$

3. Minimum mirror height $= H / 2 = 1.5\text{ m} / 2 = \mathbf{0.75\text{ m}}$.
   The required height does **not** change when she moves $2.0\text{ m}$ farther away; the geometric ratio depends solely on her body height.
   *Verification:* Ray geometry confirms $H/2$ holds for any object distance $\checkmark$

4. (a) $360^\circ / 20^\circ = 18$ (even) $\implies n = 18 - 1 = \mathbf{17\text{ images}}$.
   (b) $360^\circ / 36^\circ = 10$ (even) $\implies n = 10 - 1 = \mathbf{9\text{ images}}$.
   *Verification:* $18 \times 20^\circ = 360^\circ$ and $10 \times 36^\circ = 360^\circ \; \checkmark$

5. $n = (360^\circ / \theta) - 1 \implies 11 = (360^\circ / \theta) - 1 \implies 360^\circ / \theta = 12 \implies \theta = 360^\circ / 12 = \mathbf{30^\circ}$.
   *Verification:* $(360/30) - 1 = 12 - 1 = 11 \; \checkmark$

6. Set coordinate system: Mirror $A$ at $x = 0$, Object at $x = 2.0\text{ m}$, Mirror $B$ at $x = 6.0\text{ m}$.
   - First image in $A$: At $x = -2.0\text{ m}$. Distance from object $= 2.0 - (-2.0) = \mathbf{4.0\text{ m}}$ ($2 \times 2.0\text{ m}$).
   - First image in $B$: At $x = 6.0 + 4.0 = 10.0\text{ m}$. Distance from object $= 10.0 - 2.0 = \mathbf{8.0\text{ m}}$ ($2 \times 4.0\text{ m}$).
   *Verification:* $2 \times 2 = 4\text{ m}$ and $2 \times 4 = 8\text{ m} \; \checkmark$

7. $\text{Actual time} = 11:60 - 4:25 = \mathbf{7:35}$.
   *Verification:* $11:60 - 7:35 = 4:25 \; \checkmark$

8. (a) **False** ❌. The image remains identical in physical dimensions ($m = 1$); it only appears smaller due to visual perspective.
   (b) **False** ❌. Plane mirror images are virtual; no physical rays intersect behind the mirror.
   (c) **False** ❌. Lateral inversion is a front-to-back flip perceived as a horizontal left-to-right swap; the image is strictly erect.
   (d) **False** ❌. The required mirror height is universally $H/2$, regardless of viewing distance.

9. The lettering is painted pre-inverted so that when viewed in the rearview mirror of a preceding vehicle, lateral inversion flips the letters back into their normal readable orientation ($	ext{AMBULANCE}$), allowing drivers to react quickly without hesitation. The letters **A**, **M**, and **U** have vertical symmetry and appear unchanged when reversed.

10. The two plane mirrors are mounted **parallel to each other**, each tilted at an angle of **$45^\circ$** to the tube axis. The final image is **erect** because two successive reflections cancel the lateral inversion. Periscopes are used in submarines to survey surface waters from beneath the waves.
