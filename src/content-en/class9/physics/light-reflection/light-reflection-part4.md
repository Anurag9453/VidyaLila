# Topic 4: Spherical Mirrors: Concave and Convex

In the previous topic, we explored plane mirrors. A flat mirror behaves with absolute, unchanging predictability: its image is always virtual, erect, of identical size to the object ($m = +1$), and positioned as far behind the glass as the object stands in front. A plane mirror has only one optical "personality."

What happens, however, **when a reflecting surface is not flat, but curved?**
In this topic, we step into the captivating domain of **spherical curved mirrors**. We will explore their two fundamental forms—concave and convex—define their essential geometric landmarks (pole, centre of curvature, principal axis, aperture, and principal focus), establish the relationship $f = R/2$, and master the four standard principal rays used to track reflected light.

Throughout this topic, we assume **small-aperture mirrors and paraxial rays** (rays traveling very close to the principal axis), ensuring sharp focal points and clean geometric relationships.

---

## The Polished Soup Spoon at the Diner

During a weekend hiking trip in the mountains, Maya and Leo stopped at a roadside diner for a bowl of hot soup. The diner owner, Mr. Miller, handed Maya a brand-new, brightly polished stainless steel soup spoon.

Holding the spoon up before her face, Maya peered into the polished, bowl-shaped inner surface. She gasped in surprise: her reflected face was **greatly reduced in size and completely upside down**!

Puzzled, Maya flipped the spoon over to inspect the outer bulging back. Her face was visible once again, but with a startling transformation: the image was still **small**, but now it was **perfectly upright**!

Maya called out to Mr. Miller, "How can a single spoon show my face upside down on one side, but right-side up on the other? What kind of optical trick is this?"

Mr. Miller smiled warmly. "There is no trickery here, Maya—only curved mirror physics! A spoon is actually two completely different spherical mirrors joined back to back. The inner hollow bowl curves inward, while the outer back bulges outward. In physics, each surface has a distinct name, its own focal point, and completely different optical properties. Let us learn the vocabulary and geometry of curved mirrors first, and then we will explain the mystery of your spoon."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Spherical mirror** | A curved mirror whose reflecting surface forms part of a hollow sphere of glass or metal | The polished bowl or back of a stainless steel spoon |
| **Concave mirror** | A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface curves inward toward the centre of the sphere (*converging mirror*) | The inner bowl of a soup spoon; a dental inspection mirror |
| **Convex mirror** | A spherical mirror whose reflecting surface bulges outward away from the centre of the sphere (*diverging mirror*) | The outer back of a soup spoon; a car side rearview mirror |
| **Pole ($P$)** | The geometric centre of the reflecting surface of a spherical mirror | The exact midpoint of the curved spoon bowl |
| **Centre of curvature ($C$)** | The centre of the hollow sphere of which the spherical mirror forms a cut section | The geometric origin of the imaginary sphere |
| **Radius of curvature ($R$)** | The linear distance from the pole to the centre of curvature ($R = PC$) | The radius of the parent sphere |
| **Principal axis** | The straight reference line passing through the pole ($P$) and centre of curvature ($C$) | An axis perpendicular to the mirror surface at $P$ |
| **Aperture** | The effective linear diameter or width of the reflecting circular boundary of the mirror | The rim-to-rim width of the spoon bowl |
| **Principal focus ($F$)** | The specific point on the principal axis where rays parallel and close to the axis converge (or appear to diverge) after reflection | The bright, concentrated sunspot formed by a concave reflector |
| **Focal length ($f$)** | The linear distance from the pole to the principal focus ($f = PF$) | $f = R/2$ for small apertures |
| **Real focus** | A focus where light rays physically intersect in front of the mirror (concave mirror) | A hot focal spot capable of scorching paper |
| **Virtual focus** | A focus behind the mirror from which diverging reflected rays only appear to originate (convex mirror) | The apparent focal origin behind a convex mirror |

## Part A: What a Spherical Mirror Is and Its Two Basic Forms

Imagine a large, hollow glass sphere—like a hollow crystal ornament or bowling ball. Suppose you slice off a shallow curved cap from this sphere, exactly like slicing a thin section of peel from an orange.

This curved shell has two distinct surfaces:
1. An **inner concave surface** that curves inward toward the interior of the sphere.
2. An **outer convex surface** that bulges outward into surrounding space.

If you apply a reflective metallic coating (silver or aluminum) to one of these surfaces and protect it with backing paint, you create a **spherical mirror**:

> A **spherical mirror** is a mirror whose reflecting surface is a section of a hollow sphere.

Depending on which side is silvered and polished, we obtain two distinct classes of mirrors:

### 1. Concave Mirror

A spherical mirror whose **inner hollow surface is reflecting** (curving inward toward the centre of curvature) is a **concave mirror**.

Because a concave mirror causes a parallel bundle of incident light rays to converge to a single focal point in front of it, it is also called a **converging mirror**.
A simple mnemonic: the inward dip resembles an entrance into a **cave**.

### 2. Convex Mirror

A spherical mirror whose **outer bulging surface is reflecting** (curving outward away from the centre of curvature) is a **convex mirror**.

Because a convex mirror causes a parallel bundle of incident light rays to diverge and spread outward into space, it is also called a **diverging mirror**.

## Back to the Diner: Resolving the First Part of the Spoon Mystery

Maya's soup spoon demonstrates both mirror types in a single handheld utensil:
- The **inner hollow bowl** curves inward: it is a **concave (converging) mirror**.
- The **outer rounded back** bulges outward: it is a **convex (diverging) mirror**.

Because the two sides bend light along fundamentally different geometric paths, they project completely different images into Maya's eyes.
Mr. Miller explained: "A convex surface always produces a virtual, upright, and diminished image. A concave surface, on the other hand, can form real inverted images or magnified upright images depending on your distance from the bowl. To understand why your face was inverted, we must map out the mirror's geometric reference points."

Let us define each landmark with scientific precision:

### 1. Pole ($P$)

> The **pole ($P$)** is the geometric centre of the reflecting spherical surface of the mirror.

The pole lies directly on the physical glass/metal surface. In optical geometry, the pole serves as the **origin ($0,0$)** from which all physical distances are measured along the axis.

### 2. Centre of Curvature ($C$) and Radius of Curvature ($R$)

> The **centre of curvature ($C$)** is the geometric centre of the hollow sphere of which the mirror forms a part.
> The **radius of curvature ($R$)** is the radius of that hollow sphere, representing the linear distance from the pole to the centre of curvature:
> $$R = PC$$

Crucial geometric facts:
- The centre of curvature $C$ is **not located on the mirror surface**.
- For a **concave mirror**, $C$ lies **in front** of the reflecting surface (on the hollow side).
- For a **convex mirror**, $C$ lies **behind** the reflecting surface (inside the sphere).
- Any line drawn from $C$ to any point on the spherical mirror surface is a radius, and therefore acts as a **true perpendicular normal** to the surface at that point!

### 3. Principal Axis

> The **principal axis** is the straight reference line passing through both the pole ($P$) and the centre of curvature ($C$) of the spherical mirror.

The principal axis is perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the mirror surface at the pole $P$, serving as our central horizontal coordinate axis.

### 4. Aperture

> The **aperture** is the effective linear diameter of the circular boundary (rim) of the spherical mirror.

In Figures 4.1 and 4.2, the straight-line segment $AB$ spanning the outermost rim of the mirror represents its aperture. The aperture determines the light-gathering capacity of the mirror.

### 5. Principal Focus ($F$) and Focal Length ($f$)

When a bundle of rays parallel and close to the principal axis strikes a spherical mirror:

**For a Concave Mirror:**
The reflected rays converge inward and physically intersect at a single common point $F$ on the principal axis in front of the mirror. This point is the **principal focus ($F$)**.
Because light rays physically pass through $F$, a concave mirror possesses a **real focus**. Placing a white card at $F$ reveals a sharp, intensely bright spot of light.

> **Focal Length ($f$):**
> The linear distance from the pole of the mirror to its principal focus:
> $$f = PF$$

### 6. Relationship Between $f$ and $R$: $f = R/2$

For spherical mirrors with small apertures, the principal focus $F$ lies **exactly halfway between the pole $P$ and the centre of curvature $C$**:

$$f = rac{R}{2} \quad \iff \quad R = 2f$$

$$PF = FC = f \quad 	ext{and} \quad PC = R = 2f$$

**Derivation Outline:**
Consider a paraxial ray traveling parallel to the principal axis, striking the mirror at point $M$. The radius $CM$ is the normal to the surface at $M$.
By the Law of Reflection, the angle of incidence $ngle i = ngle r$.
Because the incident ray is parallel to the principal axis, the alternate interior angle $ngle MCF = ngle i$.
In $\Delta CFM$, since $ngle FMC = ngle r = ngle i = ngle MCF$, the triangle is isosceles, meaning $CF = FM$.
For mirrors of **small aperture**, point $M$ lies very close to pole $P$, so $FM pprox FP$.
Therefore, $CF pprox FP = f$, proving that $F$ bisects the radius $PC = R$:
$$f = rac{R}{2}$$

**Why Small Aperture Matters:**
If a mirror has a large aperture, peripheral rays striking near the outer rim reflect across the axis closer to the pole than paraxial rays do. This failure of all rays to converge at a single sharp focus is called **spherical aberration**, which blurs the image. To ensure crisp optical performance and maintain $f = R/2$, we strictly work with **small-aperture mirrors**.

## Part D: Comprehensive Comparison: Concave vs. Convex Mirrors

| Feature | Concave Mirror | Convex Mirror |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical profile** | Reflecting surface curves inward | Reflecting surface bulges outward |
| **Reflective boundary** | Inner hollow spherical surface | Outer spherical surface |
| **Optical action** | **Converging** (brings parallel rays together) | **Diverging** (spreads parallel rays apart) |
| **Focus type** | **Real focus** (rays physically intersect) | **Virtual focus** (rays appear to diverge from point) |
| **Location of $F$ and $C$** | **In front** of the reflecting surface | **Behind** the reflecting surface |
| **Field of view** | Relatively narrow | **Extremely wide** (compresses a large panorama) |
| **Everyday examples** | Spoon bowl, makeup mirror, headlight reflector | Spoon back, car side mirror, security corner dome |

The wide field of view of a convex mirror is invaluable: because outgoing rays diverge, light from a vast angular landscape is captured and directed into the driver's eye, displaying a broad panorama in a compact pane.

## Part E: New Cartesian Sign Convention (Class 10 Extension)

To apply algebraic formulas rigorously to mirrors, physicists adopt the **New Cartesian Sign Convention**:

1. The **pole ($P$)** is taken as the optical coordinate **origin $(0,0)$**.
2. The **principal axis** serves as the horizontal **$x$-axis**.
3. Light is assumed to travel from **left to right**.
4. Distances measured in the **direction of incident light (to the right of $P$) are POSITIVE (+)**.
5. Distances measured **against the direction of incident light (to the left of $P$) are NEGATIVE (−)**.
6. Vertical heights measured **above the principal axis are POSITIVE (+)**; heights **below the axis are NEGATIVE (−)**.

| Mirror Type | Sign of $R$ | Sign of $f$ | Position of $F$ and $C$ |
|---|:---:|:---:|---|
| **Concave Mirror** | **Negative (−)** | **Negative (−)** | In front of mirror (left of $P$) |
| **Convex Mirror** | **Positive (+)** | **Positive (+)** | Behind mirror (right of $P$) |

> [!NOTE]
> The relationship $f = R/2$ holds with algebraic signs:
> For concave: $f = (-R)/2 = -f$. For convex: $f = (+R)/2 = +f$.

## Part F: Activity: Estimating the Focal Length of a Concave Mirror

Because the Sun is at an immense astronomical distance, sunlight rays reaching Earth are effectively parallel to each other. When directed along the principal axis of a concave mirror, these rays converge directly at the principal focus $F$.

**Procedure:**
1. Mount the concave mirror on a bench facing an open outdoor window illuminated by direct sunlight.
2. Hold the cardboard screen in front of the mirror. Slowly move the screen back and forth along the axis until you capture a **sharp, pinpoint, intensely bright spot of light** on the screen.
3. Using the metre ruler, measure the straight-line distance between the pole $P$ of the mirror and the bright spot on the screen.

## Part G: Solved Examples

In all examples below, mirrors possess small apertures where $f = R/2$.

### Example 1: Finding $f$ from Radius of Curvature $R$
A concave mirror has a radius of curvature $R = 30	ext{ cm}$. Calculate its focal length.

**Solution:**
$$f = rac{R}{2} = rac{30	ext{ cm}}{2} = \mathbf{15	ext{ cm}}$$
*Verification:* $R = 2f = 2 	imes 15	ext{ cm} = 30	ext{ cm} \; \checkmark$

### Example 2: Finding $R$ from Focal Length $f$
A convex mirror used in an automobile has a focal length of $12	ext{ cm}$. Determine its radius of curvature.

**Solution:**
$$R = 2f = 2 	imes 12	ext{ cm} = \mathbf{24	ext{ cm}}$$
*Verification:* $f = R/2 = 24/2 = 12	ext{ cm} \; \checkmark$

### Example 3: Spatial Positions of $C$ and $F$
A concave mirror has $R = 36	ext{ cm}$. Find the distance of focus $F$ and centre of curvature $C$ from the pole, and calculate the distance between $F$ and $C$.

   $$f = PF = rac{R}{2} = rac{36	ext{ cm}}{2} = \mathbf{18	ext{ cm}}	ext{ (in front of the mirror)}$$
2. Distance of $C$ from pole:
   $$PC = R = \mathbf{36	ext{ cm}}	ext{ (in front of the mirror)}$$
3. Distance between $F$ and $C$:
   $$FC = PC - PF = 36	ext{ cm} - 18	ext{ cm} = \mathbf{18	ext{ cm}} = f$$

### Example 4: Identifying Mirror Type from Ray Behaviour
1. Parallel rays strike Mirror A and converge to a point $8	ext{ cm}$ in front of the mirror surface. Identify the mirror type and determine its $f$ and $R$.

*Verification:* $16/2 = 8	ext{ cm}$ and $12/2 = 6	ext{ cm} \; \checkmark$

### Example 8: Tracing Three Standard Rays

**Solution:**
1. The parallel ray reflects to pass through focus $F$, intersecting the principal axis at $d = \mathbf{10	ext{ cm}}$ in front of the pole.
2. $C$ is located at $R = 2f = 2 	imes 10	ext{ cm} = \mathbf{20	ext{ cm}}$ in front of the pole. A ray passing through $C$ strikes normally and **rebounds along its own path**.
3. A ray passing through $F$ emerges **parallel to the principal axis** after reflection.
*(If this same ray struck a convex mirror of $f = 10	ext{ cm}$, it would diverge as though originating from $F$, $10	ext{ cm}$ behind the glass).* $\checkmark$

### Example 10: Using $PF = FC = f$
1. In a concave mirror, the linear distance from pole $P$ to centre of curvature $C$ is $50	ext{ cm}$. Find $f$ and distance $FC$.
2. In another curved mirror, distance $PF = 9	ext{ cm}$. Find distance $FC$ and total radius $PC$.

**Solution:**
1. $PC = R = 50	ext{ cm} \implies f = PF = 50/2 = \mathbf{25	ext{ cm}}$, and $FC = PC - PF = 50 - 25 = \mathbf{25	ext{ cm}}$.

### Example 11: Unit Conversion and Ray 4 Geometry

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing concave with convex mirrors.** ❌ Remember: *concave* forms a hollow **cave** curving inward (spoon bowl); *convex* bulges outward (spoon back).
2. **Mistake 2: Measuring the radius of curvature from the focus.** ❌ $R = PC$ is measured strictly **from the pole to the centre of curvature**. The distance from focus to centre is only $FC = R/2$.
3. **Mistake 3: Writing $f = R$ instead of $f = R/2$.** ❌ The focal length is half the radius. If $R = 40	ext{ cm}$, then $f = 20	ext{ cm}$, not $40	ext{ cm}$.
4. **Mistake 4: Drawing the focus of a convex mirror in front of the mirror.** ❌ A convex mirror possesses a **virtual focus** situated **behind** the reflective surface. Placing it in front mistakenly turns it into a concave mirror.
5. **Mistake 5: Confusing the pole with the centre of curvature.** ❌ Pole $P$ is a physical point on the mirror surface; centre $C$ is the geometric origin of the parent sphere located $R$ units away.
6. **Mistake 6: Assuming concave focal length is positive under Cartesian conventions.** ❌ Because a concave mirror's focus lies in front (to the left of $P$, opposing incoming light), $f$ and $R$ are **strictly negative** (e.g., $f = -10	ext{ cm}$).
7. **Mistake 7: Applying $f = R/2$ to large-aperture mirrors.** ❌ $f = R/2$ is strictly valid for **small apertures** where marginal rays do not cause spherical aberration.

## Quick Recap

- A **spherical mirror** is a reflecting surface forming a segment of a hollow sphere.
- **Concave mirror (converging):** Curves inward; converges parallel rays to a **real focus** in front of the mirror.
- **Convex mirror (diverging):** Bulges outward; diverges parallel rays from a **virtual focus** behind the mirror.
- Key landmarks: **Pole ($P$)**, **Centre of Curvature ($C$)**, **Radius of Curvature ($R = PC$)**, **Principal Axis**, **Aperture ($AB$)**, **Principal Focus ($F$)**, **Focal Length ($f = PF$)**.
- Fundamental geometric relation for small apertures: **$f = R/2$**, meaning $PF = FC = f$ and $R = 2f$.
- The **Four Standard Rays**:
  1. Parallel to axis $	o$ reflects through focus $F$ (or appears to diverge from $F$).
  2. Through focus $F 	o$ reflects parallel to principal axis.
  3. Through centre $C 	o$ reflects back along its own path ($ngle i = 0^\circ$).
  4. At pole $P 	o$ reflects symmetrically across the principal axis ($ngle r = ngle i$).
- **Cartesian sign convention:** Concave mirror has **negative** $f$ and $R$; convex mirror has **positive** $f$ and $R$.
- Convex mirrors provide a far wider **field of view** than concave or plane mirrors.
