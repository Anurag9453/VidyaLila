# Topic 1: Light, Shadows, and the Pinhole Camera

In our study of sound, we discovered that **mechanical waves strictly require a material medium**: air, water, or a solid lattice. If an electric bell rings inside an evacuated glass jar, no sound can be heard. Yet space is vastly empty—nearly 150 million kilometres of vacuum separate Earth from the Sun—and despite this cosmic void, sunlight warms our planet and illuminates our world every day. 

The entity crossing this vacuum is **light**—a form of radiant energy that stimulates our retina and produces the sensation of vision. In this opening topic, we investigate what light is, how fast it travels, why it moves in straight lines, how shadows form with sharp and fuzzy regions, and how a tiny pinhole in a dark box can project a vivid, inverted picture of the outside world.

---

## The School Shadow Puppet Theatre

On the evening of the annual school arts exhibition, Elena and Leo were assisting the stage director, Mr. Miller, behind the main auditorium screen. A translucent white muslin sheet was suspended across the stage opening. Positioned several metres behind the sheet was an electric filament lamp.

Between the lamp and the muslin screen, Leo held cardboard cutout puppets mounted on slender wooden rods. Elena watched from the front rows of the darkened auditorium, where only the silhouette cast on the screen was visible.

Leo picked up a cutout of a roaring lion, measuring exactly 10 cm from head to tail. As he moved it back and forth between the lamp and the screen, Elena called out two curious observations:

- When Leo moved the lion **closer to the lamp**, its shadow on the auditorium screen grew **enormous**, but the outline of the lion's mane became **hazy, soft, and blurry**.
- When Leo moved the lion **closer to the muslin sheet**, the shadow shrank until it was **nearly the exact size of the cardboard cutout**, and its edges became **razor-sharp**.

Elena asked, "Mr. Miller, why does the shadow become huge and blurry near the bulb, but small and crisp near the screen?"

Mr. Miller smiled. "That is an excellent observation. The answer lies in the fundamental geometry of light: **light travels in straight lines**, and our lamp is an extended source rather than an infinitesimally small mathematical point. Let us explore the physics of light first, and then we will calculate the exact dimensions of Leo's lion shadow."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Light** | A form of radiant energy that travels through space and stimulates the retina to produce vision | Sunlight, light emitted by a LED bulb |
| **Luminous object** | An object that generates and emits its own light | The Sun, stars, burning candle flame, glowing firefly |
| **Non-luminous object** | An object that does not generate light; it becomes visible only by reflecting light into our eyes | The Moon, a printed book, a tabletop, a mirror |
| **Transparent** | A material that allows light to pass through almost completely, permitting clear visibility | Clear window glass, pure water, still air |
| **Translucent** | A material that transmits light diffusely, scattering it so objects behind cannot be seen clearly | Frosted glass, tracing paper, tissue paper |
| **Opaque** | A material that absorbs or reflects all incident light, allowing none to pass through | A wooden board, a sheet of metal, a brick wall |

| **Light year** | A unit of astronomical distance equal to the distance light travels in one Julian year | $\approx 9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$; used for interstellar distances |
| **Ray** | A narrow straight line showing the path of light, marked with an arrowhead showing direction | A straight line with an arrowhead ($-\rightarrow$) |
| **Beam** | A collection or bundle of many light rays | A flashlight beam, a spotlight beam |
| **Shadow** | A dark region formed behind an opaque object where light rays are obstructed | Your silhouette cast on the pavement in the sun |

| **Penumbra** | The partially illuminated outer fringe of a shadow where only part of the light source is blocked | The soft, blurry border surrounding a shadow |

## Part A: What Light Is and How Materials Interact With It

### 1. Light Is Energy and Needs No Material Medium

**Light** is an electromagnetic wave and a form of radiant energy. When light enters our eyes and falls on the retina, photochemical reactions trigger nerve impulses sent to the brain, producing the sensation of sight.

The single most striking physical difference between light and sound is their propagation requirement:

- **Sound** is a mechanical vibration of particles. It strictly requires a material medium (air, water, metal) and **cannot propagate through a vacuum**.
- **Light requires no material medium.** Light travels effortlessly across empty space. Sunlight crosses 150 million kilometres of interplanetary vacuum to sustain life on Earth. An astronaut on the surface of the Moon sees the bright Sun against an inky black sky, yet cannot hear a companion speak without two-way radio headsets.

Light can also traverse transparent material media such as air, water, oil, and glass, although its speed is slightly reduced in denser media (which leads to refraction, as we will study in later chapters).

### 2. Luminous and Non-Luminous Objects

Objects in the universe are divided into two fundamental optical categories based on whether they produce light:

| Luminous Objects (Emit Their Own Light) | Non-Luminous Objects (Reflect Incident Light) |
|---|---|
| The Sun, distant stars | The Moon, orbiting planets, asteroids |
| A glowing incandescent filament, LED, fluorescent tube | A textbook, a wooden desk, a chair |
| A burning candle flame, an oil lamp flame | A plane mirror, a polished silver plate |
| A glowing firefly (*bioluminescence*) | A sheet of notebook paper, our human skin |
| An active smartphone display screen | A switched-off smartphone screen |

A natural question arises: if a textbook emits no light of its own, **how do our eyes see it?**
When ambient light from the Sun or an overhead lamp strikes the printed pages, the paper scatters (reflects) that light in all directions. A portion of this scattered light enters our pupil and focuses onto the retina. In a pitch-black room with zero light sources, the book is completely invisible because there are no light rays to be reflected.

The **Moon** is a classic non-luminous body. Although the full Moon shines brilliantly in the night sky, it produces no light of its own. It acts as an enormous cosmic reflector, bouncing incident sunlight toward Earth. A mirror operates on the exact same principle: it is non-luminous, merely redirecting the light that strikes its polished silver surface.

### 3. Transparent, Translucent, and Opaque Materials

When a beam of light strikes a material boundary, the substance responds in one of three ways:

| Material Type | Behaviour of Light | Everyday Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Transparent** | Transmits nearly all incident light with negligible scattering; objects behind can be seen distinctly | Optical glass, clean still water, clear air, acrylic plastic |
| **Translucent** | Transmits light partially while scattering rays in random directions; shapes behind appear blurred | Frosted privacy glass, butter paper, tracing paper, thin cloth |
| **Opaque** | Blocks all light by absorption or reflection; no light penetrates through | Hardwood board, thick cardboard, stone, steel plate |

Two practical nuances must be kept in mind:

- **Thickness matters:** A single sheet of tissue paper allows some diffuse light to pass through, whereas a thick stack of cardboard blocks light completely. Even clear ocean water becomes pitch-black several hundred metres beneath the surface because cumulative absorption extinguishes all visible light.
- **Shadow formation:** Only **opaque** (and to a lesser degree, translucent) objects cast distinct shadows, because they prevent the rectilinear rays of light from reaching the surface behind them.

## Part B: The Speed of Light

Light travels at the ultimate speed limit of the physical universe. In a vacuum, the speed of light is denoted by the universal constant $c$:

$$c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} = 300,000,000\text{ m/s} \approx 300,000\text{ km/s}$$

In air at standard temperature and pressure, light travels at practically the same speed ($2.997 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$, a negligible reduction of less than $0.03\%$).

Compare this cosmic speed with the speed of sound in air ($v_{\text{sound}} \approx 340\text{ m/s}$):

$$\frac{c}{v_{\text{sound}}} = \frac{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}}{340\text{ m/s}} \approx 8.8 \times 10^5 \approx 900,000$$

Light travels nearly **nine hundred thousand times faster than sound**! This explains why you see the flash of lightning instantaneously across the horizon, but hear the rolling rumble of thunder several seconds later.

Using the kinematic relation $\text{time} = \text{distance} / \text{speed}$:

- **From the Sun to Earth:** Distance $d \approx 1.5 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$ ($150\text{ million km}$).
  $$t = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = 500\text{ s} = 8\text{ minutes } 20\text{ seconds}$$
  When you gaze at the afternoon Sun, you are seeing sunlight that left the solar surface 8 minutes and 20 seconds ago.
- **From the Moon to Earth:** Distance $d \approx 3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m}$.
  $$t = \frac{3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = 1.28\text{ s}$$
  Moonlight reaches Earth in barely over one second.

### The Light Year: A Unit of Distance, Not Time

Because astronomical distances between stars are staggering when measured in metres or kilometres, astronomers employ a larger unit:

> **1 light year (ly)** is the total **distance** that a ray of light traverses through vacuum in one Julian year ($365.25\text{ days}$), equivalent to approximately **$9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$**.

Although the name contains the word "year", **a light year is strictly a unit of length/distance**, never time. The closest star system to our Solar System, Proxima Centauri, lies approximately $4.24\text{ light years}$ away, meaning its light takes $4.24\text{ years}$ to reach Earth.

## Part C: Rectilinear Propagation of Light

In a homogeneous, isotropic transparent medium (such as uniform air or pure water), **light travels strictly in straight lines**. This property is known as the **rectilinear propagation of light** (*rectus* = straight, *linea* = line).

### Experimental Evidence

1. **Formation of sharp shadows:** When an opaque barrier is placed in the path of light, a dark shadow forms behind it with well-defined boundaries. If light could bend readily around macroscopic obstacles (like sound waves bend around doors and corners), light would curve behind the obstacle and eliminate the shadow entirely.
2. **Beams in dusty or foggy air:** When sunlight enters a dark room through a narrow chink in a window blind, dust particles scatter light into our eyes, illuminating a perfectly straight, razor-sharp beam of light.
3. **The straight vs. bent tube experiment:** Looking through a long, straight hollow cylinder at a distant candle flame, the flame is clearly visible. If the tube is bent into an elbow or curve, the flame immediately vanishes from sight because light rays travel straight and strike the inner wall of the tube rather than turning around the bend.

```
 Straight tube (candle flame is visible):

   (o)[========================]  * Candle flame
   Eye <----------- Light ray ----------

 Bent tube (candle flame is blocked):

   (o)==========                                                      ==========  * Candle flame
   (Light travels in straight lines; obstructed by the tube bend)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – The straight vs. bent tube demonstration. The candle flame is seen only through a straight tube, proving that light propagates along straight lines in a uniform medium.

### Rays and Beams of Light

To represent optical pathways geometrically:
- A **ray** of light is an idealized, infinitely narrow straight line along which light energy travels, drawn with an arrowhead indicating the direction of propagation.
- A **beam** of light is a bundle composed of many parallel, diverging, or converging rays:
  - **Parallel beam:** All rays travel parallel to one another. (Light from distant sources like the Sun forms an effectively parallel beam at Earth's surface.)
  - **Convergent beam:** Rays originate from different directions and meet (converge) at a single focus point.
  - **Divergent beam:** Rays originate from a single localized point and spread out (diverge) over an expanding wavefront, such as light radiating from a small torch bulb.

```
 Parallel beam:

 -------------------------------->
 -------------------------------->
 -------------------------------->

 Convergent beam (rays meet at focus P):

 -------\
         ------\
 ------------------------------> * P
         ------/
 -------/

 Divergent beam (rays radiate outwards from source S):

                  /------------->
           /------
 S *----------------------------->
           \------
                  \------------->
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Three geometric configurations of light beams: parallel, convergent, and divergent. In each diagram, arrowheads denote the direction of energy flow.

## Part D: How Shadows Are Formed

### 1. Point Source: Sharp Shadows

A shadow is the geometric silhouette formed on a surface behind an opaque object that intercepts a beam of light. The physical structure and boundary sharpness of the shadow depend fundamentally on two parameters:
1. The physical dimensions of the light source (a point source vs. an extended source).
2. The relative distances between the light source, the opaque object, and the intercepting screen.

Consider first an idealized **point source** of light—a source whose physical dimensions are so small that it can be treated as a single geometric point. Light rays diverge from this single origin in all directions.

Rays that graze the outermost edges of the opaque puppet continue in straight paths until they strike the screen, defining the exact perimeter of the shadow. Inside this perimeter, all light rays are completely obstructed. Outside it, full illumination falls on the screen. Consequently, a point source produces a **sharp, well-defined shadow with no blurry borders**.

```
                                                              │
                                                         -----#  <- Upper shadow boundary
                                                --------/     #
                                     ----------/              #
                            --------/                         #
                 -----#----/                                  #
        --------/     #                                       #
  *----/              #                                       #  <- Complete shadow (30 cm)
        --------\     #                                       #
Lamp             -----#----\                                  #
                            --------\                         #
                  Puppet             ----------\              #
                                                --------\     #
                                                         -----#  <- Lower shadow boundary
                                                              │
                                                            Screen
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Shadow formation by a point source of light. When the lamp-to-puppet distance is $20\text{ cm}$ and the lamp-to-screen distance is $60\text{ cm}$, a $10\text{ cm}$ puppet casts a sharp shadow $30\text{ cm}$ tall on the screen (magnification factor of 3).

### 2. Shadow Size: Why Distance Changes Everything

In Figure 1.3, the diverging rays form two similar geometric triangles:
1. The smaller triangle formed by the lamp and the height of the puppet.
2. The larger triangle formed by the lamp and the height of the shadow cast on the screen.

Because these triangles are geometrically similar, the ratio of their corresponding sides is strictly equal:

$$\frac{\text{Height of Shadow}}{\text{Height of Object}} = \frac{\text{Distance from Lamp to Screen}}{\text{Distance from Lamp to Object}}$$

$$\text{Height of Shadow} = \text{Height of Object} \times \frac{D_{\text{screen}}}{d_{\text{object}}}$$

This fundamental ratio explains the behaviour observed by Leo and Elena:
- **As the object approaches the lamp** ($d_{\text{object}}$ decreases), the denominator shrinks, causing the shadow height to **grow dramatically**.
- **As the object moves away from the lamp toward the screen** ($d_{\text{object}} \to D_{\text{screen}}$), the ratio approaches 1, and the shadow shrinks toward the true physical size of the object. When the object rests directly against the screen, the shadow is exactly equal in size to the object itself.

### 3. Extended Source: Umbra and Penumbra

A real light bulb or fluorescent tube is not an infinitesimal point; it is an **extended source** having measurable physical dimensions. Every individual point across the surface of the glowing filament acts as an independent point emitter.

Because rays originate from different positions across the lamp, they cast shadows whose boundaries are slightly offset on the screen. This creates two distinct regions in the shadow:

- **Umbra** (*Latin for shade*): The central core region of total darkness. From any point within the umbra, the light source is completely occluded by the opaque object; no direct light reaches this zone.
- **Penumbra** (*Latin: paene = almost, umbra = shade*): The outer annular region of partial darkness surrounding the umbra. From within the penumbra, only a fraction of the extended light source is occluded; direct rays from other portions of the lamp illuminate this zone, creating a soft, blurry gradient.

```
                                                              │
                                                           ---│:
                                                     -----/   │:
                                              ------/         │: <- Penumbra (partial shadow)
                                       ------/                │:
                                ------/             ----------│#
                          -----/ ------------------/          │#
            ----------#---------/                             │#
  O--------/ -----/   #                                       │#
  O   ------/         #                                       │# <- Umbra (total darkness)
  O--------\ -----\   #                                       │#
            ----------#---------\                             │#
Lamp                      -----\ ------------------\          │#
                  Puppet        ------\             ----------│#
                                       ------\                │:
                                              ------\         │: <- Penumbra (partial shadow)
                                                     -----\   │:
                                                           ---│:
                                                            Screen
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Shadow cast by an extended light source. The region marked `#` is the umbra (total obstruction). The bordering regions marked `:` represent the penumbra (partial illumination), where rays from the upper half of the lamp reach the screen while rays from the lower half are blocked.

> [!NOTE]
> **A shadow is never a reflection!** A shadow is a region where light energy has been intercepted and blocked by an opaque obstacle. Reflection, by contrast, occurs when light strikes a surface and bounces back into the same medium.

## Back to the Shadow Show: Resolving the Mystery

Let us now solve the puzzle Elena and Leo encountered on the stage with Mr. Miller.
The lamp on the stage has a filament diameter of several centimetres, making it an extended source.

**Case 1: Puppet close to the lamp**
- Distance from lamp to puppet: $d_1 = 20\text{ cm}$.
- Distance from lamp to screen: $D = 60\text{ cm}$.
- Height of puppet: $h_o = 10\text{ cm}$.

Using our similar triangles ratio:

$$\text{Shadow height} = 10\text{ cm} \times \frac{60\text{ cm}}{20\text{ cm}} = 10\text{ cm} \times 3 = 30\text{ cm}$$

Because the puppet is located close to the extended bulb, the divergence angle of the rays originating from opposite edges of the bulb is large. The penumbra expands into a wide, prominent border, making the lion's mane look **hazy and blurry**.

**Case 2: Puppet moved close to the muslin screen**
- Distance from lamp to puppet: $d_2 = 50\text{ cm}$.
- Distance from lamp to screen: $D = 60\text{ cm}$.

$$\text{Shadow height} = 10\text{ cm} \times \frac{60\text{ cm}}{50\text{ cm}} = 10\text{ cm} \times 1.2 = 12\text{ cm}$$

Because the puppet is now positioned $50\text{ cm}$ from the lamp (and merely $10\text{ cm}$ from the screen), rays grazing the puppet converge over the short $10\text{ cm}$ gap. The penumbra becomes vanishingly narrow, leaving the shadow **crisp, sharp, and nearly true to size ($12\text{ cm}$ vs. $10\text{ cm}$)**.

Mr. Miller concluded with a practical directing tip:
"If you want a gigantic, dramatic shadow beast for a spooky scene, place the puppet close to the lamp—accepting that its outline will be soft. If you want a sharp, detailed silhouette that mimics fine puppetry, hold the puppet as close to the muslin screen as possible!"

## Part E: Eclipses: Cosmic Shadows

An astronomical eclipse is simply **shadow formation operating on a planetary scale**, resulting from the rectilinear propagation of light from an extended source (the Sun).

**1. Solar Eclipse (Eclipse of the Sun):**

A **solar eclipse** occurs when the Moon passes directly between the Sun and the Earth during a New Moon phase, casting its shadow upon the Earth's surface.

Because the Sun is an enormous extended light source, the Moon's shadow cone divides into an umbra and a penumbra:
- **Total Solar Eclipse:** Observers situated within the narrow path of the Moon's **umbra** on Earth experience total darkness in broad daylight. The solar disk is entirely blocked, revealing the delicate solar corona.
- **Partial Solar Eclipse:** Observers situated within the broader **penumbra** zone see only a portion of the solar disk covered by the dark silhouette of the Moon.

```
                                                            │
  -------------------------->                               │
                                                            │
  -------------------------->                        -------│:
                                      --------------/       │:  <- Penumbra: Partial eclipse
  --------------------------> #------\                      │:
                              #       --------------\       │:
  --------------------------> #                      -------│#  <- Umbra: Total solar eclipse
                              #       --------------/       │:
  --------------------------> #------/                      │:
                                      --------------\       │:
  -------------------------->                        -------│:
                            Moon                            │
  -------------------------->                               │
                                                            │
       Sunlight                                           Earth
```

> **Figure 1.5** – Geometry of a solar eclipse (not to scale). Sunlight converging past the Moon creates a dark central umbral cone where it touches Earth's surface, surrounded by an extensive penumbral region.

> [!WARNING]
> Never observe the Sun directly with the naked eye or through ordinary sunglasses during any phase of an eclipse. Intense solar infrared and ultraviolet rays can burn retinal tissue permanently (*solar retinopathy*). Always use certified eclipse glasses (ISO 12312-2) or an indirect projection method such as a pinhole projector.

**2. Lunar Eclipse (Eclipse of the Moon):**

A **lunar eclipse** occurs when the Earth passes directly between the Sun and the Moon during a Full Moon phase, casting Earth's vast shadow onto the lunar surface.

```
                                                         │
  ------------------------>                              │
                                                  -------│:
                                   --------------/       │: <- Penumbra
  ------------------------> #-----/                      │:
                            #             ---------------│#
  ------------------------> #            /               │# <- Umbra (Total lunar eclipse)
                            #            \               │#
  ------------------------> #-----\       ---------------│#
                                   --------------\       │:
  ------------------------>                       -------│: <- Penumbra
                           Earth                         │
                                                        Moon
```

> **Figure 1.6** – Geometry of a lunar eclipse (not to scale). Earth blocks sunlight from reaching the Moon. When the entire Moon enters Earth's deep umbra, it appears dim and coppery-red due to sunlight refracted through Earth's atmosphere.

When the entire Moon enters Earth's umbral cone, a **total lunar eclipse** occurs. During totality, the Moon does not vanish completely; instead, it glows with a dim, eerie coppery-red color. This occurs because sunlight skimming Earth passes through its dense atmospheric rim: blue light is scattered away, while red wavelengths are gently refracted into Earth's shadow cone and illuminate the Moon.

## Part F: The Pinhole Camera

A **pinhole camera** is a simple optical imaging device consisting of a light-tight rectangular enclosure with a tiny circular aperture (pinhole) pierced through one end and a translucent screen (such as oiled tracing paper or frosted glass) on the opposite wall.

When the pinhole is directed toward a brightly illuminated outdoor object (such as a tree or building), an **inverted (upside-down), real image** appears on the screen.

```
                           Pinhole       Screen
  #-\                         │             │
  #  ---\                     │             │
  #      ----\                │             │
  #           ----\           │             │
  #                ----\      │             │
  #                     ---\  │             │
  #--------------------------> o-\-----------#  <- Base of tree
Tree                          │  ---\       #
                              │      ----\  #
                              │           --#  <- Top of tree (inverted!)
                              │             │
                              │             │
                              │             │
  <--------------------------><------------->
         Object distance (u)   Screen distance (v)
```

> **Figure 1.7** – Ray diagram of a pinhole camera. Rays from the top of the tree pass through the narrow pinhole and continue in a straight line to strike the bottom of the screen. Rays from the base of the tree strike the top of the screen, creating an inverted real image.

The formation of an inverted image is direct, undeniable proof of the **rectilinear propagation of light**:
- A light ray originating from the topmost leaf of the tree travels in a straight line through the tiny aperture and strikes the lower portion of the translucent screen.
- A light ray originating from the bottom of the tree trunk travels straight through the aperture to strike the upper portion of the screen.
- Because all rays must intersect and cross at the tiny aperture, the orientation of the image is completely inverted vertically and laterally.

The image produced by a pinhole camera is **real** because the light rays physically intersect at the screen; the image can be caught on a physical piece of paper.

### Pin-Hole Size: Sharpness vs. Brightness

The optical performance of a pinhole camera involves an inherent compromise between image clarity and illumination:

- **When the pinhole is very small:** Each point on the object sends an extremely narrow cone of light rays through the pinhole, illuminating a tiny, distinct patch on the screen. The resulting image is **exceptionally sharp and clear**, but **dim**, because very little light energy enters the camera.
- **When the pinhole is enlarged:** A wide pinhole acts as a cluster of multiple adjacent pinholes. Overlapping rays from different parts of the object illuminate overlapping spots on the screen. The image becomes **much brighter**, but noticeably **blurred and fuzzy**.

$$\text{Small pinhole} \implies \text{Sharp but dim} \qquad \vert \qquad \text{Large pinhole} \implies \text{Bright but blurry}$$

### Image Dimensions and Similar Triangles

Referring to the geometry of Figure 1.7, the straight rays passing through the pinhole form two vertically opposite, similar triangles:
1. Object height $h_o$ at object distance $u$ from the pinhole.
2. Image height $h_i$ at screen distance $v$ from the pinhole.

By geometric similarity:

$$\frac{\text{Height of image } (h_i)}{\text{Height of object } (h_o)} = \frac{\text{Screen distance from pinhole } (v)}{\text{Object distance from pinhole } (u)}$$

The **magnification ($m$)** is defined as:

$$m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{v}{u}$$

From this formula, two fundamental operational rules emerge:
- **Increasing the screen distance ($v$):** Moving the screen further back from the pinhole increases $v$, resulting in a **larger image**, though the image becomes dimmer as the same light energy spreads across a greater area.
- **Increasing the object distance ($u$):** Moving the camera further away from the object increases $u$, resulting in a **smaller image**.

### Practical Applications of Pinhole Projection

1. **Safe observation of solar eclipses:** Projecting the Sun's image onto a card inside a darkened pinhole viewer allows observers to track the progression of an eclipse with zero risk of retinal injury.
2. **Camera Obscura:** Historical artists (such as Vermeer and Da Vinci) utilized room-sized *camerae obscurae* to project landscapes and architecture onto tracing paper for accurate perspective drafting.
3. **Natural pinhole images under tree canopies:** On a sunny afternoon, the overlapping gaps between dense leaves in a tree canopy act as natural pinholes, casting dozens of miniature circular or crescent-shaped images of the solar disk onto the shaded sidewalk below.

## Part G: Solved Examples

In all calculations below, the speed of light in vacuum/air is taken as $c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$.

### Example 1: Travel Time of Sunlight to Earth
The mean distance from the Sun to the Earth is approximately $1.5 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}$ ($150\text{ million km}$). Calculate the time required for sunlight to reach Earth.

**Solution:**
$$\text{Distance } d = 1.5 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}, \quad c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$$
$$t = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{1.5 \times 10^{11}\text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = 0.5 \times 10^3\text{ s} = 500\text{ s}$$

Converting to minutes and seconds:
$$500\text{ s} = 8\text{ minutes and } 20\text{ seconds} \quad (8 \times 60 = 480\text{ s}, \; 500 - 480 = 20\text{ s})$$
$$\text{Check: } 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} \times 500\text{ s} = 1.5 \times 10^{11}\text{ m} \; \checkmark$$

### Example 2: Travel Time of Moonlight to Earth
The mean orbital distance of the Moon from Earth is $3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m}$. Calculate how long reflected moonlight takes to reach an observer on Earth.

**Solution:**
$$t = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = 1.28\text{ s}$$
$$\text{Check: } 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} \times 1.28\text{ s} = 3.84 \times 10^8\text{ m} \; \checkmark$$

### Example 3: Time Taken by Light to Cross a 6 m Room
How long does light take to traverse an ordinary living room measuring $6\text{ m}$ in length?

**Solution:**
$$d = 6\text{ m}$$
$$t = \frac{6\text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = 2 \times 10^{-8}\text{ s} = 20\text{ nanoseconds } (0.00000002\text{ s})$$
Because $20\text{ ns}$ is far beneath the threshold of human perception, light appears to fill the room instantaneously when a wall switch is flipped.
$$\text{Check: } 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} \times 2 \times 10^{-8}\text{ s} = 6\text{ m} \; \checkmark$$

### Example 4: Distance Traveled by Light in 1 Microsecond (1 µs)
Calculate the distance traversed by a laser pulse in $1\text{ microsecond}$ ($1\text{ }\mu\text{s} = 10^{-6}\text{ s}$).

**Solution:**
$$d = c \times t = (3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}) \times 10^{-6}\text{ s} = 300\text{ m}$$
$$\text{Check: } \frac{300\text{ m}}{3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}} = 10^{-6}\text{ s} \; \checkmark$$

### Example 5: How Many Metres in a Light Year?
Determine the exact physical length of one light year in metres, assuming a year consists of $365.25\text{ days}$.

**Solution:**
1. Calculate the number of seconds in one year:
   $$t = 365.25 \times 24 \times 3600\text{ s} = 31,557,600\text{ s} \approx 3.156 \times 10^7\text{ s}$$
2. Multiply by the vacuum speed of light:
   $$d = c \times t = (3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}) \times (3.15576 \times 10^7\text{ s}) = 9.467 \times 10^{15}\text{ m} \approx 9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$$

In kilometres:
$$1\text{ light year} \approx 9.46 \times 10^{12}\text{ km}$$
$$\text{Dimensional verification: } [\text{Speed}] \times [\text{Time}] = (\text{m/s}) \times \text{s} = \text{m } (\text{length}) \; \checkmark$$

### Example 6: Size of a Shadow from a Point Lamp
A cardboard puppet $10\text{ cm}$ tall is placed $20\text{ cm}$ in front of a point lamp. A vertical projection sheet stands $60\text{ cm}$ from the lamp. Calculate:
1. The height of the shadow on the screen.
2. The new shadow height if the puppet is moved to a distance of $40\text{ cm}$ from the lamp.

**Solution:**
1. Using the similar triangles formula:
   $$\frac{h_{\text{shadow}}}{h_{\text{object}}} = \frac{D}{d} = \frac{60\text{ cm}}{20\text{ cm}} = 3$$
   $$h_{\text{shadow}} = 10\text{ cm} \times 3 = 30\text{ cm}$$
2. Moving the puppet to $d = 40\text{ cm}$:
   $$\frac{h_{\text{shadow}}}{10\text{ cm}} = \frac{60\text{ cm}}{40\text{ cm}} = 1.5$$
   $$h_{\text{shadow}} = 10\text{ cm} \times 1.5 = 15\text{ cm}$$
   As the puppet moves farther from the lamp ($20\text{ cm} \to 40\text{ cm}$), the shadow shrinks ($30\text{ cm} \to 15\text{ cm}$). $\checkmark$

### Example 7: Pinhole Camera Image of a Distant Tree
A pinhole camera with an internal screen distance of $12\text{ cm}$ is pointed at a tree $3\text{ m}$ tall standing $6\text{ m}$ away from the aperture. Calculate the height of the image formed on the translucent screen.

**Solution:**
$$h_o = 3\text{ m}, \quad u = 6\text{ m}, \quad v = 12\text{ cm} = 0.12\text{ m}$$
Using the magnification formula:
$$m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{v}{u}$$
$$h_i = h_o \times \frac{v}{u} = 3\text{ m} \times \frac{0.12\text{ m}}{6\text{ m}} = 0.06\text{ m} = 6\text{ cm}$$
The image formed is inverted and measures $6\text{ cm}$ in height.
$$\text{Check: } \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{0.06}{3} = 0.02, \quad \frac{v}{u} = \frac{0.12}{6} = 0.02 \; \checkmark$$

### Example 8: Magnification and Adjusting the Screen Distance
For the setup in Example 7:
1. Calculate the magnification $m$.
2. If the back screen is pulled outward to a distance of $24\text{ cm}$ from the pinhole, find the new image height and magnification.

**Solution:**
1. Initial magnification:
   $$m = \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{6\text{ cm}}{300\text{ cm}} = 0.02\text{ (dimensionless)}$$
2. New screen distance $v' = 24\text{ cm} = 0.24\text{ m}$:
   $$h_i' = 3\text{ m} \times \frac{0.24\text{ m}}{6\text{ m}} = 0.12\text{ m} = 12\text{ cm}$$
   $$m' = \frac{12\text{ cm}}{300\text{ cm}} = 0.04$$
   Doubling the screen depth ($12\text{ cm} \to 24\text{ cm}$) doubles both the image height ($6\text{ cm} \to 12\text{ cm}$) and the magnification ($0.02 \to 0.04$). $\checkmark$

### Example 9: Calculating the Object Distance
A pinhole camera with a depth of $10\text{ cm}$ forms a $5\text{ cm}$ high inverted image of a student who is $1.5\text{ m}$ tall. How far is the student standing from the camera aperture?

**Solution:**
$$h_i = 5\text{ cm} = 0.05\text{ m}, \quad h_o = 1.5\text{ m}, \quad v = 10\text{ cm} = 0.10\text{ m}$$
$$\frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{v}{u} \implies u = \frac{v \times h_o}{h_i} = \frac{0.10\text{ m} \times 1.5\text{ m}}{0.05\text{ m}} = \frac{0.15}{0.05} = 3\text{ m}$$
The student is standing exactly $3\text{ m}$ from the pinhole.
$$\text{Check: } \frac{v}{u} = \frac{0.10}{3} = \frac{1}{30}, \quad \frac{h_i}{h_o} = \frac{0.05}{1.5} = \frac{1}{30} \; \checkmark$$

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing the Moon is a luminous source.** ❌ The Moon produces zero light of its own. It reflects roughly $12\%$ of the sunlight incident upon its dusty regolith. It is strictly non-luminous.
2. **Mistake 2: Assuming light, like sound, requires a material medium to propagate.** ❌ Sound is a mechanical compression wave needing physical molecules; light is an oscillating electromagnetic wave that travels unhindered across the deepest vacuum of space.
3. **Mistake 3: Confusing a shadow with an optical reflection.** ❌ A shadow is a region of darkness formed where an opaque body obstructs rectilinear rays. A reflection occurs when rays strike an interface and bounce back into the original medium.
4. **Mistake 4: Thinking a larger pinhole always yields a better image in a pinhole camera.** ❌ Enlarging the pinhole increases light throughput (making the image brighter), but creates overlapping ray bundles that severely blur the focus. A tiny aperture is required for a sharp, high-resolution image.
5. **Mistake 5: Treating a light year as a measurement of elapsed time.** ❌ Despite the word "year", a light year is a fundamental unit of astronomical distance ($9.46 \times 10^{15}\text{ m}$). Elapsed time is measured in seconds, hours, or years.

## Quick Recap

- **Light** is radiant energy that produces the sensation of vision, propagating at $c = 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ in vacuum and air without requiring any material medium.
- **Luminous bodies** generate and emit their own light (Sun, flames, LEDs, fireflies); **non-luminous bodies** are seen only when they scatter or reflect ambient light (Moon, books, mirrors).
- In a homogeneous medium, light exhibits **rectilinear propagation** (travels strictly in straight lines), as demonstrated by sharp shadows, beam paths through dust, and the straight-tube test.
- A **point source** casts a completely sharp shadow. An **extended source** produces a deep central **umbra** (zero illumination) bordered by a soft, fuzzy **penumbra** (partial illumination).
- **Eclipses** are planetary shadows: a solar eclipse occurs when the Moon's shadow falls on Earth; a lunar eclipse occurs when Earth's shadow engulfs the Moon.
- A **pinhole camera** produces an inverted, real image on a screen because rectilinear rays cross at the aperture. Its magnification is given by $m = h_i / h_o = v / u$. A smaller aperture yields a sharper but dimmer image; moving the screen further back increases image size.
