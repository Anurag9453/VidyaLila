# Topic 7: Prisms, Dispersion, Rainbows, Atmospheric Refraction, and the Human Eye

In preceding topics, we explored the refraction of monochromatic light across flat rectangular boundaries and curved spherical lenses. We treated light as a unified beam with a singular refractive index in a given medium. However, sunlight and white ambient room light are not monochromatic; they are complex composite mixtures of multiple electromagnetic wavelengths spanning the visible spectrum.

When white light enters a medium whose boundary surfaces are not parallel—such as a glass prism or an airborne raindrop—each constituent wavelength travels at a slightly different speed, bending through a distinct angle. This fundamental phenomenon is called **dispersion**. In this comprehensive concluding topic, we analyze the optics of triangular prisms, the splitting and recombination of white light, atmospheric optical wonders (such as the rainbow, twinkling stars, and crimson sunsets), and the biological marvel that detects them all: the human eye, including its anatomical structure, accommodation, common vision defects, and lens corrections. We conclude with a full chapter wrap-up summarizing all key concepts of refraction.

## Rainbows at the Eye Clinic

Ruhi sat in the bright reception lobby of the community vision center awaiting her routine annual eye examination. Beside her sat her older brother, Liam, a high school physics student. Sunlight streamed through a large decorative cut-glass prism suspended in the south-facing bay window.

As the sunbeam traversed the triangular glass ornament, a brilliant, vivid ribbon of color projected onto the white tiled floor: deep red transitioning smoothly through orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, and vibrant violet.

"Look at that, Liam!" Ruhi pointed with delight. "A miniature rainbow right inside the doctor's office! Does the glass ornament paint those bright colors onto the sunlight?"

Liam smiled. "No, Ruhi. The glass does not add any paint or pigment whatsoever. Sunlight looks white, but it actually contains all those colors blended together. The triangular glass merely separates them because each color bends at a slightly different angle."

Just then, Dr. Bennett stepped out of the examination room. "Ruhi, we are ready for your vision assessment!" Inside the clinic, Dr. Bennett guided Ruhi through several tests: reading letters off the illuminated Snellen eye chart from 	ext{ m}$ away, testing near vision with a reading card, and measuring the curvature of her cornea with a digital auto-refractor.

When Dr. Bennett finished, she explained, "Your eyes are remarkably healthy, Ruhi, but your eyeball is just a tiny fraction of a millimeter longer than average from front to back. As a result, distant light rays focus slightly in front of your retina—a condition called myopia, or near-sightedness. A gentle diverging concave spectacle lens will shift the focus perfectly onto your retina."

Ruhi turned to Dr. Bennett. "So my eye works just like the lenses we learned about in school, and the decorative glass in your lobby splits light just like raindrops after an afternoon shower?"

"Precisely, Ruhi," Dr. Bennett confirmed. "Both the prism and your eye operate on the fundamental laws of refraction. Let us explore the physics behind each."

We will resolve Ruhi's eye examination in Part F. First, let us examine the optics of a triangular glass prism.

## Part A: Refraction through a Triangular Glass Prism

### Geometry of a Triangular Glass Prism

A triangular glass prism is an optical medium bounded by:
- Two identical triangular end faces that are parallel to each other.
- Three rectangular lateral surfaces that are inclined relative to each other.

The line along which two refracting rectangular faces intersect is called the **refracting edge**. The angle between the two lateral refracting faces is termed the **angle of the prism**, denoted by $. The third rectangular face, opposite the refracting angle, serves as the **base** of the prism.

### Path of a Ray through a Prism (Angle of Deviation $\delta$)

When a monochromatic ray of light strikes one refracting face obliquely:
1. **First Refraction (Air to Glass):** The ray enters from an optically rarer medium (air,  pprox 1.0$) into an optically denser medium (glass,  pprox 1.5$). By Snell's law, the ray bends **toward the normal** at the first surface ( < i$).
2. **Propagation through Glass:** The refracted ray travels through the body of the prism toward the second inclined face.
3. **Second Refraction (Glass to Air):** Upon reaching the second boundary, the ray passes from glass back into air. Here, it bends **away from the normal** ( > r_2$), where $ is the **angle of emergence**.
4. **Direction of Deviation:** Because the two refracting faces are inclined at an angle $ rather than parallel, the emerging ray does not emerge parallel to the incident ray. Instead, it suffers a net directional deflection toward the thicker base of the prism.

The total angle between the direction of the incident ray (extended forward) and the direction of the emergent ray (extended backward) is defined as the **angle of deviation**, denoted by $\delta$ (or $).

1. The angle of incidence ($): as $ increases, $\delta$ initially decreases to a minimum value (called the **angle of minimum deviation**, $\delta_{	ext{min}}$) and subsequently increases.
2. The refracting angle of the prism ($): a larger prism angle produces greater deviation.
3. The refractive index ($) of the prism material: higher optical density increases deviation.
4. The wavelength (color) of incident light: shorter wavelengths bend more.

## Part B: Dispersion of White Light into a Spectrum

### Isaac Newton's Classic Experiment

In 1665, Sir Isaac Newton performed a landmark experiment in a darkened chamber. Allowing a narrow pencil of white sunlight to enter through a small pinhole in a window shutter, he placed an equilateral triangular glass prism in its path:
- Upon exiting the prism and striking a distant white projection screen, the beam did not cast a round white spot.
- Instead, it formed an elongated, vibrant band of colors: **Red, Orange, Yellow, Green, Blue, Indigo, and Violet** (acronym: **VIBGYOR**).
- Red light was deflected the least, remaining at the upper boundary. Violet light suffered the greatest deviation, bending furthest toward the base.

Newton demonstrated that the prism did **not generate** the colors; rather, white sunlight is naturally composed of a continuous mixture of colors, and the prism acts as an optical separator.

Light is an electromagnetic wave. In vacuum, all electromagnetic wavelengths travel at an identical speed:  = 3 	imes 10^8	ext{ m/s}$.

2. In glass, red light travels faster than violet light ({	ext{red}} > v_{	ext{violet}}$).
3. Because refractive index is defined as  = c/v$, the refractive index of glass is slightly **smaller for red light and larger for violet light**:
   15990n_{	ext{red}} < n_{	ext{violet}}15990
   For crown glass: {	ext{red}} pprox 1.514$, {	ext{violet}} pprox 1.532$.
4. By Snell's law, a larger refractive index results in greater bending. Consequently, **violet light bends the most, while red light bends the least**.

### Recombining the Spectrum: Newton's Inverted Prism Experiment

To conclusively prove that the prism was not coloring the light, Newton placed an identical second prism behind the first, inverted with respect to the first (base facing upward):
1. The first prism dispersed incoming white sunlight into the colored spectrum.
2. The second inverted prism accepted the dispersed rays and refracted them in the opposite direction, canceling the angular deviation.
3. The rays converged and emerged from the second prism as a single, unified beam of **pure white light**.

This experiment confirmed that **white light is an additive mixture of the seven primary spectral color bands**.

### Selective Absorption: Why Coloured Objects Appear Coloured

An opaque object appears colored because it selectively reflects certain wavelengths of ambient light while absorbing all others:
- A red apple absorbs violet, blue, green, and yellow light, reflecting only red light into the eye.
- A leaf appears green because chlorophyll absorbs blue and red wavelengths for photosynthesis, reflecting green light.
- A white sheet reflects all incident wavelengths equally. A black surface absorbs all incident visible light, reflecting none.

## Part C: Formation of the Rainbow

A rainbow is a magnificent natural spectrum produced by the dispersion of sunlight by millions of tiny, spherical water droplets suspended in the atmosphere following a rain shower.

### Optical Mechanism inside a Raindrop (Primary Rainbow)

Each suspended spherical raindrop acts as a miniature spherical prism:
1. **Refraction and Dispersion at Entry:** Parallel white sunlight strikes the curved upper surface of a raindrop obliquely. As light enters water ( pprox 1.33$), it refracts and disperses into its constituent colors (violet bending more steeply than red).
2. **Total Internal Reflection at Rear Surface:** The dispersed light travels through the drop and strikes the back inner water-air interface at an angle exceeding the critical angle for water ( pprox 48.6^\circ$). It undergoes total internal reflection, bouncing back forward through the droplet.
3. **Refraction and Dispersion at Exit:** The reflected rays reach the front surface of the drop and emerge back into the air. Upon exiting, they undergo a final refraction, widening the angular separation between colors.

Red light emerges from the raindrop at an angle of approximately **^\circ* relative to the original incident sunbeam, while violet light emerges at approximately **^\circ*.

Because an observer standing on the ground looks upward toward raindrops at different elevations:
- Drops higher in the sky send red light into the observer's pupil (while their violet rays pass above the head).
- Drops lower in the sky send violet light into the observer's pupil (while their red rays land on the chest).
- Consequently, the **primary rainbow exhibits red along its outer arc and violet along its inner arc**.

### Conditions Required to Observe a Rainbow

1. The Sun must be shining brightly in one hemisphere of the sky.
2. Raindrops or mist must be suspended in the opposite hemisphere.
3. The observer must stand with their **back facing the Sun**, looking toward the shower.

## Part D: Atmospheric Refraction and Scattering Phenomena

### Twinkling of Distant Stars

Stars appear as point sources of light located trillions of kilometers away. As starlight traverses Earth's atmosphere:
- Earth's atmosphere consists of continuously shifting layers of air with fluctuating temperatures and optical densities.
- The refractive index of these turbulent air pockets varies constantly from millisecond to millisecond.
- As the narrow beam of starlight undergoes continuous, chaotic refraction, its apparent position and intensity entering the pupil fluctuate rapidly. This rapid fluctuation in perceived brightness is perceived as **twinkling**.
- *Why planets do not twinkle:* Planets are much closer to Earth and act as extended disc sources consisting of millions of individual light points. The intensity fluctuations from different points on a planet's disc cancel out on average, resulting in a steady, non-twinkling glow.

### Advanced Sunrise and Delayed Sunset

Because atmospheric density decreases with increasing altitude, light entering Earth's atmosphere from space encounters progressively denser optical layers:
- Rays arriving from the Sun when it is situated slightly below the true horizon bend downward toward the normal.
- An observer on Earth traces these refracted rays backward in a straight line, perceiving the Sun above the horizon.
- This causes the Sun to become visible approximately **	ext{ minutes}$ before actual geometric sunrise**, and remain visible for approximately **	ext{ minutes}$ after geometric sunset**, lengthening each daylight period by about 4 minutes.

### Why the Daytime Sky Is Blue and Sunsets Are Crimson (Rayleigh Scattering)

- **Daytime Blue Sky:** Gas molecules in the atmosphere (, O_2$) are much smaller than visible light wavelengths. According to Rayleigh's scattering law, the intensity of scattered light is inversely proportional to the fourth power of wavelength ( \propto 1/\lambda^4$). Shorter wavelengths (blue and violet) are scattered nearly ten times more intensely than longer red wavelengths, bathing the daytime sky in blue light.
- **Crimson Sunsets:** At dawn and dusk, sunlight traverses a much thicker atmospheric path to reach the observer. Nearly all shorter blue and green wavelengths are scattered away laterally along the journey, allowing only the least-scattered long red and orange wavelengths to penetrate directly to the observer's eyes.

## Part E: The Human Eye: Structure and Function

The human eye is an extraordinary natural optical camera that focuses light onto a light-sensitive neurological layer to form miniature, real, inverted images.



### Key Anatomical Components of the Human Eye

1. **Cornea:** The transparent, bulging spherical membrane forming the front exterior window of the eyeball. **Over 	ext{ to }80\%$ of the total refraction of light takes place at the curved air-cornea interface.**
2. **Iris:** A dark, circular muscular diaphragm situated behind the cornea that gives the eye its distinctive color (brown, blue, green). It regulates the quantity of light entering the eye.
3. **Pupil:** The central circular aperture in the iris. In dim lighting, the iris muscles dilate the pupil (widening it to 	ext{ mm}$) to admit more light; in intense glare, the pupil constricts ($< 2	ext{ mm}$) to prevent retinal damage.
4. **Crystalline Lens:** A transparent, flexible biconvex lens composed of fibrous, jelly-like proteins. Suspended behind the pupil by **ciliary muscles**, it provides fine, adjustable focusing for objects at varying distances.
5. **Ciliary Muscles:** Circular muscular fibers attached to the eye lens. By contracting or relaxing, they alter the curvature (focal length) of the flexible lens.
6. **Retina:** The delicate, light-sensitive inner membrane lining the rear interior surface of the eyeball. It contains roughly 120 million **rods** (highly sensitive to dim light and motion) and 6 million **cones** (sensitive to bright light and color).
7. **Optic Nerve:** A dense bundle of over one million nerve fibers that transmits electrical impulses from the retinal photoreceptors to the visual cortex of the brain.
8. **Blind Spot:** The small anatomical exit point on the retina where the optic nerve leaves the eyeball. Lacking photoreceptor cells entirely, it is insensitive to light.

### Optical Image Formation on the Retina

Light from an external object enters the cornea, passes through the aqueous humor, pupil, crystalline lens, and vitreous humor, and converges onto the retina.
The image formed on the retina is:
- **Real** (formed by physically converging rays).
- **Inverted** (upside down).
- **Diminished** (substantially smaller than the physical object).
Photoreceptor cells on the retina convert light photons into electrochemical impulses. The brain's visual cortex processes these signals, interpreting the scene right-side up.

### Power of Accommodation of the Crystalline Lens

The ability of the human eye to alter the focal length of its crystalline lens to focus clearly on both distant and nearby objects is called **accommodation**:
- **Viewing Distant Objects ( = \infty$):** The ciliary muscles **fully relax**, pulling the suspensory ligaments tight. The eye lens becomes **thin and relatively flat** with a large radius of curvature and a maximum focal length ( pprox 2.5	ext{ cm}$, equal to the eyeball diameter). Parallel rays converge cleanly on the retina without muscular strain.
- **Viewing Nearby Objects ( pprox 25	ext{ cm}$):** The ciliary muscles **contract**, releasing ligament tension. The elastic lens bulges into a **thicker, more spherical shape**, decreasing its focal length. This increased converging power brings diverging rays from a nearby book to a sharp focus on the retina.

**Limits of Healthy Vision:**
- **Far Point:** The farthest distance at which an object can be seen distinctly. For a healthy eye, the far point is at **infinity** ( = \infty$).
- **Near Point (Least Distance of Distinct Vision, $):** The minimum distance at which an object can be viewed sharply without ocular strain. For a healthy young adult,  = \mathbf{25	ext{ cm}}$.

### Persistence of Vision and Binocular Stereoscopic Depth

- **Persistence of Vision:** The visual impression on the retina does not vanish immediately when light ceases; it persists for approximately **/16	ext{ of a second}* (/bin/zsh.06	ext{ s}$). If sequential static frames are projected at a rate exceeding 16 frames per second (cinematography typically uses 24 or 60 fps), the brain perceives continuous, fluid motion.
- **Binocular Stereoscopic Vision:** Having two forward-facing eyes spaced about .5	ext{ cm}$ apart gives humans a ^\circ$ visual field and two slightly different perspectives. The visual cortex merges these dual signals into a three-dimensional representation, providing accurate depth perception and distance judgment.

## Part F: Common Vision Defects and Optical Correction

### 1. Myopia (Short-Sightedness or Near-Sightedness)

**Defect Description:** A person suffering from myopia can see nearby objects distinctly, but distant objects appear blurred and indistinct. The far point is not at infinity; it has drawn closer to a finite distance (e.g., .5	ext{ m}$).

**Underlying Physical Causes:**
1. Excessive curvature of the cornea or crystalline lens (the lens is too thick and has excessive converging power).
2. Elongation of the eyeball from front to back (the distance between lens and retina is too large).

**Optical Problem:** Parallel rays from a distant object converge too steeply, coming to a focus **in front of the retina**.

**Method of Correction:** Myopia is corrected by placing a **concave (diverging) lens** of appropriate focal length in front of the eye. The concave lens diverges incoming parallel rays slightly, making them appear to originate from the person's defective far point. The eye lens then focuses these rays precisely onto the retina.

### 2. Hypermetropia (Long-Sightedness or Far-Sightedness)

**Defect Description:** A person suffering from hypermetropia can view distant objects clearly, but nearby objects (such as reading material) appear blurred. The near point is no longer at 	ext{ cm}$; it has receded farther away (e.g., 	ext{ cm}$ or 	ext{ m}$).

**Underlying Physical Causes:**
1. Insufficient curvature of the cornea or crystalline lens (the lens is too flat and has too little converging power).
2. The eyeball is too short from front to back.

**Optical Problem:** Diverging rays from an object at the standard near point (	ext{ cm}$) are not converged sufficiently and would come to a focus **behind the retina**.

**Method of Correction:** Hypermetropia is corrected by wearing a **convex (converging) lens**. The convex lens provides additional converging power, shifting rays from 	ext{ cm}$ so they appear to originate from the person's recessed near point. The eye lens then brings them to a sharp focus on the retina.
The corrective lens has a **positive focal length and positive power ( > 0$)**.

### 3. Additional Vision Conditions: Presbyopia, Astigmatism, and Cataract

- **Presbyopia (Aging Eye):** With advancing age (usually past 40), the ciliary muscles weaken and the crystalline lens loses its natural flexibility. The near point recedes, making close-up reading difficult. It is corrected using reading glasses with weak convex lenses, or **bifocal / progressive lenses** (upper part concave for distant viewing, lower part convex for reading).
- **Astigmatism:** Caused by an irregular, non-spherical curvature of the cornea (curved like an oval rugby ball rather than a basketball). Light rays focus at different depths in horizontal and vertical planes, causing distorted vision. Corrected using **cylindrical lenses**.
- **Cataract:** With age or medical conditions, the clear protein structure of the crystalline lens becomes cloudy and opaque, causing progressive vision loss. Corrected by surgical removal of the cloudy natural lens and implantation of an artificial plastic **intraocular lens (IOL)**.

### Resolving Ruhi's Eye Camp Story

Ruhi's examination revealed mild **myopia**: her eyeball was slightly elongated, causing distant light rays to focus in front of her retina. Dr. Bennett prescribed a light pair of spectacles with **concave lenses**. When Ruhi slipped them on, distant signs and charts snapped into crisp, razor-sharp focus!

## Part H: Vision Care, Screen Hygiene, and Cornea Donation

### Healthy Vision Habits and Eye Safety

1. **Proper Reading Posture and Lighting:** Hold reading material at least 	ext{ cm}$ from your eyes under gentle, diffuse ambient illumination. Never read in a moving vehicle or in dim candlelight.
2. **Digital Eye Strain (The 20-20-20 Rule):** When using computers, tablets, or smartphones for prolonged periods, follow the **20-20-20 guideline**: every 20 minutes, take a 20-second break and gaze at an object located at least 20 feet (	ext{ m}$) away to allow your ciliary muscles to relax fully.
3. **Ultraviolet and Impact Protection:** Always wear certified UV400 sunglasses outdoors in intense sunlight to prevent long-term retinal and lens damage. Wear protective polycarbonate safety goggles in chemistry laboratories and metal/wood workshops.

Over 10 million individuals worldwide suffer from corneal blindness resulting from infections, thermal injuries, or chemical burns. Corneal transplant surgery (keratoplasty) replaces cloudy, scarred corneas with healthy donor tissue:
- Corneas must be harvested by medical teams within 6 hours of death.
- A single cornea donor can restore the precious gift of clear sight to two blind individuals.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing a prism creates new colors.**
   A glass prism does not generate or dye colors. It merely acts as a dispersive separator of the composite wavelengths already existing in white light.
2. **Mistake 2: Assuming all colors travel at different speeds in outer space.**
   In a vacuum, all electromagnetic waves travel at the exact same invariant speed ( = 3 	imes 10^8	ext{ m/s}$). Dispersion occurs only when light enters a material medium (glass, water, plastic).
3. **Mistake 3: Confusing the corrective lenses for myopia and hypermetropia.**
   - Myopia (eyes focus too close) requires a **concave (diverging, minus) lens**.
   - Hypermetropia (eyes focus too far) requires a **convex (converging, plus) lens**.
4. **Mistake 4: Thinking the image formed on the retina is upright.**
   The eye's converging optics form a real, **inverted** (upside-down) image on the retina. The visual cortex of the brain flips and interprets the neurological data right-side up.

## Quick Recap

- **Refraction in a Prism:** An incident ray bends toward the normal at the first face and away from the normal at the second face, resulting in a net deviation $\delta = i + e - A$ toward the prism base.
- **Dispersion:** The splitting of white light into constituent visible wavelengths (VIBGYOR). Violet light bends the most (highest $, slowest speed in glass); red light bends the least.
- **Rainbow Formation:** Sunlight undergoes refraction-dispersion upon entering an airborne water droplet, total internal reflection at the rear droplet wall, and refraction upon exiting.
- **Atmospheric Phenomena:** Density gradients cause light bending, resulting in twinkling stars, advanced sunrise (2 minutes), and delayed sunset (2 minutes). Rayleigh scattering produces blue daytime skies and red sunsets.
- **The Human Eye:** Light refracts primarily at the cornea and fine-focuses through the crystalline lens onto the retina to form an inverted, real, diminished image.
- **Accommodation:** Ciliary muscles adjust lens thickness to focus clearly from the near point (	ext{ cm}$) out to infinity ($\infty$).
- **Vision Correction:** Myopia (focus in front of retina) is corrected with a **concave lens** ( < 0$); hypermetropia (focus behind retina) is corrected with a **convex lens** ( > 0$).
