# Topic 1: What Is Refraction?

In our study of reflection, we observed that when light encounters a polished boundary—such as a silvered glass mirror—a major fraction of the incident beam **rebounds back into the original medium**, obeying the strict geometric rule $i = r$. However, mirrors represent only one category of optical boundaries. What occurs when light encounters an interface between two transparent media, such as air and water, or air and polished glass?

Because water and glass are transparent, light does not simply rebound at the boundary; it **transmits into the second medium**. But does it continue traveling in its original, uninterrupted straight path? The answer is both surprising and foundational to optics: the moment a light ray crosses obliquely into a new transparent medium, it **abruptly bends**. This change in the direction of propagation of light when passing obliquely from one transparent medium into another is known as **refraction**.

---

## The Swimming Pool's Optical Illusion

It was a calm late afternoon at the community aquatic centre. Leo was sitting on the tiled ledge at the shallow end of the swimming pool with his legs dangling into the clear water. The head lifeguard, Dr. Bennett, was supervising from his observation station nearby.

Leo had been staring intently at his feet for several minutes. Finally, he looked up and called out, "Dr. Bennett, this swimming pool seems to be playing tricks on my eyes!"

Dr. Bennett walked over with an amused smile. "Playing tricks? In what way, Leo?"

"Look down here," Leo said, pointing into the water. "The mosaic tiles on the pool floor look so close that the water seems barely knee-deep—perhaps half a metre at most. But when I stepped into this exact spot yesterday, the water reached all the way to my chest! And look at my legs: underwater, they appear noticeably shortened, and right at the waterline, they look sharply bent and displaced sideways, as though they have been broken in two. My legs certainly are not broken. Is the pool creating an optical illusion?"

Dr. Bennett chuckled. "The pool is not playing tricks on you, Leo. **Light** is responsible for this phenomenon. When light reflected from your feet travels upward through the water and crosses the boundary into the air, the light rays **bend away from their original path**. Your brain assumes that light always travels in perfectly uninterrupted straight lines, so your eyes trace the rays back to a position higher than where your feet actually are. That bending is called **refraction**. Let us investigate why and how light bends, and then we will calculate the exact geometric secrets behind your swimming pool's illusion."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Refraction** | The bending or change in direction of a light ray when it crosses obliquely from one transparent medium into another | A laser beam bending as it enters a tank of water from air |
| **Medium** | Any transparent substance or material through which light propagates | Air, water, crown glass, diamond, vacuum |
| **Interface (boundary)** | The physical surface separating two distinct transparent media | The flat top surface of water where air and water meet |
| **Incident ray** | The incoming light ray approaching the interface before entering the second medium | A ray of sunlight traveling through air toward a glass pane |
| **Refracted ray** | The light ray that enters the second medium and continues propagating with a changed direction | The bent light ray traveling inside water |
| **Normal ($N$)** | An imaginary reference line drawn perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the interface at the point of incidence | A vertical reference line perpendicular to a horizontal water surface |
| **Angle of incidence ($i$)** | The angle formed between the incident ray and the normal at the point of incidence | $i = 30^\circ$ measured relative to the normal |
| **Angle of refraction ($r$)** | The angle formed between the refracted ray and the normal in the second medium | $r = 19.47^\circ$ measured relative to the normal |
| **Optically rarer medium** | A transparent medium in which light travels comparatively faster | Air relative to water; water relative to glass |
| **Optically denser medium** | A transparent medium in which light travels comparatively slower | Glass relative to air; diamond relative to water |

## Part A: What Is Refraction and Why Does Light Bend?

### The Speed of Light Varies Across Media

Light travels at its maximum possible speed in a vacuum (and practically at the same speed in air), which is approximately:

$$c \approx 3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s} = 300,000\text{ km/s}$$

However, when light enters a material medium such as water, its speed drops to approximately $2.26 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$. When it enters common glass, its speed decelerates further to roughly $2.0 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$. Every transparent material presents an optical resistance that slows light down to a characteristic speed.

This variation in speed is the **fundamental physical cause of refraction**. To understand intuitively why a change in speed forces a change in direction, consider a helpful mechanical analogy. Imagine a two-wheeled axle or cart rolling rapidly across a smooth, paved highway toward an expanse of deep sand or mud. If the cart approaches the mud boundary at an **oblique angle**:

1. One wheel strikes the boundary and enters the mud first, immediately slowing down.
2. The opposite wheel remains on the smooth pavement, continuing to travel at high speed.
3. Because one side slows down while the other continues rapidly forward, the entire axle pivots and **turns toward the slower medium**.

Light waves behave in an analogous geometric manner. Because different portions of an incoming wave-front arrive at the interface at slightly different instants, the sudden deceleration across an oblique boundary forces the path of the light beam to bend.

> **Definition of Refraction:** Refraction is the phenomenon wherein a ray of light bends (changes its direction of propagation) at the boundary separating two transparent media when incident **obliquely**. The **fundamental cause** of refraction is the **change in the speed of light** as it passes from one medium into another.

Two essential principles must be firmly remembered:
- **Direction changes only upon oblique incidence:** If light enters the interface at an angle, it bends. (What happens when it strikes perpendicularly is addressed in Part B.)
- **Speed always changes across media:** Whether light strikes an interface obliquely or perpendicularly, its speed in the second medium changes inevitably. Bending occurs specifically when that transition happens at an angle.

*(Note on wave properties: During refraction, the **frequency** and **colour** of the light remain strictly invariant because frequency is determined solely by the emitting source. Only the speed $v$ and wavelength $\lambda$ change according to $v = f\lambda$. Hence, a beam of blue light remains blue inside water.)*

### A Geometric Sketch of Refraction

Consider the ray diagram below, showing a light ray traveling from air (above) into a glass block (below) across a flat horizontal boundary.


> **Figure 1.1** – A light ray incident from air into glass. The point $O$ is the point of incidence, and the dashed vertical line $N$ is the normal perpendicular to the interface. The incident ray strikes at $O$. A small fraction of the energy reflects back into the air as the reflected ray obeying $i = r$. The major portion enters the glass as the **refracted ray**. The angle between the incident ray and the normal is $i$, while the angle between the refracted ray and the normal is $r$.

Let us examine each geometric component carefully:
- **Point of incidence ($O$):** The precise point on the boundary where the incoming ray strikes.
- **Incident ray:** The path along which light travels toward the interface in the first medium.
- **Refracted ray:** The path along which light travels inside the second medium after crossing the boundary.
- **Normal ($N$):** An imaginary line constructed perpendicular to the boundary at the point of incidence, extending into both media.
- **Angle of incidence ($i$):** The angle between the incident ray and the normal.
- **Angle of refraction ($r$):** The angle between the refracted ray and the normal.

> **Crucial Rule:** Angles $i$ and $r$ are **always measured relative to the normal**, never relative to the physical surface of the boundary.
>
> **Distinction Between Reflected and Refracted Rays:** The reflected ray stays in the **initial medium** (air), rebounding off the surface. The refracted ray penetrates into the **second medium** (glass), continuing forward along a deflected trajectory.

## Part B: In Which Direction Does Light Bend? Three Simple Rules

Having established that light bends upon entering a new medium, the next question is directional: does the ray bend toward the normal, or away from the normal? The direction of bending depends entirely on whether light is traveling from an optically rarer medium into an optically denser medium, or vice versa.

### Optically Denser and Rarer Media: An Essential Distinction

A medium in which light travels comparatively slower is defined as **optically denser**. Conversely, a medium in which light travels comparatively faster is defined as **optically rarer**. Air is optically rarer than water; water is optically rarer than glass.

> **Caution: Optical Density versus Mass Density:**
> Optical density measures the degree to which a medium **decelerates light**. It is entirely distinct from **mass density** (mass per unit volume, measured in $\text{kg/m}^3$).
> 
> *Classic Example:* **Kerosene floats on water** because its mass density ($\approx 800\text{ kg/m}^3$) is less than that of water ($1000\text{ kg/m}^3$). Yet, kerosene is **optically denser than water** because light travels slower in kerosene than in water ($v_{\text{kerosene}} \approx 2.08 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ versus $v_{\text{water}} \approx 2.26 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$). Never assume that a physically lighter liquid is optically rarer!

### Rule 1: From Rarer to Denser Medium — Towards the Normal

When a ray of light passes from an **optically rarer medium** into an **optically denser medium** (such as from air into water, or from air into glass), the ray **bends towards the normal**. Consequently, the angle of refraction is strictly smaller than the angle of incidence:

$$r < i$$

```
                      N
                      :
          AIR (Rarer) :
               \      :
                \     :
                 \    :
                  ↘   :
                   \  :
                i   \ :
                     \:
======================O=================================  Interface
                      :\
                      :\
                      : \
                      : ↘ r
                      :  \    Refracted Ray
                      :   \
                      :   \  GLASS (Denser)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Refraction from a rarer medium (air) to a denser medium (glass). The light ray bends towards the normal, resulting in $r < i$.

Because light slows down upon entering glass, its wave-fronts compress and tilt inward, pulling the direction of propagation closer to the perpendicular normal line.

### Rule 2: From Denser to Rarer Medium — Away from the Normal

When a ray of light passes from an **optically denser medium** into an **optically rarer medium** (such as from glass into air, or from water into air), the ray **bends away from the normal**. Consequently, the angle of refraction is strictly larger than the angle of incidence:

$$r > i$$

```
                      N
                      :
          AIR         :              ↗  Refracted Ray in Air
          (Rarer)     :          ↗
                      :  r   ↗
                      :  ↗
======================O=================================  Interface
                     ↗:
                    ↗ :
                   ↗  :
                  ↗ i :
                 ↗    :   GLASS (Denser)
                ↗     :
```

> **Figure 1.3** – Refraction from a denser medium (glass) to a rarer medium (air). The light ray bends away from the normal, resulting in $r > i$.

As the light exits the glass and enters air, it accelerates abruptly. The wave-front expands and pivots away from the normal, tilting closer to the interface.

### Rule 3: Normal Incidence — No Deviation

If a light ray strikes the interface **along the normal** (that is, perpendicular to the boundary at an angle of $90^\circ$ to the surface), the angle of incidence is:

$$i = 0^\circ \quad \implies \quad r = 0^\circ$$

In this case, the light ray **passes straight through without any deviation whatsoever**. Returning to our mechanical axle analogy: if the cart drives straight into the sand perpendicular to the boundary, both wheels enter the sand at the exact same instant. Both wheels decelerate synchronously, so no pivoting occurs. The cart continues in a straight line, albeit at a reduced speed.

```
                      N
                      :
                      ↓      AIR
                      ↓
                      ↓
======================O=================================  Interface
                      ↓
                      ↓      GLASS
                      ↓
                      :
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Normal incidence ($i = 0^\circ$). The light ray enters perpendicular to the interface and travels straight through undeviated ($r = 0^\circ$). Although its direction does not change, its speed decreases inside the glass.

### Summary of the Three Rules

| Optical Transition | Direction of Bending | Angular Relationship |
|---|---|---|
| **Rarer to Denser** (e.g., Air to Water/Glass) | Towards the normal | $r < i$ |
| **Denser to Rarer** (e.g., Water/Glass to Air) | Away from the normal | $r > i$ |
| **Along the Normal** (Perpendicular incidence) | No bending (undeviated) | $i = 0^\circ, r = 0^\circ$ |

## Part C: The Laws of Refraction

Just as reflection is governed by universal laws, refraction is strictly governed by the **Two Fundamental Laws of Refraction**.

### Law 1

> **First Law of Refraction (Coplanarity):** The incident ray, the refracted ray, and the normal to the interface at the point of incidence all lie in the **same geometric plane**.

This means that refraction is entirely a two-dimensional geometric phenomenon. A ray striking an interface does not twist sideways out of the plane defined by the incoming ray and the normal.

### Law 2: Snell's Law

> **Second Law of Refraction (Snell's Law):** For a given pair of optical media and for light of a given colour (wavelength), the ratio of the sine of the angle of incidence to the sine of the angle of refraction is a constant:
>
> $$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \text{constant}$$

This constant ratio is denoted by the symbol $n$ (or $\mu$) and is called the **refractive index of the second medium with respect to the first medium**:

$$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n$$

*(Note: In Class 9, we apply Snell's Law in this direct form; the comprehensive theoretical framework of refractive index is expanded in Topic 2.)*

To compute angles using Snell's Law, the trigonometric sine values for standard angles are helpful:

| Angle ($\theta$) | $30^\circ$ | $45^\circ$ | $60^\circ$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| **$\sin \theta$** | $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5000$ | $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.7071$ | $\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} \approx 0.8660$ |

A critical qualitative takeaway from Snell's Law is that **the greater the refractive index $n$, the greater the bending of light** toward the normal (for a given angle of incidence $i$, a larger $n$ yields a smaller $r$). Light bends more sharply when entering glass ($n \approx 1.5$) than when entering water ($n \approx 1.33$).

### The Reversibility of Light Paths

A fundamental theorem in optics is the **Principle of Reversibility of Light**:
> If the path of a ray of light is reversed, it exactly retraces its previous trajectory.

If a ray traveling in air at an incident angle $i = 30^\circ$ enters glass at a refracted angle $r = 19.47^\circ$, then an identical ray directed from inside the glass toward the boundary at an incident angle of $19.47^\circ$ will emerge into the air at an angle of exactly $30^\circ$.

## Part D: Refraction in Everyday Life

Refraction is not merely a laboratory curiosity; it is responsible for numerous striking phenomena in our daily environment.

### 1. A Pencil or Straw Appears Bent in a Glass of Water

When a pencil or drinking straw is placed obliquely into a transparent tumbler filled with water, two distinct effects are observed:
1. The pencil appears **broken or sharply bent** at the air-water interface.
2. The submerged portion appears **thicker and displaced upward**.

**Explanation:** Light rays originating from any point on the submerged section travel through water and strike the surface. Upon emerging into air (denser to rarer), the rays bend **away from the normal**. When these diverging rays enter an observer's eye, the eye traces them backward in straight lines. The virtual rays intersect at a point higher than the physical pencil tip, creating a virtual image that is elevated toward the surface. Because the portion above water is seen directly without deviation, the pencil appears disjointed and bent at the boundary.

### 2. The Disappearing and Reappearing Coin in a Bowl (Activity)

**Apparatus:** An opaque ceramic bowl or mug, a metal coin, clean water, and a laboratory partner.

**Procedure:**
1. Place the coin at the dead centre of the bottom of the empty opaque bowl.
2. Position the bowl on a level table. Slowly back away until the rim of the bowl just obstructs your line of sight to the coin. At this point, the coin is completely invisible.
3. Keep your head and eyes **strictly stationary**. Instruct your partner to pour water gently into the bowl without disturbing the coin.
4. As the water level rises, the coin magically **reappears in full view**, even though your eye has not moved!

**Scientific Reason:** Before water was added, light rays reflected from the coin traveled in straight lines and were blocked by the opaque rim. When water fills the bowl, rays from the coin strike the water-air interface obliquely and bend **away from the normal**. This deflection bends the rays downward over the lip of the bowl and into your eye. Your brain assumes rectilinear propagation, perceiving the coin at an **elevated virtual position** above the bottom of the bowl.

### 3. The Apparent Raising of a Pool or Pond Bed

This phenomenon directly resolves Leo's dilemma at the swimming pool: **the bottom of a water reservoir always appears shallower than it actually is.**

```
                          N
                          :       Observer's Eye (E)
                          :     /
   AIR (Rarer)            :   ↗
                          : /
==========================A===================== Surface
                        ./:
                      . / :
   WATER            P' ↗  :
   (Denser)         : /   :
                    :/    :
                    P     :
                          :
```

> **Figure 1.5** – Optical geometry of apparent depth. A point $P$ lies on the true pool bed. A ray traveling from $P$ strikes the surface at $A$. Passing from water (denser) into air (rarer), it bends away from the normal toward the observer's eye at $E$. The observer projects this ray backward along a straight dashed line to point $P'$. The bottom is perceived at $P'$, which is elevated above the true bottom $P$. The perceived depth is the **apparent depth**, which is strictly less than the **real depth**.

The quantitative formula relating real depth, apparent depth, and refractive index is developed thoroughly in Topic 3.

### 4. A Fish Viewed from the Bank Is Not Where It Appears

When an observer standing on a riverbank looks at a fish swimming in clear water, the fish acts just like point $P$ in Figure 1.5. Light rays traveling from the fish bend away from the normal upon exiting into air, causing the observer to see a virtual image of the fish at $P'$, located **above and slightly displaced from its actual position**.

For this reason, an experienced traditional spear-fisher never aims directly at the visible fish! If they throw the spear straight at the image, the spear will sail harmlessly **above** the target. To strike the fish successfully, the spear-fisher must deliberately aim **significantly below** the visible image.

### 5. Further Natural Phenomena Governed by Refraction

- **Twinkling of Stars:** Starlight travels through millions of kilometres of empty space along a straight path. However, upon entering Earth's atmosphere, it passes through layers of air whose temperatures and densities fluctuate continuously due to convection currents. Because the optical density of each atmospheric pocket changes from moment to moment, the starlight is refracted erratically, causing the apparent position and intensity of the star to fluctuate rapidly—a phenomenon known as atmospheric scintillation or twinkling.
- **Shimmering Air Over Hot Roads:** On a hot summer day, heat radiating from asphalt creates rising layers of hot, low-density air beneath cooler, denser air. Light passing through these turbulent air layers bends unpredictably, causing distant vehicles and scenery to appear wavy, distorted, and shimmering.
- **Advanced Sunrise and Delayed Sunset:** Because Earth's atmospheric density increases toward the surface, sunlight entering the atmosphere bends continuously downward toward the surface. As a result, observers on Earth can see the Sun approximately **2 minutes before it physically crosses the astronomical horizon** at dawn, and continue to see it for roughly **2 minutes after it has physically dipped below the horizon** at dusk.
- **Mirages:** The shimmering illusion of water puddles on hot desert sands or asphalt highways is an atmospheric refraction phenomenon involving extreme bending and total internal reflection, which we investigate thoroughly in Topic 4.

## Part E: Solved Examples

*(In all examples below, angles $i$ and $r$ are measured relative to the normal. Standard trigonometric values: $\sin 30^\circ = 0.5000$, $\sin 45^\circ = 0.7071$, $\sin 60^\circ = 0.8660$.)*

### Example 1: Direction of Bending (8 Pairs of Media)

For each pair of media, state whether the light ray bends **towards the normal** or **away from the normal**, and justify with optical densities.

| Optical Transition | Nature of Transition | Direction of Bending | Angular Relation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Air to Glass | Rarer to Denser | **Towards the normal** | $r < i$ |
| 2. Glass to Air | Denser to Rarer | **Away from the normal** | $r > i$ |
| 3. Air to Water | Rarer to Denser | **Towards the normal** | $r < i$ |
| 4. Water to Air | Denser to Rarer | **Away from the normal** | $r > i$ |
| 5. Water to Glass ($n_{\text{glass}} > n_{\text{water}}$) | Rarer to Denser | **Towards the normal** | $r < i$ |
| 6. Glass to Water | Denser to Rarer | **Away from the normal** | $r > i$ |
| 7. Water to Kerosene ($n_{\text{kerosene}} > n_{\text{water}}$) | Rarer to Denser | **Towards the normal** | $r < i$ |
| 8. Kerosene to Water | Denser to Rarer | **Away from the normal** | $r > i$ |

**Check:** Whenever the second medium has a higher optical density, light slows down and bends toward the normal; whenever the second medium has a lower optical density, light accelerates and bends away from the normal. Note that whether water is "rarer" or "denser" depends strictly on the medium it is compared with: water is denser relative to air, but rarer relative to glass. Always compare the pair!

### Example 2: Air to Glass ($i = 30^\circ$, $n = 1.5$)

A ray of light traveling in air strikes the surface of a flat glass block at an angle of incidence $i = 30^\circ$. Given that the refractive index of glass is $n = 1.5$, calculate the angle of refraction $r$.

**Solution:**
1. According to Snell's Law:

   $$\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n \implies \sin r = \frac{\sin i}{n}$$

2. Substitute the given values:

   $$\sin 30^\circ = 0.5$$

   $$\sin r = \frac{0.5}{1.5} = \frac{1}{3} \approx 0.3333$$

3. Determine the angle whose sine is $0.3333$:

   $$r = \arcsin(0.3333) \approx 19.47^\circ$$

**Check:** $r = 19.47^\circ < i = 30^\circ$. This confirms Rule 1: light passing from a rarer medium (air) to a denser medium (glass) bends towards the normal. Furthermore, $\frac{\sin 30^\circ}{\sin 19.47^\circ} = \frac{0.5000}{0.3333} = 1.50$ ✅.

### Example 3: Air to Water ($i = 45^\circ$, $n = 1.33$)

A light ray passes from air into water at an angle of incidence $i = 45^\circ$. For water, $n = 1.33$. Find the angle of refraction $r$.

**Solution:**
1. From Snell's Law:

   $$\sin r = \frac{\sin i}{n}$$

2. Using $\sin 45^\circ \approx 0.7071$:

   $$\sin r = \frac{0.7071}{1.33} \approx 0.5317$$

3. Finding the inverse sine:

   $$r = \arcsin(0.5317) \approx 32.1^\circ$$

**Check:** $r = 32.1^\circ < 45^\circ$ (bends towards the normal). Checking the ratio: $\frac{0.7071}{0.5317} \approx 1.33$ ✅.

### Example 4: Air to Glass ($i = 60^\circ$, $n = 1.5$)

A ray in air strikes a glass plate ($n = 1.5$) at an incident angle of $i = 60^\circ$. Find the angle of refraction $r$.

**Solution:**
1. From Snell's Law:

   $$\sin r = \frac{\sin 60^\circ}{n} = \frac{0.8660}{1.5} \approx 0.5773$$

2. Calculate $r$:

   $$r = \arcsin(0.5773) \approx 35.26^\circ$$

**Check:** $r = 35.26^\circ < 60^\circ$. Checking the ratio: $\frac{0.8660}{0.5773} = 1.50$ ✅.
*Observation:* Comparing Examples 2 and 4 shows that as $i$ increases from $30^\circ$ to $60^\circ$, $r$ increases from $19.47^\circ$ to $35.26^\circ$, but $r$ remains consistently smaller than $i$.

### Example 5: Reverse Path (Glass to Air)

In Example 2, the ray traveling inside the glass moved at an angle of $19.47^\circ$ to the normal. Suppose a light source placed inside the glass directs a ray toward the air interface at an angle of incidence of $19.47^\circ$. At what angle will the ray emerge into the air?

**Solution:**
1. By the **Principle of Reversibility of Light**, if the path of a ray is reversed, it must retrace its original trajectory. Therefore, since a ray incident at $30^\circ$ in air refracted at $19.47^\circ$ in glass, a ray incident at $19.47^\circ$ in glass must emerge into air at **$30^\circ$**.
2. We verify this quantitatively using Snell's Law for a denser-to-rarer transition:

   $$\frac{\sin(\text{angle in air})}{\sin(\text{angle in glass})} = n$$

   $$\sin(\text{angle in air}) = n \times \sin(19.47^\circ) = 1.5 \times 0.3333 = 0.5000$$

   $$\text{Angle in air} = \arcsin(0.5000) = 30^\circ$$

**Check:** The angle in air ($30^\circ$) is strictly larger than the angle in glass ($19.47^\circ$), confirming Rule 2: light traveling from denser to rarer medium bends away from the normal ✅.

### Example 6: Ray Incident Normally along the Normal

A ray of light in air strikes a calm water surface ($n = 1.33$) perpendicularly along the normal. Determine $i$, $r$, and the subsequent path of the ray.

**Solution:**
1. Because the ray is aligned along the normal, the angle between the ray and the normal is:

   $$i = 0^\circ$$

2. Applying Snell's Law:

   $$\sin r = \frac{\sin i}{n} = \frac{\sin 0^\circ}{1.33} = \frac{0}{1.33} = 0 \implies r = 0^\circ$$

3. The ray passes into the water **completely undeviated in a straight line**.

**Check:** $i = r = 0^\circ$. Note that while the direction of propagation is unaltered, the speed of the light ray drops from $3 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ to $2.26 \times 10^8\text{ m/s}$ the instant it crosses the boundary.

### Example 7: Angle of Deviation ($\delta = i - r$)

The angle of deviation $\delta$ measures how much a light ray has turned away from its original straight-line path. When passing from a rarer to a denser medium, the deviation is given by $\delta = i - r$. Calculate $\delta$ for Examples 2, 3, and 4.

| Case | Incident Angle ($i$) | Refracted Angle ($r$) | Angle of Deviation ($\delta = i - r$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Example 2 (Air to Glass) | $30^\circ$ | $19.47^\circ$ | $30^\circ - 19.47^\circ = \mathbf{10.53^\circ}$ |
| Example 3 (Air to Water) | $45^\circ$ | $32.1^\circ$ | $45^\circ - 32.1^\circ = \mathbf{12.90^\circ}$ |
| Example 4 (Air to Glass) | $60^\circ$ | $35.26^\circ$ | $60^\circ - 35.26^\circ = \mathbf{24.74^\circ}$ |

**Check:**
- Example 2: $19.47^\circ + 10.53^\circ = 30^\circ$ ✅
- Example 3: $32.1^\circ + 12.9^\circ = 45^\circ$ ✅
- Example 4: $35.26^\circ + 24.74^\circ = 60^\circ$ ✅
*Notice:* As the angle of incidence grows larger, the ray experiences greater deviation ($\delta$ rises from $10.53^\circ$ to $24.74^\circ$). At normal incidence ($i = 0^\circ$), $\delta = 0^\circ$.

### Example 8: Determining Refractive Index from Measured Angles

In a physics laboratory session, a student directs a narrow pencil of light from air into an unknown transparent acrylic block. The student measures an angle of incidence $i = 40^\circ$ and an angle of refraction $r = 25^\circ$. Given $\sin 40^\circ = 0.6428$ and $\sin 25^\circ = 0.4226$, determine the refractive index $n$ of the acrylic block.

**Solution:**
1. Apply Snell's Law:

   $$n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}$$

2. Substitute the measured trigonometric values:

   $$n = \frac{0.6428}{0.4226} \approx \mathbf{1.52}$$

**Check:** $n \times \sin r = 1.52 \times 0.4226 = 0.6424 \approx 0.6428$ (difference of $0.0004$ is due to standard rounding) ✅. The value $n \approx 1.52$ is characteristic of crown glass and standard acrylic plastic.

### Example 9: Leo's Question: Does the Pool Deceive Us? (Resolution of the Story)

Let us return to Leo's question to Dr. Bennett: *Why do the pool tiles look so close, and why do submerged legs look bent and broken?*

Dr. Bennett sketched the optical geometry of Figure 1.5 on his clipboard and explained:

1. **Why the pool floor appears shallow:** Light rays reflecting off tile point $P$ at the true bottom travel upward through the water. As they exit into air (denser to rarer), they **bend away from the normal**. When these rays enter Leo's eyes, his brain projects them backward along straight lines, meeting at virtual point $P'$, which is elevated above the true floor $P$. Because every point on the bottom is visually elevated, the entire floor appears raised, making the pool look substantially shallower than it truly is. Dr. Bennett warned: *"Never judge the depth of an unfamiliar body of water by sight alone—water is always deeper than it appears!"*
2. **Why submerged legs appear bent:** Light originating from each submerged point on Leo's legs similarly bends away from the normal at the surface, forming a virtual image that is elevated and foreshortened. Meanwhile, the upper portions of his legs above the water are viewed directly along straight, unrefracted paths. At the exact boundary of the waterline, the sudden mismatch between the unrefracted upper leg and the raised virtual image of the lower leg creates the visual appearance of a sharp, broken bend.

**Conclusion:** The swimming pool is not deceiving us. Light simply follows the fundamental physics of wave propagation: changing speed across media forces rays to bend, and human depth perception naturally traces rays backward in straight lines.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Measuring angles from the interface rather than the normal**
   - *Error:* If a problem states "a ray strikes a water surface at an angle of $30^\circ$ to the surface," writing $i = 30^\circ$.
   - *Correction:* The angle of incidence is always measured relative to the normal. If the ray makes $30^\circ$ with the surface, $i = 90^\circ - 30^\circ = 60^\circ$.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming light always bends towards the normal**
   - *Error:* Drawing a refracted ray bending toward the normal regardless of the media involved.
   - *Correction:* Light bends **towards the normal** only when passing from a **rarer into a denser** medium ($r < i$). When passing from a **denser into a rarer** medium, it bends **away from the normal** ($r > i$).

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing optical density with physical mass density**
   - *Error:* Believing that because kerosene floats on water (lower mass density), it must be optically rarer than water.
   - *Correction:* Kerosene is physically lighter than water, but it is **optically denser** ($n \approx 1.44$) than water ($n \approx 1.33$). Light travels slower in kerosene than in water.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing that normally incident light bends**
   - *Error:* Assuming that light entering a dense glass block must bend even when $i = 0^\circ$.
   - *Correction:* At normal incidence ($i = 0^\circ$), light does not bend ($r = 0^\circ$). It continues undeviated, though its speed decreases.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming that the frequency or colour of light changes during refraction**
   - *Error:* Claiming that yellow light changes colour when entering water.
   - *Correction:* Frequency is determined by the source and remains strictly unchanged during refraction. Only speed and wavelength change proportionally.

6. **Mistake 6: Conflating the reflected ray with the refracted ray**
   - *Error:* Confusing the reflected beam with the refracted beam in a ray diagram.
   - *Correction:* The reflected ray stays in the original medium (rebounding into air). The refracted ray crosses the interface and propagates inside the second medium.

## Quick Recap

- **Refraction:** The bending of a ray of light when it passes obliquely from one transparent medium into another, caused by the **change in the speed of light**.
- **Geometric Elements:** Incident ray, refracted ray, point of incidence, interface, and normal. The angle of incidence ($i$) and angle of refraction ($r$) are **always measured relative to the normal**.
- **Directional Rules:**
  - **Rarer to Denser:** Light slows down and bends **towards the normal** ($r < i$).
  - **Denser to Rarer:** Light speeds up and bends **away from the normal** ($r > i$).
  - **Normal Incidence:** When $i = 0^\circ$, $r = 0^\circ$; light passes **undeviated**, but its speed changes.
- **Optical Density:** A measure of how much a medium slows down light. It is independent of mass density (e.g., kerosene is less dense in mass than water, but optically denser).
- **Laws of Refraction:**
  1. The incident ray, refracted ray, and normal at the point of incidence are **coplanar**.
  2. **Snell's Law:** $\frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = n$ (constant for a given colour and pair of media).
- **Reversibility of Light:** Reversing the direction of a ray causes it to retrace its exact geometric path.
- **Natural Consequences:** Apparent raising of pool and pond bottoms, bent appearance of a submerged pencil, reappearance of a coin in a bowl of water, twinkling of stars, and advanced sunrise / delayed sunset.

If the ray is now directed backward from inside the water along the exact same path, at what angle with the normal will it emerge into the air? Name the optical principle that guarantees this outcome.

10. Classify each of the following as exhibiting "bending due to refraction across a medium change" or "no bending":
    (a) A straw partially submerged in a glass of water
    (b) A flashlight beam directed perpendicularly along the normal into an aquarium
    (c) A coin becoming visible over the rim of a mug as water is poured in
    (d) A ray of light propagating across a room filled with still air at uniform temperature
    (e) The shimmering twinkling of a star viewed from Earth's surface

### Answers

1. (a) Air (rarer) to water (denser): **Towards the normal** ($r < i$).
   (b) Crown glass (denser) to air (rarer): **Away from the normal** ($r > i$).
   (c) Water (rarer) to crown glass (denser): **Towards the normal** ($r < i$).
   (d) Kerosene (denser) to water (rarer): **Away from the normal** ($r > i$).

2. By Snell's Law:
   $$\sin r = \frac{\sin i}{n} = \frac{\sin 30^\circ}{1.33} = \frac{0.5000}{1.33} \approx \mathbf{0.3759}$$
   Comparing with the given values, $0.3759$ lies between $\sin 22^\circ = 0.3746$ and $\sin 23^\circ = 0.3907$, very close to $22^\circ$.
   Thus, $r \approx \mathbf{22.1^\circ}$ (approximately $22^\circ$).
   *Check:* $\frac{0.5000}{0.3759} \approx 1.33$ ✅, and $r = 22.1^\circ < 30^\circ$ ✅.

3. By Snell's Law:
   $$\sin r = \frac{\sin 60^\circ}{1.33} = \frac{0.8660}{1.33} \approx \mathbf{0.6511}$$
   The value $0.6511$ lies between $\sin 40^\circ = 0.6428$ (difference: $0.0083$) and $\sin 41^\circ = 0.6561$ (difference: $0.0050$). Since it is closer to $0.6561$, $r$ is closest to the whole-degree value of **$41^\circ$** (exact: $r \approx 40.6^\circ$).
   *Check:* $\frac{0.8660}{0.6511} \approx 1.33$ ✅.

4. Using Snell's Law:
   $$n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r} = \frac{\sin 30^\circ}{\sin 20^\circ} = \frac{0.5000}{0.3420} \approx \mathbf{1.46}$$
   *Check:* $1.46 \times 0.3420 = 0.4993 \approx 0.5000$ ✅.

5. Light travels from a denser medium (glass) to a rarer medium (air). Applying Snell's Law:
   $$\sin(\text{angle in air}) = n \times \sin(\text{angle in glass}) = 1.5 \times \sin 30^\circ = 1.5 \times 0.5000 = \mathbf{0.7500}$$
   Given $\sin 48.6^\circ = 0.7500$, the angle with the normal in air is **$48.6^\circ$**.
   *Check:* $48.6^\circ > 30^\circ$ (bends away from the normal as expected for denser to rarer) ✅.

6. (a) **False** ❌. Optical density is independent of mass density; kerosene is physically lighter than water but optically denser ($n \approx 1.44 > 1.33$).
   (b) **True** ✅. At normal incidence $i = 0^\circ$, so $r = 0^\circ$; light propagates undeviated (only speed changes).
   (c) **False** ❌. The angle of incidence is strictly measured between the ray and the normal, never the boundary surface.
   (d) **False** ❌. Light bends towards the normal only when entering a denser medium; it bends away from the normal when entering a rarer medium.
   (e) **False** ❌. Frequency (and hence colour) is determined by the source and remains invariant during refraction; only speed and wavelength change.

7. Rays of light reflecting from the pool bed travel through water and cross into air. Because they pass from an optically denser medium into a rarer medium, they bend **away from the normal**. An observer's eye traces these bent rays backward along straight lines to a virtual focal point that lies **higher** than the physical pool bed, making the apparent depth less than the real depth.

8. The spear-fisher must aim **below the visible fish**. Light rays from the fish bend away from the normal upon exiting the water, causing its virtual image to appear elevated and shifted upward relative to its true position. Aiming directly at the image would cause the spear to pass harmlessly above the fish.

9. The ray will emerge into the air at an angle of **$45^\circ$** with the normal. The governing principle is the **Principle of Reversibility of Light**, which states that reversing a light ray forces it to retrace its exact previous optical path.
   *Check:* $1.33 \times \sin 32.1^\circ = 1.33 \times 0.5314 = 0.7068 \approx \sin 45^\circ = 0.7071$ ✅.

10. (a) **Bending due to refraction across a medium change** (water to air).
    (b) **No bending** (normal incidence along the normal, $i = 0^\circ$).
    (c) **Bending due to refraction across a medium change** (rays bend away from the normal to clear the rim).
    (d) **No bending** (light travels through a homogeneous medium of uniform optical density in straight lines).
    (e) **Bending due to refraction** (continuous atmospheric refraction through fluctuating thermal layers).
