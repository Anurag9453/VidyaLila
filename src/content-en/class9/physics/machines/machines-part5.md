# Topic 5: Inclined Plane, Wedge, Screw, and Wheel-and-Axle

In previous topics, we analyzed levers and pulleys. In a pulley system, the number of rope strands sharing the load determines its ideal mechanical advantage. Yet the family of simple machines extends far beyond pivoted beams and cables.

Whenever you roll a heavy cart up a wheelchair ramp, chop firewood with a sharp steel axe, tighten a threaded bolt with a wrench, or turn a circular doorknob, you are operating a simple machine. In this topic, we will explore the remaining four classical simple machines: the **inclined plane (ramp)**, the **wedge**, the **screw**, and the **wheel-and-axle**. For each, we will determine its mechanical advantage, velocity ratio, and operational trade-offs.

**Throughout this topic, $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$** (meaning $1\text{ kg}$ exerts a gravitational weight of $10\text{ N}$). All calculations assume ideal conditions unless friction or real efficiencies are explicitly stated.

## Loading Heavy Drums at the Warehouse

At a logistics distribution depot, Mr. Sterling was supervising warehouse operations. A transport truck stood parked at the loading bay, with its flatbed floor elevated exactly **$1\text{ m}$** above the concrete pavement. The task was to load heavy steel drums of liquid adhesive, each weighing **$1200\text{ N}$** (a mass of $120\text{ kg}$), into the cargo hold. Hoisting a $1200\text{-N}$ drum vertically was physically impossible for a single worker, so Mr. Sterling utilized heavy wooden loading planks to roll them up.

Sophia, a Class 9 student visiting with her father, watched the operations attentively. At first, a worker positioned a short, steep $2\text{-meter}$ plank between the ground and the truck floor. Mr. Sterling pushed the drum upward, but it required tremendous muscular exertion and heavy straining. He then replaced the short plank with a long, gentle $4\text{-meter}$ plank. This time, the drum rolled upward smoothly with comfortable, steady effort.

Sophia observed the change and asked, "Mr. Sterling, using the longer plank clearly required much less pushing force. Does that mean the longer ramp actually **saved work**? Did it reduce the total amount of energy needed to load the drum?"

Mr. Sterling wiped his brow and smiled. "It certainly saved physical strain on my back, Sophia! But whether it reduced the total mechanical work required is a question we can answer precisely with physics."

Let us analyze the inclined plane to answer Sophia's question with exact calculations.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Inclined Plane (Ramp)** | A flat, rigid surface tilted at an angle to the horizontal, used to elevate heavy loads | Loading ramp, wheelchair ramp |
| **Length of Incline ($l$)** | The slanting distance along the surface of the ramp over which the effort acts | Length of the wooden plank |
| **Height ($h$)** | The vertical elevation through which the load is lifted | Height of the truck bed above ground |
| **Wedge** | A portable double inclined plane placed back-to-back, used to split, cut, or secure objects | Axe blade, chisel, knife edge, nail |
| **Thickness ($t$)** | The width of the blunt, driven head of a wedge | The spine thickness of an axe blade |

| **Thread** | The continuous helical ridge winding around the shaft of a screw | The spiral ridge of a wood screw |
| **Pitch ($p$)** | The axial distance between two adjacent threads; the distance the screw advances in one full revolution | A machine bolt with a $1.5\text{-mm}$ pitch |
| **Wheel-and-Axle** | A large wheel (radius $R$) rigidly attached to a smaller concentric axle (radius $r$) rotating together | Doorknob, steering wheel, screwdriver |
| **Windlass** | A classic wheel-and-axle mechanism featuring a hand crank driving a rope drum to hoist well buckets | Water well hoisting crank |

## Part A: Inclined Plane (Ramp)

### How an Inclined Plane Works

An **inclined plane** is a flat, rigid surface set at a slope between a lower level and an elevated level. Instead of lifting a heavy load of weight $L$ vertically through a height $h$, we push or pull the load along the slanted ramp surface of length $l$.

A longer, gentler incline creates an extended path, but dramatically reduces the required pushing force.

```
                              ╱│ ← Truck flatbed floor (height h)
                            ╱  │
               l (plank)  ╱    │
                        ╱      │ h (vertical rise)
                      ╱        │
                    ╱          │
 ─────────────────╱────────────┴─────────────
  Ground
```

> **Figure 5.1** – An inclined plane: The slanting length of the ramp is $l$, and the direct vertical elevation is $h$.

### Ideal Mechanical Advantage: MA = l / h

In an ideal (frictionless) inclined plane, the mechanical **work input equals the work output**:
- **Work Input:** Effort $E$ applied parallel to the incline acting over the slanting length $l$:
  $$\text{Work Input} = E \times l$$
- **Work Output:** Load $L$ elevated through the vertical height $h$:
  $$\text{Work Output} = L \times h$$

Equating input and output for an ideal machine:
$$E \times l = L \times h$$

Dividing both sides by $E \times h$:
> $$\mathbf{\text{Ideal MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{l}{h}}$$

Furthermore, because the effort moves along the incline through distance $l$ while the load ascends vertically through height $h$:
> $$\mathbf{\text{VR} = \frac{d_E}{d_L} = \frac{l}{h}}$$

In an ideal inclined plane, $\text{MA} = \text{VR} = l / h$.

*Note on Nomenclature:* Here, $l$ is strictly the **slanted length along the ramp**, and $h$ is strictly the **vertical height**. Neither trigonometry nor angles are required when $l$ and $h$ are directly known.

### Resolving Sophia's Question with Numbers

Let us compare the two planks used by Mr. Sterling for the $1200\text{-N}$ drum ($h = 1\text{ m}$):

**Plank 1 (Short, Steep Incline):** $l = 2\text{ m}$, $h = 1\text{ m}$, $\text{Load} = 1200\text{ N}$.
- $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{2\text{ m}}{1\text{ m}} = \mathbf{2}$
- $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{1200\text{ N}}{2} = \mathbf{600\text{ N}}$
- $\text{Work Input} = E \times l = 600\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{1200\text{ J}}$

**Plank 2 (Long, Gentle Incline):** $l = 4\text{ m}$, $h = 1\text{ m}$, $\text{Load} = 1200\text{ N}$.
- $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{4\text{ m}}{1\text{ m}} = \mathbf{4}$
- $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{1200\text{ N}}{4} = \mathbf{300\text{ N}}$
- $\text{Work Input} = E \times l = 300\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = \mathbf{1200\text{ J}}$

**Direct Vertical Lift (No Machine):**
- $\text{Work} = \text{Load} \times h = 1200\text{ N} \times 1\text{ m} = \mathbf{1200\text{ J}}$

*Verification:* In all three cases, the mechanical work required is exactly $1200\text{ J}$ ✅.

Mr. Sterling explained: "You see, Sophia? The longer ramp halved the required pushing force from $600\text{ N}$ down to $300\text{ N}$, but it doubled the distance from $2\text{ m}$ to $4\text{ m}$. In an ideal world, the total mechanical work is identical. **We traded distance for force.**"

### Real Inclined Planes and Friction

In reality, rolling or sliding friction between the drum and the plank opposes motion. The worker must exert extra effort to overcome both gravity and friction:
$$\text{Real Effort} > \frac{L \times h}{l}$$
Consequently, $\text{Real MA} < \text{VR}$, and efficiency $\eta = (\text{MA} / \text{VR}) \times 100\% < 100\%$.

### Common Applications of the Inclined Plane

- **Warehouse and Vehicle Ramps:** Allowing hand trucks to load flatbeds smoothly.
- **Children's Playground Slides:** A smooth incline trading vertical fall for a safe, sloped descent.
- **Mountain Roads with Switchbacks (Hairpin Bends):** Rather than climbing straight up a mountain wall (steep incline requiring excessive engine force), roads wind gently back and forth. The extended road length provides a large $l$, reducing the gradient and force required.
- **Staircases:** A segmented inclined plane dividing vertical rise into comfortable horizontal steps.
- **Wheelchair Accessibility Ramps:** Gentle slopes ($l / h \ge 12$) enabling wheelchair users to ascend independently.

## Part B: Wedge

A **wedge** is a portable device formed by joining **two inclined planes back-to-back**. While an inclined plane remains stationary while a load slides up it, a wedge is **driven directly into or under an object** to separate, split, or hold it in place.

```
          Effort F (e.g., hammer blow)
                ↓
          ┌─────────┐   ← Thickness t (broad driving head)
           ╲       ╱
            ╲     ╱   ← Slanting slope length l
             ╲   ╱
              ╲ ╱
               V   ← Sharp penetrating edge
```

> **Figure 5.2** – A wedge: The two slanting sides represent two inclined planes of length $l$ joined back-to-back, with a total head thickness $t$.

For an ideal wedge:

> $$\mathbf{\text{Ideal MA} = \frac{\text{Length of Slanted Side } l}{\text{Thickness of Head } t} = \frac{l}{t}}$$

A long, thin, razor-sharp wedge has a large ratio of $l / t$, providing a massive mechanical advantage that easily overcomes the intense cohesive strength of solid wood or metal.

**Everyday Examples of Wedges:**
- **Woodchopping Axe and Splitting Maul:** Splits logs by converting a downward axe strike into powerful lateral spreading forces.
- **Chisels and Knives:** The cutting edge is ground into a fine wedge.
- **Nails and Needles:** Taper to a sharp point, pushing timber or fabric fibers aside.
- **Doorstops:** A tapered rubber wedge wedged beneath a door converts horizontal door pressure into high vertical friction against the floor.
- **Agricultural Plow:** A hardened steel wedge cutting and turning agricultural soil.

## Part C: Screw

A **screw** is simply **an inclined plane wrapped helically around a central cylinder**.

You can prove this elegant relationship yourself: cut a right-angled triangle out of paper (an inclined plane) and wrap it tightly around a cylindrical pencil. The slanting hypotenuse forms a continuous, spiral ridge winding around the pencil. That spiral ridge is the **thread** of a screw!

### Pitch of a Screw

The axial distance between two consecutive thread crests is known as the **pitch ($p$)**. When a screw is rotated through **one complete revolution ($360^\circ$)**, it advances into the material by an axial distance exactly equal to its pitch:
$$\text{Axial Advance in 1 Revolution} = p$$

```
   ┌────────────────────────┐
   │ ╱  ╱  ╱  ╱  ╱  ╱  ╱  ╱ │   ← Helical threads along shaft
   └────────────────────────┘
     ←p→
     Distance between adjacent threads = Pitch p
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Pitch $p$: The linear advance made by a screw during one full rotation.

### Mechanical Advantage of a Screw Jack

In a screw mechanism (such as an automotive screw jack or bench vice), the input effort $E$ acts at the end of a lever handle of length $r$, sweeping through the circumference of a circle ($2\pi r$) during one revolution. Meanwhile, the heavy load $L$ advances by the small pitch distance $p$.

By the principle of work ($E \times d_E = L \times d_L$):
$$E \times (2\pi r) = L \times p$$

> $$\mathbf{\text{Ideal MA} = \text{VR} = \frac{\text{Circumference of Effort Circle}}{\text{Pitch of Screw}} = \frac{2\pi r}{p}}$$

*(Throughout this topic, we use $\pi \approx 3.14$.)*

Because the handle radius $r$ is typically tens of centimeters while the pitch $p$ is only a few millimeters, the ratio $\frac{2\pi r}{p}$ is enormous—often ranging between $200$ and $800$! This explains why Mr. Sterling's screw jack in Topic 2 could lift an entire van with modest manual effort.

**Examples of Screws:** Threaded machine bolts, jar lids, bottle caps, automotive screw jacks, carpenter's bench vices, helical drill bits, and Archimedes screws.

## Part D: Wheel-and-Axle

A **wheel-and-axle** consists of two rigidly joined concentric circular cylinders of different diameters: a larger **wheel** of radius $R$ bonded to a smaller **axle** of radius $r$. The assembly rotates together on a common central axis.

The input **effort is applied tangentially to the rim of the large wheel**, while the **load is carried by a rope wound around the smaller axle**.

```
           Effort applied at wheel rim
                 v
           ╭───────────╮
          ╱   ╭─────╮   ╲
         │    │Axle │    │     R = Radius of large wheel
         │    │  r  │    │     r = Radius of small axle
          ╲   ╰─────╯   ╱
           ╰───────────╯
                 │
               Load suspended from axle cord
```

> **Figure 5.4** – Wheel-and-axle: Effort acts on the outer circumference of radius $R$, while the load hangs from the axle of radius $r$.

In one complete rotation of the assembly:
- The effort moves through a distance equal to the circumference of the wheel: $d_E = 2\pi R$.
- The load moves through a distance equal to the circumference of the axle: $d_L = 2\pi r$.

Therefore, the velocity ratio is:
$$\text{VR} = \frac{d_E}{d_L} = \frac{2\pi R}{2\pi r} = \frac{R}{r}$$

For an ideal, frictionless system:

> $$\mathbf{\text{Ideal MA} = \text{VR} = \frac{\text{Radius of Wheel (}R\text{)}}{\text{Radius of Axle (}r\text{)}} = \frac{R}{r}}$$

The larger the wheel relative to the axle, the greater the mechanical advantage.

### Analogy to a Continuous Rotating Lever

A wheel-and-axle can be conceptualized as a **continuous, 360-degree lever**: the central axis acts as the fulcrum, the radius of the wheel $R$ acts as the effort arm, and the radius of the axle $r$ acts as the load arm.

**Everyday Examples of the Wheel-and-Axle:**
- **Doorknobs:** Turning the broad outer knob rotates the narrow internal latch spindle easily.
- **Automotive Steering Wheel:** A wide steering wheel allows drivers to turn heavy front wheels with modest arm force.
- **Screwdrivers:** The thick plastic handle represents the wheel, and the thin metal shaft represents the axle (which is why a screwdriver multiplies rotational torque).
- **Water Well Windlass:** A crank handle of radius $R$ winding a rope drum of radius $r$.
- **Water Faucet Handles and Bicycle Pedals.**

## Part E: The Four Simple Machines in Summary

| Machine | Governing Dimensions | Ideal MA ($\text{VR}$) | Typical Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Inclined Plane** | Length along slope $l$, vertical height $h$ | $\frac{l}{h}$ | Cargo ramp, wheelchair ramp, mountain road, stairs |
| **Wedge** | Slanted side length $l$, head thickness $t$ | $\frac{l}{t}$ | Woodchopping axe, chisel, knife blade, nail, plow |
| **Screw (Jack)** | Handle length $r$, thread pitch $p$ | $\frac{2\pi r}{p}$ | Machine bolt, jar lid, screw jack, bench vice |
| **Wheel-and-Axle** | Wheel radius $R$, axle radius $r$ | $\frac{R}{r}$ | Doorknob, steering wheel, windlass, screwdriver |

In every ideal machine, $\text{MA} = \text{VR}$. In real physical machines, friction ensures $\text{MA} < \text{VR}$, with efficiency $\eta = (\text{MA} / \text{VR}) \times 100\%$.

## Part F: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Inclined Plane Effort and MA):** A loading ramp has a length of $6\text{ m}$ and reaches a storage deck $1.5\text{ m}$ high. Calculate the ideal mechanical advantage and the effort needed to roll an $800\text{-N}$ machine crate up the ramp.

- $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{6\text{ m}}{1.5\text{ m}} = \mathbf{4}$
- $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{800\text{ N}}{4} = \mathbf{200\text{ N}}$

*Check:* $\text{Work Input} = 200\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = 1200\text{ J} = 800\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \text{Work Output}$ ✅.

**Example 2 (Determining Required Incline Length):** A worker must hoist a safe weighing $900\text{ N}$ to an elevation of $1.5\text{ m}$. If the worker can exert an effort of only $300\text{ N}$, what minimum length of ramp is required under ideal conditions?

- $\text{MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{900\text{ N}}{300\text{ N}} = 3$
- $l = \text{MA} \times h = 3 \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.5\text{ m}}$

*Check:* $300\text{ N} \times 4.5\text{ m} = 1350\text{ J} = 900\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m}$ ✅.

**Example 3 (Real Incline with Friction):** An inclined plane has $l = 5\text{ m}$ and $h = 1\text{ m}$. An effort of $125\text{ N}$ is required to push a $500\text{-N}$ barrel up the slope. Determine the actual $\text{MA}$, the $\text{VR}$, the efficiency, the ideal effort, and the energy lost to friction.

- $\text{Actual MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{500\text{ N}}{125\text{ N}} = \mathbf{4}$
- $\text{VR} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{5\text{ m}}{1\text{ m}} = \mathbf{5}$
- $\eta = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{VR}} \times 100\% = \frac{4}{5} \times 100\% = \mathbf{80\%}$
- $\text{Ideal Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{VR}} = \frac{500\text{ N}}{5} = \mathbf{100\text{ N}}$
- $\text{Work Input} = 125\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = 625\text{ J}$; $\text{Work Output} = 500\text{ N} \times 1\text{ m} = 500\text{ J}$
- $\text{Energy Lost} = 625\text{ J} - 500\text{ J} = \mathbf{125\text{ J}}$ (dissipated as heat)

*Check:* $\frac{500\text{ J}}{625\text{ J}} = 0.80 = 80\%$ ✅.

**Example 4 (Mechanical Advantage of a Wedge):** A timber wedge has a slanting slope length of $10\text{ cm}$ and a head thickness of $2\text{ cm}$. Calculate its ideal mechanical advantage.

- $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{t} = \frac{10\text{ cm}}{2\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{5}$

*Check:* Both lengths are in centimeters, giving a dimensionless ratio of 5 ✅.

**Example 5 (Splitting Force of an Axe):** An axe blade has a slanting blade length of $12\text{ cm}$ and a spine thickness of $3\text{ cm}$. When struck with an effective driving effort of $50\text{ N}$, what lateral outward splitting force does it exert on the log?

- $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{t} = \frac{12\text{ cm}}{3\text{ cm}} = 4$
- $\text{Splitting Force (Load)} = \text{MA} \times \text{Effort} = 4 \times 50\text{ N} = \mathbf{200\text{ N}}$

*Check:* $\frac{200\text{ N}}{50\text{ N}} = 4$ ✅.

**Example 6 (Screw Jack MA):** An automotive screw jack features a crank handle of length $40\text{ cm}$ and a screw thread pitch of $5\text{ mm}$. Calculate its ideal mechanical advantage. (Take $\pi = 3.14$).

- Convert to consistent SI units: $r = 40\text{ cm} = 0.4\text{ m}$; $p = 5\text{ mm} = 0.005\text{ m}$.
- Circumference $= 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.14 \times 0.4\text{ m} = 2.512\text{ m}$.
- $\text{MA} = \frac{2\pi r}{p} = \frac{2.512\text{ m}}{0.005\text{ m}} = 502.4 \approx \mathbf{502}$

*Check:* $502.4 \times 0.005 = 2.512\text{ m}$ ✅.

**Example 7 (Lifting Force of a Screw Jack):** Using the jack from Example 6 ($\text{MA} = 502.4$), what ideal effort is needed to hoist a vehicle corner imposing a load of $10000\text{ N}$?

- $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{10000\text{ N}}{502.4} \approx \mathbf{19.9\text{ N}}$ (approximately $20\text{ N}$)

*Check:* In one full turn, work input $= 19.9\text{ N} \times 2.512\text{ m} \approx 50\text{ J}$, and work output $= 10000\text{ N} \times 0.005\text{ m} = 50\text{ J}$ ✅. A tiny effort of $20\text{ N}$ lifts a one-ton mass.

**Example 8 (Wheel-and-Axle Performance):** A wheel-and-axle assembly has a wheel radius of $30\text{ cm}$ and an axle radius of $5\text{ cm}$. What effort applied at the rim is required to raise a load of $600\text{ N}$?

- $\text{MA} = \frac{R}{r} = \frac{30\text{ cm}}{5\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{6}$
- $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{600\text{ N}}{6} = \mathbf{100\text{ N}}$

*Check:* $100\text{ N} \times 6 = 600\text{ N}$ ✅.

**Example 9 (Well Windlass):** A water well windlass features a crank handle radius of $40\text{ cm}$ and a rope drum radius of $10\text{ cm}$. Find the effort needed to hoist a water bucket weighing $200\text{ N}$.

- $\text{MA} = \frac{R}{r} = \frac{40\text{ cm}}{10\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{4}$
- $\text{Effort} = \frac{200\text{ N}}{4} = \mathbf{50\text{ N}}$

*Check:* $50\text{ N} \times 4 = 200\text{ N}$ ✅.

**Example 10 (Comparing Wheelchair Ramps):** A building entrance requires a vertical rise of $0.3\text{ m}$. The combined weight of a visitor and their wheelchair is $900\text{ N}$. Ramp A has a length of $1.5\text{ m}$; Ramp B has a length of $3.6\text{ m}$. Calculate the ideal effort for both ramps.

- **Ramp A:**
  $$\text{MA} = \frac{1.5\text{ m}}{0.3\text{ m}} = 5 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{Effort} = \frac{900\text{ N}}{5} = \mathbf{180\text{ N}}$$
- **Ramp B:**
  $$\text{MA} = \frac{3.6\text{ m}}{0.3\text{ m}} = 12 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \text{Effort} = \frac{900\text{ N}}{12} = \mathbf{75\text{ N}}$$

*Check:* Work done on A: $180\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = 270\text{ J}$. Work done on B: $75\text{ N} \times 3.6\text{ m} = 270\text{ J}$. Direct vertical lift: $900\text{ N} \times 0.3\text{ m} = 270\text{ J}$ ✅. Ramp B requires less than half the pushing force of Ramp A, making it vastly safer and more accessible.

**Example 11 (Efficiency of a Real Wheel-and-Axle):** A wheel-and-axle with $R = 20\text{ cm}$ and $r = 4\text{ cm}$ lifts a load of $400\text{ N}$ when driven by an effort of $100\text{ N}$. Determine its $\text{VR}$, $\text{MA}$, and efficiency.

- $\text{VR} = \frac{R}{r} = \frac{20\text{ cm}}{4\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{5}$
- $\text{MA} = \frac{400\text{ N}}{100\text{ N}} = \mathbf{4}$
- $\eta = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{VR}} \times 100\% = \frac{4}{5} \times 100\% = \mathbf{80\%}$

*Check:* If the load ascends $0.1\text{ m}$, the effort travels $5 \times 0.1\text{ m} = 0.5\text{ m}$. $\text{Work Input} = 100\text{ N} \times 0.5\text{ m} = 50\text{ J}$; $\text{Work Output} = 400\text{ N} \times 0.1\text{ m} = 40\text{ J}$. Efficiency $= 40 / 50 = 80\%$ ✅.

**Example 12 (Mountain Switchback Road):** A mountain road traverses a total distance of $2000\text{ m}$ to overcome a vertical rise of $200\text{ m}$. What ideal tractive force must a $20000\text{-N}$ delivery vehicle generate to climb the pass?

- $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{2000\text{ m}}{200\text{ m}} = 10$
- $\text{Force} = \frac{20000\text{ N}}{10} = \mathbf{2000\text{ N}}$

*Check:* $\text{Work} = 2000\text{ N} \times 2000\text{ m} = 4,000,000\text{ J} = 20000\text{ N} \times 200\text{ m}$ ✅. Winding switchbacks keep the engine force within safe limits.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing a gentler ramp reduces total work**
   As proven in Sophia's scenario, whether a ramp is $2\text{ m}$ or $4\text{ m}$ long, the ideal work required to elevate a given load through height $h$ remains strictly fixed ($1200\text{ J}$). A gentler ramp reduces instantaneous effort, but increases travel distance proportionately.

2. **Mistake 2: Inverting l and h in the inclined plane formula**
   Remember: **$\text{MA} = l / h$** ($\text{slant length} \div \text{vertical height}$). Since the slanting hypotenuse $l$ is always longer than the vertical height $h$, the ideal $\text{MA}$ of a ramp is always greater than 1. Dividing $1.5 \div 6 = 0.25$ is an inversion error.

3. **Mistake 3: Inverting R and r in the wheel-and-axle formula**
   When effort is applied to the wheel and load is on the axle, **$\text{MA} = R / r$** ($\text{large radius} \div \text{small radius}$). If effort were applied to the axle to turn a large wheel (as in the rear wheel of a bicycle), $\text{MA}$ would be less than 1, acting as a speed multiplier. Always identify where the effort is applied.

4. **Mistake 4: Mixing millimeters, centimeters, and meters in screw calculations**
   In Example 6, the handle radius was $40\text{ cm}$ and pitch was $5\text{ mm}$. If you compute $\frac{2 \times 3.14 \times 40}{5}$, you get $50.24$, introducing a 10-fold error! Always convert all dimensions to standard meters ($r = 0.4\text{ m}$, $p = 0.005\text{ m}$) before dividing.

5. **Mistake 5: Failing to recognize that a wedge is an inclined plane**
   A wedge is not a separate physical invention; it is structurally two inclined planes joined back-to-back. The only difference is operational: an inclined plane stays fixed while the load moves, whereas a wedge moves while penetrating the load.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming screw MA depends solely on pitch**
   The mechanical advantage of a screw depends equally on the **length of the turning handle** and the **thread pitch**: $\text{MA} = 2\pi r / p$. A longer crank handle increases $\text{MA}$ just as effectively as a finer thread pitch.

## Quick Recap

- **Inclined Plane:** A sloped flat surface; ideal $\text{MA} = \text{VR} = l / h$. A longer ramp reduces the required effort, but demands a longer travel path.
- Work is conserved under ideal conditions: $\text{Work Input} = E \times l = L \times h = \text{Work Output}$.
- Real ramps experience friction, resulting in $\eta < 100\%$ and energy dissipation as heat.
- **Wedge:** Two inclined planes joined back-to-back; ideal $\text{MA} = l / t$ (axes, chisels, knife edges, nails).
- **Screw:** An inclined plane wrapped helically around a cylinder. Pitch ($p$) is the linear advance per revolution. For a screw jack, $\text{Ideal MA} = \text{VR} = 2\pi r / p$.
- **Wheel-and-Axle:** Concentric cylinders rotating together; ideal $\text{MA} = \text{VR} = R / r$. Can be viewed as a continuous rotating lever.
- Always convert all linear measurements to consistent units (preferably meters) before evaluating ratios.

## Practice Questions

1. A warehouse ramp is $8\text{ m}$ long and reaches a platform elevated $2\text{ m}$ above the ground. Calculate the ideal mechanical advantage and the effort needed to push an $800\text{-N}$ packing crate up the ramp.

2. A heavy machine weighing $600\text{ N}$ must be elevated through a vertical height of $1.2\text{ m}$ using an ideal ramp. If the available pushing effort is limited to $150\text{ N}$, what minimum length of ramp is required?

3. An inclined plane has a length of $l = 6\text{ m}$ and a height of $h = 1.5\text{ m}$. A load of $840\text{ N}$ is pushed up the slope by an effort of $280\text{ N}$. Calculate the actual mechanical advantage, velocity ratio, operational efficiency, and the energy dissipated by friction.

4. A splitting wedge has a slanting slope length of $15\text{ cm}$ and a head thickness of $3\text{ cm}$. Find its ideal mechanical advantage. What lateral splitting force does it exert on a log when struck with an effort of $80\text{ N}$?

5. An automotive screw jack has a crank handle of length $0.5\text{ m}$ and a thread pitch of $4\text{ mm}$. Calculate its ideal mechanical advantage, and determine the effort needed to lift a vehicle corner imposing a load of $7850\text{ N}$. (Take $\pi = 3.14$).

6. A wheel-and-axle hoist has a wheel radius of $50\text{ cm}$ and an axle radius of $10\text{ cm}$. Determine its ideal mechanical advantage and the effort required to elevate a load of $750\text{ N}$.

7. A water well windlass features a crank handle radius of $60\text{ cm}$ and a rope drum radius of $15\text{ cm}$. Determine the effort required to hoist a water bucket weighing $240\text{ N}$ under ideal conditions.

8. State whether each of the following statements is True or False:
   (a) A gentler incline reduces the total mechanical work needed to elevate a load to a given height.
   (b) A wedge is structurally equivalent to two inclined planes joined back-to-back.
   (c) A screw with a smaller pitch has a larger mechanical advantage, assuming handle length is kept constant.
   (d) For a wheel-and-axle with effort on the wheel, the ideal mechanical advantage is $r / R$.

9. A wheel-and-axle assembly with $R = 25\text{ cm}$ and $r = 5\text{ cm}$ lifts a $300\text{-N}$ load when driven by an effort of $75\text{ N}$. Find its velocity ratio, mechanical advantage, and efficiency.

10. Explain why mountain highways incorporate serpentine hairpin switchbacks rather than climbing straight up a mountain ridge. Frame your explanation in terms of force and travel distance.

### Answers

1. $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{8\text{ m}}{2\text{ m}} = \mathbf{4}$.
   $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{800\text{ N}}{4} = \mathbf{200\text{ N}}$.
   *Check:* $200\text{ N} \times 8\text{ m} = 1600\text{ J} = 800\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m}$ ✅.

2. $\text{MA} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{Effort}} = \frac{600\text{ N}}{150\text{ N}} = 4$.
   $l = \text{MA} \times h = 4 \times 1.2\text{ m} = \mathbf{4.8\text{ m}}$.
   *Check:* $150\text{ N} \times 4.8\text{ m} = 720\text{ J} = 600\text{ N} \times 1.2\text{ m}$ ✅.

3. $\text{MA} = \frac{840\text{ N}}{280\text{ N}} = \mathbf{3}$.
   $\text{VR} = \frac{l}{h} = \frac{6\text{ m}}{1.5\text{ m}} = \mathbf{4}$.
   $\eta = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{VR}} \times 100\% = \frac{3}{4} \times 100\% = \mathbf{75\%}$.
   $\text{Work Input} = 280\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = 1680\text{ J}$.
   $\text{Work Output} = 840\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = 1260\text{ J}$.
   $\text{Energy Dissipated} = 1680\text{ J} - 1260\text{ J} = \mathbf{420\text{ J}}$.
   *Check:* $\frac{1260}{1680} = 0.75 = 75\%$ ✅.

4. $\text{MA} = \frac{l}{t} = \frac{15\text{ cm}}{3\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{5}$.
   $\text{Splitting Force} = \text{MA} \times \text{Effort} = 5 \times 80\text{ N} = \mathbf{400\text{ N}}$.
   *Check:* $\frac{400}{80} = 5$ ✅.

5. Effort circumference $= 2 \times 3.14 \times 0.5\text{ m} = 3.14\text{ m}$.
   Pitch $p = 4\text{ mm} = 0.004\text{ m}$.
   $\text{MA} = \frac{3.14\text{ m}}{0.004\text{ m}} = \mathbf{785}$.
   $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{7850\text{ N}}{785} = \mathbf{10\text{ N}}$.
   *Check:* $10\text{ N} \times 785 = 7850\text{ N}$ ✅.

6. $\text{MA} = \frac{R}{r} = \frac{50\text{ cm}}{10\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{5}$.
   $\text{Effort} = \frac{\text{Load}}{\text{MA}} = \frac{750\text{ N}}{5} = \mathbf{150\text{ N}}$.
   *Check:* $150\text{ N} \times 5 = 750\text{ N}$ ✅.

7. $\text{MA} = \frac{R}{r} = \frac{60\text{ cm}}{15\text{ cm}} = 4$.
   $\text{Effort} = \frac{240\text{ N}}{4} = \mathbf{60\text{ N}}$.
   *Check:* $60\text{ N} \times 4 = 240\text{ N}$ ✅.

8. (a) **False** ❌. A gentler slope reduces the required pushing effort, but the mechanical work required (under ideal conditions) remains identical because the distance increases proportionately.
   (b) **True** ✅. A wedge is geometrically formed by two inclined planes placed base-to-base.
   (c) **True** ✅. Because $\text{MA} = 2\pi r / p$, a smaller pitch $p$ increases the mechanical advantage.
   (d) **False** ❌. When effort is applied at the wheel, ideal $\text{MA} = R / r$.

9. $\text{VR} = \frac{R}{r} = \frac{25\text{ cm}}{5\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{5}$.
   $\text{MA} = \frac{300\text{ N}}{75\text{ N}} = \mathbf{4}$.
   $\eta = \frac{\text{MA}}{\text{VR}} \times 100\% = \frac{4}{5} \times 100\% = \mathbf{80\%}$.
   *Check:* Lifting load $0.1\text{ m}$ requires effort to move $0.5\text{ m}$: $\text{Work Input} = 75 \times 0.5 = 37.5\text{ J}$; $\text{Work Output} = 300 \times 0.1 = 30\text{ J}$; $\frac{30}{37.5} = 80\%$ ✅.

10. A direct ascent up a mountainside represents a steep inclined plane with a very small length $l$, requiring an enormous driving force that vehicle engines cannot deliver. Serpentine switchbacks dramatically increase the road length $l$ for the same vertical rise $h$. This large ratio of $l / h$ significantly reduces the required driving force, allowing vehicles to climb steadily at the cost of traveling a longer distance.
