# Topic 3: Magnetisation and Demagnetisation

In the previous topic, we explored the invisible architecture of magnetic fields: closed continuous loops radiating externally from North to South, crowding densely near the poles, and governing the orientation of compass needles across the globe. This raises a natural and fundamental question: **how is an ordinary, unmagnetised piece of iron or steel transformed into a magnet, and how does an active magnet lose its magnetic power?**

In this topic, we examine the physical processes of **magnetisation** (creating magnets) and **demagnetisation** (destroying magnetism). Using the intuitive physical concept of **magnetic domains ("tiny magnets")**, we uncover the microscopic mechanisms behind these transformations, evaluate temporary versus permanent magnets, and learn the essential engineering practices required to preserve magnetic strength over time.

---

## A Steel Needle and a Dropped Magnet in the School Lab

During an afternoon physics practical session, Mr. Thorne handed **Liam** and **Maya** an ordinary, unmagnetised carbon steel sewing needle and said: "First, bring the tip of this needle near that shallow dish of small steel dressmaker pins. Observe what happens."

Liam held the needle close to the pins. Not a single pin stirred. "Mr. Thorne, it has no effect whatsoever; it is just an ordinary piece of steel."

Mr. Thorne then produced a strong bar magnet. "Now, take one specific pole of this magnet—let us use its North pole—and stroke the needle along its length from one end to the other in a single direction. When you reach the far end, lift the magnet high away from the needle in a wide arc before bringing it back to the start. Repeat this unidirectional stroking motion smoothly 30 to 40 times."

Liam followed the instructions with meticulous care. When he held the needle over the dish once more, **the needle instantly picked up four steel pins.** Liam gasped in surprise. "It actually turned into a magnet!"

On a nearby storage shelf lay an older bar magnet. Liam recalled that a student had accidentally knocked it off the bench onto the hard tile floor several times the previous week, and on another occasion, it had been left sitting right beside an electric laboratory hotplate. Mr. Thorne smiled and said: "Now test this old bar magnet with the pins, Liam."

Originally, this bar magnet could easily lift 15 pins at its pole face. When Liam offered it to the dish, it weakly picked up only 4 pins.

Liam frowned thoughtfully. "Mr. Thorne, **where did the magnetism come from** in my steel needle? Did it flow out of the bar magnet like liquid? And **where did the magnetism go** in that dropped, heated bar magnet?"

Mr. Thorne smiled. "No magnetism flowed into the needle, Liam, and none leaked into the floor. The secret lies entirely within the microscopic structure of the metal itself." We will solve both of Liam's questions as we explore this topic.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnetisation** | The physical process of converting an unmagnetised ferromagnetic material into a magnet | Stroking a steel sewing needle with a bar magnet |
| **Demagnetisation** | The physical process by which a magnet loses part or all of its magnetic strength | Heating a magnet red-hot or dropping it repeatedly |
| **Magnetic material** | A substance that experiences strong magnetic attraction and can be magnetised | Iron, steel, nickel, cobalt, alnico |
| **Non-magnetic material** | A substance that experiences no perceptible magnetic force and cannot be magnetised | Copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass |
| **Temporary magnet** | A magnet that acquires magnetism readily but loses it almost immediately when the magnetising influence is removed | An unhardened soft iron nail, an electromagnet core |
| **Permanent magnet** | A magnet fabricated from a material that retains its magnetism for years after being magnetised | A carbon steel bar magnet, an alnico speaker magnet |
| **Soft iron** | Annealed, chemically pure iron that is easily magnetised and demagnetised | The magnetic core of an electric bell |
| **Alnico and ticonal** | Specialized ferromagnetic alloys of aluminium, nickel, cobalt, and iron engineered for powerful permanent magnets | High-performance loudspeaker and motor magnets |
| **Magnetic keeper** | A bar of soft iron placed across the poles of stored permanent magnets to maintain closed magnetic circuits | Soft iron strips bridging paired bar magnets in storage |
| **Magnetic domain ("tiny magnets")** | Microscopic regions within a ferromagnetic material where atomic magnetic moments are naturally aligned parallel | The microscopic dipoles that align during stroking |

## Part A: Magnetic Materials and the "Tiny Magnets" Model

### Which Materials Can Be Magnetised?

Not all substances in nature can be transformed into magnets.
- **Ferromagnetic materials:** Substances such as **iron, steel, nickel, cobalt**, and specialized alloys (such as **alnico** and **ticonal**) interact strongly with magnetic fields. They can be readily magnetised to become artificial magnets.
- **Non-magnetic materials:** Substances such as **copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass, and paper** cannot be magnetised under any circumstances. No matter how many thousands of times a copper wire is stroked with a powerful magnet, it will never acquire magnetic poles or pick up steel pins.

### The Tiny Magnets Model: Where Did the Magnetism Come From?

To understand how an unmagnetised steel bar becomes a magnet, nineteenth-century physicists developed the **molecular magnet model** (formalized in modern quantum physics as the theory of **magnetic domains**).

Imagine every ferromagnetic object as being composed of billions of sub-microscopic magnetic units, or **"tiny magnets"**. Each microscopic unit possesses its own tiny North pole and South pole.
- **In an unmagnetised specimen:** Thermal agitation and internal forces cause these tiny magnets to point randomly in all possible directions, forming closed microscopic loops. Because their orientations are completely disordered, the magnetic field of one tiny magnet is canceled by the opposing field of another. As a result, **the net external magnetic field of the entire piece is zero**.
- **In a magnetised specimen:** An external magnetic influence forces these tiny magnets to pivot and align parallel to one another, pointing in the same direction. When aligned, their microscopic fields reinforce one another constructively. One end of the specimen accumulates an excess of uncancelled North poles, while the opposite end accumulates an excess of uncancelled South poles. The piece now functions as a **complete permanent magnet**.

```
  Unmagnetised specimen                Magnetised specimen
  (Tiny magnets randomly oriented)     (Tiny magnets aligned parallel)

   ↗   ↙   ↑   ←   ↘                   S  → → → → → → →  N
   ↓   →   ↖   ↗   ↙                      → → → → → → →
   ←   ↘   ↑   ↙   →                      → → → → → → →
   ↖   ↓   ↗   ←   ↘                      → → → → → → →

   Microscopic fields cancel out:       Microscopic fields add up:
   Net external field = 0               Net North pole at right, South at left
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The "tiny magnets" (domain) model of magnetism. In an unmagnetised piece, random orientations cancel all external forces. In a magnetised piece, parallel alignment produces strong resultant North and South poles.

We can now resolve **Liam's first question**:
When Liam stroked the sewing needle with the bar magnet, **no physical magnetism "flowed" into the steel from the outside.** The tiny magnets were already present inside the needle from the moment it was manufactured. In the unmagnetised needle, they were jumbled in complete disarray. The external magnetic pole pulled these microscopic dipoles into disciplined, parallel alignment.

> **Magnetisation:** The physical process of aligning the randomly oriented microscopic magnetic domains within a ferromagnetic material so that their magnetic fields reinforce one another.

### What Happens When a Magnet is Broken into Pieces?

In Topic 1, we learned that cutting a bar magnet in half produces two complete magnets, and that magnetic monopoles do not exist. The domain model makes the physical reason immediately intuitive:

Inside the magnet, all the tiny magnets are already aligned in rows. When you saw the magnet across its midpoint, you do not isolate the poles; you simply expose the freshly severed interior surfaces. One exposed surface presents an unbroken array of North-pointing tips, while the opposing exposed surface presents an array of South-pointing tails.

```
 Original magnet:        S [→ → → → → → → →] N

 Broken into two:        S [→ → → →] N     S [→ → → →] N
                           Piece 1           Piece 2

 Broken into four:       S[→ →]N  S[→ →]N  S[→ →]N  S[→ →]N
```

> **Figure 3.2** – Severing a magnet exposes internal conjugate poles. Every fragment retains parallel domain alignment, emerging as a complete dipolar magnet.

## Part B: Methods of Magnetisation

Artificial magnets can be produced using four distinct physical methods:

### Method 1: Single-Touch (Single-Stroke) Method

In the single-touch method, an unmagnetised steel bar is stroked repeatedly along its length using **a single pole of a permanent magnet**.

**Step-by-step procedure:**
1. Place the unmagnetised steel bar flat on a non-magnetic wooden workbench, marking its ends $A$ and $B$.
2. Place one pole of a strong bar magnet (for example, its **North pole**) onto End $A$ of the steel bar.
3. Stroke the magnet firmly and steadily along the bar from **End $A$ to End $B$**.
4. When the magnet reaches End $B$, **lift it high into the air** along a wide vertical arc.
5. Bring the magnet back through the air to End $A$, place it down, and repeat the stroke. Perform this cycle **30 to 40 times in the exact same direction**.

**Polarity Rule:**
As the stroking North pole moves from $A$ to $B$, its attractive force drags the South-seeking tails of the tiny magnets along behind it. Consequently, the South poles are pulled toward the trailing end where the stroke finishes.

> **Polarity Rule for Single Touch:** *The trailing end (the end where the stroke finishes and the magnet is lifted) always acquires the **opposite polarity** to that of the stroking pole.*

Thus, if stroking with a **North pole** from $A$ to $B$:
- End $B$ (trailing end) becomes a **South (S) pole**.
- End $A$ (starting end) becomes a **North (N) pole**.

```
        S
        ║   Bar magnet (N pole stroking downwards)
        N  →  →  →  →  →  →  ↗  (Lift high at End B)
   ┌──────────────────────────┐
   │ A                      B │   Steel bar
   └──────────────────────────┘
      N                      S
   (Starting end)         (Trailing end)
```

> **Figure 3.3** – Single-touch magnetisation. Stroking from $A$ to $B$ with an $N$ pole creates a South pole at trailing end $B$ and a North pole at starting end $A$.

**Critical precautions:**
- **Always stroke unidirectionally:** Never scrub the magnet back and forth. Stroking backward undoes the alignment achieved on the forward pass, neutralizing the domain alignment.
- **Lift the magnet high on the return path:** If the magnet is dragged backward across the bar, it will demagnetise the specimen.
- **Use a strong magnet:** A weak magnet cannot exert sufficient torque to align stubborn domains in hardened steel.

### Method 2: Double-Touch (Divided-Touch) Method

The double-touch method produces a stronger and more symmetrical magnetization than the single-touch method by utilizing **two permanent bar magnets simultaneously**.

**Step-by-step procedure:**
1. Place the steel bar $AB$ horizontally on a wooden board.
2. Take two strong bar magnets. Place their **opposite poles** (one North pole and one South pole) together at the exact center of the steel bar, separated by a small non-magnetic wooden or cardboard spacer.
3. Draw both magnets simultaneously outward toward the ends—the North pole stroking toward **End $B$**, and the South pole stroking toward **End $A$**.
4. Upon reaching the ends, lift both magnets high above the bar, return them through the air to the center, and repeat the outward strokes **30 to 40 times**.

**Polarity:**
- End $B$ (stroked by the North pole) becomes a **South (S) pole**.
- End $A$ (stroked by the South pole) becomes a **North (N) pole**.

```
     S pole magnet ←  ·  → N pole magnet
        (Both start at center and stroke outward)

   ┌───────────────────────────────────┐
   │ A                               B │   Steel bar
   └───────────────────────────────────┘
     N                                 S
   (S-pole stroke finishes here)     (N-pole stroke finishes here)
```

> **Figure 3.4** – Double-touch magnetisation. Opposite poles stroke outward from the center, producing symmetrical and powerful magnetization.

### Method 3: Electrical Method (Fastest and Most Effective)

The modern, industrial, and most powerful laboratory method for manufacturing permanent magnets is the **electrical method**.

A steel bar is inserted inside a **solenoid**—a long, cylindrical coil comprising hundreds of turns of insulated copper wire. The solenoid is connected in series with a switch and a **direct current (DC)** power source (such as a battery or DC power supply).

```
       ┌── DC Source ──┐
       │               │
   ┌───┴───────────────┴───┐
   │ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○ ○   │   Solenoid coil with steel bar inside
   │ [N═════════════════S] │   (Magnetic polarity determined by DC current direction)
   └───────────────────────┘
```

> **Figure 3.5** – Electrical magnetisation using a solenoid carrying direct current (DC). The intense internal magnetic field aligns domains within seconds.

When direct current energizes the coil, it generates an intense, uniform magnetic field throughout the interior cavity. Under this immense magnetic influence, the domains in the steel bar snap into near-perfect parallel alignment almost instantaneously (within a few seconds). When the current is switched off and the bar is removed, it emerges as a powerful permanent magnet.

The magnetic polarity of the bar depends strictly on the direction of current flow through the solenoid (governed by the right-hand grip rule, detailed in Topic 4). Reversing the direction of the DC current reverses the magnetic poles of the resulting magnet.

> **Safety Warning:** Electrical magnetisation activities must be performed strictly using low-voltage DC cells under teacher supervision for only a few seconds at a time. Leaving current on will cause severe resistive heating, melting wire insulation and posing burn hazards. **Never attempt this using household alternating current (AC) mains sockets**, which carry lethal voltages and will demagnetise rather than magnetise the specimen!

### Method 4: Magnetisation by the Earth's Magnetic Field

Because the Earth is enveloped in a continuous geomagnetic field, ferromagnetic materials can be weakly magnetised simply by using the Earth's field.

If a soft iron or steel rod is oriented precisely along the magnetic North–South direction (tilted downward at the local angle of magnetic dip) and struck sharply and repeatedly with a heavy hammer, the mechanical shock loosens the atomic domains. The ambient geomagnetic field then coaxes these agitated domains into weak alignment. Long-standing vertical steel structural beams and underground iron water pipes often acquire weak residual magnetism through this exact mechanism over decades.

### Testing the Newly Magnetised Needle

To confirm conclusively that Liam's steel needle has become a functional magnet, two rigorous physical tests are applied:
1. **The Repulsion Test:** Bring a known bar magnet near the needle. Present its North pole to one end of the needle. If that end is **repelled**, the needle is definitively a magnet, and that end is a North pole. (Recall from Topic 1: **repulsion is the only sure test of magnetism**).
2. **The Directive Suspension Test:** Float the needle on a small slice of cork in a bowl of still water or suspend it from an unspun silk thread. If the needle rotates and consistently comes to rest pointing along the geographic North–South line, it has acquired true permanent magnetism.

## Part C: Temporary and Permanent Magnets

Ferromagnetic materials exhibit vastly different behaviors in their ability to acquire and retain magnetic alignment. We classify them into two primary categories:

- **Soft magnetic materials (e.g., Soft Iron):** Annealed, pure iron has high magnetic permeability and low coercivity. Its domains align with remarkable ease under weak magnetic fields, but because the crystal lattice offers minimal resistance to domain rotation, thermal vibrations scramble the domains the instant the external field is removed. Soft iron forms **temporary magnets**.
- **Hard magnetic materials (e.g., Carbon Steel, Alnico, Ticonal):** Hardened carbon steel and complex alloys contain internal metallurgical barriers (carbide precipitates and lattice strains) that resist domain motion. Magnetising them requires intense external fields. However, once their domains are forced into alignment, these internal barriers lock them securely in place. They retain their magnetism for years, forming **permanent magnets**.

| Characteristic | Soft Iron (Temporary) | Steel / Alnico / Ticonal (Permanent) |
|---|---|---|
| **Ease of magnetisation** | Extremely easy; requires weak fields | Difficult; requires intense fields |
| **Retention of magnetism** | Loses magnetism almost instantly | Retains magnetism for years |
| **Magnetic classification** | Temporary magnet | Permanent magnet |
| **Primary applications** | Electromagnet cores, electric bells, transformer cores, magnetic keepers | Bar magnets, compass needles, loudspeakers, DC motor magnets |

> **A Helpful Mnemonic:** *Easy to acquire, easy to lose (temporary soft iron). Difficult to acquire, difficult to lose (permanent steel).*

## Part D: Demagnetisation: How Magnets Lose Their Magnetism

We can now answer **Liam's second question**:
Where did the magnetism go in the dropped, heated bar magnet?
**The magnetism did not leak out or vanish into space.** The total magnetic material remained completely unchanged. What was destroyed was the **orderly, parallel alignment of the microscopic domains**. The mechanical shocks from dropping and the thermal energy from the heater agitated the atomic lattice, throwing the tiny magnets back into random, chaotic orientations where their fields cancel out once more.

> **Demagnetisation:** The physical process of disrupting the parallel alignment of magnetic domains within a magnet, restoring a random state of zero net external magnetization.

Permanent magnets can be demagnetised through five distinct mechanisms:

1. **Intense Heating:**
   Heating a magnet imparts kinetic thermal energy to its constituent atoms. As temperature rises, thermal vibrations overcome the magnetic forces holding the domains in alignment, causing them to tumble into random disorder.
   At a specific threshold temperature called the **Curie temperature** (or Curie point), ferromagnetic materials lose their permanent magnetism completely. For pure iron, the Curie temperature is approximately **$770\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$**. Even at temperatures well below the Curie point, moderate heat noticeably degrades magnetic strength.

2. **Severe Mechanical Impact (Hammering or Dropping):**
   Dropping a magnet repeatedly onto a hard concrete floor or striking it vigorously with a hammer sends mechanical shockwaves through the crystal lattice. These violent mechanical vibrations dislodge domains from their pinned positions, allowing them to flip into random orientations.

3. **Alternating Current (AC) Demagnetisation:**
   The standard industrial method to demagnetise precision tools, watches, and steel components is to insert the magnet inside a solenoid carrying **alternating current (AC)**. AC reverses its directional polarity 50 or 60 times every second ($50\text{ to }60\text{ Hz}$). As the current oscillates, the internal field rapidly flips the domains back and forth. The magnet is then slowly withdrawn several meters away from the active solenoid. As the oscillating field weakens smoothly to zero, the domains freeze in completely randomized directions, leaving the piece thoroughly demagnetised.

4. **Improper Storage (Self-Demagnetisation):**
   When a bar magnet is stored isolated in open air, its own external North and South poles create an opposing magnetic field (a *demagnetising field*) that passes backward through the magnet's interior. Over months and years, this continuous opposing field gradually coaxes the domains near the ends out of alignment.

5. **Exposure to Strong Opposing Magnetic Fields:**
   Subjecting a permanent magnet to an external magnetic field oriented in the reverse direction can force its domains to rotate backward, degrading or completely inverting its polarity.

### Why Magnetic Keepers Are Used

To prevent self-demagnetisation during long-term storage, permanent magnets are stored using **magnetic keepers**.

Two bar magnets are arranged side-by-side with **unlike poles adjacent** (North beside South), separated by a central non-magnetic wooden slat. Across both exposed ends, strips of soft iron—the **keepers**—are placed bridging the adjacent pole pairs.

```
      Keeper                               Keeper
   (Soft iron)                          (Soft iron)
     ┃  [N═══════════════════S]  ┃
     ┃                           ┃
     ┃  [S═══════════════════N]  ┃
```

> **Figure 3.6** – Proper storage of bar magnets in conjugate pairs with soft iron keepers. Field lines travel continuously through the iron, forming closed, low-reluctance magnetic circuits that prevent domain degradation.

Why does this preserve magnetic strength?
1. Soft iron has high magnetic permeability: it magnetises instantly by induction under the influence of the adjacent pole faces.
2. The field lines emerging from the North pole of the upper magnet enter the soft iron keeper, travel through the metal, and pass directly into the South pole of the lower magnet.
3. The magnetic field lines are thus channeled through a **continuous closed loop of ferromagnetic material**, with virtually no stray fields leaking into the surrounding air. Because no opposing demagnetising field is created, the internal domains remain locked in parallel alignment indefinitely.

## Part E: Care of Magnets and Everyday Applications

### Proper Care and Handling of Magnets

To ensure permanent magnets retain their strength for decades:
- **Never drop magnets** onto hard floors or strike them with tools.
- **Never expose magnets to open flames, electric heaters, or high temperatures.**
- **Always store bar magnets in conjugate pairs** with unlike poles adjacent and bridged by **soft iron keepers**.
- **Store horseshoe magnets with a single soft iron keeper** bridging their parallel pole faces.
- **Isolate magnets from strong external alternating magnetic fields.**

> **A Critical Clarification:** Lifting and holding ferromagnetic objects does **not** wear out or drain a magnet. A magnet does not consume "magnetic fuel" when holding a paper clip. Only heat, impacts, improper storage, or reverse fields can degrade its domain alignment.

### Magnets in Daily Devices and Vulnerabilities

Many modern electronic, communication, and data storage systems utilize microscopic magnetic encoding and are highly vulnerable to stray magnetic fields:
- **Credit and debit cards:** Encoded data on magnetic stripes consists of microscopic domains. Brushing a card against a strong neodymium magnet alters these domain alignments, permanently corrupting account data.
- **Mechanical wristwatches:** Balance wheels and hairsprings can become magnetised, causing erratic timekeeping.
- **Traditional computer hard disk drives (HDDs) and magnetic tapes:** Magnetic recording media store binary data via localized domain polarities. Exposure to powerful external fields scrambles recorded bits, resulting in catastrophic data loss.

### Specific Applications of Temporary and Permanent Magnets

| Magnet Type | Material Used | Practical Engineering Applications |
|---|---|---|
| **Temporary** | Annealed Soft Iron | Electromagnet cores for scrap-lifting cranes, electric bells, relays, circuit breakers, power transformers, magnetic keepers |
| **Permanent** | High-Carbon Steel | Navigational compass needles, simple bar magnets, magnetic door catches, screwdriver tips |
| **Permanent** | Alnico / Ticonal / Ferrite | Acoustic loudspeaker voice-coil magnets, dynamic microphones, DC electric motors, bicycle dynamos, MRI pre-polarizers |

**Example 1 (Single touch: 6 polarity scenarios):** An unmagnetised steel sewing needle has two marked extremities: End A (the threaded eye, on the left) and End B (the sharp point, on the right). Applying the rule that *the trailing end where the stroke finishes acquires the opposite polarity to the stroking pole*, determine the magnetic poles produced at End A and End B for each of the following 6 stroking procedures:

| Case | Stroking Pole | Stroke Direction | Trailing End | Trailing End Polarity | Starting End Polarity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\text{N}$ | A to B | B | **S** | A: **N** |
| 2 | $\text{S}$ | A to B | B | **N** | A: **S** |

| 4 | $\text{S}$ | B to A | A | **N** | B: **S** |

        S
        ║   Magnet (N pole stroking downwards)

   ┌───────────────────────────┐

   └───────────────────────────┘

> **Figure 3.7** – Case 1 illustrated: Stroking from Eye (A) to Point (B) with an $N$ pole produces a South pole at the point (B) and a North pole at the eye (A).

**Verification:** In every single case, the two ends develop conjugate (opposite) polarities ✅. Trailing end is always opposite to the stroking pole ✅. Cases 1 and 3 use an N pole but reversed directions, producing inverted polarities ✅. Case 5 is identical to Case 1 ✅.

**Example 4 (Quantifying demagnetisation by pin counts):** In Mr. Thorne's laboratory, a freshly magnetised bar magnet initially lifted 15 steel dressmaker pins. After being dropped onto the floor 10 times, its lifting capacity dropped to 10 pins. Subsequently, after being left near an electric laboratory heater, its capacity plummeted to 4 pins. Treating pin count as a direct measure of magnetic strength:
(a) Calculate the percentage loss in magnetic strength caused by mechanical dropping.
(b) Calculate the total percentage loss in magnetic strength from the original baseline after thermal heating.

**Solution:**
(a) Percentage loss due to mechanical dropping:
$$\Delta N_{\text{drop}} = 15 - 10 = 5\text{ pins}$$
$$\text{Percentage loss} = \frac{5}{15} \times 100\% = \mathbf{33.3\%}$$
(b) Total percentage loss from original baseline after heating:
$$\Delta N_{\text{total}} = 15 - 4 = 11\text{ pins}$$
$$\text{Total percentage loss} = \frac{11}{15} \times 100\% = \mathbf{73.3\%}$$

**Verification:**
$33.3\%$ of $15 = 0.333 \times 15 = 5\text{ pins lost} \rightarrow 15 - 5 = 10\text{ pins remaining}$ ✅.
$73.3\%$ of $15 = 0.733 \times 15 = 11\text{ pins lost} \rightarrow 15 - 11 = 4\text{ pins remaining}$ ✅.
Mechanical shock caused approximately a one-third loss; thermal heating compounded this to nearly a three-quarters loss.

- **Electromagnet core for a salvage crane:** **Soft iron.** Must magnetise intensely when current flows and demagnetise completely the moment current is switched off to drop the scrap load instantly.

**Example 6 (Why keepers are manufactured from soft iron rather than steel):** Explain why magnetic keepers are always fabricated from soft iron rather than hardened steel.

**Solution:**
The sole function of a keeper is to provide a low-reluctance bridge that channels magnetic field lines smoothly between opposite pole faces.
- **Soft iron** magnetises instantly by induction when placed across the poles, providing an ideal path for the lines, and demagnetises completely the instant it is removed. It acts strictly as a passive magnetic conduit.
- If **steel** were used, it would gradually become permanently magnetised itself. Upon removal, the steel keeper would possess its own independent magnetic poles, complicating handling and distorting the field of the primary magnets.

**Verification:** Keepers must be temporary conduits, not permanent secondary magnets ✅.

**Example 7 (Fundamental physical reasoning):**
(a) *Can a permanent magnet attract a piece of solid brass?* **No.** Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc, both of which are non-ferromagnetic.
(b) *Can an electrical copper wire be converted into a magnet by stroking it with a permanent magnet?* **No.** Copper atoms do not possess permanent unpaired electron dipole moments that form magnetic domains. Without domains, copper cannot be magnetised by stroking, heating, or electrical solenoids.

**Verification:** Both brass and copper are categorized as non-magnetic materials in our foundational taxonomy ✅.

**Example 8 (Distinguishing an unmagnetised steel bar from a magnet):** You are given two identical-looking steel rods: Rod 1 is an unmagnetised carbon steel bar, and Rod 2 is a permanently magnetised bar magnet. Using only a known test magnet, explain how you would unambiguously identify which rod is the magnet.

**Solution:**
Bring the North pole of the known test magnet near one end of Rod 1, and then near the opposite end of Rod 1.
- If Rod 1 is an **unmagnetised steel bar**, both ends will be **attracted** to the test magnet's North pole (and both ends will also be attracted to its South pole).
- Now repeat with Rod 2. Because Rod 2 is a **permanent magnet**, one of its ends will be attracted, but its opposite end will be **vigorously repelled** by the test magnet's North pole.
- **The rod that exhibits magnetic repulsion is definitively the permanent magnet.**

**Verification:** Unmagnetised ferromagnetic objects only exhibit attraction; repulsion is the infallible test of a true magnet ✅.

**Example 9 (Subdividing a magnetised needle):** A $6\text{ cm}$ magnetised steel sewing needle is cut with wire snips into three equal segments measuring $2\text{ cm}$ each.
(a) How many functional magnets now exist?
(b) How many North poles and how many South poles are there in total?

**Solution:**
(a) Because magnetic monopoles cannot exist, each severed segment maintains its internal domain alignment. Therefore, there are **3 complete, independent magnets**.
(b) Each complete magnet possesses 1 North pole and 1 South pole. Thus:
$$\text{Total North poles} = 3, \quad \text{Total South poles} = 3$$
$$\text{Total poles} = 3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6\text{ poles}}$$

**Verification:** $3 \text{ pieces} \times 2\text{ cm} = 6\text{ cm}$ ✅; Total poles $= 3\text{ N} + 3\text{ S} = 6\text{ poles}$ ✅.

**Example 10 (Identifying a flawed magnetisation technique):** In an experiment, Liam stroked a steel rod with the North pole of a magnet 20 times from left to right, and then 20 times from right to left in a continuous scrubbing motion. When tested, the rod failed to pick up any pins. Explain why this technique failed.

**Solution:**
Stroking the rod from left to right rotated the microscopic domains so that their South poles pointed toward the right end. Stroking in the reverse direction (from right to left) exerted opposing magnetic torques, rotating the domains back in the opposite direction. The two opposing operations canceled each other out completely:
$$\text{Net alignment effect} = (+20\text{ strokes}) + (-20\text{ strokes}) = 0$$
The domains were left in their original randomized state. To succeed, **strokes must always be performed in a single, consistent direction, lifting the magnet high into the air on each return path**.

**Verification:** Unidirectional alignment is an indispensable condition of mechanical stroke magnetisation ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Scrubbing back and forth during single-touch magnetisation.**
   Students often rub the permanent magnet vigorously to-and-fro across the steel specimen like an eraser. This bidirectional scrubbing repeatedly reverses domain alignment, leaving the specimen completely unmagnetised or severely weakened. Strokes must always be strictly unidirectional.

2. **Mistake 2: Believing that magnetism "flows" into the needle like a liquid.**
   Magnetism is not a material fluid transferred from the stroking magnet into the steel. The microscopic magnetic dipoles (domains) are already present inside the steel; stroking merely aligns these pre-existing domains into parallel order.

3. **Mistake 3: Assuming steel loses magnetism as quickly as soft iron.**
   Soft iron has very low magnetic coercivity and loses its magnetism almost instantly when removed from an external field. Steel has high coercivity; once magnetised, its internal crystalline structure locks domains firmly in place, retaining permanent magnetism for years.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing that heating or hammering strengthens a magnet.**
   Some students imagine that heating or hammering "forges" a stronger magnet. The physical reality is the exact opposite: thermal kinetic agitation and mechanical shockwaves violently disrupt domain alignment, severely weakening or completely demagnetising the specimen.

5. **Mistake 5: Dismissing magnetic keepers as unnecessary packaging.**
   Keepers are not decorative packing materials; they are vital functional components. Without soft iron keepers, isolated bar magnets produce self-demagnetising fields that degrade their own domain alignment over time.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming picking up objects wears out a magnet.**
   Lifting steel pins or holding notes on a refrigerator consumes zero magnetic energy and causes zero loss of magnetization. A permanent magnet will maintain its strength indefinitely unless exposed to thermal energy, mechanical impacts, reverse fields, or improper storage.

## Quick Recap

- Ferromagnetic substances (iron, steel, nickel, cobalt, alnico) are composed of microscopic **magnetic domains ("tiny magnets")**.
- In an **unmagnetised** state, domains point randomly in all directions and cancel out externally. In a **magnetised** state, domains are aligned parallel, producing strong resultant North and South poles.
- **Magnetisation methods:**
  - **Single touch:** Stroking unidirectionally with one pole 30–40 times; trailing end acquires opposite polarity to stroking pole.
  - **Double touch:** Stroking outward from center with two opposite poles simultaneously.
  - **Electrical method:** Placing specimen inside a solenoid carrying direct current (DC); fastest, most uniform, and most powerful method.
  - **Earth's field method:** Aligning an iron rod North–South and striking it with a hammer.
- **Testing a magnet:** **Repulsion is the only infallible test**; directive suspension aligns North–South.
- **Temporary vs Permanent magnets:**
  - **Soft iron:** Easily magnetised, loses magnetism immediately $\rightarrow$ **temporary magnet** (electromagnet cores, electric bell armatures, keepers).
  - **Steel / Alnico:** Hard to magnetise, retains magnetism for years $\rightarrow$ **permanent magnet** (compass needles, bar magnets, speakers).
- **Demagnetisation methods:** Intense heating (above the **Curie temperature**, $\approx 770\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$ for iron), mechanical shocks (hammering/dropping), alternating current (AC) solenoids, improper storage (self-demagnetisation), and opposing magnetic fields.
- **Magnetic keepers:** Soft iron strips bridging unlike poles of paired magnets in storage; they channel field lines into closed loops, preventing self-demagnetisation.
- Severing a magnet produces smaller, complete dipoles; magnetic monopoles do not exist.
- Magnetic data on credit cards, hard disks, and recording tapes can be destroyed by proximity to strong permanent magnets.

### Answers

1. **Magnetisation** is the physical process of aligning the randomly oriented microscopic magnetic domains within a ferromagnetic material so that their magnetic fields reinforce one another.
   *Domain explanation:* In an unmagnetised steel needle, the microscopic magnetic dipoles ("tiny magnets") point in random, chaotic directions, so their individual magnetic fields cancel out to zero. When stroked repeatedly in one direction with a magnetic pole, the external field exerts magnetic torques that force these tiny magnets to rotate into parallel alignment. Their microscopic fields add together, producing a resultant North pole at one end and a South pole at the other.

2. (a) By the trailing-end rule, the end where the stroke finishes acquires the opposite polarity to the stroking pole. Since stroking is from P to Q using a South pole, **End Q becomes a North (N) pole**.
   (b) **End P becomes a South (S) pole**.
   (c) *Sketch description:* Draw a horizontal steel bar labeled P on the left and Q on the right. Draw a vertical bar magnet above it with its S pole pointing downward, contacting the bar at P and sweeping rightward with arrows $\rightarrow \rightarrow \rightarrow$ toward Q, lifting along a curved arc at Q.    - The South pole strokes toward the right end: trailing end acquires opposite polarity $\rightarrow$ **Right end becomes a North (N) pole**.
   *Verification:* Left end = S, Right end = N; conjugate pair ✅.

4. Unidirectional stroking ensures that the microscopic domains are consistently rotated into one unified direction. If the magnet is scrubbed back and forth, the forward stroke aligns domains in one direction, while the backward stroke immediately rotates them in the opposite direction. These opposing actions neutralize each other, leaving the domains in randomized disorder and producing **zero net magnetization**.

5. (a) **Ease of magnetisation:** Soft iron is magnetised very easily under weak fields; carbon steel is difficult to magnetise and requires intense fields.

   *Applications:* Soft iron is used in **electromagnet cores** (or electric bells); carbon steel is used in **compass needles** (or permanent bar magnets).

   (b) Ticonal speaker magnet: **Permanent magnet**
