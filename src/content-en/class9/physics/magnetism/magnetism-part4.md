# Topic 4: Magnetic Effect of Electric Current

In the previous topic, we established that magnetism originates in magnetic materials, and that magnetic properties can be induced, weakened, or completely destroyed. At the conclusion of that topic, we posed a profound question: **can magnetic effects ever be produced without a natural or artificial permanent magnet?**

In this topic, we discover that the answer is a resounding **yes**: a **flowing electric current** generates an invisible magnetic field in the surrounding space. In other words, the source of magnetism need not be a piece of lodestone or magnetized steel; it can simply be a conducting wire carrying an electric charge in motion. We will learn how to determine the direction of this magnetic field using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule, examine how bending a wire into a loop concentrates the field, and explore how winding wire into a cylindrical coil—known as a **solenoid**—creates an artificial magnetic field virtually indistinguishable from that of a permanent bar magnet.

---

## A Compass Needle Leaps in the Physics Lab

On a bright Tuesday morning in the physics laboratory, Dr. Bennett placed several items on the demonstration bench: a 1.5 V dry cell, a plug key switch, insulated connecting leads, a thick bare copper wire, and a small magnetic plotting compass. Maya, sitting attentively in the front row, watched closely as Dr. Bennett aligned the apparatus.

Dr. Bennett turned to the class. "Maya, picture a scene from the spring of 1820. A Danish professor of natural philosophy named **Hans Christian Oersted** was delivering a lecture in Copenhagen. On his laboratory table stood an electric battery and an early magnetic compass. While demonstrating how electric current flows through a wire, he happened to close the circuit switch right over the compass. To his astonishment—and that of his students—**the magnetic compass needle twitched violently and deflected sideways**! Up until that moment, the entire scientific world believed that electricity and magnetism were two entirely independent, unrelated phenomena."

Maya frowned thoughtfully. "Dr. Bennett, a magnetic compass only responds to a magnetic field. But a copper wire isn't magnetic! Copper doesn't even attract iron filings. How could a wire make the needle move?"

Dr. Bennett smiled warmly. "That is the central mystery. Let us recreate Oersted's historic demonstration right here."

Dr. Bennett positioned the compass flat on the table, allowing its needle to settle naturally along the Earth's magnetic north-south axis. Next, he stretched the straight copper wire directly over and parallel to the needle. He closed the switch for just two seconds. **Instantaneously, the needle swung sharply to one side!** 

When Dr. Bennett opened the switch, cutting off the current, the needle promptly settled back to its north-south alignment. Then, he reversed the connections to the cell terminals and tapped the switch once more: **this time, the needle swung sharply in the opposite direction!**

Maya gasped in excitement. "Dr. Bennett! The moment electricity flows through the wire, the wire acts just like a magnet! Does that mean electricity and magnetism are actually two sides of the same coin?"

"Precisely, Maya," replied Dr. Bennett. "By the end of this topic, we will resolve the full physics behind this compass leap, and answer your question completely."

| Term | Meaning | Example |

## Part A: Oersted's Experiment

### Set-up

To reproduce Oersted's classic experiment safely and reliably, we assemble a simple circuit requiring only basic components:
1. One or two 1.5 V electrochemical cells (such as standard AA or D-cell batteries).
2. A straight length of thick copper wire (low electrical resistance).
3. A plug key or momentary push-button switch.
4. A small magnetic plotting compass resting on a level, non-magnetic surface.

The procedural steps are straightforward:
1. Place the magnetic compass on the wooden bench. The needle naturally aligns itself with the local geomagnetic meridian, pointing toward Earth's magnetic north and south (as established in [magnetism-part2.md](magnetism-part2.md)).
2. Suspend or lay the straight conducting wire **directly above and parallel** to the magnetic needle, orienting the wire along the north-south direction.
3. Connect the ends of the wire through the switch to the battery terminals.

```
       wire oriented North-South directly above the compass
   N  ──────────────────────────────────────────────────  S
       ←──  conventional current direction  ───┐
        ┌──────┐                                │
        │needle│ (magnetic compass)         cell & switch
        └──────┘                                │
   (wire positioned parallel, above needle)    ─┘
```

> **Figure 4.1** – Oersted's experimental setup: a conducting wire is positioned directly above and parallel to a magnetic compass needle, connected in series with a cell and switch.

### What Do We Observe?

When the circuit is operated, three distinct phenomena occur:

| Action Performed | Observable Physical Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Switch closed (current ON)** | The compass needle immediately swings away from its north-south orientation, showing a distinct **deflection**. |
| **Battery polarity reversed (current reversed)** | The compass needle immediately deflects in the **opposite direction**. |
| **Switch opened (current OFF)** | The magnetic needle promptly returns to its original north-south equilibrium alignment. |

### Conclusion

> **An electric current flowing through a conductor creates a magnetic field in the surrounding space.** This phenomenon is known as the **magnetic effect of electric current**. As long as charge flows, the magnetic field persists; the moment the current ceases, the magnetic field disappears completely.

**Laboratory Safety Warning:** In this demonstration, use **only low-voltage dry cells (1.5 V to 3 V)** and close the switch for **only a few seconds at a time**. Because a thick copper wire offers very little electrical resistance ($R \approx 0$), connecting it directly across a battery constitutes a near short-circuit. High currents can cause the wire and cell to overheat rapidly, potentially causing burns or battery leakage. **Never, under any circumstances, attempt this experiment using mains electricity or wall sockets!** Mains voltages (110 V to 240 V) present severe, life-threatening risks of electrocution, explosive short-circuits, and fire.

### Another Important Observation

The compass needle does not need to physically touch the copper wire. Even when the wire is suspended several centimetres above the compass with an air gap between them, the needle responds instantly. The magnetic influence travels invisibly through the air: the needle is interacting with the **magnetic field** generated by the current, not with the physical copper metal itself.

## Part B: Magnetic Field Around a Straight Wire

### The Field is Circular

What is the geometric shape of the magnetic field produced by a long, straight current-carrying wire? 

To visualize this field, we can perform a classic card experiment:
1. Pass a straight, vertical copper wire through a small hole drilled in the centre of a horizontal, rigid sheet of white cardboard.
2. Connect the wire to a DC power supply and a rheostat.
3. Sprinkle fine iron filings evenly over the cardboard surface.
4. Turn on the current and gently tap the cardboard edge with a pencil.

As the friction is broken by tapping, the iron filings align themselves under the magnetic forces, forming a pattern of **concentric circular rings** centred on the wire.

> The magnetic field lines around a straight current-carrying conductor are **concentric circles** lying in planes perpendicular to the conductor. The field lines do not travel straight along the wire; they **encircle** the wire continuously.

### Current Flowing Into or Out of the Page

When sketching three-dimensional magnetic phenomena on flat paper, physicists use standard cross-sectional vector notations:
- **$\odot$ (dot inside a circle):** represents current flowing **out of the page**, perpendicular to the paper directly toward the viewer (like the sharp tip of an approaching arrow).
- **$\otimes$ (cross inside a circle):** represents current flowing **into the page**, perpendicular to the paper directly away from the viewer (like the stabilizing fletching feathers of a receding arrow).

The circular magnetic field lines around these two configurations rotate in opposite senses:

```
          ←←←←
        ↙      ↖
       ↓   ⊙    ↑        Counter-clockwise (anticlockwise):
        ↘      ↗         up on right, left on top, down on left
          →→→→
```

> **Figure 4.2** – Current directed out of the page ($\odot$): the concentric magnetic field lines encircle the conductor in a **counter-clockwise** direction.

```
          →→→→
        ↗      ↘
       ↑   ⊗    ↓        Clockwise:
        ↖      ↙         right on top, down on right, left on bottom
          ←←←←
```

> **Figure 4.3** – Current directed into the page ($\otimes$): the concentric magnetic field lines encircle the conductor in a **clockwise** direction.

### Variation of Field Strength with Distance

The magnetic field strength around a straight conductor depends systematically on two physical quantities:
1. **Distance from the conductor ($r$):** The magnetic field is strongest in the immediate vicinity of the wire, where the concentric field lines are packed tightly together. As the radial distance from the wire increases, the field lines spread farther apart, showing that the magnetic field **decreases inversely with distance** ($B \propto 1/r$).
2. **Magnitude of the electric current ($I$):** Increasing the current through the conductor produces a proportionally stronger magnetic field at every point ($B \propto I$). Doubling the current doubles the field strength at any given radial distance.

## Part C: Direction of the Magnetic Field: Right-Hand Thumb Rule

To determine the rotational direction of the magnetic field lines around any straight current-carrying conductor without trial and error, we employ a universal mnemonic rule.

> **Right-Hand Thumb Rule (Maxwell's Right-Hand Grip Rule):** Imagine grasping the straight conductor in your **right hand** such that your outstretched **thumb points in the direction of conventional electric current**. The curvature of your **curled fingers** then indicates the direction of the magnetic field lines encircling the wire.

*Crucial note on convention:* The direction of current is always taken as **conventional current** (the flow of positive charge from positive to negative potential), exactly as established in [electricity-part2.md](../electricity/electricity-part2.md).

### Wire Carrying Current Upwards or Downwards

Consider a vertical conducting wire viewed from directly above:

```
   Current Flowing UPWARDS              Current Flowing DOWNWARDS
   (toward the observer: ⊙)             (away from the observer: ⊗)

        ↑  (thumb points up)                 ↓  (thumb points down)
        │                                    │
   Viewed from above:                   Viewed from above:
   field is COUNTER-CLOCKWISE (⟲)       field is CLOCKWISE (⟳)
```

> **Figure 4.4** – Right-Hand Thumb Rule: current directed upwards produces a counter-clockwise magnetic field when viewed from above; current directed downwards produces a clockwise field.

### Verifying the Rule

Let us verify our two-dimensional representations using our right hand:
- For current emerging from the page ($\odot$), point your right thumb directly toward your face. Notice how your four fingers naturally curl in a **counter-clockwise** circle. This matches Figure 4.2 perfectly.
- For current plunging into the page ($\otimes$), point your right thumb directly away from you into the paper. Your curled fingers now wrap in a **clockwise** circle. This matches Figure 4.3 perfectly.

### Verifying Direction with a Magnetic Compass

In any magnetic field, the direction of the field at a given point is defined by the direction in which the **North pole of a small magnetic compass needle points** (as covered in [magnetism-part2.md](magnetism-part2.md)).

Because the field lines form continuous loops around a horizontal wire, the field directly **above** the wire points in the exact opposite direction to the field directly **below** the wire. Consequently, if a compass is placed immediately above a wire, its North pole deflects in one direction; if the compass is moved directly below the wire, its North pole deflects toward the opposite side!

## Part D: Magnetic Field of a Circular Loop

What occurs if we take a straight conducting wire and bend it into a single circular loop?

Every infinitesimal segment of the curved wire acts as a straight conductor, generating its own concentric circular field lines. However, because the wire is curved into a ring, something remarkable happens inside the loop:
- In the interior region of the loop, the magnetic field contributions from all parts of the circumference point in the **exact same direction**.
- As a result, the magnetic field lines reinforce one another, creating a **strong, concentrated magnetic field** near the centre that points perpendicularly through the plane of the loop along its central axis.
- As one moves farther away from the wire into the surrounding space, the field lines curve around the outside of the ring, forming closed loops that resemble those of a thin magnetic disk.

```
         wire loop viewed in cross-section
        ⊗  ⊗  ⊗         Top segment: current flows into page (⊗)
      (                 )
     (        ←←←       )    Inside the loop, all magnetic field
      (      ←←←←       )    lines reinforce in the same direction (leftwards)
        ⊙  ⊙  ⊙         Bottom segment: current flows out of page (⊙)
```

> **Figure 4.5** – Magnetic field of a circular current loop: inside the loop, the field lines reinforce each other to produce a strong, nearly uniform axial field.

### The Two Faces of a Loop: North and South

Because magnetic field lines emerge from one face of the circular loop and curve around to enter the opposite face, a current-carrying loop behaves exactly like a very thin circular **magnetic disk** or button magnet. One face behaves as a **North pole** (where field lines emerge), while the opposite face behaves as a **South pole** (where field lines enter).

To identify which face is North and which is South without a compass, we use the **Clock Rule**:

> **Clock Rule:** Look directly at one face of the circular loop:
> - If the conventional current appears to circulate in a **counter-clockwise (anticlockwise)** direction, that face is a **North pole** ($N$).
> - If the conventional current appears to circulate in a **clockwise** direction, that face is a **South pole** ($S$).

```
   Viewing this face:                   Viewing this face:

        ┌──←──┐                              ┌──→──┐
        ↓     ↑                              ↑     ↓
        └──→──┘                              └──←──┘
   Current is COUNTER-CLOCKWISE          Current is CLOCKWISE
   This face is a NORTH POLE (N)         This face is a SOUTH POLE (S)
```

> **Figure 4.6** – The Clock Rule for identifying the magnetic polarity of a circular current-carrying loop.

*Observation:* If you look at a loop from the front and see counter-clockwise current (North pole), someone standing behind the loop looking at the rear face sees that very same current circulating clockwise (South pole). Every magnetic loop inherently possesses two opposite poles!

### Multiple Turns: A Coil

If a wire is wound into a circular coil containing $n$ identical turns in close contact carrying the same current, each individual turn generates an identical magnetic field in the same orientation. 

Because magnetic fields superimpose linearly, the total magnetic field at the centre of the coil is the sum of the fields of all individual turns:

$$\text{Total Field of Coil} \approx n \times \text{Field of a Single Turn}$$

A coil of 50 turns produces a magnetic field approximately **50 times stronger** than a single-turn loop carrying the same current.

## Part E: The Solenoid (Class 10 Overlap)

> A **solenoid** is a long helical coil consisting of many closely spaced, parallel turns of insulated conducting wire wound in the shape of a cylinder.

```
    End A                                                    End B
    (((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((
     Viewed from End B: current circulates COUNTER-CLOCKWISE (North pole)
     Viewed from End A: current circulates CLOCKWISE (South pole)
    (((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((((
```

> **Figure 4.7** – Construction of a solenoid: insulated copper wire wound in a tight cylindrical helix. When energized, one end becomes a magnetic North pole and the other a South pole.

### Magnetic Field of a Solenoid

When an electric current passes through a solenoid, the individual magnetic fields of its many adjacent turns merge to form an overall magnetic field pattern that is **virtually identical to that of a permanent bar magnet**:

```
          ╭────────────────←────────────────╮
          │       ╭────────←────────╮       │
          ↓       ↓                 ↑       ↑
    ──→───────    S(((((((((((((((((N   ──→──────
                   ──→  ──→  ──→  ──→
          ↑       ↑  (inside: from S to N) ↓       ↓
          │       ╰────────←────────╯       │
          ╰────────────────←────────────────╯
       (outside: emerges from N end, loops to S end)
```

> **Figure 4.8** – The magnetic field of an energized solenoid: outside, field lines emerge from the North end and enter the South end; inside the core, field lines are straight, parallel, and highly uniform.

The magnetic field exhibits two distinct regions:

| Region | Magnetic Field Characteristics |
|---|---|
| **Outside the solenoid** | Exactly resembles a bar magnet: curved lines emerge from the **North pole**, diverge outward, and curve back to enter the **South pole**. Far from the ends, the external field is relatively weak. |
| **Inside the solenoid** | The field lines are **straight, parallel lines** running parallel to the central cylinder axis from South to North. The interior field is **very strong, steady, and uniform** throughout the central cross-section. |
| **Continuous closed loops** | The interior and exterior field lines join smoothly to form **continuous closed loops**, confirming that magnetic field lines never terminate. |

### Polarity of a Solenoid: Right-Hand Rule for a Coil

To determine which end of a solenoid acts as the North pole and which acts as the South pole, two equivalent methods are used:

> **Right-Hand Grip Rule for a Solenoid (Coil):** Curl the fingers of your **right hand** around the solenoid cylinder in the direction of the **conventional electric current** flowing through the wire turns. Your outstretched **thumb points directly toward the magnetic North pole** of the solenoid (the end from which magnetic field lines emerge).

Alternatively, you can apply the **Clock Rule** at each individual end face:
- Look directly into one open end of the cylinder:
  - If the current circulates **counter-clockwise**, that end is the **North pole**.
  - If the current circulates **clockwise**, that end is the **South pole**.

Both rules invariably yield the exact same physical polarity, as we will verify in the worked examples.

## Part F: Factors Affecting the Strength of a Solenoid (Class 10 Overlap)

The magnetic field strength inside a solenoid can be controlled with remarkable precision. It depends fundamentally on three parameters:

| Factor | Physical Effect on Magnetic Field |
|---|---|
| **1. Current magnitude ($I$)** | Increasing the electric current directly intensifies the magnetic field ($B \propto I$). Doubling the current doubles the magnetic field strength. |
| **2. Turn density ($n = N/L$)** | Increasing the number of turns per unit length (winding turns closer together along the same length) strengthens the field proportionally ($B \propto n$). |
| **3. Nature of core material** | Inserting a rod of **soft iron** into the interior core multiplies the magnetic field strength hundreds to thousands of times (forming an **electromagnet**, the focus of Topic 5). |

### Reversing the Direction of Current

If the battery connections are reversed so that current flows through the turns in the opposite direction:
- The **magnitude (strength)** of the magnetic field remains completely unchanged (assuming the current magnitude is the same).
- The **polarity of the solenoid reverses completely**: the end that previously behaved as a North pole becomes a South pole, and the end that was a South pole becomes a North pole.

### Comparison Between a Bar Magnet and a Solenoid

| Characteristic | Permanent Bar Magnet | Energized Solenoid |
|---|---|---|
| **External field pattern** | Continuous loops emerging from N to S | Identical pattern emerging from N to S |
| **Poles** | Fixed North and South poles | North and South poles determined by current direction |
| **Adjustable strength?** | **No**; fixed during manufacture | **Yes**; easily varied by altering current or turn density |
| **Switchable ON and OFF?** | **No**; permanently magnetic | **Yes**; instantaneous control (current off = magnetism off) |
| **Reversible polarity?** | **No**; requires physically rotating the magnet | **Yes**; simply reverse the direction of electric current |

## Part G: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Determining the Field Direction Around a Straight Wire: 6 Cases):**  
Apply the Right-Hand Thumb Rule to determine the magnetic field direction for each of the following configurations:

| Case | Orientation and Current Direction | Viewing Perspective | Magnetic Field Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Vertical wire carrying current straight **upwards** | Viewed from directly above | **Counter-clockwise (anticlockwise)** |
| 2 | Vertical wire carrying current straight **downwards** | Viewed from directly above | **Clockwise** |
| 3 | Horizontal wire with current emerging **out of page ($\odot$)** | Observer looking at page | **Counter-clockwise** |
| 4 | Horizontal wire with current plunging **into page ($\otimes$)** | Observer looking at page | **Clockwise** |
| 5 | Wire in plane of page carrying current from **left to right** | In the region **above** the wire | Pointing **out of the page ($\odot$)** (and **into page ($\otimes$)** below) |
| 6 | Wire in plane of page carrying current from **right to left** | In the region **above** the wire | Pointing **into the page ($\otimes$)** (and **out of page ($\odot$)** below) |

*Step-by-step reasoning:* Point the right thumb along the specified current vector and observe the curling fingers. In Case 5, with the thumb pointing rightward, fingers emerge out of the paper in the region above the wire and plunge down into the paper below the wire. Case 6 reverses this geometry completely.  
*Consistency check:* Reversing the current in Case 1 to Case 2 flips the circulation from counter-clockwise to clockwise $\checkmark$. Reversing Case 3 to Case 4 does the same $\checkmark$.

**Example 2 (Compass Needle Deflection Under a Wire):**  
A horizontal wire carries current directed from South to North, positioned directly above a magnetic compass needle. Toward which cardinal direction will the North pole of the compass deflect? What happens if the current is reversed?

*Solution:*
1. Position your right hand over the wire with your thumb pointing North (along conventional current).
2. Your curled fingers wrap around the wire: above the wire, fingers curl toward the East; wrapping underneath the wire, your fingers point toward the **West**.
3. Because the compass needle is situated directly underneath the wire, it experiences a magnetic field directed toward the **West**.
4. The North pole of the compass needle will deflect toward the **West**.
5. When current is reversed (flowing North to South), your right thumb points South, causing the field beneath the wire to point toward the **East**. The compass needle deflects toward the **East**.

*Consistency check:* Reversing the current direction reverses the direction of magnetic field and hence the needle deflection $\checkmark$. If the compass were placed above the wire instead, the deflections would be reversed $\checkmark$.

---

**Example 3 (Determining Solenoid Polarity: 6 Cases):**  
A cylindrical solenoid is observed from one end. Determine the magnetic polarity of both ends and the direction of the internal field vector using both the Clock Rule and the Right-Hand Grip Rule.

| Case | End Observed | Current Circulation Seen | Observed End Polarity | Opposite End Polarity | Right-Hand Thumb Points |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Left end (horizontal axis) | Counter-clockwise | **North (N)** | **South (S)** | Toward the left end |
| 2 | Left end (horizontal axis) | Clockwise | **South (S)** | **North (N)** | Toward the right end |
| 3 | Right end (horizontal axis) | Counter-clockwise | **North (N)** | **South (S)** | Toward the right end |
| 4 | Right end (horizontal axis) | Clockwise | **South (S)** | **North (N)** | Toward the left end |
| 5 | Top end (vertical axis) | Counter-clockwise | **North (N)** (top) | **South (S)** (bottom) | Upward |
| 6 | Top end (vertical axis) | Clockwise | **South (S)** (top) | **North (N)** (bottom) | Downward |

*Reasoning:* If one face exhibits counter-clockwise circulation, looking from the opposite end at the exact same current loop reveals clockwise circulation. Therefore, one end is invariably North and the other South.  
*Consistency check:* In every case, the Clock Rule and the Right-Hand Grip Rule identify the identical North pole $\checkmark$.

**Example 4 (Effect of Doubling Current on Field Strength):**  
A solenoid carrying a current of $0.5\text{ A}$ produces an axial magnetic field of a certain strength. If the current is increased to $1.0\text{ A}$ while keeping the turn density and dimensions constant, how does the magnetic field change?

*Solution:*
- The magnetic field of an air-core solenoid is directly proportional to current: $B \propto I$.
- The current ratio is:
  $$\frac{I_{\text{new}}}{I_{\text{initial}}} = \frac{1.0\text{ A}}{0.5\text{ A}} = 2$$
- Therefore, the magnetic field strength **doubles** ($\approx 2\times$ stronger).

*Consistency check:* $0.5\text{ A} \times 2 = 1.0\text{ A} \checkmark$. Proportional scaling holds directly for ideal air-core solenoids.

---

**Example 5 (Effect of Doubling Turn Density):**  
A solenoid measuring $10\text{ cm}$ in length is wound with 100 turns. If the same $10\text{ cm}$ length is rewound with 200 turns packed more tightly together, what happens to the internal magnetic field strength for the same current?

*Solution:*
- Initial turn density:
  $$n_1 = \frac{100\text{ turns}}{10\text{ cm}} = 10\text{ turns/cm}$$
- New turn density:
  $$n_2 = \frac{200\text{ turns}}{10\text{ cm}} = 20\text{ turns/cm}$$
- Ratio of turn density:
  $$\frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{20}{10} = 2$$
- Since $B \propto n$, the magnetic field strength **doubles** ($\approx 2\times$ stronger).

*Consistency check:* Note that the length remained constant at $10\text{ cm}$. If the length had also doubled to $20\text{ cm}$, the turn density would remain $200 / 20 = 10\text{ turns/cm}$, leaving the field unchanged $\checkmark$.

---

**Example 6 (Calculating Total Turns in a Solenoid):**  
A laboratory solenoid measures $10\text{ cm}$ in length and is wound with a turn density of $20\text{ turns per cm}$. How many total turns does the solenoid possess?

*Solution:*
$$\text{Total turns } N = (\text{turn density } n) \times (\text{length } L)$$
$$N = 20\text{ turns/cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 200\text{ turns}$$

*Consistency check:* $\frac{200\text{ turns}}{10\text{ cm}} = 20\text{ turns/cm} \checkmark$.

---

**Example 7 (Total Length of Wire Required for a Coil):**  
An experimenter wishes to construct a flat circular coil consisting of 50 turns, with each turn having a radius of $2\text{ cm}$ ($0.02\text{ m}$). Calculate the total length of insulated copper wire needed. (Use $\pi \approx 3.1416$.)

*Solution:*
1. Circumference of a single turn:
   $$C = 2\pi r = 2 \times 3.1416 \times 0.02\text{ m} = 0.12566\text{ m} \quad (12.57\text{ cm})$$
2. Total wire length for 50 turns:
   $$L_{\text{total}} = 50 \times 0.12566\text{ m} = 6.283\text{ m} \approx 6.28\text{ m}$$

*Consistency check:* $6.283\text{ m} / 50 = 0.12566\text{ m} \checkmark$.

---

**Example 8 (Current in a Coil from Ohm's Law):**  
A 50-turn solenoid having an electrical resistance of $6\ \Omega$ is connected across a $3\text{ V}$ battery. Determine the electric current flowing through the solenoid.

*Solution:*
According to Ohm's Law ($V = I \times R$, from [electricity-part4.md](../electricity/electricity-part4.md)):
$$I = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{3\text{ V}}{6\ \Omega} = 0.5\text{ A}$$

*Consistency check:* $V = I \times R = 0.5\text{ A} \times 6\ \Omega = 3\text{ V} \checkmark$.

---

**Example 9 (Does Greater Electrical Resistance Produce a Stronger Field?):**  
Suppose the $3\text{ V}$ battery from Example 8 is connected instead to a different coil made of thinner wire that has an electrical resistance of $12\ \Omega$. What happens to the current and the resulting magnetic field?

*Solution:*
1. Calculate the new current:
   $$I_{\text{new}} = \frac{V}{R} = \frac{3\text{ V}}{12\ \Omega} = 0.25\text{ A}$$
2. The current is halved from $0.5\text{ A}$ to $0.25\text{ A}$.
3. Because magnetic field depends directly on **current** ($B \propto I$), halving the current **halves** the magnetic field strength.
4. *Conclusion:* Higher electrical resistance does *not* create a stronger magnetic field; on the contrary, for a constant voltage supply, higher resistance chokes the current and produces a substantially **weaker** magnetic field.

*Consistency check:* $0.25\text{ A} \times 12\ \Omega = 3\text{ V} \checkmark$.

**Example 10 (Which Magnetic Device Can Be Switched Off or Reversed?):**  
Between a permanent bar magnet and a solenoid, which device can be instantly demagnetized at will? Which device can have its magnetic poles swapped without physically rotating it?

*Solution:*
- **The Solenoid:**
  - Can be switched off instantly: opening the electrical switch terminates current flow ($I = 0$), causing the magnetic field to collapse immediately.
  - Can have its poles reversed electronically: reversing the connections to the battery terminals reverses the current direction, instantly interchanging the North and South poles.
- A permanent bar magnet cannot be switched off or have its poles electronically reversed; its magnetization is permanently locked into its microscopic ferromagnetic domain structure (Topic 3).

*Consistency check:* Perfectly aligns with the comparative table in Part F $\checkmark$.

**Example 11 (Interaction Between Two Parallel Current-Carrying Wires):**  
Two long, straight parallel wires are positioned close together. Both carry steady currents flowing in the **same direction**. What mechanical effect do they exert on each other?

*Solution:*
- Wire 1 creates a concentric circular magnetic field in the space occupied by Wire 2.
- Wire 2 is a current-carrying conductor immersed in this external magnetic field, and therefore experiences a magnetic force (an interaction explored mathematically in Topic 6).
- By applying the magnetic field rules and Lorentz force principles, two parallel currents flowing in the **same direction attract each other**.
- Conversely, two parallel wires carrying currents in **opposite directions repel each other**.

*Consistency check:* Like charges repel, but parallel like currents attract $\checkmark$.

**Example 12 (Identifying Unmarked Solenoid Polarity with a Compass):**  
A student energizes an unmarked solenoid lying horizontally. A small magnetic compass is brought near the left end face of the solenoid. The **North-seeking tip of the compass needle points directly into the left end face**. What is the magnetic polarity of the left end of the solenoid?

*Solution:*
1. Fundamental rule of magnetism: opposite poles attract, like poles repel ([magnetism.md](magnetism.md)).
2. The North pole of the compass needle is drawn toward and points directly at an attractive magnetic pole.
3. Therefore, the left end face of the solenoid must be a **South magnetic pole ($S$)**.
4. The right end face must consequently be the **North magnetic pole ($N$)**.

*Consistency check:* If the compass is moved to the right end, its North tip is repelled outward, and its South tip points toward the right end, confirming the right end is North $\checkmark$.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing a wire produces a magnetic field even when no current flows.**  
   A stationary copper wire has no magnetic field. The magnetic field exists *only* when electric charges are actively moving (current is flowing). When current is zero, the field is zero.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming magnetic field lines run parallel along the length of a straight wire.**  
   Field lines do *not* travel along the wire like water flowing through a garden hose. Instead, they form closed **concentric circular rings** that encircle the wire in planes perpendicular to the wire.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing the Right-Hand Thumb Rule with Fleming's Left-Hand Rule.**  
   The Right-Hand Thumb Rule determines the direction of the **magnetic field** generated by a current. Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (covered in Topic 6) determines the **mechanical force** exerted on a conductor placed inside an external magnetic field.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing a solenoid has no distinct magnetic poles.**  
   An energized solenoid possesses distinct, well-defined North and South poles at its opposing cylinder ends, behaving exactly like a permanent bar magnet.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming a compass needle must physically touch the wire to deflect.**  
   Magnetic fields exert non-contact forces through space. The compass deflects through the air without any physical contact with the conductor.

6. **Mistake 6: Believing reversing current changes the field strength.**  
   Reversing the direction of current reverses only the **direction (polarity)** of the magnetic field; it leaves the **numerical field strength** completely unchanged.

7. **Mistake 7: Thinking a coil with higher electrical resistance produces a stronger magnetic field.**  
   Magnetic field strength depends directly on current ($I$), not resistance ($R$). Connecting a higher resistance coil to a fixed voltage battery actually *reduces* current, producing a significantly weaker field.

## Quick Recap

- **Oersted's Discovery (1820):** An electric current flowing through a conductor creates an invisible magnetic field in the surrounding space.
- Reversing the current direction reverses the direction of compass needle deflection. Cutting off the current eliminates the field immediately.
- The magnetic field lines around a straight current-carrying wire form **concentric circular loops** in planes perpendicular to the wire.
- **Right-Hand Thumb Rule:** Grasp the conductor with the right hand so the thumb points along conventional current; the curled fingers indicate the circular direction of magnetic field lines.
- Current emerging out of the page ($\odot$) produces a **counter-clockwise** field; current entering into the page ($\otimes$) produces a **clockwise** field.
- The magnetic field of a straight wire weakens with increasing radial distance ($B \propto 1/r$) and strengthens with greater current ($B \propto I$).
- In a circular loop, the magnetic fields from all segments reinforce inside the ring, creating a concentrated axial field at the centre.
- **Clock Rule:** Viewing a loop face, counter-clockwise current indicates a **North pole** ($N$); clockwise current indicates a **South pole** ($S$).
- A coil with $n$ closely wound turns produces a magnetic field approximately $n$ times stronger than a single turn.
- A **solenoid** is a long cylindrical helical coil. Its external field matches that of a permanent bar magnet; its internal field consists of strong, uniform, parallel lines running from South to North.
- **Solenoid Polarity:** Curl right-hand fingers along current turns; the thumb points directly to the **North pole**.
- Solenoid strength increases with: (1) higher current, (2) greater turn density ($n = N/L$), and (3) insertion of a soft iron core.
- Electromagnetism demonstrates that electricity and magnetism are unified aspects of the same fundamental physical phenomenon.

### Answers

1. Oersted's experiment demonstrated that **an electric current creates a magnetic field in the space surrounding the conductor**. When the circuit switch is closed (current on), the nearby magnetic compass needle immediately deflects away from its resting north-south orientation. When the current direction is reversed, the compass needle deflects in the opposite direction. When the switch is opened (current off), the needle returns to its original magnetic north-south alignment.

2. A straight thick wire provides very little electrical resistance; connecting it directly across a battery causes a large surge of current (a near short-circuit). This rapidly overheats the wire and battery, posing burn and leakage hazards. Therefore, the experiment should be performed with only 1.5 V cells for only a few seconds at a time. **Mains electricity (110 V to 240 V) must never be used**, as it can deliver lethal electric shocks, catastrophic arc explosions, and fire hazards.

3. The magnetic field lines form **concentric circular rings** centred on the wire in planes perpendicular to the conductor. Using the Right-Hand Thumb Rule, with the thumb pointing into the page ($\otimes$), the fingers curl in a **clockwise** direction.

4. With current directed downwards, point the right thumb straight down. Viewing from directly above, the curled fingers indicate a **clockwise** magnetic field circulation.

5. They will deflect in **opposite directions**. The concentric circular field lines wrap around the wire in a continuous loop: the horizontal field vector above the wire points in one direction, while the horizontal field vector underneath the wire points in the diametrically opposite direction.

6. As radial distance increases, the magnetic field strength **decreases inversely with distance** ($B \propto 1/r$). If the current is doubled, the magnetic field strength at any given distance is **doubled** ($B \propto I$).

7. **Clock Rule:** Looking directly at a face of a circular loop, if current circulates counter-clockwise, that face is a **North pole**; if current circulates clockwise, that face is a **South pole**. In the given case, counter-clockwise current indicates that the front face is a **North pole ($N$)**.

8. By the Clock Rule, clockwise current at the left end indicates a **South pole ($S$)**. Looking from the opposite (right) end, that same current circulates counter-clockwise, making the right end a **North pole ($N$)**. Using the Right-Hand Grip Rule: wrapping the right fingers along the current turns causes the right thumb to point toward the right end, confirming the right end is the North pole ($N$). Thus, **Left end = South, Right end = North**.

9. Total turns:
   $$N = n \times L = 30\text{ turns/cm} \times 15\text{ cm} = 450\text{ turns}$$
   If 450 turns are wound over $5\text{ cm}$:
   $$n_{\text{new}} = \frac{450\text{ turns}}{5\text{ cm}} = 90\text{ turns/cm}$$
   The new turn density is $90 / 30 = 3\times$ greater. Assuming identical current, the internal magnetic field strength will become approximately **3 times stronger**.  
   *Check:* $450 / 15 = 30 \checkmark$; $90 / 30 = 3 \checkmark$.
