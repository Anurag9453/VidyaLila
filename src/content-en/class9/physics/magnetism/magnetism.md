# Topic 1: Magnets and Magnetic Poles

In our study of electricity, we explored the nature of electric charges in motion: currents coursing through conducting wires, illuminating incandescent filaments, and producing thermal energy in heating appliances. In this final physics domain, we encounter a physical phenomenon that appears strikingly distinct at first glance, yet is fundamentally intertwined with electricity: **magnetism**. In subsequent topics, we will discover how moving electric charges generate magnetic fields, and conversely, how moving magnetic fields induce electric currents. Before exploring these profound connections, however, we must build a rigorous foundational understanding of magnets: what they are, what materials they act upon, and how their magnetic poles behave.

A critical safety note at the outset: **exploring magnets is fascinating, but fundamental precautions must always be observed.** Keep strong permanent magnets well away from smartphones, laptops, magnetic-stripe credit and debit cards, mechanical wristwatches, computer storage devices, and medical implants such as cardiac pacemakers. All investigations and demonstrations discussed in this topic use low-strength, safe laboratory magnets and lightweight iron objects. Under no circumstances should experiments ever be attempted near domestic electrical sockets or high-voltage lines.

## Magic in the Salvage Yard

Mr. Thorne operated a bustling scrap-metal salvage and recycling yard situated near the industrial docks. One sunny Saturday morning, Maya accompanied her father to the yard, eager to observe how thousands of kilograms of decommissioned machinery and household refuse were processed. Towering over the sorting bays was a heavy-duty hydraulic crane. Suspended from its cable was a massive, circular disc made of solid iron.

Mr. Thorne engaged the crane controls. The circular disc descended smoothly toward a chaotic mound of assorted debris. The heap contained an indiscriminate mixture of iron framing nails, rusted steel bolts, discarded food cans, thick copper cables, crushed aluminium beverage cans, shattered glass bottles, and rigid plastic casings. The disc came to rest directly above the heap. Suddenly, with a rapid succession of metallic clicks and clatters, **scores of iron nails, heavy bolts, and steel cans leaped into the air and snapped tightly to the disc's underside.**

When the operator hoisted the disc high into the air, the clinging mass of metal rose with it, ready to be deposited into a transport container. Yet, down in the debris pile, **the copper wiring, aluminium cans, plastic containers, and glass shards lay completely undisturbed**, as though the crane had never approached them.

Maya watched with wide eyes. "Mr. Thorne, how does that crane know what to pick up and what to leave behind? Why didn't it grab everything in the pile? Does it have a sensor that sees the objects?"

Mr. Thorne chuckled warmly. "It doesn't use cameras or optical sensors, Maya. The disc interacts solely with the physical **material composition** of each item. Let us first examine the science of magnets and their poles; by the middle of this topic, the precise mechanism behind this sorting magic will be crystal clear." We will return to solve Maya's puzzle in Part B.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnet** | An object or material that produces a magnetic field and exerts an attractive force on ferromagnetic substances | A steel bar magnet, an iron fridge magnet |
| **Magnetic material** | A substance that experiences a strong attractive force when placed near a magnet and can be magnetised | Iron, steel, nickel, cobalt |
| **Non-magnetic material** | A substance that experiences no perceptible attraction to a magnet under ordinary conditions | Copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass |
| **Magnetic pole** | The localized region near each end of a magnet where its magnetic attractive power is concentrated | The north and south ends of a bar magnet |
| **North pole (N)** | The pole of a freely suspended magnet that points toward the Earth's geographic north (north-seeking pole) | The red-painted end of a laboratory bar magnet |
| **South pole (S)** | The pole of a freely suspended magnet that points toward the Earth's geographic south (south-seeking pole) | The blue-painted or marked S-end of a bar magnet |
| **Directive property** | The property of a freely suspended or pivoted magnet to align itself consistently along the geographic North–South line | A balanced compass needle resting at equilibrium |
| **Attraction and repulsion** | The mechanical forces exerted between magnetic bodies: unlike poles pull together (attract); like poles push apart (repel) | An N pole pulling an S pole; two N poles pushing apart |
| **Magnetic keeper** | A piece of soft iron placed across the poles of stored permanent magnets to maintain closed magnetic circuits and prevent demagnetisation | Soft iron strips placed across the ends of paired bar magnets |

## Part A: A Brief History of Magnets and Their Types

### A Brief Historical Note

Ancient folklore recounts that a shepherd named Magnes was tending his flock in the mountainous region of Magnesia in Asia Minor. According to legend, the iron nails embedded in the soles of his sandals and the iron tip of his wooden crook became firmly stuck to a black rock on the hillside. The word **magnet** is believed to originate from the name of this region or its legendary discoverer. Historical records show that natural magnetic stones, known as **lodestones**, were discovered and studied in ancient China, India, and Greece. Centuries ago, navigators realized that when a slender fragment of lodestone was suspended freely from a thread or floated on a cork in water, it consistently rotated until one end pointed northward—creating the world's earliest navigational **compasses**.

### Natural and Artificial Magnets

- **Natural magnets:** Naturally occurring minerals that possess intrinsic magnetic properties. The most famous example is **lodestone**, a naturally magnetized variety of the mineral **magnetite** (an iron oxide with the chemical formula $\text{Fe}_3\text{O}_4$). Natural magnets typically possess irregular geometric shapes and comparatively weak, non-uniform magnetic attraction.
- **Artificial (manufactured) magnets:** Magnets fabricated artificially by humans from ferromagnetic metals such as **iron, steel, nickel, and cobalt**, as well as specialized metal alloys. Virtually all modern functional magnets are artificial. A prime example is **alnico** (an alloy of aluminium, nickel, and cobalt, combined with iron). Alnico alloys produce exceptionally strong, durable permanent magnets. In Topic 3, we will examine the precise physical methods used to manufacture these artificial magnets.

### Common Shapes of Magnets

Depending on the mechanical application and experimental requirements, artificial magnets are manufactured in diverse geometric forms:

 2. Horseshoe magnet (curved U-profile)   :      ╭═════════╮
                                                ═╯         ╰═
                                                N           S

 3. U-shaped magnet                       :     N           S
                                                ║           ║
                                                ║           ║

- **Bar magnet:** The standard rectangular or cylindrical rod ubiquitous in physics laboratories for demonstrating basic magnetic phenomena.
- **Horseshoe and U-shaped magnets:** The bar is curved so that both the North and South poles sit adjacent to each other on the same plane. Because both poles act together on an object simultaneously, they exert a far stronger lifting force across small air gaps.
- **Cylindrical, disc, and ring magnets:** Widely utilized in electric acoustic speakers, electric motors, magnetic door catches, and precision sensor assemblies.
- **Magnetic needle:** A lightweight, sharp-pointed magnetic steel pointer balanced delicately on a frictionless central pivot, enabling it to rotate horizontally in response to external magnetic forces.
- **Flexible sheet magnet (fridge magnet):** Fabricated by embedding finely ground ferromagnetic powder into a flexible vinyl or synthetic rubber polymer matrix. These sheets are engineered with alternating parallel strips of north and south polarity on one face, allowing them to cling securely to steel refrigerator panels.

## Part B: Which Materials Are Attracted?

### Magnetic and Non-Magnetic Materials

All materials in the physical universe respond to magnetic fields, but their responses differ by many orders of magnitude. In introductory physics, we classify materials into two distinct categories based on their observable interaction with ordinary magnets:

| Category | Typical Materials | Interaction with a Magnet |
|---|---|---|
| **Magnetic materials** | Iron, steel, nickel, cobalt, and specialized alloys (e.g., alnico) | Strongly attracted; can be permanently or temporarily magnetised |
| **Non-magnetic materials** | Copper, aluminium, brass, bronze, zinc, lead, gold, silver, plastics, glass, wood, paper, rubber | No perceptible attraction under everyday conditions |

Two crucial practical clarifications:
1. **Aluminium:** Certain metals, such as aluminium, exhibit an extremely weak form of magnetism called *paramagnetism*. Under laboratory conditions with hyper-sensitive analytical balances, an ultra-strong magnetic field produces an almost undetectable pull on aluminium. In everyday domestic, industrial, and classroom settings, however, this force is completely negligible, and aluminium is treated strictly as **non-magnetic**.
2. **Stainless steel:** Many domestic kitchen utensils, cutlery, and sinks are fabricated from specific austenitic stainless steel alloys containing high concentrations of chromium and nickel (such as Grade 304). The crystalline structure of austenitic stainless steel renders it non-magnetic or only negligibly magnetic. In contrast, standard carbon steels (found in paper clips, framing nails, structural girders, and food cans) are strongly ferromagnetic.

### Resolving the Salvage Yard Mystery

We are now ready to resolve Maya's question from our opening narrative. Mr. Thorne explained:

"The giant crane disc is an industrial lifting magnet. It possesses no cognitive ability to 'see' or 'choose' items. It simply generates an intensely powerful magnetic field that exerts strong attractive forces exclusively on **magnetic materials**.
- In the scrap heap, the **nails, framing bolts, and heavy machinery parts** are made of plain carbon iron and steel.
- Discarded **tin cans** are actually manufactured from thin sheet steel coated with a microscopic protective film of tin; because the underlying body is steel, they are strongly magnetic.
- In contrast, the **copper electric cabling, crushed aluminium cans, plastic bottles, and glass jars** consist of non-magnetic substances. For these materials, the magnetic force is zero. The magnet is completely 'blind' to them."

Maya smiled with realization: "So the crane did not sort the items by intelligence; the physical properties of iron and steel sorted themselves!"

## Part C: Fundamental Properties of Magnets

### Property 1: Attraction of Magnetic Materials

A magnet exerts a powerful attractive force on objects fabricated from ferromagnetic materials: iron, steel, nickel, and cobalt. When brought near a scatter of steel pins or iron filings, the magnet pulls them inward toward itself.

### Property 2: Attraction is Strongest at the Poles

Does a magnet attract objects uniformly along its entire body? We investigate this experimentally.

**Experiment 1 (Iron filings distribution).** Place a clean sheet of stiff white paper over a standard laboratory bar magnet resting on a horizontal table. Gently sprinkle fine iron filings evenly across the paper surface, then lightly tap the edge of the paper with a pencil.

```
   Iron filings    ░▒▓▓▓▒░                           ░▒▓▓▓▒░    Iron filings
                  ░▓▓▓▓▓▓░  [ N ═══════════ S ]    ░▓▓▓▓▓▓░
    (dense clump)  ░▒▓▓▓▒░      sparse at center      ░▒▓▓▓▒░     (dense clump)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – Distribution of iron filings on a bar magnet. The filings cluster heavily near the two ends, while the central region remains largely bare.

Observation reveals that the iron filings do not distribute themselves uniformly. Instead, they congregate in dense, bristling clusters **near the two extremities**, while virtually no filings adhere to the center of the bar.

The two distinct regions near the ends of a magnet where its attractive power is greatest are defined as its **magnetic poles**. Note an important detail: the magnetic poles are not located precisely at the mathematical geometric edges of the bar, but lie **slightly inward from each end**. The central region of the bar exhibits practically zero magnetic attraction and is referred to as the **neutral zone**.

### Property 3: Directive Property (Aligning North–South)

**Experiment 2 (Suspended bar magnet).** Suspend a uniform bar magnet horizontally from its center of gravity using a fine, unspun silk or nylon thread attached to a non-magnetic wooden retort stand. Ensure there are no air drafts or stray magnetic objects nearby. Alternatively, float the bar magnet on a flat disc of cork in a wide basin of water.

```
               Geographic North
                     ▲
                     N
                     ║
    Thread ────────► ║   Suspended bar magnet
                     ║
                     S
                     ▼
               Geographic South
```

> **Figure 1.3** – A freely suspended bar magnet consistently aligns its longitudinal axis with the Earth's North–South geographic direction.

Disturb the magnet gently by spinning it horizontally, then release it. The magnet oscillates back and forth and eventually comes to rest along a definite direction: **approximately along the geographic North–South line**. Repeat this disturbance multiple times; every single time, the exact same end of the magnet swings around to point northward, and the opposite end points southward.

- The pole that consistently points toward the Earth's geographic north is named the **north-seeking pole**, or simply the **North pole (N)**.
- The pole that consistently points toward the Earth's geographic south is named the **south-seeking pole**, or simply the **South pole (S)**.

This intrinsic tendency of a freely suspended magnet to orient itself in a definite geographical direction is termed its **directive property**. All navigational compasses rely directly on this fundamental property. Magnetic poles never appear in isolation: every magnet possesses at least one pair of complementary poles ($N$ and $S$).

### Property 4: Like Poles Repel, Unlike Poles Attract

**Experiment 3 (Testing pole interactions).** Suspend a bar magnet from a thread so that it hangs freely at rest. Take a second bar magnet in your hand, and systematically present its poles to the poles of the suspended magnet.

```
  Unlike poles (N–S): ATTRACTION          [ N ══════ S ]   [ N ══════ S ]
                                                       ◄──►
                                                  S and N pull together

  Like poles (N–N): REPULSION             [ S ══════ N ]   [ N ══════ S ]
                                                       ◄──►  Both push apart
```

> **Figure 1.4** – Fundamental law of magnetic poles: unlike poles attract, whereas like poles repel.

1. When the North pole of the handheld magnet is brought near the **South pole** of the suspended magnet, the suspended pole swings rapidly toward the handheld magnet: **they attract**.
2. When the North pole of the handheld magnet is brought near the **North pole** of the suspended magnet, the suspended pole swings violently away: **they repel**.
3. Similarly, bringing a South pole near a South pole results in mutual **repulsion**, while bringing a South pole near a North pole results in mutual **attraction**.

We summarize this fundamental law of magnetism as:

> **Like magnetic poles repel each other; unlike magnetic poles attract each other.**

### Repulsion is the Sure Test of Magnetism

Suppose you are handed a shiny metal bar, and you observe that one end of this bar is strongly **attracted** to the North pole of a test magnet. Can you conclusively declare that the metal bar is a permanent magnet?

**No, you cannot.** Recall that a magnet attracts all unmagnetised ferromagnetic objects (such as ordinary carbon steel or soft iron). An ordinary iron nail is strongly attracted to the North pole of a magnet, and it is equally attracted to the South pole. Therefore, simple attraction demonstrates only that the object contains ferromagnetic material—it does not prove that the object is permanently magnetised.

However, if an end of the mystery bar **repels** the North pole of the test magnet, the conclusion is absolute: the mystery bar **must be a magnet**, and that end must be a North pole. An unmagnetised piece of iron can never produce magnetic repulsion.

> **Repulsion is the only sure and conclusive test of magnetism.** Attraction may occur between two opposite poles or between a magnet and an unmagnetised ferromagnetic body; repulsion occurs strictly between two like magnetic poles.

### Property 5: Magnetic Poles Always Exist in Pairs (No Monopoles)

What happens if you cut a bar magnet in half right through its neutral center? One might hypothesize that the North pole would be isolated on the left half and the South pole on the right half.

**Physical reality is fundamentally different.** When a bar magnet is severed in two, **each half immediately behaves as a complete, independent magnet**, possessing its own North pole and South pole. If these halves are subdivided again into quarters, eighths, and sixteenths, every individual fragment continues to emerge as a complete dipolar magnet.

```
 Original magnet:        [ N ══════════════════ S ]

 Broken once (2 pieces): [ N ═════ S ] [ N ═════ S ]

 Broken twice (4 pieces):
                         [ N ══ S ] [ N ══ S ] [ N ══ S ] [ N ══ S ]
```

> **Figure 1.5** – Subdividing a magnet produces smaller, complete magnets. Magnetic monopoles do not exist in classical physics.

Even if you divide a magnet down to microscopic atomic domains, each constituent fragment retains both an $N$ and an $S$ pole. An isolated single magnetic pole—known as a **magnetic monopole**—has never been observed in nature.

> **Magnetic poles always exist in inseparable conjugate pairs ($N$ and $S$). It is impossible to obtain an isolated magnetic monopole.**

### Property 6: Non-Contact Force and Inverse Variation with Distance

Unlike mechanical contact forces (such as friction or tension) that require physical contact between interacting bodies, magnetic force is a **non-contact force**. A magnet exerts forces across empty space, through ambient air, and directly through non-magnetic media such as paper, cardboard, wood, water, and glass.

**Experiment 5 (Action through non-magnetic barriers).** Place a steel paper clip on top of a thin sheet of cardboard. Hold a strong bar magnet beneath the cardboard. As you move the magnet horizontally beneath the sheet, the paper clip glides smoothly across the upper surface, mimicking every motion of the magnet beneath.

```
         ┌─┐
         └─┘   ◄── Steel paper clip (above cardboard)
    ─────────────────────  Sheet of non-magnetic cardboard
        [ N ═══ S ]   ◄── Bar magnet (manipulated from below)
```

> **Figure 1.6** – Magnetic force acts across non-magnetic barriers without requiring direct physical contact.

Now, gradually increase the distance between the magnet and the clip by stacking additional sheets of cardboard (5, 10, 20 sheets). Notice that as the separation distance increases, the magnetic attraction diminishes rapidly, until the paper clip ceases to respond. This demonstrates that **magnetic force varies inversely with distance**: it is powerful at close range and drops off sharply as the separation increases.

### The Compass and Magnetic Poles: A Preview

A magnetic compass needle is simply a tiny, delicately balanced bar magnet. When brought near a laboratory bar magnet, the needle's **North-seeking tip** is pulled toward the bar magnet's **South pole** and driven away from its **North pole**, in precise accordance with the law of attraction and repulsion. We will examine the detailed mechanics of magnetic compasses and their mapping capabilities in Topic 2.

### Measuring Magnetic Strength: Counting Picked-Up Pins

A practical laboratory method to quantify the relative magnetic strength along the length of a bar magnet is the **pin-lift method**. An identical collection of small steel dressmaker pins or miniature paper clips is introduced to different marked positions along the magnet's body. The number of pins that cling securely to each position provides an empirical measure of magnetic field strength.

**Experiment 6.** A $12\text{ cm}$ bar magnet is rested horizontally. Pins are offered at the $0\text{ cm}$ mark (left pole), the $6\text{ cm}$ mark (exact midpoint), and the $12\text{ cm}$ mark (right pole). A typical laboratory trial yields:
- At the $0\text{ cm}$ mark ($N$ pole): **12 pins**
- At the $6\text{ cm}$ mark (midpoint): **2 pins**
- At the $12\text{ cm}$ mark ($S$ pole): **11 pins**

```
 Pin distribution along a 12 cm bar magnet (each # = 1 pin)

 N pole (0 cm)    : ############ 12
 Center (6 cm)    : ## 2
 S pole (12 cm)   : ########### 11
```

> **Figure 1.7** – Empirical pin distribution along a bar magnet, demonstrating extreme concentration of attractive force at the poles and minimal attraction at the center.

Total pins held at the two extremities: $12 + 11 = 23\text{ pins}$, compared to only $2\text{ pins}$ at the geometric center. The pole regions exhibit $\frac{12}{2} = 6$ times the attractive capacity of the center.

### Everyday Applications of Magnets

Permanent and temporary magnets play indispensable roles across modern engineering and daily life:
- **Refrigerator door gaskets:** Flexible magnetic rubber strips encased within the vinyl door perimeter create an airtight thermal seal against the steel appliance body.
- **Navigational compasses:** Pivoted magnetic needles guide marine shipping, wilderness hikers, and aviation systems.
- **Acoustic transducers (loudspeakers and microphones):** Permanent magnets interact with current-carrying voice coils to convert electrical signals into mechanical sound waves, and vice versa.
- **Electric motors and dynamos:** Powerful magnets supply the magnetic fields essential for converting electrical energy to mechanical work, and mechanical work to electrical power.
- **Magnetic data storage:** Magnetic strips on credit and transit cards, magnetic recording tapes, and legacy computer hard disk platters store encoded digital data via microscopic magnetic domains.
- **Magnetic cabinetry catches:** Small permanent magnets hold cupboard doors closed smoothly without mechanical latches.
- **Magnetic construction toys:** Structural play sets utilize neodymium or ferrite magnets to link structural nodes.
- **Industrial magnetic separators:** Powerful magnetic drums and overhead cranes extract tramp iron from bulk grains, coal, foundry sand, and municipal recycling streams (as seen in Mr. Thorne's salvage yard).
- **Medical Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI):** Massive superconducting magnets generate uniform magnetic fields up to $1.5\text{ to }3.0\text{ tesla}$ inside hospital scanners to produce detailed internal diagnostic images of human soft tissues.
- **Magnetic Levitation (Maglev) trains:** Powerful magnetic arrays lift and propel high-speed passenger trains above frictionless guideways at speeds exceeding $400\text{ km/h}$.

### Proper Care and Storage of Magnets

Permanent magnets can slowly degrade or lose their magnetization entirely if mishandled. To maintain their magnetic longevity:
1. **Store in conjugate pairs with soft iron keepers:** Place bar magnets side-by-side with **unlike poles adjacent** (an $N$ pole next to an $S$ pole), separated by a non-magnetic wooden spacer. Bridge each pair of adjacent poles with a strip of unmagnetised soft iron called a **magnetic keeper**.
2. **Never expose to extreme thermal agitation:** Heating a magnet above moderate temperatures causes thermal vibration of its atomic lattice, disordering its magnetic alignment and permanently destroying its magnetism (detailed in Topic 3).
3. **Avoid mechanical impacts:** Do not drop magnets onto hard concrete floors, strike them with hammers, or subject them to severe mechanical shocks.
4. **Isolate from sensitive electronic devices:** Maintain safe working distances from smartphones, credit cards, computer hard drives, mechanical watches, and medical cardiac pacemakers.

```
 Paired bar magnets in storage (unlike poles adjacent):

    ┌──────────────────────────────────┐
    │  [ N ═════════ S ]               │  ◄── Soft iron keeper across both ends
    │  [ S ═════════ N ]               │
    └──────────────────────────────────┘

 Horseshoe magnet:

       ╭═════════╮
      ═╯         ╰═
      N           S
      ╚═══════════╝   ◄── Single soft iron keeper bridging the adjacent pole faces
```

> **Figure 1.8** – Proper storage of permanent magnets using soft iron keepers. The keepers provide closed low-reluctance magnetic pathways that preserve magnetic strength.

### Example 1: Classifying 15 Objects as Magnetic or Non-Magnetic

**Problem:** A classroom salvage collection contains 15 everyday items:
(1) an iron carpentry nail, (2) a piece of bare copper wire, (3) a carbon steel paper clip, (4) an aluminium soda can, (5) a molded plastic comb, (6) a solid glass marble, (7) a wooden meter ruler, (8) an iron machine bolt, (9) a solid brass padlock key, (10) a natural rubber band, (11) a strip of pure nickel wire, (12) a sheet of paper, (13) a tin-plated steel food can, (14) a stainless steel pair of scissors with high carbon content, and (15) a strip of cotton fabric. Classify each object as magnetic or non-magnetic.

**Solution:**
- **Magnetic objects (6 items):**
  1. Iron carpentry nail (contains pure or carbon-alloyed iron)
  2. Carbon steel paper clip (ferromagnetic steel alloy)
  3. Iron machine bolt (ferromagnetic iron)
  4. Pure nickel wire (nickel is a ferromagnetic element)
  5. Tin-plated steel food can (steel structural core)
  6. High-carbon steel scissor blades (ferromagnetic steel alloy)

  1. Bare copper wire (copper is non-magnetic)
  2. Aluminium soda can (aluminium exhibits no perceptible attraction)
  3. Molded plastic comb (synthetic polymer; non-magnetic)
  4. Solid glass marble (silicate glass; non-magnetic)
  5. Wooden meter ruler (cellulose wood; non-magnetic)
  6. Solid brass padlock key (copper-zinc alloy; non-magnetic)
  7. Natural rubber band (elastomer polymer; non-magnetic)
  8. Sheet of paper (wood pulp cellulose; non-magnetic)
  9. Cotton fabric (organic cellulose fiber; non-magnetic)

**Verification:** $\text{Total items} = 6 + 9 = 15$ items ✅

### Example 2: Attraction or Repulsion Between 8 Pole Pairs

**Problem:** Determine whether the mechanical interaction is attractive or repulsive for each of the following 8 pairs of magnetic poles:

| Pair No. | Interacting Poles | Mechanical Response |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\text{N}$ and $\text{N}$ | Repulsion |
| 2 | $\text{N}$ and $\text{S}$ | Attraction |
| 3 | $\text{S}$ and $\text{S}$ | Repulsion |
| 4 | $\text{S}$ and $\text{N}$ | Attraction |
| 5 | $\text{N}$ and $\text{N}$ | Repulsion |
| 6 | $\text{S}$ and $\text{N}$ | Attraction |
| 7 | $\text{S}$ and $\text{S}$ | Repulsion |
| 8 | $\text{N}$ and $\text{S}$ | Attraction |

**Solution:**
Applying the fundamental law of magnetism:
- **Like poles repel:** Pairs 1, 3, 5, and 7 involve like poles ($\text{N–N}$ or $\text{S–S}$). Total repulsive pairs = **4**.
- **Unlike poles attract:** Pairs 2, 4, 6, and 8 involve unlike poles ($\text{N–S}$ or $\text{S–N}). Total attractive pairs = **4**.

**Verification:** $4\text{ (repel)} + 4\text{ (attract)} = 8\text{ total pairs}$ ✅

### Example 3: Cutting a 12 cm Bar Magnet into Three Equal Pieces

**Problem:** A continuous $12\text{ cm}$ bar magnet is cut transversally into three equal segments. Determine:
(a) the physical length of each resulting piece,
(b) the number of individual magnets formed, and
(c) the total number of magnetic poles in existence.

**Solution:**
(a) Length of each segment:
$$\text{Length} = \frac{12\text{ cm}}{3} = \mathbf{4\text{ cm}}$$
(b) Because magnetic monopoles cannot exist, cutting a magnet does not isolate poles; each severed piece becomes a complete, functional dipole. Thus, there are **3 complete magnets**.
(c) Each complete magnet possesses exactly 2 conjugate poles ($1\text{ N}$ and $1\text{ S}$). Total poles:
$$\text{Total poles} = 3 \times 2 = \mathbf{6\text{ poles}} \quad (3\text{ North poles and } 3\text{ South poles})$$

**Verification:** $3 \times 4\text{ cm} = 12\text{ cm}$ ✅; Total poles = $6$ ✅

### Example 4: Identifying Poles Using a Compass

**Problem:** The North-seeking tip of a magnetic compass needle is brought near the ends of an unmarked bar magnet. The needle's North tip is strongly attracted to End A of the bar, but is vigorously repelled by End B. Identify the magnetic polarity of End A and End B.

**Solution:**
1. The needle's tip is a **North pole**.
2. According to the law of poles, unlike poles attract. Since the needle's North tip is pulled toward End A, **End A must be a South (S) pole**.
3. Like poles repel. Since the needle's North tip is pushed away from End B, **End B must be a North (N) pole**.

**Verification:** The two opposite ends of the bar exhibit conjugate polarities ($\text{End A} = \text{S}$, $\text{End B} = \text{N}$), which is consistent with the physical nature of a dipolar bar magnet ✅.

### Example 5: Analyzing Pin Attraction Along a Bar Magnet

**Problem:** A physics student places steel pins along a $12\text{ cm}$ bar magnet and records the maximum number of pins supported at regular $2\text{ cm}$ intervals measuring from the left extremity ($0\text{ cm}$):

| Position (from left end) | Number of Pins Held |
|---|---|
| $0\text{ cm}$ | 12 |
| $2\text{ cm}$ | 7 |
| $4\text{ cm}$ | 3 |
| $6\text{ cm}$ | 2 |
| $8\text{ cm}$ | 3 |
| $10\text{ cm}$ | 6 |
| $12\text{ cm}$ | 11 |

(a) Identify the positions of the magnetic poles.
(b) Identify the location of the neutral zone.
(c) Calculate the difference and the ratio in holding capacity between the left extremity and the midpoint.

**Solution:**
(a) Magnetic poles are defined as regions of maximum attraction. The pin count peaks at the two extremities: $12\text{ pins}$ at $0\text{ cm}$ and $11\text{ pins}$ at $12\text{ cm}$. Therefore, the **magnetic poles are situated near the two ends ($0\text{ cm}$ and $12\text{ cm}$)**.
(b) The weakest holding capacity occurs at the exact geometric midpoint ($6\text{ cm}$), where only $2\text{ pins}$ are held. The **neutral zone is at the $6\text{ cm}$ mark**.
(c) Difference in holding capacity:
$$\Delta = 12 - 2 = \mathbf{10\text{ pins}}$$
Ratio of holding capacity:
$$\text{Ratio} = \frac{12}{2} = \mathbf{6\text{ times}}$$

**Verification:** The distribution is symmetrical: at $4\text{ cm}$ and $8\text{ cm}$, the pin count is identical ($3\text{ pins}$); at $2\text{ cm}$ ($7\text{ pins}$) and $10\text{ cm}$ ($6\text{ pins}$), the counts match within experimental error ✅.

### Example 6: An Object Attracted at Both Ends: Magnet or Iron?

**Problem:** Two identical-looking metal rods, labelled Rod P and Rod Q, are resting on a laboratory bench. When one end of Rod P is presented to Rod Q, it firmly attracts **both** the left end and the right end of Rod Q. Can both rods be permanent magnets? Determine the true physical nature of Rod P and Rod Q.

**Solution:**
- If Rod Q were a permanent magnet, it would possess both a North pole at one end and a South pole at the other end. Consequently, presenting a specific magnetic pole of Rod P to Rod Q would inevitably cause **attraction at one end and repulsion at the other end**.
- Because attraction occurs at *both* ends of Rod Q and no repulsion is observed anywhere, Rod Q does not possess fixed magnetic poles. Therefore, **Rod Q cannot be a magnet; it is simply an unmagnetised ferromagnetic rod (such as soft iron or plain steel)**.
- For Rod P to exert an attractive force on an unmagnetised iron rod, **Rod P must be a permanent magnet**.

**Verification:** A permanent magnet attracts both ends of an unmagnetised ferromagnetic object through magnetic induction, but an unmagnetised object can never repel ✅.

### Example 7: Reversing One Magnet in a Pair

**Problem:** Two identical bar magnets, $M_1$ and $M_2$, are aligned longitudinally on a frictionless track such that the South pole of $M_1$ faces the North pole of $M_2$. In this initial state, they strongly attract each other. Magnet $M_2$ is now lifted, rotated horizontally by $180^\circ$ so that its poles are reversed, and replaced on the track. Describe and explain the resulting mechanical interaction.

```
 Initial state:   [ N ══════ S ]  ◄──►  [ N ══════ S ]    S faces N: ATTRACTION
                     M1                    M2

 Reversed state:  [ N ══════ S ]  ◄──►  [ S ══════ N ]    S faces S: REPULSION
                     M1                    M2 (reversed)
```

> **Figure 1.9** – Reversing magnet $M_2$ changes the opposing interface from unlike poles ($\text{S–N}$) to like poles ($\text{S–S}$), transforming attraction into mutual repulsion.

**Solution:**
- In the initial configuration, the adjacent facing poles are the South pole of $M_1$ and the North pole of $M_2$. Because unlike poles attract, the magnets accelerate toward each other with an attractive force.
- When $M_2$ is reversed end-for-end, its South pole now faces the South pole of $M_1$.
- Because like magnetic poles repel, the attractive force vanishes and is replaced by a **strong repulsive force**, causing the two magnets to push apart.

**Verification:** Exactly 2 mechanical states: unlike poles ($\text{attraction}$) and like poles ($\text{repulsion}$) ✅.

### Example 8: Determining Poles of a Suspended Magnet Facing East

**Problem:** Elena suspends an unmarked bar magnet horizontally by a fine thread and allows it to come to rest at equilibrium. She stands directly behind the magnet, facing eastward toward the rising Sun. She notices that End A of the suspended magnet points toward her **left hand**, while End B points toward her **right hand**. Identify the magnetic polarity of End A and End B.

**Solution:**
1. When facing eastward:
   - The direction to one's left is **Geographic North**.
   - The direction to one's right is **Geographic South**.
2. End A points toward Elena's left, meaning it points toward **Geographic North**.
3. By definition, the pole of a freely suspended magnet that points toward geographic north is its **North-seeking pole (N pole)**. Therefore, **End A is the North pole**.
4. End B points toward Elena's right, which is Geographic South. Therefore, **End B is the South pole**.

**Verification:** Facing East: Left = North ($\text{End A}$), Right = South ($\text{End B}$) ✅.

### Example 9: Scrap Yard Sorting Analysis

**Problem:** In an industrial sorting demonstration, a scrap hopper contains 20 items:
- 8 forged iron brackets
- 5 lengths of copper grounding wire
- 4 crushed aluminium beverage cans
- 3 discarded plastic containers

An industrial lifting magnet is lowered into the hopper. How many items will the magnet pick up, and how many items will remain behind in the hopper?

**Solution:**
- **Items picked up (magnetic):** The magnet attracts only ferromagnetic substances. The 8 forged iron brackets are made of iron.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Assuming magnets attract all metals.**
   A widespread misconception is that any metallic object will stick to a magnet. In reality, magnets attract only ferromagnetic materials (primarily iron, steel, nickel, and cobalt). Common metals such as copper, aluminium, brass, gold, and silver are non-magnetic and experience no perceptible attraction under ordinary conditions.

2. **Mistake 2: Believing that cutting a magnet in half isolates an N pole and an S pole.**
   Students often imagine that severing a bar magnet at its center yields a separate, isolated North pole and a separate South pole. In reality, every cut produces two smaller, complete dipolar magnets, each with its own conjugate North and South poles. Magnetic monopoles do not exist in classical physics.

3. **Mistake 3: Thinking poles are located at the absolute geometric tips of the magnet.**
   Magnetic poles are not mathematical points located at the extreme outer edge. They are concentrated volumes located slightly inward (typically a few millimeters inside) from the physical ends of the bar.

4. **Mistake 4: Relying on attraction as proof of magnetism.**
   Observing that an unknown metallic rod is attracted to a magnet does not prove that the rod is a magnet. An unmagnetised piece of soft iron or steel will also be attracted to both poles of a magnet. **Repulsion is the only infallible, definitive test of magnetism**, as it occurs exclusively between two like magnetic poles.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming magnetic forces require direct physical contact.**
   Magnetic force is an action-at-a-distance (non-contact) force. A magnet can attract a paper clip or deflect a compass needle through air, vacuum, sheets of paper, wooden boards, and glass plates without ever touching the object directly. The force diminishes smoothly as the intervening separation distance increases.

6. **Mistake 6: Assuming like poles attract because the Earth's North attracts a compass North.**
   A compass needle's North pole points toward geographic north. Some learners mistakenly infer that like poles attract (N attracting N). In reality, the Earth acts as if a giant magnet is buried within it, with its **magnetic South pole located near the geographic North pole**. Thus, the North pole of the compass needle is obeying the fundamental law of unlike poles attracting ($\text{N–S}$).

## Quick Recap

- A **magnet** is a body that produces a magnetic field and exerts attractive forces on ferromagnetic materials.
- **Natural magnets** (such as lodestone / magnetite) occur naturally in the Earth's crust, whereas **artificial magnets** are fabricated from iron, steel, nickel, cobalt, and specialized alloys such as alnico.
- Common magnet shapes include **bar, horseshoe, U-shaped, cylindrical, disc, ring, compass needle, and flexible rubber sheet magnets**.
- Materials are categorized as **magnetic** (strongly attracted; e.g., iron, carbon steel, nickel, cobalt) or **non-magnetic** (no perceptible attraction; e.g., copper, aluminium, brass, wood, plastic, glass).
- The attractive force of a magnet is concentrated near its two extremities, termed **magnetic poles**. The central region exhibits virtually zero attraction and is called the **neutral zone**.
- When suspended freely, a magnet orients itself along the geographic North–South direction (**directive property**). The end pointing northward is the **North-seeking pole (N)**; the end pointing southward is the **South-seeking pole (S)**.
- Fundamental law of magnetism: **Like poles repel each other; unlike poles attract each other.**
- **Repulsion is the only sure test of magnetism.** Attraction occurs between opposite poles as well as between a magnet and an unmagnetised ferromagnetic body; repulsion occurs exclusively between like poles of two magnets.
- Magnetic poles **always exist in pairs ($N$ and $S$)**; cutting a magnet produces smaller, complete dipoles. Isolated magnetic monopoles do not exist.
- Magnetic force is a **non-contact force** that acts through non-magnetic media (air, paper, wood, glass) and weakens rapidly with increasing separation distance.
- Magnets should be stored in pairs with opposite poles adjacent, buffered by non-magnetic spacers, and closed with **soft iron keepers** across their ends. They must be protected from excessive heat, mechanical impacts, and proximity to sensitive electronic devices.
