# Topic 5: Density and Measuring Volume

In the previous topic, we learned that no scientific measurement is completely free from uncertainty: errors exist, accuracy and precision describe distinct aspects of quality, percentage error reveals the relative importance of an error, and significant figures establish how many digits in an answer are experimentally justified. Up to this point, we have explored fundamental measurements of length, mass, and time, along with their SI units and instruments.

Now, we will bring these concepts together. By measuring mass and volume simultaneously, we will determine a vital physical property—**density**—and discover how the volume of an irregularly shaped stone can be measured using the principle of water displacement. Because this is the concluding topic of the chapter, it also includes a comprehensive **Chapter Wrap-up**.

## Two Paperweights: Identical Dimensions, Astonishingly Different Masses

On the workbench of the artisan sculptor Mr. Harrison rested two ornamental paperweights. Both appeared identical to the eye: smooth, perfectly formed cubes measuring $5\text{ cm}$ on each side. One had been chiseled from solid natural stone; the other had been cast from gypsum plaster with concealed internal air pockets.

Mr. Harrison's apprentice, Liam, picked up the first cube, set it down, and lifted the second. He gasped in surprise. "Mr. Harrison, look at this! Both blocks have the exact same shape and size, yet one feels remarkably heavy while the other feels light as a feather!"

Mr. Harrison smiled. "Equal physical dimensions do not mean an equal amount of matter inside, Liam. The matter packed into each unit of space is entirely different. In physics, we call this compactness of matter **density**. Let us quantify it with numbers."

Moments later, Mr. Harrison handed Liam an irregularly shaped, jagged pebble from the garden. "Suppose I want you to carve a miniature pendant from this stone. How will you determine its exact volume? It has no straight edges, no flat faces, and no simple geometry!"

Liam paused, deep in thought. By the end of this topic, we will resolve both puzzles mathematically.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Density ($\rho$)** | The mass contained per unit volume of a substance ($\rho = m/V$) | Granite stone: $2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$ |
| **Volume ($V$)** | The three-dimensional space occupied by a body or substance | Cube volume: $125\text{ cm}^3$ |
| **Measuring cylinder** | A graduated vertical glass or transparent plastic cylinder used to measure liquid volume | A $100\text{ mL}$ graduated cylinder |
| **Meniscus** | The curved upper surface of a liquid column in a narrow container | The downward-curving concave surface of water |
| **Displacement method** | A technique to determine the volume of an irregular solid by measuring the volume of fluid it displaces | Water level rises from $50\text{ mL}$ to $68\text{ mL}$ |
| **Irregular solid** | A solid object lacking a simple geometric symmetry, for which no standard mathematical volume formula exists | A natural pebble or rough mineral fragment |

## Part A: What is Density?

### How Compact is the Matter?

Imagine a large block of styrofoam and an identical-sized block of solid iron. Both occupy the exact same spatial volume, yet the iron block possesses a vastly greater mass. In iron, atomic mass is packed tightly into every cubic centimeter, whereas styrofoam consists largely of empty cellular air space.

This fundamental relationship between mass and volume is called **density**:

$$\mathbf{\text{Density} = \frac{\text{Mass}}{\text{Volume}}}$$

In algebraic notation, density is represented by the Greek letter $\rho$ (rho):
$$\mathbf{\rho = \frac{m}{V}}$$

- In the **SI system**, mass is measured in kilograms ($\text{kg}$) and volume in cubic meters ($\text{m}^3$), giving the SI base unit **$\text{kg/m}^3$**.
- In routine laboratory bench work, mass is often measured in grams ($\text{g}$) and volume in cubic centimeters ($\text{cm}^3$), yielding **$\text{g/cm}^3$**.
- The conversion factor between these units is:
$$\mathbf{1\text{ g/cm}^3 = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

Density is an **intrinsic property of a material**. A giant 100-kilogram iron beam and a tiny 1-gram iron nail have the exact same density ($7800\text{ kg/m}^3$).

### Typical Densities of Common Substances

| Substance | Approximate Density ($\text{kg/m}^3$) | Approximate Density ($\text{g/cm}^3$) |
|---|---|---|
| Air (sea level, $20^\circ\text{C}$) | 1.2 | 0.0012 |
| Wood (pine / oak, typical) | $\approx 600$ (varies with species) | $\approx 0.6$ |
| Pure Water ($4^\circ\text{C}$) | **1000** | **1.0** |
| Aluminum | 2700 | 2.7 |
| Iron / Steel | 7800 | 7.8 |
| Copper | 8900 | 8.9 |
| Mercury (liquid metal) | 13,600 | 13.6 |

*(Note: Densities vary slightly with temperature and purity. The density of pure water—$1000\text{ kg/m}^3 = 1.0\text{ g/cm}^3$—is a vital reference benchmark across physics).*

### Example 1: Density of an Iron Block

A solid iron block has a mass of $390\text{ g}$ and a volume of $50\text{ cm}^3$. Determine its density in $\text{g/cm}^3$ and in $\text{kg/m}^3$.

$$\rho = \frac{m}{V} = \frac{390\text{ g}}{50\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{7.8\text{ g/cm}^3}$$

Converting to SI units:
$$7.8\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 1000 = \mathbf{7800\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

**Check:** $7.8\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 50\text{ cm}^3 = 390\text{ g}$ ✅ (matches the expected iron density in our reference table).

### Example 2: Calculating Mass from Volume

A structural aluminum bracket has a volume of $0.002\text{ m}^3$. The density of aluminum is $2700\text{ kg/m}^3$. Determine its mass.

$$m = \rho \times V = 2700\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 0.002\text{ m}^3 = \mathbf{5.4\text{ kg}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{5.4\text{ kg}}{0.002\text{ m}^3} = 2700\text{ kg/m}^3$ ✅

### Example 3: Calculating Volume from Mass

A machine part contains $17.8\text{ kg}$ of pure copper. Given that the density of copper is $8900\text{ kg/m}^3$, find its volume in $\text{m}^3$ and in $\text{cm}^3$.

$$V = \frac{m}{\rho} = \frac{17.8\text{ kg}}{8900\text{ kg/m}^3} = \mathbf{0.002\text{ m}^3}$$

In cubic centimeters:
$$0.002\text{ m}^3 \times 1{,}000{,}000\text{ cm}^3/\text{m}^3 = \mathbf{2000\text{ cm}^3}$$

**Check:** $8900\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 0.002\text{ m}^3 = 17.8\text{ kg}$ ✅

### Example 4: Converting Density Units

**(a) Mercury has a density of $13.6\text{ g/cm}^3$. Express this in $\text{kg/m}^3$:**
$$13.6\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 1000 = \mathbf{13{,}600\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

**(b) Seasoned oak wood has a density of $600\text{ kg/m}^3$. Express this in $\text{g/cm}^3$:**
$$600\text{ kg/m}^3 \div 1000 = \mathbf{0.6\text{ g/cm}^3}$$

**Check:** $0.6 \times 1000 = 600\text{ kg/m}^3$ ✅

### Example 5: Comparing Two Specimens

Sample A has a mass of $270\text{ g}$ and occupies $100\text{ cm}^3$. Sample B has the exact same mass of $270\text{ g}$, but occupies $300\text{ cm}^3$. Which sample has the higher density?

$$\rho(A) = \frac{270\text{ g}}{100\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{2.7\text{ g/cm}^3}$$
$$\rho(B) = \frac{270\text{ g}}{300\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{0.9\text{ g/cm}^3}$$

Sample A is three times denser than Sample B ($2.7 > 0.9$). Even though both contain identical mass, Sample B spreads that mass over three times more volume.

**Check:** $\frac{2.7}{0.9} = 3$, matching the volume ratio $\frac{300}{100} = 3$ ✅

## Part B: Measuring Volume

To calculate density, we obtain the mass directly from a laboratory balance. Determining volume depends on whether the object has a regular or irregular geometry.

### 1. Regular Solids: Geometric Formulas

| Geometric Shape | Volume Formula |
|---|---|
| Rectangular Block (Cuboid) | $\text{Length} \times \text{Width} \times \text{Height} = l \times w \times h$ |
| Cube (edge length $a$) | $a^3$ |
| Solid Cylinder (radius $r$, height $h$) | $\pi r^2 h \quad (\pi \approx 3.14 \text{ or } 22/7)$ |
| Sphere (radius $r$) | $\frac{4}{3} \pi r^3$ |

Linear dimensions are measured using a metric ruler or a Vernier caliper (Topic 3). Ensure all dimensions share identical units before calculating volume.

### Example 6: Volume and Density of an Iron Cuboid

An iron cuboid measures $10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm}$ and has a mass of $1560\text{ g}$.
$$V = 10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm} \times 4\text{ cm} = \mathbf{200\text{ cm}^3}$$
$$\rho = \frac{1560\text{ g}}{200\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{7.8\text{ g/cm}^3} = \mathbf{7800\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

**Check:** $7.8 \times 200 = 1560\text{ g}$ ✅

### Example 7: Volume and Density of a Metal Cylinder

A solid metal cylinder has a radius $r = 3.5\text{ cm}$ and a height $h = 10\text{ cm}$. Its mass is $3080\text{ g}$. Take $\pi = 22/7$.
$$V = \pi r^2 h = \frac{22}{7} \times (3.5\text{ cm})^2 \times 10\text{ cm} = \frac{22}{7} \times 12.25\text{ cm}^2 \times 10\text{ cm} = 38.5\text{ cm}^2 \times 10\text{ cm} = \mathbf{385\text{ cm}^3}$$
$$\rho = \frac{3080\text{ g}}{385\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{8\text{ g/cm}^3} = \mathbf{8000\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

**Check:** $8\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 385\text{ cm}^3 = 3080\text{ g}$ ✅

### 2. Measuring Liquid Volume: The Graduated Cylinder

Liquid volume is measured directly using a graduated cylinder marked in milliliters ($\text{mL}$).
$$\mathbf{1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3 \quad \text{and} \quad 1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL} = 1000\text{ cm}^3 = 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3}$$

Due to surface tension, water curves upward slightly at the glass container walls, forming a curved boundary called a **meniscus**.
- Water forms a **concave meniscus** (curving downward in the center).
- Always position your eye **level with the liquid surface** and read the graduation line corresponding to the **bottom-most point of the meniscus**.

```
        Eye Position (Directly horizontal at meniscus level)
               o --------------------->
    |      |
    |\    /|   <- Liquid climbs container wall
    | \__/ |   <- Bottom of concave meniscus
    |------|--- 40 mL   <- Read correct value here!
    |      |
    |------|--- 30 mL
    |______|
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Reading a graduated cylinder: Align line of sight horizontally with the bottom of the meniscus. Viewing from above or below introduces parallax error.

In Figure 5.1, the correct volume reading is **$40\text{ mL}$**.

### 3. Irregular Solids: The Water Displacement Method

For an irregularly shaped stone, pebble, or metal scrap, no mathematical formula applies. We exploit an elegant physical law: **an insoluble solid fully submerged in a fluid displaces a volume of fluid exactly equal to its own physical volume.**

**Laboratory Procedure:**
1. Pour water into a graduated cylinder to a level sufficient to submerge the specimen completely. Record this initial volume: $V_1$.
2. Tie a fine thread around the stone and lower it gently into the water until fully submerged, ensuring no air bubbles adhere to the surface and no water splashes out.
3. Record the new water level: $V_2$.
4. Calculate the volume of the irregular solid:
$$\mathbf{V_{\text{solid}} = V_2 - V_1}$$

```
     Initial Level (V1)                  Submerged Level (V2)
       |      |                              |      |
       |      |                              |  ~~~ |  <- V2 = 68 mL
       |~~~~~ | <- V1 = 50 mL                |~~~~~ |
       |  50  |                              |  (@) |  <- Submerged stone
       |______|                              |______|
```

> **Figure 5.2** – Water Displacement Method: Submerging the stone causes the water column to rise from $V_1 = 50\text{ mL}$ to $V_2 = 68\text{ mL}$. The volume increase ($18\text{ mL}$) equals the stone's volume.

If the object is too large for a standard cylinder, an **overflow can (eureka can)** is used: fill the can until water drips from the side spout, lower the object inside, and collect the displaced overflow in a measuring cylinder. The collected liquid volume equals the object's volume.

> [!NOTE]
> This displacement technique applies directly to dense objects that **sink completely in water** and **do not dissolve**. Objects that float or dissolve require alternative immersion fluids (such as paraffin oil) or sinker weights, topics explored in the chapter on Fluids.

### Example 8: Density of an Irregular Pebble

A student measures an irregular pebble using water displacement. The initial water level in a cylinder is $50\text{ mL}$, and upon complete immersion, the level rises to $68\text{ mL}$. On an electronic balance, the pebble's mass is $140.4\text{ g}$.
$$\text{Volume } V = V_2 - V_1 = 68\text{ mL} - 50\text{ mL} = \mathbf{18\text{ mL}} = \mathbf{18\text{ cm}^3}$$
$$\rho = \frac{140.4\text{ g}}{18\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{7.8\text{ g/cm}^3} = \mathbf{7800\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

**Check:** $7.8\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 18\text{ cm}^3 = 140.4\text{ g}$ ✅ (The density indicates the pebble is rich in iron mineral ore).

## Part C: Laboratory Determination of Solid Density

**Apparatus:** Electronic balance, graduated measuring cylinder ($100\text{ mL}$ capacity), water, thin sewing thread, and a solid test specimen (e.g., an irregular chunk of aluminum alloy).

**Experimental Steps:**
1. Check the balance for zero error. Measure and record the dry mass ($m$) of the specimen.
2. Fill the cylinder partially with water; read and record $V_1$ at the bottom of the meniscus.
3. Secure the specimen with fine thread; submerge it gently and record $V_2$.
4. Calculate displaced volume $V = V_2 - V_1$.
5. Compute density $\rho = m / V$.
6. Repeat the procedure across three trials to minimize random experimental error (Topic 4) and compute the mean density.

**Sample Laboratory Data Table:** Solid aluminum sample of certified mass $m = 54.2\text{ g}$.

| Trial | $V_1\text{ (mL)}$ | $V_2\text{ (mL)}$ | Displaced Volume $V = V_2 - V_1\text{ (cm}^3\text{)}$ | Calculated $\rho = m/V\text{ (g/cm}^3\text{)}$ |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | 40 | 60 | 20 | 2.71 |
| **2** | 30 | 51 | 21 | 2.58 |
| **3** | 25 | 45 | 20 | 2.71 |

$$\text{Mean Density } \bar{\rho} = \frac{2.71 + 2.58 + 2.71}{3} = \frac{8.00}{3} \approx \mathbf{2.67\text{ g/cm}^3}$$

The standard reference handbook value for this aluminum alloy is $2.70\text{ g/cm}^3$.
$$\text{Percentage Error} = \left(\frac{|2.67 - 2.70|\text{ g/cm}^3}{2.70\text{ g/cm}^3}\right) \times 100\% = \frac{0.03}{2.70} \times 100\% \approx \mathbf{1.1\%}$$

**Check:** $\frac{0.03}{2.70} \approx 0.0111 \implies 1.11\%$ ✅ (an excellent experimental result well within standard school lab tolerances).

## Part D: Solving the Workshop Mystery

Let us return to Mr. Harrison and Liam's sculptures:

### 1. The Two Cube Paperweights

Both paperweights are cubes with edge length $a = 5\text{ cm}$:
$$V = a^3 = 5\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm} = \mathbf{125\text{ cm}^3}$$

Liam placed each on the workshop electronic balance:
- Natural stone cube mass $= 337.5\text{ g}$
- Cast plaster cube mass $= 100.0\text{ g}$

Calculating their densities:
$$\rho(\text{stone}) = \frac{337.5\text{ g}}{125\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{2.7\text{ g/cm}^3} = \mathbf{2700\text{ kg/m}^3}$$
$$\rho(\text{plaster}) = \frac{100.0\text{ g}}{125\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{0.8\text{ g/cm}^3} = \mathbf{800\text{ kg/m}^3}$$

Now Liam understood completely: both blocks occupy an identical volume of $125\text{ cm}^3$, but the granite stone packs $337.5\text{ g}$ of tightly bound mineral atoms, whereas the porous plaster packs only $100\text{ g}$ of matter interspersed with microscopic air cavities!

**Check:** $2.7 \times 125 = 337.5\text{ g}$ ✅ and $0.8 \times 125 = 100\text{ g}$ ✅

### 2. The Jagged Stone

Liam poured $60\text{ mL}$ of water into a graduated cylinder ($V_1 = 60\text{ mL}$).
He suspended the jagged stone on a thread and submerged it completely: the water level rose to $95\text{ mL}$ ($V_2 = 95\text{ mL}$).
On the balance, the stone had a mass of $94.5\text{ g}$.

$$\text{Volume } V = 95\text{ mL} - 60\text{ mL} = 35\text{ mL} = \mathbf{35\text{ cm}^3}$$
$$\rho = \frac{94.5\text{ g}}{35\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{2.7\text{ g/cm}^3}$$

Mr. Harrison grinned. "Look at that density: $2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$. It is the exact same natural granite as our first paperweight! Now you know both its volume and its material makeup without having to measure a single straight side."

**Check:** $2.7\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 35\text{ cm}^3 = 94.5\text{ g}$ ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Mixing non-coherent units (e.g., grams with cubic meters)**
   - Incorrect: Dividing $390\text{ g}$ by $0.00005\text{ m}^3$ to get $7{,}800{,}000\text{ g/m}^3$.
   - Correct: Maintain consistent unit systems: either CGS ($\text{g/cm}^3 \rightarrow 7.8\text{ g/cm}^3$) or SI ($\text{kg/m}^3 \rightarrow 7800\text{ kg/m}^3$).

2. **Mistake 2: Confusing milliliters ($\text{mL}$) with liters ($\text{L}$)**
   - Remember that $1\text{ L} = 1000\text{ mL} = 1000\text{ cm}^3$. Treating a $2\text{ L}$ bottle as $2\text{ mL}$ causes a 1000-fold calculation error.

3. **Mistake 3: Reading a liquid meniscus incorrectly**
   - For water, always read the bottom-most point of the concave curve with your line of sight held horizontally. Reading from above or below introduces parallax error.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing that larger objects have greater density**
   - A 10-ton steel anchor and a 10-gram steel paperclip have the exact same density ($7800\text{ kg/m}^3$). Cutting an object in half halves both its mass and its volume, leaving the ratio $\rho = m/V$ unchanged. Density depends on the material, not the sample size!

5. **Mistake 5: Writing incorrect density units**
   - Never write $\text{kg/m}^2$ (which is mass per area) or simply $\text{kg}$ (which is mass). Density is mass per three-dimensional volume: **$\text{kg/m}^3$** or **$\text{g/cm}^3$**.

## Quick Recap

- **Density = Mass / Volume ($\rho = m/V$):** The concentration of mass within a unit volume.
- SI unit is **$\text{kg/m}^3$**; common laboratory unit is **$\text{g/cm}^3$**. Key conversion: **$1\text{ g/cm}^3 = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$**.
- Water benchmark: $\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 = 1.0\text{ g/cm}^3$. Typical solids: aluminum ($2700$), iron ($7800$), copper ($8900$), mercury ($13{,}600$).
- Density is an intrinsic material property independent of sample size or shape.
- **Volume of regular solids:** Cuboid ($l \times w \times h$), cube ($a^3$), cylinder ($\pi r^2 h$).
- **Volume of liquids:** Graduated cylinder, reading the bottom of the concave meniscus at eye level. $1\text{ mL} = 1\text{ cm}^3$.
- **Volume of irregular insoluble solids:** Water displacement method ($V = V_2 - V_1$).

## Practice Questions

5. Liquid mercury has a density of $13{,}600\text{ kg/m}^3$. Calculate the mass of $500\text{ cm}^3$ of mercury in kilograms.

6. A solid metallic sample is lowered into a graduated measuring cylinder. The water meniscus rises from $35\text{ mL}$ to $47\text{ mL}$. If the sample's mass is $96\text{ g}$, find its volume and density.

7. A solid aluminum cylinder has a radius of $7\text{ cm}$ and a height of $10\text{ cm}$. The density of aluminum is $2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$. Calculate its mass in grams and in kilograms (take $\pi = 22/7$).

8. In a laboratory density experiment, a student determines the density of a copper block to be $8.7\text{ g/cm}^3$. The accepted standard density is $8.9\text{ g/cm}^3$. Calculate the percentage error, rounding to one decimal place.

9. An iron beam of density $7800\text{ kg/m}^3$ is cut into two equal halves. What is the density of each half? Explain your reasoning.

10. A student inspects the water level in a measuring cylinder from an angle looking downward from above. Explain why this creates an erroneous reading and state the correct technique. Additionally, a block has a mass of $2\text{ kg}$ and a volume of $500\text{ cm}^3$; find its density in $\text{g/cm}^3$.

### Answers

1. $\rho = \frac{600\text{ g}}{250\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{2.4\text{ g/cm}^3}$.
   In SI units: $2.4\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 1000 = \mathbf{2400\text{ kg/m}^3}$.
   *Check:* $2.4 \times 250 = 600\text{ g}$ ✅

2. $V = 2\text{ m} \times 1.5\text{ m} \times 1\text{ m} = 3\text{ m}^3$.
   $m = \rho \times V = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 3\text{ m}^3 = \mathbf{3000\text{ kg}}$.
   *Check:* $\frac{3000}{3} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$ ✅

3. $V = \frac{m}{\rho} = \frac{30\text{ kg}}{600\text{ kg/m}^3} = \mathbf{0.05\text{ m}^3}$.
   In $\text{cm}^3$: $0.05 \times 1{,}000{,}000 = \mathbf{50{,}000\text{ cm}^3}$.
   *Check:* $600 \times 0.05 = 30\text{ kg}$ ✅

4. (a) $0.9\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 1000 = \mathbf{900\text{ kg/m}^3}$.
   (b) $2700\text{ kg/m}^3 \div 1000 = \mathbf{2.7\text{ g/cm}^3}$.
   *Check:* $2.7 \times 1000 = 2700\text{ kg/m}^3$ ✅

5. $500\text{ cm}^3 = 500 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^3 = 0.0005\text{ m}^3$.
   $m = 13{,}600\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 0.0005\text{ m}^3 = \mathbf{6.8\text{ kg}}$.
   *Check:* $\frac{6.8}{0.0005} = 13{,}600\text{ kg/m}^3$ ✅

6. $V = 47\text{ mL} - 35\text{ mL} = 12\text{ mL} = \mathbf{12\text{ cm}^3}$.
   $\rho = \frac{96\text{ g}}{12\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{8\text{ g/cm}^3} \quad (= 8000\text{ kg/m}^3)$.
   *Check:* $8 \times 12 = 96\text{ g}$ ✅

7. $V = \pi r^2 h = \frac{22}{7} \times 7\text{ cm} \times 7\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 22 \times 7 \times 10 = 1540\text{ cm}^3$.
   $m = \rho \times V = 2.7\text{ g/cm}^3 \times 1540\text{ cm}^3 = \mathbf{4158\text{ g}} = \mathbf{4.158\text{ kg}}$.
   *Check:* $\frac{4158}{1540} = 2.7\text{ g/cm}^3$ ✅

8. $\text{Percentage Error} = \frac{|8.9 - 8.7|\text{ g/cm}^3}{8.9\text{ g/cm}^3} \times 100\% = \frac{0.2}{8.9} \times 100\% = 2.247\dots\% \approx \mathbf{2.2\%}$.
   *Check:* $8.9 \times 0.022 = 0.196 \approx 0.2$ ✅

9. The density remains **$7800\text{ kg/m}^3$**. Cutting the beam in half halves both mass and volume simultaneously, leaving the ratio $\rho = m/V$ unchanged. Density is an intrinsic physical property of iron.

10. Looking from above causes parallax error, making the meniscus appear artificially higher or lower than its true mark. The eye must be held horizontally level with the cylinder, reading the lowest point of the concave meniscus.
    Density calculation: $2\text{ kg} = 2000\text{ g}$.
    $\rho = \frac{2000\text{ g}}{500\text{ cm}^3} = \mathbf{4\text{ g/cm}^3} \quad (= 4000\text{ kg/m}^3)$.
    *Check:* $4 \times 500 = 2000\text{ g}$ ✅
