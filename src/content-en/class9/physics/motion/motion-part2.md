# Topic 2: Distance and Displacement

In Topic 1, we learned that describing whether an object is moving or stationary requires a declared reference point, and positions can be mapped on a coordinate number line. Now comes the natural next question: when an object moves, **how do we quantify how far it traveled?** This sounds simple, but everyday language masks a critical scientific distinction. "How far" can mean two entirely different things: the actual ground covered versus the direct straight-line distance from start to finish. In this topic, we master these two foundational concepts: **distance** and **displacement**.

---

## Mr. Evans's Delivery Route

Mr. Evans, a neighborhood postal courier, starts his morning delivery route from the central post office. Today's route follows a precise sequence:

- From the **Post Office** to the **Town Library**: directly **400 m East**.
- From the **Library** to the **High School**: directly **300 m North**.
- At the school, he drops off the morning packages and returns **along the exact same streets**: school back to the library, then library back to the post office.

Maya, a Class 9 student standing by the school entrance, saw Mr. Evans finish packing his courier bag and asked, "Mr. Evans, how much ground did you cover on foot today?"

Mr. Evans calculated, "Well, 400 meters out to the library, 300 meters up to the school, and then 300 meters back plus another 400 meters to return to the depot—that makes a total of **1,400 meters**."

Maya followed up with a playful second question: "And once you walk through the post office doors, how far away will you be from where you started?"

Mr. Evans laughed, "Well, once I step back through the depot doors, I am right back at my starting desk—so exactly **zero meters**!"

Maya paused: "He walked 1,400 meters of hard physical effort, yet his net shift from where he started is 0 meters? How can both statements be scientifically true?" Both statements are entirely true because they measure **two different physical quantities**. Let us examine the exact vocabulary before resolving Mr. Evans's route completely.

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Path** | The actual continuous trajectory traced by a moving object through space | Post office $\to$ library $\to$ school $\to$ depot |
| **Distance** | The total length of the actual path traversed by an object, regardless of direction | 1,400 m |
| **Initial position** | The starting location of an object at the beginning of the motion interval | The Post Office ($0\text{ m}$) |
| **Final position** | The ending location of an object at the conclusion of the motion interval | Returning to the Post Office |
| **Displacement** | The shortest straight-line vector directed from an object's initial position to its final position | 500 m directed Northeast |
| **Scalar** | A physical quantity specified completely by magnitude (numerical value and unit) alone | Distance, mass, time, temperature |
| **Vector** | A physical quantity requiring both magnitude and direction for its complete description | Displacement, velocity, force |
| **Magnitude** | The numerical size or extent of a physical quantity | 500 m |

---

## Part A: Distance (How Much Ground Was Covered)

**Distance** is the total length of the actual path traveled by an object during its motion.

Key characteristics of distance:
- Think of distance as an automotive **odometer reading**: every step you take, forward, backward, or around curves, continuously adds to the total meter count.
- Distance has **no direction associated with it**. Saying "1,400 m" is completely sufficient.
- Distance is **never negative**. It is either zero (if the object remained strictly stationary) or positive.
- Distance **never decreases with time**. Even when turning around and walking back toward the start, the distance counter continues to accumulate positively.
- Distance is a **scalar quantity**.
- SI unit: **meter ($\text{m}$)**. For longer geographical trips, kilometers are standard ($1\text{ km} = 1,000\text{ m}$).

Throughout Mr. Evans's round, every step added to his total distance: $400\text{ m}$, then $700\text{ m}$, then $1,000\text{ m}$, reaching $1,400\text{ m}$. Reversing direction did not subtract a single meter.

---

## Part B: Displacement (Where You Are Relative to the Start)

**Displacement** is the shortest straight-line distance directed from an object's initial position to its final position, along with the direction pointing from start to end.

Displacement does not care about the winding curves or detours taken along the way. It depends solely on the **endpoints**: where did the motion begin, and where did it end?

- Magnitude of displacement = the straight-line length connecting the initial point directly to the final point.
- Direction = points straight from the initial position toward the final position.
- Displacement is a **vector quantity**: specifying only a magnitude leaves the description incomplete. Saying "500 m" is partial; specifying "500 m directed Northeast" provides the complete displacement.
- Displacement **can be zero**, even after hours of motion, whenever the object returns to its exact starting point.
- Along a straight single axis (one-dimensional motion), we indicate direction compactly using **positive ($+$) and negative ($-$) signs**.

### Using Positive (+) and Negative (−) Signs Along a Straight Line

When motion is restricted to a straight line, we assign one direction as positive (e.g., East as $+$); the opposite direction automatically becomes negative (West as $-$). Similarly, upward is typically taken as $+$, and downward as $-$.

```
  West (-)                                        East (+)
  <------+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+-----+------>
        -3    -2    -1     0    +1    +2    +3  ... (m)
```

> **Figure 2.1** – Direction along a one-dimensional coordinate axis: eastward displacements carry a positive sign ($+$), while westward displacements carry a negative sign ($-$).

A displacement of "$-4\text{ m}$" indicates a magnitude of $4\text{ m}$ pointing in the negative direction (West). The minus sign never implies "less than zero distance"—it strictly represents **reversed spatial direction**.

> **Note:** The simple $+/-$ sign convention applies specifically to straight-line motion. For two-dimensional paths (such as turning at right angles), we determine the displacement magnitude using the Pythagorean theorem and state the direction in words (e.g., "Northeast").

## Part C: Analyzing Mr. Evans's Route Step-by-Step

Recall the Pythagorean theorem for right-angled triangles: the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the two perpendicular legs ($c^2 = a^2 + b^2$).

```
                      High School (S)
                         *
                        /|
                       / |
          Straight    /  | 300 m (North)
          line path  /   |
           500 m    /    |
                   /     |
    Post Office   *------*  Town Library (L)
        (P)       400 m (East)
```

> **Figure 2.2** – Mr. Evans's journey: from P to L is 400 m East, and L to S is 300 m North. The direct straight-line displacement from P to S is 500 m.

### Halfway: Reaching the School

- Distance $= 400\text{ m} + 300\text{ m} = \mathbf{700\text{ m}}$
- Displacement (straight-line arrow from P to S):
  $$\text{Displacement} = \sqrt{400^2 + 300^2} = \sqrt{160,000 + 90,000} = \sqrt{250,000} = \mathbf{500\text{ m}}\text{ directed Northeast}$$

**Consistency Check:** $500^2 = 250,000$ and $400^2 + 300^2 = 160,000 + 90,000 = 250,000$ ✅. Furthermore, $500\text{ m} < 700\text{ m}$, confirming that the direct straight-line displacement is shorter than the path distance.

### The Full Round Trip: Returning to the Post Office

- Distance $= 400\text{ m} + 300\text{ m} + 300\text{ m} + 400\text{ m} = \mathbf{1,400\text{ m}}$
- Displacement: initial position and final position are identical (the Post Office), so direct separation $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$.

**Consistency Check:**
- Forward legs: $400 + 300 = 700\text{ m}$.
- Return legs: $300 + 400 = 700\text{ m}$.
- Total distance: $700 + 700 = 1,400\text{ m}$ ✅.
- Initial point $=$ Final point $\implies \text{Displacement} = 0\text{ m}$ ✅.

Now Maya's puzzle is fully resolved: Mr. Evans walked **1,400 meters** of distance, but his net change in position—his **displacement**—was exactly **0 meters**. Both answers are completely correct because they measure two distinct physical properties: distance records path history, while displacement records net change in position.

## Part D: Solved Examples

### Example 1: 6 m East, Then 2 m West

A student walks 6 m East along a straight sidewalk, stops, turns around, and walks 2 m West. Find the distance and displacement.

Let East be designated as $+$.
- Distance $= 6\text{ m} + 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{8\text{ m}}$
- Displacement $= (+6\text{ m}) + (-2\text{ m}) = \mathbf{+4\text{ m}}$ (meaning **4 m East**)

**Check:** The student started at $0$, walked to $+6$, and stepped back $2$, ending at coordinate $+4\text{ m}$ ✅. Notice that the displacement magnitude ($4\text{ m}$) is strictly less than the distance ($8\text{ m}$) ✅.

### Example 2: From Home to the Store and Back

Maya walks 150 m from her house to the local grocery store and walks directly back home along the same sidewalk.
- Distance $= 150\text{ m} + 150\text{ m} = \mathbf{300\text{ m}}$
- Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$ (initial and final positions are both home)

**Check:** $(+150\text{ m}) + (-150\text{ m}) = 0\text{ m}$ ✅. Distance is non-zero, but displacement is zero.

### Example 3: Half-Lap Around a Circular Track

A circular track has a radius of $r = 14\text{ m}$. An athlete starts at point A and runs half a lap along the circumference to reach the diametrically opposite point B. (Use $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$).

```
          .  ~  ~  ~  .
       ~                 ~
      ~                   ~
     A *-------------------* B
      ~         O           ~
       ~                   ~
          .  ~  ~  ~  .

   A to B along the circular perimeter: distance = pi * r
   A to B along straight connecting line: displacement = 2r
```

> **Figure 2.3** – Half-lap on a circular track: the traveled path equals half the circumference ($\pi r$), while the direct displacement equals the diameter ($2r$).

The perimeter of a half-circle equals $\frac{2\pi r}{2} = \pi r$.
- Distance $= \pi r = \frac{22}{7} \times 14\text{ m} = 22 \times 2 = \mathbf{44\text{ m}}$
- Displacement $= \text{straight line from A to B} = \text{diameter} = 2r = 2 \times 14\text{ m} = \mathbf{28\text{ m}}\text{ directed from A to B}$

**Check:** $28\text{ m} < 44\text{ m}$ ✅. The straight chord is significantly shorter than the curved arc.

### Example 4: A Full Lap Around the Same Track

The same athlete ($r = 14\text{ m}$) continues running and completes one full lap, returning to point A.
- Distance $= \text{full circumference} = 2\pi r = 2 \times \frac{22}{7} \times 14\text{ m} = \mathbf{88\text{ m}}$
- Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$ (initial and final points are identical)

**Check:** A full lap distance is twice the half-lap distance ($2 \times 44 = 88\text{ m}$ ✅). Displacement is $0\text{ m}$ ✅.

### Example 5: Mr. Evans's Route at Each Landmark

During his return trip, Mr. Evans arrives back at the **Town Library**. What are his total distance traveled and displacement relative to the Post Office at that exact moment?
- Distance $= 400\text{ m}\text{ (P to L)} + 300\text{ m}\text{ (L to S)} + 300\text{ m}\text{ (S to L)} = \mathbf{1,000\text{ m}}$
- He is physically at the Library, which is $400\text{ m}$ East of the Post Office:
  $$\text{Displacement} = \mathbf{400\text{ m directed East}}$$

**Check:** Straight-line distance from P to L is $400\text{ m}$ ✅. $400\text{ m} < 1,000\text{ m}$ ✅. Notice how displacement grew from $400\text{ m} \to 500\text{ m}$, decreased back to $400\text{ m}$, and finally reached $0\text{ m}$. **Displacement can increase, decrease, or vanish; distance can only accumulate.**

### Example 6: A Bus on a Straight Highway

An intercity bus drives 60 km East along a straight highway, stops, and then reverses 20 km West. Determine the distance and displacement.

Let East be positive ($+$).
- Distance $= 60\text{ km} + 20\text{ km} = \mathbf{80\text{ km}}$
- Displacement $= (+60\text{ km}) + (-20\text{ km}) = \mathbf{+40\text{ km}}$ (**40 km East**)

**Check:** In meters, $40\text{ km} = 40,000\text{ m}$ and $80\text{ km} = 80,000\text{ m}$. $40,000\text{ m} < 80,000\text{ m}$ ✅.

### Example 7: A Ball Dropped from 10 m and Bouncing Up 4 m

A rubber ball is dropped from an initial height of 10 m above the ground. It strikes the floor and rebounds vertically upward to a height of 4 m. Find the distance traveled and displacement relative to the release point.

Let upward be designated as positive ($+$).
- Distance $= 10\text{ m (downward)} + 4\text{ m (upward)} = \mathbf{14\text{ m}}$
- Initial position $= +10\text{ m}$ above the floor; final position $= +4\text{ m}$ above the floor.
  $$\text{Displacement} = \text{Final} - \text{Initial} = 4\text{ m} - 10\text{ m} = \mathbf{-6\text{ m}}\text{ (meaning } 6\text{ m downward)}$$

**Check:** The ball ends up $10 - 4 = 6\text{ m}$ below where it was dropped ✅. Magnitude of displacement ($6\text{ m}$) is less than distance ($14\text{ m}$) ✅.

### Example 8: Negative Displacement on a Number Line

A cyclist starts at coordinate position $+5\text{ m}$ along a straight east-west bike path and rides continuously West until reaching coordinate position $-3\text{ m}$ without turning around. Find the displacement and distance.
- Displacement $= \text{Final} - \text{Initial} = (-3\text{ m}) - (+5\text{ m}) = \mathbf{-8\text{ m}}$ (**8 m West**)
- Distance $= \mathbf{8\text{ m}}$ (since the motion was in a single direction along a straight line)

**Check:** From $+5$ to $0$ is $5\text{ m}$, and from $0$ to $-3$ is $3\text{ m}$, giving $5 + 3 = 8\text{ m}$ ✅. Because there was no reversal of direction along this straight path, the magnitude of displacement equals the distance traveled ($|-8\text{ m}| = 8\text{ m}$).

### Example 9: 30 m North, Then 40 m East

A hiker walks 30 m North through an open field, turns 90 degrees, and walks 40 m East. Determine the total distance and resultant displacement.
- Distance $= 30\text{ m} + 40\text{ m} = \mathbf{70\text{ m}}$
- Displacement using Pythagoras:
  $$\text{Displacement} = \sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = \sqrt{900 + 1,600} = \sqrt{2,500} = \mathbf{50\text{ m directed Northeast}}$$

**Check:** $50^2 = 2,500$ and $30^2 + 40^2 = 900 + 1,600 = 2,500$ ✅. $50\text{ m} < 70\text{ m}$ ✅. This corresponds to the standard $3-4-5$ right triangle scaled by $10$.

### Example 10: A 400-Meter Athletic Running Track

An Olympic running track measures 400 m in one complete loop. An athlete runs around the track:
- **After 1 lap:** Distance $= \mathbf{400\text{ m}}$, Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$ (athlete returns to starting line).
- **After 3 laps:** Distance $= 3 \times 400\text{ m} = \mathbf{1,200\text{ m}}$, Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$.

**Check:** At the completion of every whole number of laps, initial and final coordinates coincide, so displacement remains identically $0\text{ m}$ while distance accumulates by $400\text{ m}$ per lap ✅.

## Part E: When Are Distance and Displacement Equal?

Comparing all of our solved examples reveals a clear and universal geometric principle:

> The magnitude of displacement is **equal to distance only when an object moves along a straight line in a single, unchanging direction without turning back** (as in Example 8).

In every other circumstance, the magnitude of displacement is **strictly less than distance**:

| Motion Scenario | Distance | Magnitude of Displacement | Comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight line, single direction (Example 8) | 8 m | 8 m | Equal |
| Straight line with partial reversal (Example 1) | 8 m | 4 m | Displacement < Distance |
| Perpendicular L-shaped path (Example 9) | 70 m | 50 m | Displacement < Distance |
| Half-circle arc (Example 3) | 44 m | 28 m | Displacement < Distance |
| Full closed loop (Example 4) | 88 m | 0 m | Displacement < Distance |

This establishes a fundamental law of kinematics:

$$\mathbf{|\text{Displacement}| \le \text{Distance}}$$

The shortest spatial path connecting any two distinct points in Euclidean space is a straight line. Any curved or zig-zag path must be longer than that straight line, making distance greater than or equal to displacement magnitude.

## Scalar vs. Vector Quantities

Let us summarize the broader conceptual distinction introduced here:

- **Scalar Quantities:** Described completely by magnitude alone (a number and a unit). Direction has no physical meaning for them.
  - Examples: **mass** ($5\text{ kg}$), **time** ($10\text{ s}$), **distance** ($1,400\text{ m}$), **temperature** ($25\text{ }^\circ\text{C}$), **speed**.
- **Vector Quantities:** Require both magnitude and direction for their complete specification.
  - Examples: **displacement** ($500\text{ m Northeast}$), **velocity** ($20\text{ m/s East}$), **force** ($10\text{ N downward}$).

Stating *"a mass of 5 kg directed North"* makes no physical sense because mass is isotropic (non-directional). In contrast, stating *"the town is located 5 km away"* is insufficient to navigate there because you must know in which direction to travel.

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Calling the total path length displacement.**
   For Mr. Evans's round trip, stating that his displacement is $1,400\text{ m}$ is incorrect. $1,400\text{ m}$ is the total path length, which is **distance**. His net displacement for the completed round trip is $0\text{ m}$.

2. **Mistake 2: Stating displacement without specifying a direction.**
   Writing *"Displacement = 500 m"* is incomplete. The complete vector specification is *"500 m directed Northeast"* (or along a straight coordinate axis, "$+500\text{ m}$" or "$-500\text{ m}$"). Without direction, it is not a vector.

3. **Mistake 3: Assuming displacement cannot be zero when distance is non-zero.**
   As seen in Example 2 and Example 4, distance can be $300\text{ m}$ or $88\text{ m}$ while displacement is strictly $0\text{ m}$. Whenever an object finishes at its starting location, displacement is always zero.

4. **Mistake 4: Adding perpendicular displacement vectors directly like scalars.**
   In Example 9, adding $30\text{ m}$ North and $40\text{ m}$ East as $30 + 40 = 70\text{ m}$ gives the distance, not the displacement! The displacement is the straight hypotenuse determined by the Pythagorean theorem: $\sqrt{30^2 + 40^2} = 50\text{ m}$. Remember that $|\text{Displacement}|$ can never exceed distance.

5. **Mistake 5: Omitting physical units or mixing mismatched units.**
   Writing *"Distance = 1400"* is physically meaningless without units. Always write **1,400 m**. When combining kilometers and meters, always convert to consistent SI units ($1\text{ km} = 1,000\text{ m}$) before calculating.

6. **Mistake 6: Interpreting a negative displacement as "less than zero distance."**
   A displacement of $-6\text{ m}$ signifies a physical magnitude of $6\text{ m}$ pointing in the negative coordinate direction. Distance itself can never be negative.

## Quick Recap

- **Distance** = total length of the actual path traversed. Scalar quantity. Always $\ge 0$. Never decreases over time. SI unit: $\text{m}$.
- **Displacement** = shortest straight-line vector directed from initial position to final position. Vector quantity. Can be positive, negative, or zero. SI unit: $\text{m}$.
- In one-dimensional straight-line motion, direction is represented using **$+$ and $-$** signs.
- For perpendicular 2D displacements, magnitude is found using Pythagoras: $\sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$.
- On a circular track of radius $r$:
  - **Half lap:** Distance $= \pi r$, Displacement $= 2r$ (diameter).
  - **Full lap:** Distance $= 2\pi r$, Displacement $= 0$.
- For any closed round trip, **Displacement $= 0$**, while **Distance $> 0$**.
- Universal Kinematic Rule: **$|\text{Displacement}| \le \text{Distance}$**. Equality holds strictly for straight-line motion without reversals.
- **Scalars** have magnitude only (mass, time, distance, speed). **Vectors** have magnitude and direction (displacement, velocity, force).

## Practice Questions

1. Define distance and displacement. State two key physical differences between them.
2. A student walks 8 m East and then 3 m West along a straight hallway. Choosing East as the positive direction, determine the total distance and the displacement.
3. Sophia walks from her home to school, located 600 m away along a straight street. After reaching the school, she walks 200 m back toward home and stops at a bookstore. Find her total distance and her displacement from home.
4. An athlete completes 2 full laps around a standard 400-meter oval track and stops at the exact starting mark. Find the total distance covered and the net displacement.
5. A circular park has a radius of 7 m. A jogger starts at point P on the perimeter and runs half a circle along the boundary. Find the distance and the displacement. (Use $\pi = \frac{22}{7}$). What are the values after a full lap?
6. A delivery van travels 12 m North and then 5 m East. Calculate the total distance traveled and the magnitude and direction of the resultant displacement.
7. A tennis ball is thrown vertically upward from ground level, reaches a maximum height of 5 m, and falls back to be caught at ground level. Determine the total distance and displacement. Using upward as positive, state the displacement with its algebraic sign.
8. A courier walks from the depot to an office (400 m East), and then to a bakery (300 m North of the office). Find the distance and displacement for this outbound journey. Then determine the total distance and displacement if the courier walks back to the depot along the same path.
9. Can the magnitude of displacement ever exceed the distance traveled by an object? Can an object have zero distance while having a non-zero displacement? Explain your reasoning.
10. State whether each statement is True or False:
    (a) Distance can have a negative numerical value.
    (b) Displacement can be zero even when total distance is non-zero.
    (c) Displacement is a scalar physical quantity.
    (d) Distance and displacement magnitude are equal for unidirectional straight-line motion.
    (e) Mass is a vector quantity.

### Answers

1. **Distance** is the total path length traversed (scalar, $\ge 0$, non-decreasing). **Displacement** is the shortest straight-line vector from start to finish (vector, can be $+$, $-$, or $0$). Key differences: (i) Distance is a scalar, displacement is a vector; (ii) Distance can never be negative or decrease, displacement can be negative or zero; (iii) Magnitude of displacement is always $\le$ distance.
2. East is $+$. Distance $= 8 + 3 = \mathbf{11\text{ m}}$. Displacement $= (+8) + (-3) = \mathbf{+5\text{ m}}$ (**5 m East**).
   Check: $8 - 3 = 5\text{ m}$ ✅.
3. Distance $= 600 + 200 = \mathbf{800\text{ m}}$. Taking school direction as $+$, Displacement $= +600\text{ m} - 200\text{ m} = \mathbf{+400\text{ m}}$ (**400 m toward the school**).
   Check: Ending position is $600 - 200 = 400\text{ m}$ from home ✅.
4. Distance $= 2 \times 400 = \mathbf{800\text{ m}}$. Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$ (ends at the start line).
5. Half lap: Distance $= \pi r = \frac{22}{7} \times 7 = \mathbf{22\text{ m}}$. Displacement $= 2r = 2 \times 7 = \mathbf{14\text{ m directed across the diameter}}$.
   Full lap: Distance $= 2\pi r = 2 \times 22 = \mathbf{44\text{ m}}$; Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$.
   Check: $14\text{ m} < 22\text{ m}$ ✅; $44 = 2 \times 22$ ✅.
6. Distance $= 12 + 5 = \mathbf{17\text{ m}}$. Displacement $= \sqrt{12^2 + 5^2} = \sqrt{144 + 25} = \sqrt{169} = \mathbf{13\text{ m directed Northeast}}$.
   Check: $13^2 = 169 = 144 + 25$ ✅; $13\text{ m} < 17\text{ m}$ ✅.
7. Distance $= 5\text{ m (up)} + 5\text{ m (down)} = \mathbf{10\text{ m}}$. Starts and ends at ground level $\implies \text{Displacement} = \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$ ($+5\text{ m} + (-5\text{ m}) = 0\text{ m}$).
8. Outbound journey: Distance $= 400 + 300 = \mathbf{700\text{ m}}$; Displacement $= \sqrt{400^2 + 300^2} = \sqrt{250,000} = \mathbf{500\text{ m directed Northeast}}$.
   Full trip (including return): Distance $= 400 + 300 + 300 + 400 = \mathbf{1,400\text{ m}}$; Displacement $= \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$.
   Check: $500^2 = 250,000 = 160,000 + 90,000$ ✅.
9. No. Displacement is the straight-line distance, which is the shortest possible path between two points; therefore, $|\text{Displacement}| \le \text{Distance}$ always. A distance of zero means no motion occurred, so the final position must be the initial position, making displacement strictly zero. Hence, zero distance with non-zero displacement is physically impossible.
10. (a) **False** ❌: Distance is scalar and strictly non-negative.
    (b) **True** ✅: True for any completed round trip.
    (c) **False** ❌: Displacement has direction, making it a vector.
    (d) **True** ✅: For motion in a straight line without reversing direction.
    (e) **False** ❌: Mass has magnitude only and is a scalar.

Mastering the difference between distance and displacement unlocks the rest of kinematics. Whenever analyzing a motion problem, always clarify: *Are we measuring total path length (distance), or the net directional shift from start to finish (displacement)?*
