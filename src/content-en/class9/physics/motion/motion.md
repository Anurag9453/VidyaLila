# Topic 1: Rest and Motion: Who Is Moving?

In the previous chapter, we learned **how physical quantities are measured**: units such as the meter, second, and kilogram, tools like rulers and stopwatches, and how to account for experimental uncertainty. Now, these tools come alive. In this chapter, we explore **how objects move**, translating motion into the precise language of meters and seconds. We begin with a deceptively simple question: **who is truly moving, and who is at rest?**

---

## Leo's Question on the Subway

Mr. Miller was riding the city subway with his nephew Leo to visit the science museum. Both were seated comfortably next to a window in the middle carriage. Directly opposite their row sat a commuter reading a paperback book.

The train was initially halted at the terminal platform. Through the window, Leo watched the tiled pillars and a coffee kiosk on the platform. A chime echoed over the intercom, the doors slid shut, and the train smoothly accelerated forward.

Leo suddenly exclaimed, "Look, Uncle! The subway platform is rushing backward!"

Mr. Miller smiled warmly. "The platform is anchored firmly into the ground, Leo. It is our train that is moving forward."

Leo nodded, but then turned his attention to the commuter sitting across from them. "Here is another puzzle. That gentleman looks completely still. He is not sliding forward or backward. But if our train is speeding down the tunnel, isn't he moving just as fast?"

Mr. Miller replied, "Brilliant observation! If you ask the station master standing on the platform, she will confirm that you, I, and that commuter are all hurtling past at high speed. Yet to you, he remains completely motionless. So, **what is the real truth?** Is the passenger moving, or is he at rest?"

Leo paused, genuinely puzzled. We will resolve this exact scenario by the end of this topic. First, let us build a rigorous physical understanding of what it means to be "in motion" versus "at rest."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Motion** | A continuous change in an object's position over time relative to a chosen reference point | A subway train cruising between stations |
| **Rest** | A state in which an object's position remains unchanged over time relative to a chosen reference point | A concrete pillar on the station platform |
| **Reference point (origin)** | A fixed object or location from which positions and distances are measured and compared | A station sign, a bus stop, a road marker |
| **Position** | The exact location of an object on a coordinate line relative to the origin, specified with magnitude and direction | +100 m east of the station entrance |
| **Relative** | Assessed or determined in comparison to something else (a frame of reference) | The train is in motion relative to the platform |
| **Linear motion** | Motion along a straight-line path | A car driving along a straight highway |
| **Circular motion** | Motion along a circular trajectory around a central point | A runner circling a curved track |
| **Rotational motion** | Motion in which a rigid body spins around an internal axis | A spinning ceiling fan |
| **Oscillatory motion** | Repetitive back-and-forth movement about a central equilibrium position | A swinging pendulum, a playground swing |
| **Uniform motion** | Motion in which an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time | Covering 10 m during every single second |
| **Non-uniform motion** | Motion in which an object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time | Covering 5 m in one second and 12 m in the next |

## Part A: Understanding Motion and Rest

### Recognizing Motion

Physics defines motion using a straightforward rule:

> If an object's **position changes over time**, the object is in **motion**.
> If an object's **position does not change over time**, the object is at **rest**.

However, whenever we say "position is changing," we must ask: changing relative to what? A position is never absolute; it is always described **relative to another chosen object**. That chosen object or location is called the **reference point** (or origin). Therefore, the essential first question in mechanics must always be: **"Relative to what?"**

### The Station Platform and the Train

Let us select a fixed pillar on the station platform as our reference point:
- The train's position relative to the pillar changes every second: the train is in **motion**.
- The pillar's position relative to itself (or the ground) remains identical: the pillar is at **rest**.

### Rest and Motion are Relative

Now, let us switch our reference point to Leo's seat inside the train carriage:
- The station pillar recedes farther away behind the train. Relative to the seat, the pillar is in **motion** (moving backward).
- The passenger sitting across the aisle maintains an exact distance of 1.5 m from Leo's seat at every second. Relative to Leo's seat, the passenger is at **rest**.

Thus, the exact same object can be in motion according to one reference point, yet simultaneously at rest according to another. This fundamental physical truth is expressed by stating that **rest and motion are relative**. There is no such thing as "absolute rest" or "absolute motion" in the universe—motion is meaningful only once an observer's reference frame is declared.

### Everyday Real-World Examples

| Situation | Reference Point | State (Rest or Motion) |
|---|---|---|
| A passenger sitting inside a moving bus | The bus seat | Rest |
| The same bus passenger | A tree beside the roadway | Motion |
| A roadside tree observed from a moving bus | A passenger on the bus | Motion (appears to move backward) |
| The roadside tree | The ground | Rest |
| A backpack held by a commuter in a moving bus | The bus driver | Rest |
| A station attendant on the platform | A passenger on an arriving train | Motion (appears to shift relative to the carriage) |

Notice that we casually call a tree or a lamp post "stationary" because we instinctively adopt the **ground** as our default reference frame. Unless stated otherwise in a physics problem, the Earth's surface serves as the universal reference frame.

## Part B: Describing Position: Origin and the Number Line

Describing a position is like giving someone a precise street address. Saying "I am standing past the bus stop" is incomplete: how far past? In which direction? On a straight track, we map positions using a standard **number line**:

1. Choose a reference landmark as the **origin** (zero point, $0\text{ m}$).
2. Designate one direction as **positive** ($+$) and the opposite direction as **negative** ($-$).
3. Measure distances in standard SI units: meters ($\text{m}$).

Imagine a straight boulevard with four landmarks. Let the central Bus Stop serve as our origin ($0\text{ m}$), with the direction toward the right designated as positive:

```
  -60 m        0          +40 m              +100 m
    S          B             L                  K
 ---+----------+-------------+------------------+--->  (+ direction)
  School     Bus Stop    Lamp-post           Kiosk
```

> **Figure 1.1** – Number line along a straight road: origin at Bus Stop (B). The School is located 60 m to the left (negative coordinate), while the Lamp-post (+40 m) and Kiosk (+100 m) lie to the right (positive coordinates).

Positions (relative to the Bus Stop):
- Bus Stop: **$0\text{ m}$**
- Lamp-post: **$+40\text{ m}$**
- Kiosk: **$+100\text{ m}$**
- School: **$-60\text{ m}$** (the negative sign indicates a position 60 m to the left of the origin)

### Changing the Origin Shifts the Position Coordinate

Now suppose we choose the **Lamp-post** as our new origin, retaining the rightward direction as positive. Every position coordinate shifts by subtracting $40\text{ m}$ ($\text{New Position} = \text{Old Position} - 40\text{ m}$):

- Bus Stop: $0 - 40 = \mathbf{-40\text{ m}}$
- Lamp-post: $40 - 40 = \mathbf{0\text{ m}}$
- Kiosk: $100 - 40 = \mathbf{+60\text{ m}}$
- School: $-60 - 40 = \mathbf{-100\text{ m}}$

**Consistency Check:** The physical separation between the Kiosk and the School must be independent of our coordinate choice.
- From the first origin: $100 - (-60) = 160\text{ m}$.
- From the second origin: $60 - (-100) = 160\text{ m}$ ✅.

The physical reality has not changed at all, yet the coordinate labels changed completely. Whenever you state a position, you must clearly specify the reference origin: *"located at $+100\text{ m}$ from the Bus Stop."*

## Part C: Types of Motion (Based on the Path)

Objects travel along many geometrical trajectories. Based on the shape of the path traced, we classify motion into several primary categories:

| Type of Motion | Defining Feature | Real-World Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear (Rectilinear)** | Path is a straight line | A car cruising along a straight freeway; a stone dropped vertically |
| **Circular** | Path is a circle around a fixed external center | An athlete sprinting around a circular running track |
| **Rotational** | The object turns around an internal axis passing through its own body | A spinning ceiling fan, a spinning globe |
| **Oscillatory / Vibratory** | Repetitive to-and-fro motion about a fixed equilibrium position | A playground swing, a grandfather clock pendulum |
| **Random** | Path exhibits irregular, continuously shifting directions without a predictable pattern | A fluttering butterfly, pollen grains jostling in water |

*(In rotational motion, every point on the object travels in a circle about the axis, but the object as a whole remains centered in place. In circular motion, the object as a whole travels along a circular trajectory through space.)*

### Let's Categorize Everyday Examples

| Example | Classification |
|---|---|
| A bicycle moving along a straight paved road | Linear |
| Blades of an active ceiling fan | Rotational |
| A child swinging on a playground swing | Oscillatory |
| A race car navigating a circular loop track | Circular |
| A baseball pitched straight into a catcher's glove | Linear |
| The pendulum of a grandfather clock ticking back and forth | Oscillatory |
| A toddler darting playfully through a crowded park | Random |
| A fallen leaf swirling chaotically in gusty wind | Random |
| A passenger sitting in a cabin of a giant Ferris wheel | Circular (revolving around the wheel's center) |
| A plucked acoustic guitar string | Oscillatory (vibratory) |

Note that complex everyday motions often combine multiple types simultaneously. For instance, the wheel of a moving bicycle rotates about its center axle (**rotational motion**) while simultaneously translating forward along the straight road (**linear motion**).

## Part D: Uniform and Non-Uniform Motion

Consider two electric cars, Car A and Car B, driving along a straight test track. We record the distance covered by each vehicle during consecutive **1-second intervals**:

| Time Interval | Distance Covered by Car A | Distance Covered by Car B |
|---|---|---|
| 0 s to 1 s | 10 m | 5 m |
| 1 s to 2 s | 10 m | 12 m |
| 2 s to 3 s | 10 m | 8 m |
| 3 s to 4 s | 10 m | 15 m |

- **Car A:** covers exactly 10 m during every single second. Covering equal distances in equal intervals of time is the hallmark of **uniform motion**.
- **Car B:** covers 5 m, then 12 m, then 8 m, then 15 m. Covering unequal distances in equal intervals of time defines **non-uniform motion**.

Now let us track the total cumulative distance measured from the starting line at each second:

| Elapsed Time | Cumulative Distance (Car A) | Cumulative Distance (Car B) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 s | 10 m | 5 m |
| 2 s | 20 m | 17 m |
| 3 s | 30 m | 25 m |
| 4 s | 40 m | 40 m |

**Consistency Check:**
- Car A: $10 + 10 + 10 + 10 = 40\text{ m}$ ✅.
- Car B: $5 + 12 + 8 + 15 = 40\text{ m}$ ✅.

Both cars traversed a total of $40\text{ m}$ over the $4\text{ s}$ span, but their internal dynamics were fundamentally different: Car A maintained a perfectly steady, constant pace, whereas Car B constantly sped up and slowed down.

In real life, strictly uniform motion is rare due to friction, air resistance, and traffic. A spacecraft drifting through deep space or the tip of a watch's second hand exhibits virtually uniform motion; a city bus navigating city streets exhibits non-uniform motion.

## Part E: A Brief Glimpse: Scalar and Vector Quantities

Some physical quantities are completely described by a single number along with its unit. For example, "a bottle contains 2 liters of water" or "the class period lasts 45 minutes"—nothing more is needed to understand the measurement. These are called **scalar quantities**.

However, other physical quantities remain fundamentally incomplete unless we also specify their **direction**. If someone tells you, "The treasure chest is buried 100 meters away from the bus stop," you cannot find it without knowing which way to walk: 100 meters north, south, east, or west? This is why we introduced positive ($+$) and negative ($-$) signs on our coordinate line. Quantities that require both magnitude and direction are called **vector quantities**, and we will explore them in detail in Topic 2.

## Resolving Leo's Subway Question

Let us return to Leo's question on the subway train. Suppose we set a pillar on the station platform as our origin ($0\text{ m}$), with the forward direction of train travel designated as positive. We record positions at successive moments:

| Time | Leo's Position (rel. to platform) | Commuter's Position (rel. to platform) | Commuter's Position (rel. to Leo) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 s | 0 m | 0.5 m | 0.5 m |
| 2 s | 3 m | 3.5 m | 0.5 m |
| 4 s | 10 m | 10.5 m | 0.5 m |

- **Relative to the platform:** Leo's coordinate shifts ($0\text{ m} \to 3\text{ m} \to 10\text{ m}$), and the commuter's coordinate shifts ($0.5\text{ m} \to 3.5\text{ m} \to 10.5\text{ m}$). Both individuals are in **motion**. This matches what the station guard observes.
- **Relative to Leo:** The commuter's coordinate remains permanently fixed at $0.5\text{ m}$ ($3.5 - 3 = 0.5\text{ m}$; $10.5 - 10 = 0.5\text{ m}$ ✅). To Leo, the commuter is strictly at **rest**.

Why did the platform appear to race backward to Leo? Because from the reference frame of the train, the platform's position coordinate decreases continuously in the negative direction. Both perspectives are physically valid:
- In the platform's frame: the train and all passengers are moving forward.
- In the train's frame: the platform is moving backward, while co-passengers are at rest.

As Mr. Miller explained: *"There is no single absolute description. Motion always depends on the frame from which you observe it."* The correct answer to whether the passenger is moving or stationary is: **he is in motion relative to the platform, but at rest relative to Leo.**

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Treating rest as absolute.**
   Claiming "A tree is completely at rest" is scientifically incomplete. A tree is at rest relative to the Earth, but relative to a passenger in a passing commuter train, the tree is moving backward at $60\text{ km/h}$. Always specify the reference point!
   - ❌ *"A tree is permanently at rest."*
   - ✅ *"A tree is at rest relative to the ground."*

2. **Mistake 2: Forgetting the reference point (origin).**
   Writing *"My position is $50\text{ m}$"* conveys no usable information without a known origin. Always write: *"My position is $+50\text{ m}$ from the main entrance."*

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing circular motion with oscillatory motion.**
   In circular motion, an object moves continuously around a closed circular trajectory in a single rotational sense. In oscillatory motion, an object reverses direction repeatedly, swinging back and forth across a central equilibrium point (like a playground swing). Both are periodic, but their geometry is entirely different.

4. **Mistake 4: Misinterpreting uniform motion.**
   Uniform motion requires covering **equal distances in equal intervals of time**, no matter how small those intervals are. If a vehicle covers $10\text{ m}$ during the first second and $12\text{ m}$ during the next, its motion is non-uniform, even if it traveled at high speed during both seconds.

5. **Mistake 5: Blurring the line between rotational and circular motion.**
   A spinning ceiling fan exhibits rotational motion because it spins about an axis passing through itself, remaining in the same place. A track athlete running along a circular curve exhibits circular motion because their entire body translates from one spatial location to another.

## Quick Recap

- When an object's position changes over time, it is in **motion**; when position remains constant, it is at **rest**.
- Position must always be stated relative to a defined **reference point (origin)**.
- **Rest and motion are relative**: an object can be moving according to one observer while stationary to another.
- On a straight line, position is tracked using a number line: an origin at zero, with positive ($+$) and negative ($-$) directions.
- Shifting the chosen origin alters the numerical position coordinates, but the physical separation between objects remains unchanged.
- Path-based classifications of motion include **linear, circular, rotational, oscillatory (vibratory), and random**.
- **Uniform motion** entails covering equal distances in equal intervals of time. **Non-uniform motion** involves covering unequal distances in equal intervals.
- Quantities that require only magnitude are **scalars**; quantities requiring both magnitude and direction are **vectors** (detailed in Topic 2).

What total distance did each bicycle travel over the 4 seconds?
7. State whether each statement is True or False, providing a clear physical reason:
   (a) A flagpole anchored into the ground is at rest for every observer in the universe.
   (b) The blades of a spinning ceiling fan exhibit rotational motion.
   (c) Circular motion and oscillatory motion are identical concepts.
8. A student walks across a classroom carrying a backpack. Identify:
   (a) one object that is at rest relative to the classroom floor but in motion relative to the student, and
   (b) one object that is at rest relative to the student but in motion relative to the floor.

### Answers

1. (a) **At rest**: the driver travels along with the bus; the passenger's position relative to the driver does not change.
   (b) **In motion**: the distance and position relative to the roadside tree change continuously.
   (c) **At rest**: both passengers share the train's motion; their mutual separation remains constant.
2. Relative to Liam, the coin is **at rest** (its position in his hand does not change). Relative to the platform observer, the coin is in **motion** (traveling forward at the train's speed).
3. (a) Library: **$+80\text{ m}$**; Coffee shop: **$-30\text{ m}$**.
   (b) Distance $= 80 - (-30) = 80 + 30 = \mathbf{110\text{ m}}$.
4. (a) New coordinate $= \text{Old coordinate} - 80\text{ m}$.
   - Bus stop: $0 - 80 = \mathbf{-80\text{ m}}$.
   - Coffee shop: $-30 - 80 = \mathbf{-110\text{ m}}$.
   (b) Check: Initial gap between bus stop and coffee shop $= 0 - (-30) = 30\text{ m}$. New gap $= -80 - (-110) = -80 + 110 = 30\text{ m}$ ✅.
5. (a) Oscillatory, (b) Rotational, (c) Linear, (d) Random, (e) Circular, (f) Oscillatory (vibratory).
6. Bicycle A is in **uniform motion** (covering equal distances of $12\text{ m}$ each second). Bicycle B is in **non-uniform motion**.
   - Bicycle A distance: $12 + 12 + 12 + 12 = \mathbf{48\text{ m}}$.
   - Bicycle B distance: $8 + 14 + 6 + 20 = \mathbf{48\text{ m}}$.
   Both covered the exact same total distance, but their patterns of motion differed.
7. (a) **False** ❌: The flagpole is at rest relative to the ground, but in motion relative to a passenger riding in a passing car or an astronaut orbiting the Earth.
   (b) **True** ✅: The fan spins about its fixed central axle.
   (c) **False** ❌: Circular motion follows a closed circular loop; oscillatory motion moves back and forth along the same path.
8. (a) Floor rest / student motion: The teacher's desk, chalkboard, or a classroom chair.
   (b) Student rest / floor motion: The backpack held in her hands (or her shoes, wristwatch).
