# Topic 4: Atmospheric Pressure

In the previous topic, we saw how enclosed liquids transmit pressure undiminished across all directions, enabling hydraulic machinery to perform heavy lifting. But liquids are not the only fluids: **air is also a fluid**. Air flows, adopts the shape of its environment, and exerts hydrostatic pressure. This leads to a fascinating question: we live our daily lives submerged at the bottom of a vast ocean of atmospheric air—does this air exert a physical force upon us? If so, why do we not feel crushed beneath it? In this topic, we explore the reality and magnitude of **atmospheric pressure**.

In this topic, unless stated otherwise, we use standard acceleration due to gravity **$g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$** to maintain precision with international barometric standards, density of mercury **$\rho_{\text{Hg}} = 13,600\text{ kg/m}^3$**, density of water **$\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$**, and standard atmospheric pressure **$P_{\text{atm}} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} \approx 101.3\text{ kPa}$**.

---

## Sophia's Bathroom Suction Hook

Sophia was helping tidy up the family bathroom, but there were no wall hooks left to hang a large wet bath towel. A visiting plumber, **Mr. Harrison**, pulled a small rubber **suction cup hook** from his toolbox. It consisted of a smooth, flexible rubber dome with a sturdy plastic hook mounted at its centre and a tiny release tab on its outer rim.

Mr. Harrison moistened the rubber rim slightly, placed it firmly against the smooth ceramic wall tile, and pushed the centre dome hard against the wall with the heel of his palm. Sophia watched as the rubber cup flattened out and sealed against the tile. 

"No glue, no drilling, no screws!" Sophia exclaimed, surprised. She hung her heavy, water-soaked bath towel onto the hook. The hook held the heavy towel securely without slipping a millimeter.

Then, Mr. Harrison pinched the tiny release tab at the rim and peeled it back slightly with his fingernail. *Whoosh!* With a sharp soft hiss, air rushed beneath the seal, and the hook instantly detached from the tile, tumbling into the sink along with the towel.

Sophia asked in wonder, "Mr. Harrison, what was holding that hook so firmly against the tile all that time? And why did pulling that tiny tab make it drop instantly?"

Mr. Harrison smiled, "There was never any adhesive, Sophia. The hook was held against the wall by the **unseen weight of our atmosphere**. Let us explore the physics of atmospheric pressure, and we will calculate the exact holding force of that suction cup!"

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Atmosphere** | The thick blanket of gases surrounding the Earth, held down by gravity | The envelope of air extending hundreds of kilometres above Earth |
| **Atmospheric pressure ($P_{\text{atm}}$)** | The force exerted per unit area by the weight of the air column above Earth's surface | Standard sea-level pressure is $1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ |
| **Standard atmosphere ($\text{atm}$)** | A standard unit of pressure representing average atmospheric pressure at sea level | $1\text{ atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 101.3\text{ kPa}$ |
| **Barometer** | An instrument designed specifically to measure atmospheric pressure | Torricellian mercury barometer |
| **$\text{mm of Hg}$ / $\text{cm of Hg}$** | A traditional unit of pressure defined by the vertical height of a supported mercury column | Standard pressure $= 760\text{ mm of Hg} = 76\text{ cm of Hg}$ |
| **Torr** | A unit of pressure named after Evangelista Torricelli: $1\text{ torr} = 1\text{ mm of Hg}$ | $760\text{ torr} = 1\text{ atm}$ |
| **Vacuum** | A space entirely devoid of matter and air molecules, where pressure is zero | The Torricellian space at the closed top of a mercury barometer |
| **Gauge pressure** | The excess pressure measured relative to ambient atmospheric pressure | Hydrostatic liquid pressure calculated by $P = h \rho g$ |
| **Absolute pressure** | Total actual pressure measured relative to a perfect vacuum ($P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{atm}} + P_{\text{gauge}}$) | Total pressure experienced by a deep-sea diver |

## Part A: The Weight of Air and Atmospheric Pressure

### Air Possesses Real Mass and Weight

Because air is invisible, people often assume it is weightless. However, air consists of billions of gas molecules—chiefly nitrogen ($78\%$), oxygen ($21\%$), and trace gases—each possessing mass. The Earth’s gravitational field pulls every one of these molecules downward. Consequently, **air has weight**.

Above our heads lies an atmospheric blanket extending roughly $100\text{ km}$ into space. The gravitational weight of this entire vertical column of air presses relentlessly downward onto everything at the Earth's surface. This distributed downward thrust per unit area is called **atmospheric pressure**.

Think of a tall stack of books: the book at the very bottom bears the cumulative gravitational weight of every book piled above it. We live at the very bottom of Earth's atmospheric ocean, bearing the weight of the overhead air column!

**Standard Sea-Level Atmospheric Pressure:**

> **$1\text{ atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 101,300\text{ Pa} \approx 101.3\text{ kPa}$**

### How Enormous is This Pressure?

A pressure of $1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ means that across **every single square metre** of horizontal surface, the atmosphere exerts a perpendicular downward thrust of:

$$F = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ N} \approx 100,000\text{ N}$$

This is equivalent to the gravitational weight of a **$10\text{-tonne}$ truck** resting on every square metre of ground!

### Why Are We Not Crushed by the Atmosphere?

If the air presses down with such overwhelming force, why do we not collapse under it? Two crucial physical factors protect us:

1. **Internal Fluid Pressure:** The internal fluids, blood vessels, and cells of our bodies are pressurized to match atmospheric pressure ($P_{\text{internal}} \approx P_{\text{external}}$). The outward fluid pressure inside our bodies perfectly balances the inward atmospheric push, producing zero net compressive force across our skin.
2. **Atmospheric Pressure Acts Equally in All Directions:** Just like liquid pressure in Topics 2 and 3, atmospheric pressure is isotropic. It presses upward, downward, and horizontally with equal magnitude. The air does not push our bodies in any one unbalanced direction.

Similarly, an open window in a house does not shatter because the atmosphere presses on the outdoor side and indoor side with identical pressure, resulting in zero net force.

## Part B: Simple Demonstrations of Atmospheric Pressure

### 1. Inverted Glass and Postcard Demonstration

Fill a drinking glass to the very brim with water so that the liquid slightly bulges over the rim. Place a smooth, stiff postcard or plastic card flat over the top, ensuring no air bubbles are trapped underneath. Gently hold the card against the rim with your palm, carefully invert the glass upside down over a sink, and remove your supporting hand.

```
       (Inverted Glass of Water)
          +---------------+
          |     WATER     |
          |     WATER     |
          |     WATER     |
          +===============+   <- Postcard stays sealed in place!
               ^  ^  ^
               │  │  │        Atmospheric pressure pushes upward:
                              1.013 x 10^5 Pa
```

> **Figure 4.1** – The postcard remains securely sealed against the rim of an inverted glass of water. Atmospheric air presses upward against the card with $101,300\text{ Pa}$, easily overpowering the small downward hydrostatic pressure ($h \rho g \approx 980\text{ Pa}$) of the $10\text{ cm}$ water column.

Why does the card not fall? The downward hydrostatic pressure exerted by a $10\text{ cm}$ ($0.10\text{ m}$) glass of water is:
$$P_{\text{water}} = h \rho g = 0.10\text{ m} \times 1000\text{ kg/m}^3 \times 9.8\text{ m/s}^2 = \mathbf{980\text{ Pa}}$$

Meanwhile, the ambient atmosphere presses **straight upward** against the underside of the card with roughly **$101,300\text{ Pa}$**! The upward atmospheric push is over $100$ times stronger than the downward water pressure, keeping the card pinned tightly against the glass.

### 2. The Suction Cup Hook (Sophia's Story)

When Mr. Harrison pushed the flexible rubber cup against the wall tile, the dome flattened, **squeezing out nearly all the trapped air**. 

When he released his hand, the elastic rubber tried to spring back slightly, creating a partial vacuum inside. With very few air molecules remaining inside, the internal pressure dropped significantly below atmospheric pressure ($P_{\text{inside}} \ll P_{\text{outside}}$). 

The full atmospheric pressure outside ($1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$) pressed relentlessly against the outer surface of the cup, clamping it tightly against the ceramic tile.

```
            Wall Hook
               │
          +----+----+
          | Rubber  |  <- Flexible rubber cup
       ---+   Cup   +---   Smooth ceramic tile
          | (Vacuum)|
          +---------+
        ->  High external atmospheric pressure  <-
```

> **Figure 4.2** – A suction cup sticks because external atmospheric pressure pushes it firmly against the tile, overpowering the low-pressure pocket trapped inside.

When Mr. Harrison lifted the edge tab, air immediately rushed into the pocket under the cup. The internal pressure instantly equalized with atmospheric pressure ($P_{\text{inside}} = P_{\text{outside}}$). With the net pressure difference eliminated, there was no longer any holding force, and the hook fell under the towel's weight!

### 3. Drinking Through a Drinking Straw

When you sip a drink through a straw, people often say they are "pulling the liquid up." In reality, **liquids cannot be pulled; they can only be pushed!**

- By expanding your lungs and mouth cavity, you remove air molecules from inside the straw, causing the internal air pressure in the straw to **drop below atmospheric pressure**.
- The open surface of the juice in the glass remains exposed to **full atmospheric pressure** ($1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$).
- This higher atmospheric pressure pushes down on the liquid in the glass, forcing the juice **upward through the straw and into your mouth**.

The atmosphere pushes the beverage up! If you drink from a sealed, rigid bottle where no air can enter, or if you puncture a hole in the straw above the liquid level, the drink will not rise because the pressure differential cannot be established.

### Other Everyday Demonstrations of Atmospheric Pressure

- **Medical Syringe:** Pulling the plunger back expands the chamber volume, lowering the internal pressure. Higher ambient atmospheric pressure pushes liquid medicine up through the needle into the barrel.
- **Eyedropper:** Squeezing the rubber bulb expels air; releasing it creates low internal pressure, allowing atmospheric pressure on the fluid surface to drive liquid into the glass pipette.
- **Rubber Sink Plunger:** Pushing down flattens the rubber bell, forcing out air and creating a partial vacuum that pulls water turbulence to dislodge clogs.
- **The Magdeburg Hemispheres (1654):** German physicist Otto von Guericke fitted two hollow bronze hemispheres together and evacuated the air from between them using a vacuum pump. Two teams of eight horses pulling in opposite directions could not tear the spheres apart, held purely by external atmospheric pressure!
- **Lift Pumps (Common Hand Pumps):** Lifting the pump piston creates a low-pressure chamber; atmospheric pressure pushes underground water up the pipe. (As shown in Example 5, a standard suction pump cannot draw water from depths greater than roughly $10.3\text{ metres}$.)

## Part C: The Mercury Barometer (Torricelli's Experiment)

In 1643, Italian mathematician and physicist **Evangelista Torricelli** invented the first reliable instrument to measure atmospheric pressure: the **mercury barometer**.

### Construction of a Simple Mercury Barometer

1. A clean, thick-walled glass tube roughly **$1\text{ metre}$ long**, closed at one end and open at the other, is filled completely to the brim with pure liquid mercury.
2. The experimenter covers the open end tightly with a gloved thumb, inverts the tube, and submerges the open end into an open trough (cistern) filled with mercury.
3. The thumb is carefully removed underwater.

Upon release, the mercury column drops slightly inside the tube, but then **halts completely at a vertical height of approximately $76\text{ cm}$ ($760\text{ mm}$)** above the free surface of the mercury in the trough!

```
         Closed end of glass tube
             +---+  --
             |   |   ^  Torricellian Vacuum
             |   |   v  (Zero pressure: P = 0)
      --     |===|  <-- Top of mercury column
       ^     | M |
  76   |     | E |
  cm   |     | R |      Atmospheric pressure
       v   v | C | v    presses down on open trough
      --  +--+---+--+  <-- Free surface of trough mercury
          |  Mercury |
          +----------+
```

> **Figure 4.3** – The Torricellian mercury barometer: atmospheric pressure pressing down on the open mercury in the trough supports a vertical column of mercury exactly $76\text{ cm}$ tall. The space above the column is a near-perfect vacuum.

The empty space formed above the mercury column in the sealed tube is called the **Torricellian vacuum**. Because there is no air in this space, it exerts zero downward pressure on top of the column ($P \approx 0$).

### Why Does the Mercury Column Stand at $76\text{ cm}$?

The open mercury surface in the trough is exposed directly to the atmosphere. Atmospheric pressure presses downward on the mercury in the trough, transmitting hydrostatic force through the liquid to push mercury upward into the tube. Meanwhile, the weight of the mercury column pushes downward. 

Equilibrium is reached when the downward hydrostatic pressure of the mercury column equals the atmospheric pressure:

> **$P_{\text{atm}} = P_{\text{column}} = h \rho g$**

Using $\rho_{\text{Hg}} = 13,600\text{ kg/m}^3$, $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$, and $h = 0.76\text{ m}$:

$$P_{\text{atm}} = (0.76\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{101,292.8\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}}$$

Therefore, standard atmospheric pressure at sea level is precisely equivalent to:

> **$1\text{ atm} = 76\text{ cm of Hg} = 760\text{ mm of Hg} = 760\text{ torr} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$**

### Important Units of Pressure

- **$\text{mm of Hg}$ / $\text{cm of Hg}$:** Height of a supported mercury column.
- **$\text{Torr}$:** $1\text{ torr} = 1\text{ mm of Hg}$. Thus, $760\text{ torr} = 1\text{ atm}$.
- **Converting $1\text{ mm of Hg}$ to Pascals:**
  $$P = (0.001\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{133.28\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{133.3\text{ Pa}}$$

**Verification:** $760 \times 133.3\text{ Pa} = 101,308\text{ Pa} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Does Column Height Depend on Tube Width or Tilt?

**No.** The vertical height $h$ remains strictly $76\text{ cm}$:
- **Varying Tube Diameter:** Whether the tube is wide or narrow, $P = h \rho g$ depends only on vertical height $h$, not area. The column remains at $76\text{ cm}$.
- **Tilting the Tube:** When the tube is tilted at an angle, the mercury flows further along the bore, increasing the slanted length of the liquid column. However, the **vertical height** measured perpendicular to the trough surface remains strictly **$76\text{ cm}$**!

### Why Use Mercury Instead of Water in a Barometer?

Mercury is ideal for barometers because:
1. **Exceptional Density:** Mercury is $13.6$ times denser than water ($\rho = 13,600\text{ kg/m}^3$). A mercury barometer requires a tube of manageable length ($\sim 1\text{ m}$). A water barometer would require an unmanageable tube over **$10.3\text{ metres}$ tall** (Example 5)!
2. **Negligible Vapour Pressure:** Liquid mercury evaporates very slowly at room temperature, leaving the Torricellian vacuum virtually pure. Water evaporates rapidly, filling the space with water vapour that pushes downward and distorts the reading.
3. **Non-Wetting Meniscus:** Mercury does not stick to glass, forming a crisp, convex meniscus that is easy to read accurately.

### The Aneroid Barometer

An **aneroid barometer** is a completely liquid-free ("aneroid" means *without liquid*) mechanical device. It contains a flexible corrugated metal capsule sealed with a partial internal vacuum. 

When external atmospheric pressure rises, the thin corrugated capsule compresses inward; when atmospheric pressure drops, an internal spring expands the capsule. A sensitive gear-and-lever linkage magnifies these minute deflections, rotating a needle across a calibrated dial. 

Because aneroid barometers are compact, rugged, and non-toxic, they are widely used in **weather forecasting stations** and as **altimeters** in aircraft.

### How Atmospheric Pressure Changes with Altitude

As you climb higher in altitude (such as driving up a mountain), the column of air resting above you becomes shorter and less dense. Consequently, **atmospheric pressure decreases steadily with increasing altitude**.

At an altitude of approximately **$5.5\text{ km}$ above sea level, atmospheric pressure drops to roughly half of its sea-level value** ($\sim 0.5\text{ atm}$). Aircraft altimeters measure this pressure decrease to indicate flight altitude.

Reduced atmospheric pressure at high altitudes causes several observable phenomena:
- **Difficulty breathing:** The partial pressure of oxygen is significantly reduced.
- **Nosebleeds:** High internal blood pressure pushes against lowered external air pressure, rupturing fragile nasal capillaries.
- **Lower boiling point of water:** Food takes longer to cook in open pots on mountains because water boils below $100^\circ\text{C}$ (covered in the Heat and Temperature chapter).

## Part D: Solved Examples

*(In all examples below, we take $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$, $\rho_{\text{Hg}} = 13,600\text{ kg/m}^3$, $\rho_{\text{water}} = 1000\text{ kg/m}^3$, and $P_{\text{atm}} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} \approx 101,300\text{ Pa}$.)*

### Example 1: Converting $76\text{ cm of Hg}$ into Pascals

Given $h = 76\text{ cm} = 0.76\text{ m}$, $\rho = 13,600\text{ kg/m}^3$, and $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$:

- $P = h \rho g = (0.76\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2)$
- $0.76 \times 13,600 = 10,336\text{ kg/m}^2$; $10,336 \times 9.8 = \mathbf{101,292.8\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}}$

**Verification:**  
$\frac{101,293\text{ Pa}}{133.3\text{ Pa/mm}} \approx 760\text{ mm of Hg} = 76\text{ cm of Hg}$ ✅

### Example 2: Atmospheric Force Acting on a $1\text{ m}^2$ Surface

Calculate the atmospheric thrust pressing down on a $1\text{ m}^2$ area at sea level ($P = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$).

- $F = P \times A = (1.013 \times 10^5\text{ N/m}^2) \times (1\text{ m}^2) = \mathbf{1.013 \times 10^5\text{ N}} \approx 101,300\text{ N}$

**Verification:** $F \div A = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ N} \div 1\text{ m}^2 = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Example 3: Atmospheric Force on a Tabletop

A study table measures $0.5\text{ m} \times 0.4\text{ m}$. Calculate the downward atmospheric force on the tabletop. Why does the table not collapse under this load?

- Area: $A = 0.5\text{ m} \times 0.4\text{ m} = 0.2\text{ m}^2$
- Downward thrust: $F = P \times A = (101,300\text{ N/m}^2) \times (0.2\text{ m}^2) = \mathbf{20,260\text{ N}} \approx 2 \times 10^4\text{ N}$

A force of $20,260\text{ N}$ is equivalent to the weight of a $2067\text{ kg}$ vehicle ($20,260 \div 9.8 = 2067\text{ kg}$)!  
**Why does the table not shatter?**  
The room air circulates underneath the table, pressing **upward** against the underside with an equal and opposite force of $20,260\text{ N}$.  
$$\text{Net force} = 20,260\text{ N (downward)} - 20,260\text{ N (upward)} = \mathbf{0\text{ N}}$$

**Verification:** $20,260\text{ N} \div 0.2\text{ m}^2 = 101,300\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Example 4: Atmospheric Force on a Human Palm

A student holds out their flat hand with a palm surface area of $A = 0.01\text{ m}^2$ ($100\text{ cm}^2$). Calculate the downward atmospheric force bearing on the hand.

- $F = P \times A = (101,300\text{ N/m}^2) \times (0.01\text{ m}^2) = \mathbf{1013\text{ N}}$

This force equals the gravitational weight of a $103\text{ kg}$ mass ($1013 \div 9.8 = 103\text{ kg}$)! The student does not feel weighed down because air presses upward against the back of the hand with the exact same $1013\text{ N}$, and internal blood and tissue pressure balance the lateral forces.

**Verification:** $1013\text{ N} \div 0.01\text{ m}^2 = 101,300\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Example 5: Height of a Water Barometer Column

What vertical height of water column can standard atmospheric pressure ($1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$) support?

- Using $P = h \rho g \implies h = \frac{P}{\rho g}$
- $h = \frac{101,300\text{ Pa}}{(1000\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2)} = \frac{101,300}{9800} = \mathbf{10.34\text{ m}} \approx \mathbf{10.3\text{ m}}$

**Verification:**  
$h \rho g = (10.34\text{ m}) \times (1000\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = 101,332\text{ Pa} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ ✅  
This explains why constructing a water barometer requires a four-storey glass pipe! It also proves that a surface suction pump cannot lift water from a well deeper than roughly $10.3\text{ metres}$.

### Example 6: Barometer Reading of $74\text{ cm of Hg}$ on a Hill

A mercury barometer at an elevated campsite reads $74\text{ cm of Hg}$. Calculate the atmospheric pressure in pascals and in atmospheres.

- $h = 74\text{ cm} = 0.74\text{ m}$
- $P = h \rho g = (0.74\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{98,627\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{9.86 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}}$
- In atmospheres: $\frac{98,627\text{ Pa}}{101,300\text{ Pa/atm}} = \mathbf{0.974\text{ atm}}$  
  *(Alternatively: $\frac{74\text{ cm}}{76\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{0.974\text{ atm}}$)*

**Verification:** $0.974\text{ atm} \times 101,300\text{ Pa/atm} = 98,666\text{ Pa} \approx 98,627\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Example 7: An Oil Barometer ($\rho = 800\text{ kg/m}^3$)

Suppose a barometer is filled with lubricating oil of density $800\text{ kg/m}^3$ (neglecting oil vapour). What height of oil column would standard atmospheric pressure support?

- $h = \frac{P}{\rho g} = \frac{101,300\text{ Pa}}{(800\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2)} = \frac{101,300}{7840} = \mathbf{12.92\text{ m}} \approx \mathbf{12.9\text{ m}}$

**Verification:**  
$(12.92\text{ m}) \times 800 \times 9.8 = 101,292.8\text{ Pa} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ ✅  
Because oil is lighter than water, it requires an even taller column ($12.9\text{ m}$)! Low fluid density always requires a taller barometer column.

### Example 8: Solving Sophia's Story: The Holding Force of the Suction Cup

The suction cup of Sophia's bathroom hook has a surface area $A = 20\text{ cm}^2$.  
Convert to SI units: $A = 20 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = 2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$.  
The external atmospheric pressure is $P_{\text{out}} = 1.0 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$. The air inside the cup was partially evacuated, leaving an internal pressure of $P_{\text{in}} = 0.5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$. Find the net holding force pressing the cup to the tile.

- Net pressure: $\Delta P = P_{\text{out}} - P_{\text{in}} = 1.0 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} - 0.5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 0.5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 5 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}$
- Net holding force:  
  $$F = \Delta P \times A = (5 \times 10^4\text{ N/m}^2) \times (2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2) = \mathbf{100\text{ N}}$$

**Verification:**  
A holding force of $100\text{ N}$ corresponds to supporting a mass of $m = \frac{100\text{ N}}{9.8\text{ m/s}^2} \approx 10.2\text{ kg}$! This is why the suction hook held Sophia's heavy wet towel effortlessly. When Mr. Harrison lifted the tab, air rushed in, making $P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}$, so $\Delta P = 0\text{ Pa}$, $F_{\text{net}} = 0\text{ N}$, and the hook dropped immediately!

### Example 9: Gauge vs. Absolute Pressure at $10\text{ m}$ Water Depth

Calculate the gauge pressure and absolute pressure experienced by a scuba diver swimming at a depth of $10\text{ m}$ in a freshwater lake.

- **Gauge pressure (water weight only):**  
  $$P_{\text{gauge}} = h \rho g = (10\text{ m}) \times (1000\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{98,000\text{ Pa}} = 9.8 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}$$

- **Absolute pressure (total combined pressure):**  
  $$P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{atm}} + P_{\text{gauge}} = 101,300\text{ Pa} + 98,000\text{ Pa} = \mathbf{199,300\text{ Pa}} \approx 1.993 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$$

In atmospheres: $\frac{199,300\text{ Pa}}{101,300\text{ Pa/atm}} = \mathbf{1.97\text{ atm}} \approx \mathbf{2\text{ atm}}$.

**Verification:**  
$101,300 + 98,000 = 199,300\text{ Pa}$ ✅  
At a depth of $10\text{ m}$, a diver experiences roughly $2\text{ atmospheres}$ of pressure: $1\text{ atm}$ from the Earth's atmosphere overhead, and $1\text{ atm}$ from the $10\text{ m}$ column of water!

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that "suction" is an active attractive pull.**  
   Physics recognizes no attractive force called "suction." When you drink through a straw or use a suction cup, you simply reduce the internal air pressure in a localized pocket; the **ambient external atmosphere does the pushing**!

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming atmospheric pressure pushes only downward.**  
   Atmospheric pressure acts in **all directions** (omnidirectionally). The upward push of atmospheric air is what prevents the postcard from falling off the inverted water glass (Figure 4.1).

3. **Mistake 3: Believing that a wider or tilted barometer tube alters column height.**  
   Hydrostatic pressure depends strictly on the vertical depth $h$ ($P = h \rho g$). Tilting the barometer tube causes mercury to fill a greater slanted length along the bore, but its **vertical height above the reservoir surface remains strictly $76\text{ cm}$**.

4. **Mistake 4: Confusing $760\text{ mm}$ with $76\text{ m}$.**  
   Remember: $760\text{ mm} = 76\text{ cm} = 0.76\text{ m}$. Plugging $76$ directly into $P = h \rho g$ without converting to metres yields an answer that is $100$ times too large!

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming air has no mass or weight.**  
   Air is matter with finite density ($\sim 1.2\text{ kg/m}^3$ at sea level). A typical classroom contains over $150\text{ kg}$ of air!

6. **Mistake 6: Mixing $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ and $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ within the same calculation.**  
   Choose your value of $g$ consistently. When working with mercury barometer calculations, $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ is standard for generating the precise $1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ value.

7. **Mistake 7: Expecting a table to break under thousands of newtons of atmospheric force.**  
   Air freely circulates beneath the tabletop, pushing upward with the exact same magnitude as the air pushing down from above. The net structural force is zero.

## Quick Recap

- Air is a fluid possessing mass and weight. Earth's gravitational pull on the overhead air blanket creates **atmospheric pressure**.
- **Standard sea-level atmospheric pressure:**  
  **$1\text{ atm} = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} \approx 101.3\text{ kPa} = 76\text{ cm of Hg} = 760\text{ mm of Hg} = 760\text{ torr}$**.
- We are not crushed by the atmosphere because internal blood and tissue pressures equalize external pressure, and atmospheric pressure acts equally in all directions.
- Everyday phenomena (suction cups, drinking straws, syringes, eyedroppers, sink plungers) operate by lowering internal air pressure so that **higher external atmospheric pressure pushes the fluid or object**.
- **Torricelli's Mercury Barometer:** A $1\text{ m}$ glass tube inverted in a mercury trough supports a column of mercury $76\text{ cm}$ tall, topped by a vacuum space ($P = 0$).  
  $P_{\text{atm}} = h \rho g = (0.76\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$.
- Column height is strictly vertical and independent of tube bore or angle of tilt.
- A water barometer would require an impractical height of **$10.34\text{ m}$** due to water's low density ($1000\text{ kg/m}^3$).
- **Aneroid Barometers** contain no liquid; an evacuated corrugated metal chamber expands and contracts with air pressure changes to indicate weather forecasts and aircraft altitude.
- Atmospheric pressure decreases steadily with increasing altitude (halving at $\sim 5.5\text{ km}$), causing lower boiling points and breathing challenges.
- **Absolute pressure = Atmospheric pressure + Gauge pressure** ($P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{atm}} + h \rho g$).

### Answers

1. $h = 70\text{ cm} = 0.70\text{ m}$.  
   $P = h \rho g = (0.70\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{93,296\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{9.33 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}}$.  
   *Check:* $93,296\text{ Pa} \div 133.3\text{ Pa/mm} = 699.9\text{ mm} = 70\text{ cm}$ ✅

2. Area $A = 2\text{ m} \times 1\text{ m} = 2\text{ m}^2$.  
   $F = P \times A = (1.013 \times 10^5\text{ N/m}^2) \times (2\text{ m}^2) = \mathbf{2.026 \times 10^5\text{ N}} = \mathbf{202,600\text{ N}}$.  
   The door remains stationary because air inside the house presses outward against the door with an identical force of $202,600\text{ N}$, yielding a net force of $0\text{ N}$.  
   *Check:* $202,600\text{ N} \div 2\text{ m}^2 = 101,300\text{ Pa}$ ✅

3. $h = 38\text{ cm} = 0.38\text{ m}$.  
   $P = (0.38\text{ m}) \times (13,600\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{50,646\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{5.06 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}}$.  
   Fraction of $1\text{ atm}$: $\frac{38\text{ cm}}{76\text{ cm}} = \mathbf{0.5\text{ (half an atmosphere)}}$.  
   *Check:* $0.5 \times 101,300\text{ Pa} = 50,650\text{ Pa} \approx 50,646\text{ Pa}$ ✅

4. $A = 50\text{ cm}^2 = 50 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = 5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$.  
   $\Delta P = 1.0 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} - 0.2 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 0.8 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 8 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}$.  
   $F = \Delta P \times A = (8 \times 10^4\text{ N/m}^2) \times (5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2) = \mathbf{400\text{ N}}$.  
   *Check:* $400\text{ N} \div (5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2) = 8 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}$ ✅

5. $h = \frac{P}{\rho g} = \frac{101,300\text{ Pa}}{(1200\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2)} = \frac{101,300}{11,760} = \mathbf{8.61\text{ m}}$.  
   *Check:* $(8.61\text{ m}) \times 1200 \times 9.8 = 101,254\text{ Pa} \approx 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$ ✅

6. Gauge pressure: $P_{\text{gauge}} = h \rho g = (5\text{ m}) \times (1000\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2) = \mathbf{49,000\text{ Pa}}$.  
   Absolute pressure: $P_{\text{abs}} = 101,300\text{ Pa} + 49,000\text{ Pa} = \mathbf{150,300\text{ Pa}} \approx \mathbf{1.50 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}}$ ($\sim 1.48\text{ atm}$).  
   *Check:* $150,300 \div 101,300 = 1.48\text{ atm}$ ✅

7. The vertical height remains strictly **$76\text{ cm}$**. Although tilting increases the slanted length along the glass tube to $95\text{ cm}$, liquid hydrostatic pressure depends exclusively on true vertical height ($h$). Atmospheric pressure can support only $76\text{ cm}$ of vertical mercury head.  
   *Check:* Slanted length ($95\text{ cm}$) is naturally greater than vertical height ($76\text{ cm}$) in a tilted cylinder ✅

8. Inhaling through a straw removes air molecules, lowering the internal air pressure in the straw and mouth. Meanwhile, full ambient atmospheric pressure presses down on the liquid surface in the glass, forcing the juice upward into the straw. The **atmosphere** pushes the liquid upward.

9. (a) **False** ❌. Air has mass and gravitational weight; the weight of overhead air creates atmospheric pressure.  
   (b) **False** ❌. Atmospheric pressure is isotropic, acting equally in all directions (including upward).  
   (c) **False** ❌. Hydrostatic pressure $P = h \rho g$ is independent of container diameter.  
   (d) **True** ✅. By definition, standard atmospheric pressure supports $760\text{ mm}$ of mercury.

10. Mercury is preferred because of its immense density ($13,600\text{ kg/m}^3$), which allows a manageable column of only $0.76\text{ m}$ ($76\text{ cm}$).  
    For water:  
    $$h = \frac{P}{\rho g} = \frac{101,300\text{ Pa}}{(1000\text{ kg/m}^3) \times (9.8\text{ m/s}^2)} = \mathbf{10.34\text{ m}}$$  
    A water barometer would require an unwieldy tube exceeding $10.3\text{ metres}$ (over $33\text{ feet}$) in height, and water vapour would disrupt the vacuum.  
    *Check:* $0.76\text{ m} \times 13.6 = 10.34\text{ m}$ ✅
