# Topic 1: Thrust and Pressure

In the Gravitation chapter, we saw that the Earth pulls every object downward, a force we call **weight** ($W = m \times g$). Now, let us consider a new question: when an object rests on a surface, it pushes against that surface. But how intense is this pushing effect? As we will discover, it does not depend solely on weight; it depends fundamentally on **the area over which that force is spread**. This chapter is titled **Pressure and Fluids**, and in this opening topic, we will uncover the crucial difference between **force** and **pressure**.

---

## Chef Harrison's Sharp Knife and Maya's Question

During the holidays, Maya was spending the afternoon in the kitchen watching Chef Harrison prepare fresh salads. Chef Harrison picked up a **razor-sharp chef's knife** and pressed it gently against a ripe tomato: *slice!* The tomato fell cleanly into two halves without any struggle.

Next, he picked up an old, **blunt knife**. This time, he had to push down with considerable effort, yet the knife barely broke the skin; instead, it simply squashed the tomato, causing juice to squirt out across the board.

Maya watched in fascination and asked, "Mr. Harrison, you pushed down with your hand in both cases, yet the sharp knife sliced the tomato effortlessly while the blunt one only squashed it. Why did that happen?"

Chef Harrison smiled, "The downward push—the force—might be the same, Maya, but the real secret lies in **how small an area** that force is concentrated on. Let us examine the numbers behind this together as we dive into today's physics lesson."

---

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Fluid** | Any substance that **can flow**: both liquids and gases are fluids | Water, oil, air, helium |
| **Thrust** | The total force acting **perpendicularly (at 90°)** to a surface. Unit: N | The downward weight of a book resting on a table |
| **Pressure ($P$)** | The thrust acting per **unit area ($1\text{ m}^2$)**: $P = F \div A$ | A thrust of $100\text{ N}$ distributed over $2\text{ m}^2$ yields $50\text{ Pa}$ |
| **Pascal ($\text{Pa}$)** | The SI unit of pressure: $1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ N/m}^2$ | A force of $1\text{ N}$ distributed over an area of $1\text{ m}^2$ |
| **kPa** | Kilopascal: $1\text{ kPa} = 1000\text{ Pa}$ | $15\text{ kPa} = 15,000\text{ Pa}$ |
| **bar** | A large unit of pressure: $1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 100\text{ kPa}$ | Car tyre pressures are often measured in bars |
| **Area ($A$)** | The surface area over which a force acts: SI unit $\text{m}^2$ | $20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 200\text{ cm}^2 = 0.02\text{ m}^2$ |

## Part A: What Are Fluids, Thrust, and Pressure?

### What is a Fluid?

Substances that **flow**—meaning they have no fixed shape and take the shape of whatever container holds them—are called **fluids**. Fluids come in two states of matter:

- **Liquids:** water, milk, cooking oil, petrol.
- **Gases:** air, oxygen, nitrogen, carbon dioxide.

In this chapter, we will study how fluids exert forces and how external forces act upon them. To begin, however, we start with solids, where the concepts of thrust and contact area are easiest to visualize.

### Thrust: Perpendicular Force on a Surface

When a force acts on a surface **perpendicularly (at a $90^\circ$ right angle)**, it is called **thrust**. Because thrust is simply a directional force, its SI unit is the **newton ($\text{N}$)**.

```
        Thrust (downward push)
               vvv
        +---------------+
        |    TOMATO     |
  ======+===============+======  <- Chopping board (surface)
```

> **Figure 1.1** – A knife or hand pushes perpendicularly ($90^\circ$) onto a tomato against a board. This perpendicular force is thrust.

Remember: if a force strikes a surface at an angle, only the perpendicular component that acts directly into the surface constitutes thrust. In Class 9 physics, we deal almost exclusively with forces acting perpendicular to the surface.

### When an Object's Weight Acts as Thrust

When a solid object rests on a **flat horizontal surface**, its entire **weight** presses downward against the surface as thrust. From Gravitation Topic 5, we know the weight formula:

> **$F = W = m \times g$**

Throughout this chapter, for clear and convenient calculations, we use $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ unless specified otherwise.

For example, a $2\text{ kg}$ block resting on a desk exerts a downward thrust of:
$$W = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 20\text{ N}$$

### Pressure: Thrust per Unit Area

Thrust alone does not tell the full story. The crucial factor is **how widely that thrust is distributed**. For this reason, we define **pressure**:

> **$\text{Pressure} = \frac{\text{Thrust}}{\text{Area}}$**  
>  
> **$P = \frac{F}{A}$**

**SI unit:** The unit of force $F$ is newtons ($\text{N}$), and the unit of area $A$ is square metres ($\text{m}^2$). Therefore:

> The unit of $P = \frac{\text{N}}{\text{m}^2} = \text{N/m}^2$, which is named the **pascal ($\text{Pa}$)**.  
>  
> **$1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ N/m}^2$**

*(Named in honour of the French mathematician and physicist Blaise Pascal.)*

**Physical meaning:** A pressure of $1\text{ Pa}$ means that a perpendicular force of $1\text{ N}$ is evenly distributed over an area of $1\text{ m}^2$. Because $1\text{ Pa}$ is an exceptionally gentle pressure, we frequently use larger practical units:

| Unit | Value in Pascals | Typical Context |
|---|---|---|
| $1\text{ Pa}$ | $1\text{ N/m}^2$ | Extremely delicate pressures (e.g., a sheet of paper on a desk) |
| $1\text{ kPa}$ | $1000\text{ Pa}$ | Everyday human pressures (e.g., standing on the floor) |
| $1\text{ bar}$ | $10^5\text{ Pa} = 100\text{ kPa}$ | Atmospheric pressure and vehicle tyre pressures |

### Always Convert Area into $\text{m}^2$

To calculate pressure in pascals ($\text{Pa}$), force must be in newtons ($\text{N}$) and area must strictly be in **square metres ($\text{m}^2$)**. Converting from $\text{cm}^2$ or $\text{mm}^2$ is essential:

> $1\text{ cm} = 10^{-2}\text{ m} \implies \mathbf{1\text{ cm}^2 = (10^{-2}\text{ m})^2 = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = \frac{1}{10,000}\text{ m}^2}$  
>  
> $1\text{ mm} = 10^{-3}\text{ m} \implies \mathbf{1\text{ mm}^2 = (10^{-3}\text{ m})^2 = 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2 = \frac{1}{1,000,000}\text{ m}^2}$

Keep this rule firmly in mind: converting $\text{cm}^2$ to $\text{m}^2$ requires dividing by **$10,000$, not $100$**!

## Part B: Same Force, Different Pressure: The Power of Area

The formula $P = \frac{F}{A}$ reveals two fundamental proportionalities:

- When **area decreases**, pressure **increases** (even if the force remains unchanged).
- When **area increases**, pressure **decreases**.

Think of sharing a chocolate bar between $2$ people versus $20$ people: the more people sharing the same total amount, the smaller the portion each person receives.

### How Pressure Can Be Modified

| Modification | Force $F$ | Area $A$ | Pressure $P = \frac{F}{A}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Increase force, keep area constant | Increases | Constant | **Increases** |
| Decrease force, keep area constant | Decreases | Constant | **Decreases** |
| Keep force constant, decrease area | Constant | Decreases | **Increases** |
| Keep force constant, increase area | Constant | Increases | **Decreases** |

### When We Need HIGH Pressure: Minimise Area

- **Sharp knives, scissors, axes:** The cutting edge is exceptionally thin, making contact area minuscule. Even a modest force generates enormous pressure, slicing through materials cleanly.
- **Nails, pins, needles, thumbtacks:** The pointed tip has a microscopic area, allowing a small thumb push or hammer tap to create huge pressure, piercing wood or fabric easily.
- **High-heeled stiletto shoes:** A person's weight concentrated onto a tiny heel tip creates immense pressure on the ground, potentially denting wooden floors.

### When We Need LOW Pressure: Maximise Area

- **Wide school bag straps:** The weight of books is spread over a broad shoulder area, significantly reducing pressure and preventing shoulder pain.
- **Wide tractor tyres:** Large tyre surface areas prevent heavy tractors from sinking into soft agricultural soil.
- **Bulldozer and military tank tracks (caterpillar treads):** The massive vehicle weight is distributed over continuous tracks of huge area.
- **Broad camel hooves:** Spreading a camel's weight across large, padded footpads stops it from sinking into desert sand.
- **Snowshoes and skis:** By dramatically increasing the contact area, a person can walk or glide across soft snow without sinking into it.
- **Flat-soled footwear:** Flat shoes spread body weight over a large area, exerting much lower pressure than high heels.

```
   View from below (Contact area):         Same applied downward weight W:

   Pointed nail tip:   .                    Enormous pressure (penetrates wood)
   Flat nail head:     [====]               Low pressure (comfortable to strike)
```

> **Figure 1.2** – A nail's tip concentrates force over a tiny point (enormous pressure), whereas its head spreads force over a wide area (low pressure). That is why we hit the wide head with a hammer so the sharp tip drives smoothly into the wall.

**Crucial insight:** Broadening the base of an object does **not** reduce its weight. A tractor or camel still exerts the exact same downward gravitational force; that force is simply **distributed over a larger area**, which results in **lower pressure**.

## Part C: Solved Examples

In all calculations below, we use $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$.

### Example 1: The Kitchen Story (Sharp Knife vs. Blunt Knife)

Chef Harrison applies a downward thrust of $20\text{ N}$ to both knives. The sharp knife edge measures $0.5\text{ mm} \times 10\text{ cm}$, while the blunt knife edge measures $2\text{ mm} \times 10\text{ cm}$.

**Sharp knife contact area ($A_1$):**
- $0.5\text{ mm} = 0.5 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m} = 5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}$
- $10\text{ cm} = 0.1\text{ m}$
- $A_1 = (5 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}) \times 0.1\text{ m} = \mathbf{5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2}$

**Sharp knife pressure ($P_1$):**
- $P_1 = \frac{F}{A_1} = \frac{20\text{ N}}{5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{4 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}} = 400\text{ kPa}$

**Blunt knife contact area ($A_2$):**
- $2\text{ mm} = 2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}$
- $A_2 = (2 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}) \times 0.1\text{ m} = \mathbf{2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2}$

**Blunt knife pressure ($P_2$):**
- $P_2 = \frac{F}{A_2} = \frac{20\text{ N}}{2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{10^5\text{ Pa}} = 100\text{ kPa}$

**Comparison:** $\frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{4 \times 10^5}{10^5} = \mathbf{4}$. The sharp knife produces **$4$ times as much pressure** because its contact area is $4$ times smaller ($\frac{A_2}{A_1} = \frac{2 \times 10^{-4}}{5 \times 10^{-5}} = 4$).

**Verification:**  
$P_1 \times A_1 = (4 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}) \times (5 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^2) = 20\text{ N}$ ✅  
$P_2 \times A_2 = (10^5\text{ Pa}) \times (2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2) = 20\text{ N}$ ✅  

This directly answers Maya's question: while the force in both cases was $20\text{ N}$, the sharp knife concentrated that force onto one-fourth the area, producing quadruple the pressure needed to slice the tomato skin cleanly. To produce that same cutting pressure with the blunt knife, one would need to push $4$ times harder ($80\text{ N}$), crushing the delicate tomato flesh before it ever cuts.

### Example 2: Finding Pressure Given $F$ and $A$

A thrust of $100\text{ N}$ acts on a tabletop over an area of $0.5\text{ m}^2$. Calculate the pressure.

- $P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{100\text{ N}}{0.5\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{200\text{ Pa}}$

**Verification:** $200\text{ Pa} \times 0.5\text{ m}^2 = 100\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 3: Finding Force Given $P$ and $A$

A pressure of $5000\text{ Pa}$ acts across a surface area of $0.02\text{ m}^2$. What is the applied thrust?

- Rearranging $P = \frac{F}{A} \implies F = P \times A$
- $F = 5000\text{ N/m}^2 \times 0.02\text{ m}^2 = \mathbf{100\text{ N}}$

**Verification:** $P = \frac{100\text{ N}}{0.02\text{ m}^2} = 5000\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Example 4: Finding Area Given $F$ and $P$

A thrust of $600\text{ N}$ generates a pressure of $15,000\text{ Pa}$. What is the contact area?

- $A = \frac{F}{P} = \frac{600\text{ N}}{15,000\text{ Pa}} = \mathbf{0.04\text{ m}^2}$

**Verification:** $600\text{ N} \div 0.04\text{ m}^2 = 15,000\text{ Pa}$ ✅

### Example 5: A $60\text{ kg}$ Person Standing on Two Feet vs. One Foot

A student has a mass of $60\text{ kg}$. The total contact area of both soles combined is $0.04\text{ m}^2$. Calculate the pressure exerted on the floor when standing on both feet, and when balancing on just one foot.

**Weight (thrust):**
- $F = m \times g = 60\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = \mathbf{600\text{ N}}$

**Standing on both feet:**
- $A = 0.04\text{ m}^2$
- $P = \frac{600\text{ N}}{0.04\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{15,000\text{ Pa}} = 15\text{ kPa}$

**Balancing on one foot:**
- $A_{\text{one foot}} = \frac{0.04\text{ m}^2}{2} = 0.02\text{ m}^2$
- $P = \frac{600\text{ N}}{0.02\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{30,000\text{ Pa}} = 30\text{ kPa}$

**Verification:**  
$15,000\text{ Pa} \times 0.04\text{ m}^2 = 600\text{ N}$ ✅  
$30,000\text{ Pa} \times 0.02\text{ m}^2 = 600\text{ N}$ ✅  

The person's weight remains exactly $600\text{ N}$ throughout, but halving the contact area **doubles** the pressure exerted on the floor.

### Example 6: A Brick Resting on Three Different Faces

A brick has dimensions $20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$ and a mass of $2\text{ kg}$. Calculate the pressure it exerts when placed on a table in its three different orientations.

**Weight:** $F = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = \mathbf{20\text{ N}}$ (constant in all orientations).

```
                 20 cm
            +--------------+
           /              /|
          /     10 cm    / | 5 cm
         +--------------+  |
         |              | /
         +--------------+/
```

> **Figure 1.3** – A $20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$ brick. Placing it on its largest face ($20 \times 10$) gives the greatest area and least pressure, while resting it on its smallest face ($10 \times 5$) gives the least area and greatest pressure.

**Face 1: Flat (largest face), $20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$**
- $A = 20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 200\text{ cm}^2 = 200 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = \mathbf{0.02\text{ m}^2}$
- $P = \frac{20\text{ N}}{0.02\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{1000\text{ Pa}}$

**Face 2: On its side, $20\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$**
- $A = 20\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm} = 100\text{ cm}^2 = 100 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = \mathbf{0.01\text{ m}^2}$
- $P = \frac{20\text{ N}}{0.01\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{2000\text{ Pa}}$

**Face 3: Upright on its end (smallest face), $10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$**
- $A = 10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm} = 50\text{ cm}^2 = 50 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = \mathbf{0.005\text{ m}^2}$
- $P = \frac{20\text{ N}}{0.005\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{4000\text{ Pa}}$

| Orientation | Base Dimensions | Area ($\text{m}^2$) | Pressure ($\text{Pa}$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Flat (broadest) | $20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$ | $0.02$ | $1000$ |
| On side edge | $20\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$ | $0.01$ | $2000$ |
| Upright (end) | $10\text{ cm} \times 5\text{ cm}$ | $0.005$ | $4000$ |

**Verification:**  
$1000 \times 0.02 = 20\text{ N}$ ✅  
$2000 \times 0.01 = 20\text{ N}$ ✅  
$4000 \times 0.005 = 20\text{ N}$ ✅  
The smallest face exerts the greatest pressure ($4000\text{ Pa}$), whereas the largest face produces the least pressure ($1000\text{ Pa}$), despite the force being an identical $20\text{ N}$ in all three configurations.

### Example 7: The Tip and Head of a Nail

A nail is driven into a wooden plank with a hammer strike delivering a thrust of $50\text{ N}$. The nail's tip has an area of $1\text{ mm}^2$, while the flat head has an area of $1\text{ cm}^2$. Determine the pressure at both ends.

**At the pointed tip:**
- $A_{\text{tip}} = 1\text{ mm}^2 = 1 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2$
- $P_{\text{tip}} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{1 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{5 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}} = 50,000\text{ kPa}$

**At the flat head:**
- $A_{\text{head}} = 1\text{ cm}^2 = 1 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$
- $P_{\text{head}} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{1 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}} = 500\text{ kPa}$

**Comparison:** $\frac{P_{\text{tip}}}{P_{\text{head}}} = \frac{5 \times 10^7}{5 \times 10^5} = \mathbf{100}$. The pressure at the tip is **$100$ times greater** than at the head because the tip's surface area is $100$ times smaller ($1\text{ cm}^2 = 100\text{ mm}^2$).

**Verification:**  
$(5 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}) \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2 = 50\text{ N}$ ✅  
$(5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}) \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = 50\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 8: An Elephant vs. A Person in High Heels

An elephant has a mass of $4000\text{ kg}$, and the combined area of its four feet is $0.4\text{ m}^2$. A person of mass $40\text{ kg}$ stands balanced on high-heeled shoes, with the two heels having a combined contact area of $2\text{ cm}^2$ ($1\text{ cm}^2$ each). Which exerts greater pressure on the ground?

**Elephant:**
- $F = 4000\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 40,000\text{ N}$
- $P_{\text{elephant}} = \frac{40,000\text{ N}}{0.4\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{1 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}} = 100\text{ kPa}$

**Person on high heels (assuming weight momentarily supported on heels):**
- $F = 40\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 400\text{ N}$
- $A = 2\text{ cm}^2 = 2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$
- $P_{\text{heel}} = \frac{400\text{ N}}{2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{2 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}} = 2000\text{ kPa}$

**Comparison:** $\frac{P_{\text{heel}}}{P_{\text{elephant}}} = \frac{2 \times 10^6}{1 \times 10^5} = \mathbf{20}$. The high heels exert **$20$ times more pressure** on the floor than the massive elephant, despite the elephant being $100$ times heavier ($40,000 \div 400 = 100$)!

**Verification:**  
$(10^5\text{ Pa}) \times 0.4\text{ m}^2 = 40,000\text{ N}$ ✅  
$(2 \times 10^6\text{ Pa}) \times (2 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2) = 400\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 9: Unit Conversions ($\text{cm}^2$ to $\text{m}^2$ and $\text{kPa}$)

A thrust of $30\text{ N}$ acts on an area of $15\text{ cm}^2$. Determine the pressure in pascals and in kilopascals.

- $A = 15\text{ cm}^2 = 15 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = \mathbf{0.0015\text{ m}^2}$
- $P = \frac{30\text{ N}}{0.0015\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{20,000\text{ Pa}}$
- Converting to kilopascals: $\frac{20,000\text{ Pa}}{1000} = \mathbf{20\text{ kPa}}$

**Verification:** $20,000\text{ Pa} \times 0.0015\text{ m}^2 = 30\text{ N}$ ✅

**Useful conversions to remember:**
- $2.5\text{ kPa} = 2.5 \times 1000\text{ Pa} = 2500\text{ Pa}$
- $36,000\text{ Pa} = \frac{36,000}{1000} = 36\text{ kPa}$
- $1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa} = 100\text{ kPa}$

### Example 10: School Backpack Shoulder Straps

A loaded school backpack has a mass of $6\text{ kg}$. Thin shoulder straps have a total contact area of $4\text{ cm}^2$, whereas broad padded straps provide an area of $40\text{ cm}^2$. Find the pressure felt on the student's shoulders in each case ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).

- Weight of backpack: $F = 6\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = \mathbf{60\text{ N}}$

**Thin straps:**
- $A_1 = 4\text{ cm}^2 = 4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$
- $P_1 = \frac{60\text{ N}}{4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{1.5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}} = 150\text{ kPa}$

**Broad padded straps:**
- $A_2 = 40\text{ cm}^2 = 40 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = 4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2$
- $P_2 = \frac{60\text{ N}}{4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{1.5 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}} = 15\text{ kPa}$

**Verification:**  
$(1.5 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}) \times (4 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2) = 60\text{ N}$ ✅  
$(1.5 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}) \times (4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^2) = 60\text{ N}$ ✅  

Increasing the strap area tenfold reduces the pressure **by a factor of $10$** ($150\text{ kPa} \to 15\text{ kPa}$), offering immense relief, even though the total weight ($60\text{ N}$) carried remains identical!

## Part D: A Glimpse Ahead: Fluids Exert Pressure Too

So far, we have examined pressure caused by solid objects resting on surfaces. However, **fluids** (liquids and gases) also exert pressure, pushing continuously against the **walls and bottom of their containers**. Air inside an inflated balloon exerts pressure pushing outward against the rubber wall, and water inside a household tank exerts steady outward force on the container's base and sides. How fluid pressure functions and why it increases with depth is the subject of our upcoming topics. For now, remember this foundational principle: **pressure is not unique to solids; fluids exert pressure as well**.

---

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Confusing force and pressure.**  
   Force (thrust) is measured in newtons ($\text{N}$), whereas pressure is measured in pascals ($\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2$). Force describes the total action applied, whereas pressure measures how intensely that force is concentrated on **each square metre**. The exact same force produces radically different pressures depending on contact area.

2. **Mistake 2: Leaving contact area in $\text{cm}^2$ when calculating pascals.**  
   Dividing $30\text{ N}$ by $15\text{ cm}^2$ gives $2\text{ N/cm}^2$, which is **not** $2\text{ Pa}$! To find pascals, convert to $\text{m}^2$ first: $15\text{ cm}^2 = 0.0015\text{ m}^2$, giving $P = \frac{30\text{ N}}{0.0015\text{ m}^2} = 20,000\text{ Pa}$.

3. **Mistake 3: Dividing by $100$ instead of $10,000$ to convert $\text{cm}^2$ to $\text{m}^2$.**  
   Since $1\text{ cm} = 10^{-2}\text{ m}$, squaring both sides yields $1\text{ cm}^2 = (10^{-2}\text{ m})^2 = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2 = \frac{1}{10,000}\text{ m}^2$. Dividing by $100$ mistakenly turns $200\text{ cm}^2$ into $2\text{ m}^2$ (instead of the correct $0.02\text{ m}^2$), throwing your calculated pressure off by a factor of $100$!

4. **Mistake 4: Believing that pressure depends exclusively on force.**  
   Pressure is governed by both force and area: $P = \frac{F}{A}$. While an elephant exerts a massive force, its enormous footpads keep pressure moderate. Conversely, a lightweight person standing on pointed stiletto heels concentrates a small force over a microscopic area, creating vastly higher pressure.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming wider supports reduce an object's weight.**  
   Broad tyres on a tractor or wide hooves on a camel do not make the vehicle or animal lighter. The downward force (weight) remains **identical**; it is simply spread across a larger surface area, **lowering the pressure** so it does not sink.

## Quick Recap

- **Fluid:** Any substance that can flow (liquids and gases).
- **Thrust:** The net perpendicular force ($90^\circ$) acting upon a surface. Unit: **$\text{N}$**.
- For an object at rest on a horizontal surface, thrust equals its downward **weight**: $F = W = m \times g$.
- **Pressure:** The thrust applied per unit surface area: **$P = \frac{F}{A}$**.
- **SI unit:** **pascal ($\text{Pa}$)**, where $1\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ N/m}^2$. Common multiples: $1\text{ kPa} = 1000\text{ Pa}$, $1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa}$.
- Always convert area to **$\text{m}^2$**: $1\text{ cm}^2 = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$, $1\text{ mm}^2 = 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2$.
- **Smaller area $\implies$ higher pressure** (sharp knife blades, needle tips, axe heads, nail points).
- **Larger area $\implies$ lower pressure** (wide backpack straps, snowshoes, wide tractor tyres, camel hooves).
- Varying the contact area changes pressure dramatically without altering the total applied force.
- Fluids exert pressure in all directions against the walls and floors of their containers.

3. A pressure of $8000\text{ Pa}$ acts on a surface of area $0.03\text{ m}^2$. Find the applied thrust.
4. A force of $900\text{ N}$ produces a pressure of $18,000\text{ Pa}$. Find the area on which it acts.
5. A student of mass $50\text{ kg}$ stands on both feet with a total contact area of $0.05\text{ m}^2$. Find the pressure exerted on the floor ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). What happens to the pressure if the student balances on one foot (area $0.025\text{ m}^2$)?
6. A solid wooden block of mass $5\text{ kg}$ has dimensions $50\text{ cm} \times 20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm}$. Find the greatest and least pressure it can exert on a tabletop ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).
7. A force of $12\text{ N}$ acts on an area of $6\text{ cm}^2$. Calculate the pressure in pascals and in kilopascals.
8. The sharp tip of a sewing needle has an area of $0.2\text{ mm}^2$. Find the pressure exerted when it is pushed with a force of $4\text{ N}$.
9. Why are school backpacks designed with wide shoulder straps, and why is a carpentry nail tapered to a sharp point at one end?
10. State whether each statement is True or False, providing a reason:
    (a) A camel's wide hooves decrease its body weight in soft sand.  
    (b) $1\text{ kPa} = 1000\text{ Pa}$.  
    (c) If contact area is doubled while applied force remains constant, pressure is halved.  
    (d) $1\text{ cm}^2 = 10^{-2}\text{ m}^2$.

### Answers

1. **Thrust** is the net force acting perpendicular to a surface (SI unit: **$\text{N}$**). **Pressure** is the thrust acting per unit area, $P = \frac{F}{A}$ (SI unit: **$\text{Pa} = \text{N/m}^2$**).

2. $P = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{250\text{ N}}{0.5\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{500\text{ Pa}}$.  
   *Check:* $500\text{ Pa} \times 0.5\text{ m}^2 = 250\text{ N}$ ✅

3. $F = P \times A = 8000\text{ Pa} \times 0.03\text{ m}^2 = \mathbf{240\text{ N}}$.  
   *Check:* $240\text{ N} \div 0.03\text{ m}^2 = 8000\text{ Pa}$ ✅

4. $A = \frac{F}{P} = \frac{900\text{ N}}{18,000\text{ Pa}} = \mathbf{0.05\text{ m}^2}$.  
   *Check:* $900\text{ N} \div 0.05\text{ m}^2 = 18,000\text{ Pa}$ ✅

5. Downward weight: $F = m \times g = 50\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 500\text{ N}$.  
   On two feet: $P = \frac{500\text{ N}}{0.05\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{10,000\text{ Pa}} = 10\text{ kPa}$.  
   On one foot: $P = \frac{500\text{ N}}{0.025\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{20,000\text{ Pa}} = 20\text{ kPa}$.  
   The pressure **doubles** because the contact area is cut in half while weight remains unchanged.  
   *Check:* $10,000 \times 0.05 = 500\text{ N}$ ✅, $20,000 \times 0.025 = 500\text{ N}$ ✅

6. Weight: $F = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 50\text{ N}$.  
   - Largest face: $50\text{ cm} \times 20\text{ cm} = 1000\text{ cm}^2 = 0.1\text{ m}^2$.  
     Least pressure: $P_{\text{least}} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{0.1\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{500\text{ Pa}}$.  
   - Smallest face: $20\text{ cm} \times 10\text{ cm} = 200\text{ cm}^2 = 0.02\text{ m}^2$.  
     Greatest pressure: $P_{\text{greatest}} = \frac{50\text{ N}}{0.02\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{2500\text{ Pa}}$.  
   *Check:* $500 \times 0.1 = 50\text{ N}$ ✅, $2500 \times 0.02 = 50\text{ N}$ ✅

7. $A = 6\text{ cm}^2 = 6 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$.  
   $P = \frac{12\text{ N}}{6 \times 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{20,000\text{ Pa}} = \mathbf{20\text{ kPa}}$.  
   *Check:* $20,000 \times (6 \times 10^{-4}) = 12\text{ N}$ ✅

8. $A = 0.2\text{ mm}^2 = 0.2 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^2 = 2 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2$.  
   $P = \frac{4\text{ N}}{2 \times 10^{-7}\text{ m}^2} = \mathbf{2 \times 10^7\text{ Pa}} = 20,000\text{ kPa}$.  
   *Check:* $(2 \times 10^7) \times (2 \times 10^{-7}) = 4\text{ N}$ ✅

9. Wide shoulder straps distribute the bag's weight across a larger surface area on the shoulder, significantly reducing pressure and preventing painful strain. Conversely, a nail has an extremely tiny surface area at its tip so that even moderate hammer force creates immense pressure, allowing it to penetrate wood easily.

10. (a) **False** ❌. A camel's wide hooves do not reduce its weight (gravitational pull); they simply distribute that weight over a larger area, reducing pressure so the hooves do not sink.  
    (b) **True** ✅. The metric prefix "kilo" denotes a factor of $1000$.  
    (c) **True** ✅. Since $P = \frac{F}{A}$, doubling the denominator $A$ with constant $F$ halves the resulting value $P$.  
    (d) **False** ❌. $1\text{ cm}^2 = (10^{-2}\text{ m})^2 = 10^{-4}\text{ m}^2$.

Next time you walk, push an object, or lift a bag, pause and consider: *"Over how large an area is this weight being spread?"*
