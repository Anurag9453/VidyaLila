# Topic 5: Characteristics of Sound: Loudness, Pitch, and Quality

In Topic 4, we saw that sound travels through room air at approximately $340\text{ m/s}$. That speed is governed entirely by the medium and its temperature: whether a sound is loud or faint, high-pitched or deep, it traverses the room at the exact same pace.

Yet our daily auditory experience reveals a rich, diverse world of acoustic sensations. A booming bass drum sounds entirely different from a silver flute. A small child's voice is instantly distinguishable from an adult's voice. Even when two musical instruments play the exact same musical note at the identical volume, we recognize them instantly. **How does the human auditory system perceive, categorize, and identify these sounds?**

In this topic, we examine the three defining subjective characteristics of sound: **loudness**, **pitch**, and **quality (timbre)**. We will discover how each sensory perception maps directly to a physical property of the underlying acoustic wave.

**Throughout this topic, we assume a standard speed of sound in air of $v = 340\text{ m/s}$.**

## In the Recording Studio: Flute and Violin

Inside the sound engineering booth of a media broadcast studio, audio engineer Dr. Carter sat behind a large digital mixing console. High school student Maya was visiting the studio to explore acoustic engineering. The studio walls were lined with thick, sound-absorbing foam wedges, and on the performance floor stood two musicians: a classical flutist and a concert violinist.

Dr. Carter said, "Maya, close your eyes. I am going to have each musician play concert pitch A ($440\text{ Hz}$), one after the other. Through the mixing console, I have calibrated their volume outputs to be precisely identical: neither instrument is louder or quieter than the other."

Maya closed her eyes. First, a pure, breathy tone resonated through the studio monitors. A moment later, a rich, resonant singing tone was played. Dr. Carter laughed warmly. "Spot on, Maya! But think about it from a physics standpoint: if both instruments produced the exact same pitch, and both arrived at your ears with the exact same loudness, **how could your brain distinguish between them?**"

Maya paused thoughtfully. "They felt fundamentally different. The flute sounded smooth, rounded, and airy; the violin sounded bright, buzzing, and layered."

Dr. Carter nodded. "That is the third essential characteristic of sound. The human ear evaluates every sound along three distinct dimensions. Two were identical, but the third revealed their identity. Let us examine all three in detail."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Loudness** | The physiological sensation of the magnitude or strength of an auditory stimulus; depends primarily on wave amplitude | A thunderous drumbeat vs. a soft whisper |
| **Pitch** | The sensation of the "shrillness" or "depth" of a sound; depends strictly on wave frequency | A bird's shrill chirping vs. a lion's low roar |

## Part A: Loudness

### What Is Loudness?

When an acoustic wave strikes the eardrum, the listener perceives the sound as **loud** or **soft**. This subjective perception is termed **loudness**. It represents the brain's psychological interpretation of the mechanical energy carried by the wave.

### Loudness Depends on Amplitude

Pluck a guitar string gently: it vibrates slightly and produces a faint, soft sound. Pluck that same string forcefully: it moves across a much wider swing, and the sound is loud and powerful. Strike a drumhead lightly with a drumstick vs. pounding it forcefully: the volume changes dramatically. Why?

- Plucking or striking with greater force delivers more mechanical energy to the vibrating body, increasing its **amplitude ($A$)**.
- A larger amplitude means the vibrating surface pushes more forcefully into the surrounding air, creating larger compression peaks and deeper rarefaction troughs.
- When these larger pressure variations strike the eardrum, they displace it more forcefully, which the brain interprets as a **louder sound**.

> **Loudness depends primarily on the amplitude of the vibrating source: the greater the amplitude, the louder the sound.**

```
 Faint Sound: Small Amplitude (Moderate Energy)

     *****           *****           *****
   **-----***-----***-----***-----***-----***-----**
             *****           *****           *****

 Loud Sound: Large Amplitude (High Energy, Same Frequency)

      ***             ***             ***
     *   *           *   *           *   *
    *     *         *     *         *     *
   *-------*-------*-------*-------*-------*-------*
            *     *         *     *         *     *
             *   *           *   *           *   *
              ***             ***             ***
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Both acoustic waves share the identical frequency and wavelength, but the lower wave possesses twice the amplitude, producing a significantly louder auditory perception.

### Other Factors Governing Loudness

In addition to amplitude, perceived loudness depends on three secondary factors:

1. **Distance from the Source:** As an acoustic wave spreads outward into 3D space, its mechanical energy spreads over an ever-expanding spherical surface area ($4\pi r^2$). Consequently, the acoustic energy arriving per unit area decreases with distance: sounds grow steadily fainter as you move away.
2. **Surface Area of the Vibrating Body:** A larger vibrating surface displaces a greater volume of air with each cycle. For instance, a small tuning fork produces a faint sound when held in the air, but when its base is pressed against the broad wooden top of a table, the entire table vibrates, producing a much louder sound.
3. **Auditory Sensitivity of the Listener:** Human ears exhibit different subjective sensitivities depending on age, health, and acoustic frequency. Two individuals listening to the exact same sound wave may perceive slightly different levels of loudness.

### Difference Between Loudness and Sound Intensity

- **Sound Intensity:** An objective physical quantity defined as the rate at which acoustic energy flows through a unit area perpendicular to the direction of wave travel (measured in watts per square meter, $\text{W/m}^2$). It can be measured objectively by calibrated electronic sensors and is completely independent of the listener.
- **Loudness:** The subjective psychological sensation of sound strength perceived by the human ear. While intensity rises as energy increases, perceived loudness depends on the physiological sensitivity of the ear.

### The Unit of Loudness: The Decibel (dB)

Acoustic sound levels are measured on a logarithmic scale in units called **decibels ($\text{dB}$)**. 

Because the human ear can detect an astonishingly wide dynamic range—from the faintest whisper to a jet engine—the decibel scale compresses this range into convenient numbers:

| Sound Phenomenon | Approximate Sound Level (dB) |
|---|---|
| Threshold of human hearing (faintest detectable sound) | $0\text{ dB}$ |
| Gentle rustling of leaves, faint whisper | $20\text{ dB}$ |
| Quiet library, peaceful residential room | $40 - 50\text{ dB}$ |
| Normal conversational speech | $60\text{ dB}$ |
| Heavy urban road traffic | $80\text{ dB}$ |
| Industrial machine shop, pneumatic jackhammer | $90 - 100\text{ dB}$ |
| Live rock concert, police siren at close range | $110 - 120\text{ dB}$ |
| Jet aircraft takeoff at 25 meters (threshold of acute pain) | $140\text{ dB}$ |

```

 Whisper              : ##### 20 dB
 Quiet Library        : ######### 45 dB
 Normal Conversation  : ############ 60 dB
 Heavy Traffic        : ################ 80 dB

```

> **Figure 5.2** – Relative loudness levels across everyday environments.

**Hearing Protection Threshold:** Prolonged exposure to acoustic levels exceeding **$85\text{ dB}$** can produce permanent hearing loss. Sound levels approaching **$120\text{ dB}$** cause acute physical ear pain and can rupture delicate structures inside the cochlea. Protective earplugs or earmuffs should always be worn in loud industrial environments.

## Part B: Pitch

### What Is Pitch?

Some sounds strike our ears as **shrill, piercing, and high**, such as a referee's whistle, a screeching brake, or a flute. Other sounds impress our ears as **deep, heavy, and flat**, such as a booming bass drum, a bullfrog's croak, or a lion's roar. This perceptual dimension of sound is termed **pitch**.

### Pitch Depends on Frequency

> **Pitch is determined strictly by the frequency of vibration: the higher the frequency, the higher and shriller the perceived pitch. The lower the frequency, the lower and deeper the perceived pitch.**

Familiar comparisons:
- **A mosquito's whine** has a very high pitch because its wings beat roughly 500 times per second ($500\text{ Hz}$), whereas **a honeybee's hum** has a lower pitch ($200\text{ Hz}$).
- **A child's or woman's voice** generally possesses a higher pitch than an adult male's voice. This occurs because children and adult women typically have shorter, thinner vocal cords that oscillate at higher frequencies.
- As demonstrated in Topic 3, **a thin guitar string vibrates rapidly, producing a high pitch**, whereas a thick, heavy bass string vibrates slowly, producing a deep bass pitch.

```
 Low Pitch: Low Frequency (Long Wavelength)

        *******                         *******
     ***       ***                   ***       ***
   **-------------***-------------***-------------**
                     ***       ***
                        *******

 High Pitch: High Frequency (Short Wavelength, Same Amplitude)

      ***             ***             ***
    **   **         **   **         **   **
   *-------*-------*-------*-------*-------*-------*
            **   **         **   **         **   **
              ***             ***             ***
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Both waves have identical amplitudes (and therefore equal loudness), but the lower wave completes twice as many cycles per unit time, resulting in a higher perceived musical pitch.

### Factors That Alter String Pitch

The vibrational frequency and pitch of a musical string (guitar, violin, cello, piano) depend on three physical factors:

| Adjustment | Effect on Frequency | Resulting Pitch |
|---|---|---|
| Make the string **shorter** (pressing a finger onto a fret) | **Increases** | **Higher pitch** |
| Make the string **thinner / lighter** | **Increases** | **Higher pitch** |
| Increase the string's **tension** (tightening tuning peg) | **Increases** | **Higher pitch** |

In summary: **short, thin, and tightly stretched strings vibrate at high frequencies and emit high-pitched notes.**

### Pitch vs. Amplitude: Two Independent Physical Properties

Take a precision tuning fork calibrated to $512\text{ Hz}$. Strike it very gently against a rubber pad: it emits a faint hum. Now strike it forcefully: it emits a loud, ringing tone.

- When struck gently: amplitude is small $\rightarrow$ sound is **soft**.
- When struck forcefully: amplitude is large $\rightarrow$ sound is **loud**.

However, in both trials, the steel prongs execute exactly $512$ oscillations every second. Therefore, **the frequency is unchanged ($512\text{ Hz}$), and the pitch remains completely identical.** Striking harder alters the volume (loudness), but does not change the note (pitch).

> **Pitch depends on frequency, not amplitude. Loudness depends on amplitude, not frequency.**

### Musical Notes and Frequencies: Names and Ratios

A musical scale organizes notes in ascending order of pitch. In Western music, the notes of an octave are named **Do, Re, Mi, Fa, Sol, La, Ti, (Do)**. In Indian classical music, the identical intervals are termed **Sa, Re, Ga, Ma, Pa, Dha, Ni, (Sa)**.

Each note corresponds to a distinct physical frequency. The frequencies of the natural diatonic scale (C-major) bear precise mathematical ratios relative to the root note:

| Musical Note (Solfege / Indian) | Theoretical Frequency Ratio | Frequency if Root = 262 Hz |
|---|---|---|
| Do / Sa | $1$ | $262\text{ Hz}$ |
| Re / Re | $9/8$ ($1.125$) | $295\text{ Hz}$ |
| Mi / Ga | $5/4$ ($1.250$) | $328\text{ Hz}$ |
| Fa / Ma | $4/3$ ($1.333$) | $349\text{ Hz}$ |
| Sol / Pa | $3/2$ ($1.500$) | $393\text{ Hz}$ |
| La / Dha | $5/3$ ($1.667$) | $437\text{ Hz}$ |
| Ti / Ni | $15/8$ ($1.875$) | $491\text{ Hz}$ |
| High Do / Upper Sa | $2$ ($2.000$) | $524\text{ Hz}$ |

Two standard reference notes:
- **Middle C:** Approximately $262\text{ Hz}$.
- **Concert Pitch A:** Internationally standardized at $440\text{ Hz}$.

Notice that the eighth note (High Do) has a frequency exactly **twice that of the root note ($2 \times 262\text{ Hz} = 524\text{ Hz}$)**. This two-to-one frequency ratio is defined as an **octave**. To the human ear, two notes separated by an octave sound harmonious and share the same tonal identity, merely resting in a higher musical register.

## Part C: Quality (Timbre)

### Same Loudness, Same Pitch, Yet Distinctly Different

Return to Maya's experience in the recording studio. The flute and the violin played the exact same musical note ($440\text{ Hz}$, concert pitch A) at the exact same decibel level ($60\text{ dB}$). Their pitch was identical, and their loudness was identical. Yet Maya recognized each instrument instantly.

The acoustic property that distinguishes them is called **quality** or **timbre** (pronounced *tam-ber*).

> **Quality (timbre)** is the characteristic of a musical sound that enables an observer to distinguish between two sounds possessing the identical loudness and pitch.

### Origin of Quality: The Waveform and Harmonics

No acoustic instrument produces an ideal, pure mathematical sine wave. When a violinist draws a horsehair bow across a string, the string does not vibrate simply as a single unit. 

1. It vibrates primarily across its whole length, producing the **fundamental frequency** that dictates the pitch.
2. Simultaneously, the string divides into vibrating halves, thirds, and quarters, producing a series of fainter, higher-frequency vibrations called **overtones (or harmonics)**.

The unique combination of the fundamental frequency and its characteristic overtones blends into an overall resultant wave shape called the **waveform**. Because the geometry and acoustic construction of a flute differ entirely from those of a wooden violin, their emitted waveforms possess completely different geometrical shapes.

```
 Flute: Nearly Pure, Smooth Waveform (Dominated by Fundamental)

        ***                     ***
      **   **                 **   **
     *       *               *       *
    *         *             *         *
   *-----------*-----------*-----------*-----------*
                *         *             *         *
                 *       *               *       *
                  **   **                 **   **
                    ***                     ***

 Violin: Complex Waveform (Rich in Sharp Overtones and Harmonics)

           **                      **
         **                      **
    *****    *              *****    *
   *          *            *          *            *
   -------------------------------------------------
               *          *            *          *
                *****    *              *****    *
                     **                      **
                       **                      **
```

> **Figure 5.4** – Both acoustic waves possess the exact same wavelength (pitch) and peak amplitude (loudness). However, their geometric shapes (waveforms) differ completely. This difference in waveform creates the distinct musical quality (timbre) of each instrument.

### Quality in Daily Life

- **Voice Recognition:** You can identify a close friend's voice over the telephone within a single spoken word, even without caller ID. Every human vocal tract possesses unique physical contours, producing an acoustic "vocal fingerprint" (timbre).
- **Distinguishing Instruments:** A grand piano, an acoustic guitar, a trumpet, and a saxophone can all play middle C ($262\text{ Hz}$) at the exact same loudness, yet each sounds unmistakably distinct due to timbre.

### Musical Sound vs. Noise

- **Musical Sound:** Produces a pleasing, harmonious auditory sensation. It is generated by **regular, periodic vibrations** that repeat a stable waveform over time (e.g., piano chords, violin melodies, flute passages).
- **Noise:** Produces an irritating, harsh, or jarring auditory sensation. It is generated by **irregular, non-periodic, chaotic vibrations** that lack any repeating structure (e.g., traffic clatter, screeching tires, metal grinding).

### The Three Acoustic Characteristics at a Glance

| Characteristic | Subjective Perception | Primary Physical Property | Measurement Unit | Practical Illustration |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Loudness** | Strength or faintness of sound | **Amplitude** of vibration | Decibel ($\text{dB}$) | Striking a bass drum harder creates greater amplitude and louder sound |
| **Pitch** | Shrillness or depth of sound | **Frequency** of vibration | Hertz ($\text{Hz}$) | A thin guitar string produces a higher pitch than a thick bass string |
| **Quality (Timbre)** | Tonal color and identity | **Waveform** (harmonic content) | Dimensionless | Distinguishing a piano from a flute playing the identical note |

## Part E: Solved Examples

**Example 1 (Frequency Ratio of an Octave):** A concert note vibrates at $440\text{ Hz}$, and its octave vibrates at $880\text{ Hz}$. Calculate the frequency ratio.

- $\text{Ratio} = \frac{880\text{ Hz}}{440\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{2}$ (or $2:1$)

**Check:** $440\text{ Hz} \times 2 = 880\text{ Hz}$ ✅. By definition, an octave represents an exact doubling of frequency.

**Example 2 (Comparing Pitch Between Two Notes):** Two musical strings vibrate with frequencies of $256\text{ Hz}$ and $320\text{ Hz}$. Which string emits a higher pitch? Calculate their frequency ratio and difference.

- Higher frequency corresponds to higher pitch; therefore, the **$320\text{ Hz}$ string** produces a higher pitch.
- $\text{Frequency Ratio} = \frac{320\text{ Hz}}{256\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{1.25}$ (or a ratio of $5:4$, corresponding to a musical major third).
- $\text{Frequency Difference} = 320\text{ Hz} - 256\text{ Hz} = \mathbf{64\text{ Hz}}$.

**Check:** $256\text{ Hz} \times 1.25 = 320\text{ Hz}$ ✅.

**Example 3 (Wavelengths of Middle C and Its Octave):** In air at $v = 340\text{ m/s}$, calculate the wavelength of middle C ($262\text{ Hz}$) and its higher octave ($524\text{ Hz}$).

- For Middle C ($f_1 = 262\text{ Hz}$):
  $$\lambda_1 = \frac{v}{f_1} = \frac{340\text{ m/s}}{262\text{ Hz}} \approx \mathbf{1.30\text{ m}}$$
- For the Octave ($f_2 = 524\text{ Hz}$):
  $$\lambda_2 = \frac{v}{f_2} = \frac{340\text{ m/s}}{524\text{ Hz}} \approx \mathbf{0.65\text{ m}}$$

**Check:** $\lambda_1 / \lambda_2 = \frac{1.30}{0.65} = 2$. Doubling frequency cuts wavelength in half, while speed remains fixed at $340\text{ m/s}$ ✅.

**Example 4 (Effect of Doubling Amplitude):** A $440\text{ Hz}$ tuning fork is struck softly. It is then struck much harder so that its vibrational amplitude doubles. What is the effect on its pitch and loudness?

- **Pitch:** The frequency is fixed by the physical dimensions and stiffness of the steel prongs ($440\text{ Hz}$). Thus, **the pitch remains completely unchanged**.
- **Loudness:** Doubling the amplitude increases the vibrational energy delivered to the air, making the sound **significantly louder**.

**Check:** Pitch depends on frequency ($440\text{ Hz} \rightarrow 440\text{ Hz}$); loudness depends on amplitude ✅.

**Example 5 (Arranging Environments by Sound Level):** Arrange the following sounds in order of increasing loudness: (a) a quiet library, (b) a rock concert, (c) a faint whisper, (d) heavy highway traffic, (e) normal conversation.

- Typical decibel values: whisper $\approx 20\text{ dB}$, quiet library $\approx 45\text{ dB}$, conversation $\approx 60\text{ dB}$, traffic $\approx 80\text{ dB}$, rock concert $\approx 115\text{ dB}$.
- Ascending order: **(c) whisper ($20\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ (a) quiet library ($45\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ (e) normal conversation ($60\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ (d) heavy traffic ($80\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ (b) rock concert ($115\text{ dB}$)**.

**Check:** $20 < 45 < 60 < 80 < 115$ ✅.

**Example 6 (Calculating Number of Vibrations):** How many complete oscillations are executed by a $512\text{ Hz}$ tuning fork over a duration of 3 seconds?

- $\text{Vibrations} = f \times t = 512\text{ vibrations/s} \times 3\text{ s} = \mathbf{1536\text{ vibrations}}$

**Check:** Period $T = \frac{1}{512} \approx 0.001953\text{ s}$. Total time = $1536 \times 0.001953\text{ s} \approx 3.0\text{ s}$ ✅.

**Example 7 (Identifying Which Acoustic Property Changes):** For each scenario, state whether loudness, pitch, or quality changes:

| No. | Physical Action | Property Altered | Physical Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Plucking a guitar string with greater force | **Loudness** | Amplitude of vibration increases |
| 2 | Shortening a vibrating string with a finger | **Pitch (higher)** | Shorter length increases vibrational frequency |
| 3 | Playing middle C on a piano vs. an acoustic guitar | **Quality (timbre)** | Different harmonics produce different waveforms |
| 4 | Turning down the volume knob on a stereo | **Loudness** | Decreases electrical energy and cone amplitude |
| 5 | An adult male voice vs. a young child's voice | **Pitch (primarily)** | Children's vocal cords vibrate at higher frequencies |
| 6 | Speaking in a faint whisper | **Loudness (reduced)** | Vocal cord vibrational amplitude is extremely small |
| 7 | Listening to a drumbeat from 500 meters away | **Loudness (reduced)** | Energy spreads across distance; pitch remains unchanged |
| 8 | Two different singers performing the same song | **Quality (timbre)** | Same musical notes, but distinct vocal waveforms |
| 9 | Turning a tuning peg to tighten a violin string | **Pitch (higher)** | Increased tension raises fundamental frequency |
| 10 | A silver flute vs. a brass whistle at equal pitch | **Quality (timbre)** | Both share frequency and loudness; wave profiles differ |

**Check:** Every adjustment cleanly maps to amplitude, frequency, or harmonic wave shape ✅.

**Example 8 (Evaluating Frequency vs. Amplitude Combinations):** Two acoustic waves are analyzed in the laboratory: Wave A has frequency $300\text{ Hz}$ and amplitude $2\text{ cm}$. Wave B has frequency $600\text{ Hz}$ and amplitude $1\text{ cm}$. Which wave has a higher pitch? Which wave is louder?

- **Pitch:** Determined by frequency. $600\text{ Hz} > 300\text{ Hz}$; therefore, **Wave B has the higher pitch**.
- **Loudness:** Determined by amplitude. $2\text{ cm} > 1\text{ cm}$; therefore, **Wave A is louder** (assuming equal distance and listening conditions).

**Check:** Pitch tracks frequency (B); loudness tracks amplitude (A) ✅.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Conflating loudness with pitch.**
   "A louder sound has a higher pitch." This is completely false. A bass drum produces a deafeningly loud sound with a very low pitch. A mosquito produces an almost inaudible sound with a very high pitch. Loudness depends on amplitude; pitch depends on frequency.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming that hitting a tuning fork harder increases its frequency.**
   A tuning fork's frequency is physically locked by its metal composition, length, and prong thickness. Hitting it harder increases its **amplitude** (making it louder), but its frequency and pitch remain strictly constant.

3. **Mistake 3: Linking pitch to sound speed.**
   "High-pitched sounds travel faster through air." False. In a given atmospheric medium, all frequencies travel at the exact same speed ($340\text{ m/s}$). In the wave equation $v = f\lambda$, a higher frequency $f$ simply means a shorter wavelength $\lambda$.

4. **Mistake 4: Interpreting "quality" as a value judgment of how "good" a sound is.**
   In acoustics, **quality (timbre)** does not mean "high quality" vs. "poor quality." It refers strictly to the characteristic tonal color and harmonic wave profile that distinguishes one source from another.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming decibels measure pitch.**
   Decibels ($\text{dB}$) measure sound level and relative loudness. Frequency and pitch are measured in hertz ($\text{Hz}$).

6. **Mistake 6: Believing that increasing amplitude alters pitch.**
   Increasing amplitude delivers more energy, altering only perceived **loudness**. The frequency and pitch remain completely unchanged.

## Quick Recap

- The human ear identifies sounds based on three core characteristics: **loudness**, **pitch**, and **quality (timbre)**.
- **Loudness** is the sensation of auditory strength. It depends primarily on the **amplitude** of vibration (larger amplitude = greater energy = louder sound). It also decreases with distance as wave energy spreads outward.
- Loudness is measured in **decibels ($\text{dB}$)**: $0\text{ dB}$ (hearing threshold), $20\text{ dB}$ (whisper), $60\text{ dB}$ (conversation), $80\text{ dB}$ (heavy traffic), $120\text{ dB}$ (rock concert / pain threshold). Prolonged exposure above $85\text{ dB}$ can cause hearing impairment.
- **Sound Intensity** is an objective physical quantity ($\text{W/m}^2$); **loudness** is the subjective sensory perception of that intensity.
- **Pitch** is the sensation of auditory shrillness or depth. It depends strictly on **frequency**: higher frequency yields higher pitch.
- A musical string produces a higher pitch when it is made **shorter**, **thinner**, or **tighter**.
- Middle C $\approx 262\text{ Hz}$; concert pitch A $\approx 440\text{ Hz}$. An **octave** represents an exact $2:1$ frequency ratio.
- **Quality (timbre)** allows us to distinguish two sounds having the identical loudness and pitch. It depends on the geometrical **waveform**, which arises from the unique combination of the fundamental frequency and its secondary overtones.
- Musical sound is produced by regular, periodic waveforms; noise is produced by chaotic, non-periodic vibrations.

    (d) High-pitched sounds travel faster through ambient air than low-pitched sounds.
    (e) Noise is characterized by regular, periodic waveforms.

12. The fundamental frequency of middle C is approximately $262\text{ Hz}$. Using the natural fifth ratio ($3/2$), calculate the approximate frequency of the musical note Sol (Pa) in the same octave.

13. As you walk away from a concert stage, the music grows progressively quieter. Which acoustic characteristic changes, and why does this occur? Does the musical pitch change as distance increases?

14. Identify the primary characteristic that changes in each case:
    (a) Turning down the volume slider on a smartphone.
    (b) Tightening a violin string with a peg.
    (c) An acoustic guitar and a saxophone playing middle C at equal loudness.

### Answers

1. (1) **Loudness** depends primarily on vibrational **amplitude**. (2) **Pitch** depends strictly on vibrational **frequency**. (3) **Quality (timbre)** depends on the geometric **waveform** (the presence and relative strengths of overtones).

2. $\text{Vibrations} = f \times t = 480\text{ vibrations/s} \times 5\text{ s} = \mathbf{2400\text{ vibrations}}$.
   Check: $\frac{2400}{5} = 480\text{ Hz}$ ✅.

3. (a) $\text{Ratio} = \frac{524\text{ Hz}}{262\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{2}$ (or $2:1$).
   (b) The **$524\text{ Hz}$ note** has the higher pitch because frequency is directly proportional to pitch.
   (c) An **octave**.

4. $\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340\text{ m/s}}{320\text{ Hz}} = \mathbf{1.0625\text{ m}} \approx \mathbf{1.06\text{ m}}$.
   Check: $320\text{ Hz} \times 1.0625\text{ m} = 340\text{ m/s}$ ✅.

5. $f = \frac{v}{\lambda} = \frac{340\text{ m/s}}{0.4\text{ m}} = \mathbf{850\text{ Hz}}$.
   Check: $850\text{ Hz} \times 0.4\text{ m} = 340\text{ m/s}$ ✅.

6. (a) **Loudness** changes: striking the drum with greater force increases the amplitude of the membrane's oscillations, generating higher-energy pressure waves.
   (b) **Pitch** remains unchanged: the natural vibrational frequency of the drumhead is determined by its diameter, mass, and tension, which do not change with striking force. **Quality** also remains largely identical.

7. Ascending order of loudness: **Whisper ($20\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ Normal conversation ($60\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ Heavy traffic ($80\text{ dB}$) $\rightarrow$ Rock concert ($115\text{ dB}$)**.

8. The **mosquito's whine** has a higher pitch because its wings beat at roughly $500\text{ Hz}$, compared to the honeybee's lower rate of roughly $200\text{ Hz}$. A **child's voice** has a higher pitch because shorter, thinner vocal cords vibrate at significantly higher frequencies than an adult male's vocal cords.

9. (a) Shortening the string raises its vibrational frequency, producing a **higher pitch**.
   (b) Tightening the string increases its tension, raising its frequency and producing a **higher pitch**.
   (c) Replacing it with a thicker, more massive string decreases its vibrational frequency, producing a **lower pitch**.

10. **Quality (timbre)**. It is caused by the differing **waveforms** of the two instruments: the piano and trumpet generate distinct combinations of higher-order overtones (harmonics), giving each instrument a unique acoustic signature.

11. (a) **False** ❌: Loudness depends on amplitude; pitch depends on frequency.
    (b) **False** ❌: Amplitude measures displacement energy; frequency measures cycles per second. They are independent.
    (c) **True** ✅: Decibels measure acoustic sound pressure level and loudness.
    (d) **False** ❌: Sound speed in a given medium is independent of frequency and pitch.
    (e) **False** ❌: Noise is characterized by irregular, chaotic vibrations; musical sound has regular periodic waveforms.

12. $f(\text{Sol}) = 262\text{ Hz} \times \frac{3}{2} = 262 \times 1.5 = \mathbf{393\text{ Hz}}$.
    Check: $\frac{393}{262} = 1.5$ ✅.

13. **Loudness** decreases with distance because acoustic energy radiates over an expanding spherical area, reducing the energy captured per unit area by the ear. **Pitch does not change** because wave frequency remains strictly constant during propagation.

14. (a) **Loudness** (amplitude is reduced).
    (b) **Pitch** (frequency is raised).
    (c) **Quality (timbre)** (pitch and loudness are identical, but waveforms differ).
