# Topic 2: Positive, Negative, and Zero Work

In the previous topic, we established that in physics, **work** is performed when (1) an external **force** acts on an object, and (2) the object undergoes a **displacement** along the line of action of that force. Under those conditions, $W = F \times s$, where force $F$ is in newtons ($\text{N}$), displacement $s$ is in metres ($\text{m}$), and work $W$ is measured in **joules ($\text{J}$)**.

So far, we have only examined situations where the applied force and the displacement act in the exact same direction. But what happens if a force acts in the direction **opposite** to an object's motion? What if a force acts **perpendicularly ($90^\circ$)** to the direction of travel? Does the force still perform work, and if so, what is its mathematical sign? In this topic, we will explore the three distinct cases of work: **positive, negative, and zero**.

Throughout this topic, we take **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$** so that a $1\text{ kg}$ mass experiences a downward gravitational force (weight) of $10\text{ N}$.

---

## Sophia and the Barbell in the Fitness Centre

Sophia was learning weightlifting techniques from her athletics coach, **Coach Davies**, in the school fitness centre. Coach Davies loaded a barbell to a total mass of $40\text{ kg}$ and instructed, "Sophia, lift this barbell smoothly from the floor to straight above your head."

Bracing her core, Sophia lifted the barbell steadily from the platform to overhead lockout—a vertical distance of **$1.5\text{ m}$**.

"Excellent form!" said Coach Davies. "Now hold it completely motionless overhead for **ten seconds**." Sophia's arms trembled, sweat dripped from her forehead, and her heart pounded, yet the barbell remained locked steadily in place.

After ten seconds, Coach Davies commanded, "Now lower it **slowly and under control** back to the floor." Sophia steadily guided the barbell down through the same $1.5\text{ m}$ until it rested softly on the platform.

Puffing for breath, Sophia remarked with a smile, "Coach, holding that weight stationary overhead took every ounce of energy I had—that holding stage must have been where I did the most work!"

Coach Davies chuckled. "Muscular exhaustion is biological, Sophia; mechanical work is strictly mathematical. In physics, each of those three stages has a completely different **algebraic sign**: lifting it upward, holding it still, and lowering it down. Let us break down the exact numbers together."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Positive work** | Work done when the applied force and the displacement act in the same direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$); the force promotes or assists the motion | Pushing a shopping cart forward |
| **Negative work** | Work done when the applied force opposes the direction of displacement ($\theta = 180^\circ$); the force resists or slows down the motion | Frictional resistance or car brakes slowing a vehicle |
| **Zero work** | Work done is strictly zero when displacement is zero ($s = 0$) or when the force acts perpendicular to displacement ($\theta = 90^\circ$) | Pushing a stationary wall; gravity acting on a car on a level road |
| **Perpendicular ($90^\circ$)** | Two vectors acting at a right angle to one another | An upward lifting force on a tray while walking horizontally |
| **Angle between force and motion ($\theta$)** | The geometric angle between the force vector and the displacement vector | Same direction: $0^\circ$; opposite direction: $180^\circ$; perpendicular: $90^\circ$ |

## Part A: Three Cases, Three Signs

The algebraic sign of mechanical work depends entirely on the **relative orientation between the specific force under consideration and the object's displacement**. There are three fundamental cases.

### Case 1: Force and displacement in the same direction: positive work

When the applied force acts in the same direction as the object's displacement ($\theta = 0^\circ$), the force aids the motion and transfers energy to the object.

> $$W = + F \times s$$

Everyday examples:
- You push a shopping trolley forward, and it rolls forward.
- Earth's gravity acting on a freely falling stone: gravity pulls downward, and the stone moves downward.
- The tension in a rope pulling a water bucket upward from a well: upward pull and upward displacement.

### Case 2: Force and displacement in opposite directions: negative work

When the applied force opposes the direction of displacement ($\theta = 180^\circ$), the force resists the motion and removes energy from the object.

> $$W = - F \times s$$

Everyday examples:
- **Kinetic friction** acting on a sliding crate: the crate slides forward, but friction pushes backward.
- **Gravity acting on a rising ball**: the ball travels upward, while gravity pulls downward.
- **Brakes on a moving car**: the car moves forward, while the braking pads apply a backward resistive force.
- **Lowering a heavy weight slowly**: your hands exert an upward supporting force to prevent the load from crashing down, while the load displaces downward. Here, your hands do **negative work**, while gravity does **positive work** (downward force, downward displacement).

### Case 3: Force perpendicular to displacement: zero work

When an applied force acts at a right angle ($90^\circ$) to the direction of motion, that force neither speeds up nor slows down the object along its path. Consequently, that force does **no mechanical work**.

> $$W = 0\text{ J} \quad (\text{when } \theta = 90^\circ)$$

Everyday examples:
- **Gravity acting on a car cruising on a flat, horizontal road**: gravity pulls vertically downward, but the car moves horizontally. The angle between them is $90^\circ$, so gravity does zero work.
- **A server carrying a food tray across a flat dining room**: the upward supporting force balances the tray's weight, while the tray displaces horizontally. Work done by the upward supporting force is $0\text{ J}$.
- **Normal contact force from a flat tabletop**: the table pushes upward on a sliding book, while the book moves horizontally. Work done by the normal force is $0\text{ J}$.
- **Tension in a string during circular motion**: as studied in circular motion ([motion-part7.md](../motion/motion-part7.md)), the inward centripetal force is always directed toward the centre of the circle, perpendicular to the tangential velocity ($90^\circ$). Hence, centripetal force does zero work.

### Case 3 Companion: Zero Displacement Means Zero Work

Recall the core rule from Topic 1: **no displacement, no mechanical work**, regardless of how gigantic the applied force might be.
- Holding a $40\text{ kg}$ barbell stationary overhead ($s = 0$).
- Pushing against a reinforced concrete wall with all your strength ($s = 0$).

In both situations, $F > 0$, but since $s = 0$, $W = F \times 0 = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$.

### Summary Table of the Cases

| Case | Force and Motion Orientation | Angle ($\theta$) | Sign of Work | Mathematical Formula |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Force $\rightarrow$, Displacement $\rightarrow$ (same direction) | $0^\circ$ | **Positive** | $W = + F \times s$ |
| **2** | Force $\leftarrow$, Displacement $\rightarrow$ (opposite direction) | $180^\circ$ | **Negative** | $W = - F \times s$ |
| **3** | Force $\uparrow$, Displacement $\rightarrow$ (perpendicular) | $90^\circ$ | **Zero** | $W = 0\text{ J}$ |
| **4** | Any force, but object stationary ($s = 0$) | No angle | **Zero** | $W = F \times 0 = 0\text{ J}$ |

Visualising the three cases:

```
  Case 1: Positive             Case 2: Negative            Case 3: Zero

      F ──────>                    <────── F                     ^ F
    ┌───┐ ──────> motion         ┌───┐ ──────> motion          ┌─│─┐ ──────> motion
    └───┘                        └───┘                         └───┘
  Angle: 0°                    Angle: 180°                   Angle: 90°
  W = + F s                    W = − F s                     W = 0
```

> **Figure 2.1** – The sign of work depends on the angle between the applied force and the displacement: $0^\circ$ gives positive work, $180^\circ$ gives negative work, and $90^\circ$ gives zero work.

### Negative Work Does Not Mean "Nothing Happened"

It is a common misunderstanding to assume that "negative work" implies no work was done or that the work was somehow invalid. This is completely false. Negative work means that the force **actively opposed the motion of the body**, draining mechanical energy from it.

As a preview of coming chapters: when net positive work is done on an object, the object **speeds up**; when net negative work is done on an object, the object **slows down**.

### The Sign of Work Depends on Which Force You Consider

Multiple forces frequently act on the same object at the same time, and each force can do work with a different sign!

For example, when you lift a backpack vertically upward at constant speed:
- Your hands exert an **upward force**, and the backpack moves upward: **work done by your hands is positive ($+W$)**.
- Earth's gravity exerts a **downward pull**, while the backpack moves upward: **work done by gravity is negative ($-W$)**.

Always specify clearly: **"Work done by which force?"**

Furthermore, by Newton's third law, when you push a crate forward with force $F$, the crate pushes backward on your hands with an equal force $F$. As you walk forward, your force does positive work on the crate, while the crate's reaction force does negative work on you.

## Part B: Solved Examples

### Example 1 (Gravity on a Falling Stone)

A stone of mass $2\text{ kg}$ falls vertically downward through a height of $5\text{ m}$. Find the work done by gravity.

- Downward gravitational force: $F = mg = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 20\text{ N}$ (downward)
- Displacement: $s = 5\text{ m}$ (downward)
- Since force and displacement are in the same direction ($\theta = 0^\circ$):
  $$W = + F \times s = + 20\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{+100\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $20\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = 100\text{ J}$; both point downward, so the sign is positive ✅

### Example 2 (Gravity on a Rising Stone)

The same $2\text{ kg}$ stone is thrown vertically upward and rises through $5\text{ m}$. Find the work done by gravity.

- Gravitational force: $F = 20\text{ N}$ (downward)
- Displacement: $s = 5\text{ m}$ (upward)
- Force and displacement are in opposite directions ($\theta = 180^\circ$):
  $$W = - F \times s = - 20\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{-100\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** Same magnitude ($100\text{ J}$) as Example 1, but opposing motion, so the sign is negative ✅

### Example 3 (Work Done by Friction)

A packing box slides $4\text{ m}$ along a level floor. The kinetic friction opposing the sliding motion is $10\text{ N}$. Find the work done by friction.

- Friction opposes the direction of sliding:
  $$W = - F \times s = - 10\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = \mathbf{-40\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $10\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = 40\text{ J}$; opposing direction yields $-40\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 4 (Lifting a Bag at Constant Speed: Applied Force and Gravity)

A school bag of mass $3\text{ kg}$ is lifted vertically through $2\text{ m}$ at a constant speed. Calculate (a) the work done by the lifting force, (b) the work done by gravity, and (c) the total net work done on the bag.

Constant speed implies zero acceleration; therefore, the upward lifting force exactly balances the downward gravitational pull:
- Weight $F = mg = 3\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 30\text{ N}$ (downward)
- Upward applied force $F_{\text{lift}} = 30\text{ N}$ (upward)
- Displacement $s = 2\text{ m}$ (upward)

(a) Work done by lifting force:
$$W_{\text{lift}} = + F_{\text{lift}} \times s = + 30\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{+60\text{ J}}$$

(b) Work done by gravity:
$$W_{\text{gravity}} = - F_{\text{gravity}} \times s = - 30\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{-60\text{ J}}$$

(c) Total net work done on the bag:
$$W_{\text{net}} = W_{\text{lift}} + W_{\text{gravity}} = +60\text{ J} + (-60\text{ J}) = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $30\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = 60\text{ J}$, and $+60\text{ J} - 60\text{ J} = 0\text{ J}$ ✅. Because the speed never changed, the net work done on the bag was zero!

### Example 5 (Braking Force on a Car)

A moving car is brought to a complete halt by an average braking force of $2000\text{ N}$ acting over a stopping distance of $10\text{ m}$. Find the work done by the braking force.

- The braking force acts opposite to the vehicle's forward displacement:
  $$W = - F \times s = - 2000\text{ N} \times 10\text{ m} = \mathbf{-20\,000\text{ J} = -20\text{ kJ}}$$

**Check:** $2000\text{ N} \times 10\text{ m} = 20\,000\text{ J}$; opposing motion yields $-20\,000\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 6 (Porter Carrying Luggage on a Level Platform)

A station porter carries a $20\text{ kg}$ suitcase balanced on his head and walks horizontally across a level train platform for $50\text{ m}$. What is the work done on the suitcase by the vertical supporting force exerted by his head?

- The vertical supporting force balances the suitcase's weight:
  $$F = mg = 20\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 200\text{ N} \text{ (vertically upward)}$$
- The displacement is along the flat horizontal floor:
  $$s = 50\text{ m} \text{ (horizontal)}$$
- The angle between the upward support force and the horizontal motion is $90^\circ$:
  $$W = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** No component of the upward force acts in the horizontal direction. Multiplying $200\text{ N} \times 50\text{ m} = 10\,000\text{ J}$ would be completely wrong because force and displacement are perpendicular ✅

### Example 7 (Stone Whirling on a String)

A stone tied to a string revolves in a horizontal circle. The tension in the string is $15\text{ N}$. Find the work done by the tension force during one complete circular revolution.

- At every instantaneous point on the circle, the tension force points radially toward the centre, while the instantaneous displacement is along the tangent to the circle. The angle is always $90^\circ$.
- Since the force is perpendicular to motion at every instant, the work done in every tiny interval is zero:
  $$W = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** Even though tension is non-zero ($15\text{ N}$) and the stone travels a distance equal to the circle's circumference, perpendicularity ensures zero work ✅

### Example 8 (Worker Pushing a Crate: Action and Reaction)

A warehouse worker pushes a crate forward with a constant horizontal force of $80\text{ N}$, and both move forward together through $3\text{ m}$. Find (a) the work done by the worker on the crate, and (b) the work done by the crate on the worker.

- (a) Worker on crate: Force is forward ($+80\text{ N}$), displacement is forward ($+3\text{ m}$):
  $$W = + 80\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{+240\text{ J}}$$
- (b) Crate on worker: By Newton's third law, the crate pushes backward on the worker with an equal force ($80\text{ N}$ backward), while the worker moves forward ($3\text{ m}$ forward):
  $$W = - 80\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{-240\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** Both have a magnitude of $240\text{ J}$, with opposite algebraic signs due to opposing directions ✅

### Example 9 (Pushing a Trolley Forward)

A student pushes a laboratory trolley with a forward force of $50\text{ N}$ through a distance of $6\text{ m}$. Find the work done by the student.

- Force and displacement are in the same direction:
  $$W = + 50\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = \mathbf{+300\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $50 \times 6 = 300$, sign positive ✅

## Part C: Sophia's Barbell, Now with Numbers

Now let us return to Sophia and Coach Davies in the fitness centre.
- Mass of the barbell: $m = 40\text{ kg}$
- Weight of the barbell: $F = mg = 40\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = \mathbf{400\text{ N}}$
- Vertical height: $h = 1.5\text{ m}$

**Stage 1: Lifting the barbell upward smoothly at steady speed**
- Upward lifting force exerted by Sophia: $400\text{ N}$ upward
- Displacement: $1.5\text{ m}$ upward
- Work done by Sophia's lifting force:
  $$W_{\text{Sophia}} = + 400\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{+600\text{ J}}$$
- Work done by Earth's gravity:
  $$W_{\text{gravity}} = - 400\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{-600\text{ J}}$$

**Stage 2: Holding the barbell motionless overhead for 10 seconds**
- Upward supporting force: $400\text{ N}$ upward
- Displacement: $s = 0\text{ m}$
- Work done by Sophia:
  $$W_{\text{Sophia}} = 400\text{ N} \times 0\text{ m} = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$$
- Work done by gravity:
  $$W_{\text{gravity}} = - 400\text{ N} \times 0\text{ m} = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$$

Even though Sophia's muscles were trembling and burning calories to maintain tension, the displacement was zero, so the mechanical work done on the barbell was strictly zero!

**Stage 3: Lowering the barbell slowly and steadily to the floor**
- To prevent the barbell from accelerating downward into free fall, Sophia must apply an **upward supporting force** of $400\text{ N}$ while the barbell moves downward by $1.5\text{ m}$.
- Work done by Sophia's upward supporting force:
  $$W_{\text{Sophia}} = - 400\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{-600\text{ J}} \quad (\text{force upward, displacement downward})$$
- Work done by gravity:
  $$W_{\text{gravity}} = + 400\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{+600\text{ J}} \quad (\text{force downward, displacement downward})$$

### Overview of Sophia's Lift

| Stage | Displacement | Work Done by Sophia's Force | Work Done by Gravity |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Lifting Upward** | $1.5\text{ m}$ upward | $\mathbf{+600\text{ J}}$ | $\mathbf{-600\text{ J}}$ |
| **Holding Stationary (10 s)** | $0\text{ m}$ | $\mathbf{0\text{ J}}$ | $\mathbf{0\text{ J}}$ |
| **Lowering Downward** | $1.5\text{ m}$ downward | $\mathbf{-600\text{ J}}$ | $\mathbf{+600\text{ J}}$ |

**Verification:** $400\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = 600\text{ J}$ ✅. At every stage, the sum of the two works is $0\text{ J}$ ($+600 - 600 = 0$, $0 + 0 = 0$, $-600 + 600 = 0$) ✅.

Coach Davies smiled and summed it up: "You see, Sophia? While lifting, you performed positive work on the barbell. While holding it, despite the sweat and shaking, you did zero mechanical work on the barbell. And while lowering it, your hands did negative work. That is the exact bookkeeping of physics."

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that "negative work" means no work was done or that it was imaginary.**
   Negative work is real mechanical work; it represents a force acting in opposition to displacement (removing kinetic energy from an object). Car brakes perform $-20\,000\text{ J}$ of work to halt a car. Zero work ($0\text{ J}$) represents no work done.

2. **Mistake 2: Assuming friction does positive work because it "creates heat."**
   Kinetic friction acts opposite to the direction of relative sliding motion ($\theta = 180^\circ$). Therefore, the work done by kinetic friction on a sliding object is always **negative** (e.g., $-40\text{ J}$, never $+40\text{ J}$).

3. **Mistake 3: Forgetting to specify which force's work is being calculated.**
   When lifting an object, the lifter does positive work ($+60\text{ J}$), while gravity does negative work ($-60\text{ J}$). Always clarify: *work done by which specific force?*

4. **Mistake 4: Claiming that zero net work means no individual force did work.**
   In Example 4, the net work was $0\text{ J}$, but the lifting force did $+60\text{ J}$ and gravity did $-60\text{ J}$. Individual forces can do significant work even when their sum cancels out.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming that a large force always does a large amount of work.**
   A porter carrying a $200\text{ N}$ load horizontally on his head exerts a large force, but because that force is perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the horizontal motion, the work done is strictly **$0\text{ J}$**.

## Quick Recap

- **Case 1 (Same direction, $\theta = 0^\circ$):** Force aids motion $\rightarrow$ **Positive work**, $W = + F \times s$.
- **Case 2 (Opposite direction, $\theta = 180^\circ$):** Force opposes motion $\rightarrow$ **Negative work**, $W = - F \times s$.
- **Case 3 (Perpendicular, $\theta = 90^\circ$):** Force is perpendicular to displacement $\rightarrow$ **Zero work**, $W = 0\text{ J}$.
- **Zero displacement ($s = 0$):** Regardless of force magnitude, $W = F \times 0 = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$ (e.g., holding a barbell overhead, pushing a rigid wall).
- Gravity on a car on a level road, carrying a tray horizontally, normal contact forces, and string tension in circular orbits all act at $90^\circ$ to displacement $\rightarrow$ **$W = 0\text{ J}$**.
- The algebraic sign depends entirely on the chosen force: lifting a bag gives $+W$ for the lifter and $-W$ for gravity; lowering a bag gives $-W$ for the lifter and $+W$ for gravity.
- Negative work slows down an object; positive work speeds up an object.
- **Sophia's barbell ($40\text{ kg}$, $1.5\text{ m}$):** Lifting: $+600\text{ J}$ (lifter), $-600\text{ J}$ (gravity); Holding: $0\text{ J}$; Lowering: $-600\text{ J}$ (lifter), $+600\text{ J}$ (gravity).

   (d) A huge force acting at $90^\circ$ to an object's displacement performs a huge amount of work.

### Answers

1. Downward force $F = mg = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 50\text{ N}$. Both force and displacement act downward:
   $W = + 50\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = \mathbf{+200\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $50 \times 4 = 200$ ✅

2. Force $F = 50\text{ N}$ downward, displacement $4\text{ m}$ upward (opposite directions):
   $W = - 50\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = \mathbf{-200\text{ J}}$.
   The sign is negative because the gravitational force directly opposes the upward displacement, whereas in Question 1 gravity acted in the same direction as the motion.
   Check: Magnitude is $200\text{ J}$ in both cases ✅

3. Friction opposes displacement ($\theta = 180^\circ$):
   $W = - 8\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = \mathbf{-48\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $8 \times 6 = 48$ ✅

4. Weight $F = mg = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 20\text{ N}$.
   (a) Lifting force acts upward: $W_{\text{lift}} = + 20\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{+60\text{ J}}$.
   (b) Gravity acts downward: $W_{\text{gravity}} = - 20\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{-60\text{ J}}$.
   (c) Net work: $+60\text{ J} + (-60\text{ J}) = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $20 \times 3 = 60$ ✅

5. Braking force opposes the forward displacement:
   $W = - 1500\text{ N} \times 20\text{ m} = \mathbf{-30\,000\text{ J} = -30\text{ kJ}}$.
   Check: $1500 \times 20 = 30\,000$ ✅

6. Upward supporting force $F = mg = 30\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 300\text{ N}$, while displacement is horizontal ($90^\circ$ angle).
   $W = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$.
   Reason: The upward force exerted by the porter's head acts at an angle of $90^\circ$ to the horizontal displacement, so no work is performed by that force.
   Check: Never multiply $300\text{ N} \times 80\text{ m}$ because the vectors are perpendicular ✅

7. At every point along the circular path, the tension acts radially inward, perpendicular ($90^\circ$) to the tangential displacement.
   $W = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$.
   Check: Since work is $0\text{ J}$ in every infinitesimal segment, the total work for a full revolution remains $0\text{ J}$ ✅

8. (a) **False** ❌. Negative work means the force opposes motion; zero work ($0\text{ J}$) means no work is done.
   (b) **True** ✅. The wall undergoes zero displacement ($s = 0$), so $W = 0\text{ J}$.
   (c) **False** ❌. Kinetic friction acts opposite to motion, so it does negative work.
   (d) **False** ❌. Any force acting at $90^\circ$ to displacement does strictly zero work, regardless of how large the force is.

9. Weight $F = mg = 20\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 200\text{ N}$. The bucket moves downward by $5\text{ m}$.
   (a) Tension in rope pulls upward to control descent: $W_{\text{rope}} = - 200\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{-1000\text{ J} = -1\text{ kJ}}$.
   (b) Gravity pulls downward: $W_{\text{gravity}} = + 200\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{+1000\text{ J} = +1\text{ kJ}}$.
   Check: $200 \times 5 = 1000$, and $-1000 + 1000 = 0$ ✅

10. Displacement of the barbell is zero ($s = 0\text{ m}$), so the mechanical work done on the barbell is $W = 400\text{ N} \times 0\text{ m} = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$.
    She feels tired because her skeletal muscle fibres are continuously contracting and consuming metabolic energy internally to support the load, but zero mechanical work is delivered to the barbell.
    Check: $40\text{ kg} \times 10 = 400\text{ N}$, and $400 \times 0 = 0$ ✅
