# Topic 3: Energy and Its Forms

In the previous topic, we learned that mechanical work can be **positive, negative, or zero**. When an applied force and an object's displacement act in the same direction, positive work is done—delivering something valuable to the object. But what exactly is that "something"? What does an object gain when work is done upon it, and what internal reserve does an agent spend when performing work?

That fundamental physical property is **energy**.

---

## An Evening Blackout at Maya's Home

A sudden summer thunderstorm rolled through the valley, and the neighbourhood power grid went dark. In an instant, Maya's house was plunged into pitch darkness. "Dad, the power is out! I can't see anything," Maya called out.

Her father, **Mr. Thomas**, walked into the kitchen and struck a match to light a scented wax candle on the counter. A soft, warm yellow glow illuminated the room. Next, he clicked on a heavy-duty **battery-powered LED flashlight**, casting a crisp white beam across the hallway. Finally, needing to finish dinner, he ignited the **gas stove burner**, producing a bright blue flame to heat a pot of soup.

Maya observed all three carefully: "Dad, the candle gives off light and warmth, the flashlight produces a bright beam, and the stove produces intense cooking heat. All three are doing useful things. But what is being consumed? The candle wax is slowly melting and burning down, the flashlight batteries will eventually go dead, and the gas supply in the cylinder will eventually run out. **What is actually being spent?**"

Mr. Thomas smiled warmly. "An insightful observation, Maya! In every one of those cases, what is being stored, transformed, and transferred is **energy**. Let us explore the forms and transformations of energy today and solve your three examples with exact physics principles."

## Part A: What is Energy?

In Topic 1, we learned that applying a force to displace an object performs **work**. But where does the ability to perform work come from?

A person who has not eaten all day feels weak and cannot lift heavy crates, whereas a well-nourished athlete lifts them easily. A stationary cricket ball resting on a table cannot knock over wickets, but a fast-moving cricket ball bowls them over instantly. Water sitting in a low puddle cannot turn machinery, but water stored high in a reservoir can rush through penstocks and spin massive electricity turbines.

In every case, the common thread is clear: some systems possess the **capacity to do work**, while others do not. This capacity is called **energy**.

> **Energy is defined as the capacity to do work.**
> Any object or system that possesses energy can exert a force to cause displacement—thereby doing work.

### The SI Unit of Energy: The Joule

Because work is the transfer of energy, work and energy are fundamentally the same physical concept. Energy can be thought of as your "mechanical bank balance," while work represents the actual deposits and withdrawals. If work and energy used different units, bookkeeping would be needlessly complicated.

Therefore:

> **The SI unit of energy is the joule ($\text{J}$)**, exactly identical to the unit of work.

For larger quantities of energy, we use the **kilojoule ($\text{kJ}$)** and **megajoule ($\text{MJ}$)**:
- $1\text{ kJ} = 1000\text{ J} = 10^3\text{ J}$
- $1\text{ MJ} = 1\,000\,000\text{ J} = 10^6\text{ J}$

### Energy is a Scalar Quantity

Energy has magnitude (such as $500\text{ J}$ or $2.5\text{ kJ}$), but it has **no spatial direction**. Stating "500 joules toward the west" is physically meaningless. Thus, **energy is a scalar quantity**.

### Energy is Neither a Force Nor a Physical Substance

Energy is not a push or a pull (force), nor is it physical matter with mass and volume that can be held in your hands. Energy is a **state property** of matter and radiation—an inherent capacity possessed by an object due to its motion, position, internal chemical bonds, or temperature. We observe energy through **what it accomplishes** (moving vehicles, illuminated lamps, warming heaters).

## Part B: Forms of Energy

Although energy is a unified physical quantity, it manifests in diverse observable **forms**:

| Form of Energy | Scientific Description | Familiar Everyday Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanical energy** | Energy possessed by an object due to its motion (kinetic) or its spatial position/configuration (potential) | A moving bicycle; water stored high behind a dam |
| **Thermal (heat) energy** | Internal kinetic energy of the vibrating atoms and molecules inside a substance | Boiling water; radiant heat from an oven |
| **Light (radiant) energy** | Electromagnetic radiation capable of being detected by human eyes or solar panels | Sunlight; laser beam; light from a candle flame |
| **Sound energy** | Mechanical wave energy travelling through matter via vibrating particles | Drumbeats; acoustic guitar notes; loudspeaker output |
| **Electrical energy** | Energy associated with the movement or electric potential of charged particles (electrons) | Electric current flowing through copper wires to spin a fan |
| **Chemical energy** | Potential energy stored within the molecular bonds of chemical compounds | Food nutrients, petroleum, natural gas, dry-cell batteries |
| **Nuclear energy** | Immense energy bound within the nucleus of an atom (released during fission or fusion) | The nuclear fusion powering the Sun; nuclear reactors |
| **Magnetic energy** | Energy stored in magnetic fields created by magnets or moving electric currents | A permanent magnet lifting iron scrap metal |

Mechanical energy is split into two primary categories:
1. **Kinetic energy**: energy due to an object's motion (Topic 4).
2. **Potential energy**: energy stored due to an object's position or deformed shape (Topic 5).

> **Light and sound are true forms of energy:** Sunlight dries laundry and generates electricity in solar cells (doing work). An intense sound wave from a supersonic jet or an explosion can rattle and shatter glass window panes (exerting forces and causing displacement). Anything capable of doing mechanical work is an authentic form of energy.

> **Heat vs. Temperature:** Heat is thermal energy transferred between systems; temperature is a measurement of the average kinetic energy of the particles within a substance. They are related, but not identical.

## Part C: Energy Transforms from One Form to Another

One of nature's most magnificent principles is that **energy can convert from one form into another**. This process is known as **energy transformation**.

In a burning candle, the chemical energy stored in the wax molecules transforms into thermal energy (heat) and radiant energy (light). In a battery flashlight, chemical energy inside the cell converts into electrical energy in the circuit, which the LED then converts into light.

We represent these sequences using **energy transformation chains**:

$$\text{Initial Form} \longrightarrow \text{Intermediate Form} \longrightarrow \text{Final Forms}$$

### 10 Familiar Everyday Devices and Their Energy Transformation Chains

| Number | Device / Natural Process | Energy Transformation Chain | Physical Explanation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Incandescent / LED bulb** | Electrical $\rightarrow$ Light + Heat | Electric current heats a filament or excites a semiconductor, emitting light while warming up |
| 2 | **Electric desk fan** | Electrical $\rightarrow$ Kinetic (mechanical) | Electric current in motor coils generates magnetic forces that spin the fan blades |
| 3 | **Battery flashlight** | Chemical $\rightarrow$ Electrical $\rightarrow$ Light | Chemical reactions in the battery drive electric current through the lamp circuit |
| 4 | **Smartphone speaker** | Electrical $\rightarrow$ Sound | Audio electrical waveforms cause a miniature diaphragm to oscillate, sending sound waves through the air |
| 5 | **Hammer striking a nail** | Kinetic $\rightarrow$ Heat + Sound | The moving hammer drives the nail, emitting a sharp crack (sound) while the nail head warms up slightly |
| 6 | **Photovoltaic solar cell** | Light $\rightarrow$ Electrical | Photons of sunlight strike a silicon wafer, knocking electrons loose to generate an electric current |
| 7 | **Hydroelectric power station** | Gravitational potential $\rightarrow$ Kinetic $\rightarrow$ Electrical | Water elevated high in a reservoir falls through penstocks, spins water turbines, and rotates electrical generators |
| 8 | **Human athlete running** | Chemical (food) $\rightarrow$ Kinetic + Thermal | Muscle cells metabolize glucose to contract muscles (motion) while generating metabolic body heat |
| 9 | **Stretched elastic slingshot** | Elastic potential $\rightarrow$ Kinetic | Stretched rubber bands store elastic strain energy, accelerating the stone forward upon release |
| 10 | **Burning candle** | Chemical $\rightarrow$ Thermal (heat) + Light | Hydrocarbon wax reacts with atmospheric oxygen in combustion, releasing heat and radiant light |

A clear universal pattern emerges: **whenever anything happens in nature—when an object speeds up, glows, produces sound, or changes temperature—energy is actively shifting between forms.**

```
  Dry Cell              Circuit Wires          LED Lamp
  ┌───────────┐         ┌───────────┐         ┌───────────┐
  │ Chemical  │ ──────> │Electrical │ ──────> │   Light   │ (+ small amount
  │  Energy   │         │  Energy   │         │  Energy   │    of heat)
  └───────────┘         └───────────┘         └───────────┘
```

> **Figure 3.1** – The flow of energy inside a battery-operated torch: chemical potential energy $\rightarrow$ electrical current $\rightarrow$ radiant light output.

### Energy is Not "Lost": It Changes Form

Remember Maya's initial question: *"The candle is shrinking and the battery is draining—is energy being used up and disappearing?"*

The answer is an emphatic **no**. Energy **cannot be created from nothing, nor can it be destroyed into nothingness: it only changes from one form to another.** The chemical energy stored in the candle wax did not vanish; it dissipated into the surrounding atmosphere as heat and spread outward as radiant light.

This supreme natural law is the **Law of Conservation of Energy**, which we will investigate deeply in Topic 6.

## Part D: The Relationship Between Work and Energy

Notice how naturally the concepts of work and energy interlock. In physics:

> **Work is the mechanical means by which energy is transferred between objects or converted between forms.**

This gives us two operational rules:
1. **When net work is done ON an object, the object GAINS energy.**
   *Example:* Maya lifts a physics textbook from the floor onto a high library shelf. Maya performs positive work on the book, and the book's gravitational potential energy increases by that exact amount.
2. **When an object DOES work on another object, it LOSES energy.**
   *Example:* A bowling ball strikes pins. The ball exerts a forward force displacing the pins (doing work on them); in doing so, the ball slows down and loses kinetic energy.

Because work is the transfer of energy, both must be measured in the exact same unit: the **joule ($\text{J}$)**.

### How Do We Measure Energy?

The most direct way to measure an object's energy change is to **calculate the mechanical work required to place it into that state**. If performing $150\text{ J}$ of work brings an object to a certain height or speed, the object has gained precisely $150\text{ J}$ of energy.

### Sources of Energy

Where does the usable energy on our planet originate? With very few exceptions, virtually all energy on Earth can be traced back to the **Sun**:
- **Fossil fuels** (coal, crude oil, natural gas) are the compressed, transformed remains of ancient plants and microorganisms that absorbed sunlight via photosynthesis millions of years ago.
- **Wind energy** is produced by convection currents in the atmosphere caused by the uneven heating of Earth's surface by solar radiation.
- **Hydrological energy** (rain and rivers feeding dams) is driven by the solar-powered water cycle (evaporation and condensation).
- **Biomass and food** come from plants harvesting sunlight.

Energy sources are classified into two broad categories:
- **Renewable sources:** naturally replenished on a human timescale (solar radiation, wind, flowing water, geothermal heat).
- **Non-renewable sources:** finite reserves depleted through consumption that cannot quickly regenerate (coal, petroleum, natural gas, uranium).

## Part F: Solved Examples

In all calculations below, take **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$**.

### Example 1: Energy Imparted to a Lifted Object

An object of mass $4\text{ kg}$ is lifted vertically through a height of $3\text{ m}$ at a steady speed. How much work is done, and how much energy does the object gain?

- Upward lifting force equal to weight:
  $$F = mg = 4\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 40\text{ N}$$
- Work done by the lifter:
  $$W = F \times h = 40\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{120\text{ J}}$$
- The work done on the object directly becomes its stored energy:
  $$\Delta E = \mathbf{120\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $40\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = 120\text{ J}$ ✅. Also $\frac{120\text{ J}}{3\text{ m}} = 40\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 2: Dietary Food Energy vs. Climbing Stairs

A packaged snack bar has an energy rating of **$400\text{ kJ}$**. A person of mass $50\text{ kg}$ climbs a staircase of vertical height $3\text{ m}$. How many times could this person climb this staircase using the theoretical energy in that snack bar? (Assume $100\%$ conversion efficiency; in real biology, much of the energy is released as body heat.)

- Force needed to lift the person's body weight:
  $$F = mg = 50\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 500\text{ N}$$
- Work done for a single $3\text{ m}$ climb:
  $$W = F \times h = 500\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = 1500\text{ J}$$
- Total snack energy in joules:
  $$400\text{ kJ} = 400 \times 1000\text{ J} = 400\,000\text{ J}$$
- Number of theoretical climbs:
  $$\text{Climbs} = \frac{400\,000\text{ J}}{1500\text{ J}} = 266.67 \approx \mathbf{267\text{ climbs}}$$

**Check:** $267 \times 1500\text{ J} = 400\,500\text{ J} \approx 400\,000\text{ J}$ ✅. That small snack bar packs a massive reservoir of chemical energy!

### Example 3: Writing Energy Transformation Chains

State the energy transformation sequence for each device:
- (a) **Electric clothes iron:** Electrical energy $\rightarrow$ Thermal (heat) energy
- (b) **Acoustic percussion drum:** Kinetic energy of the drumstick $\rightarrow$ Sound energy (+ small thermal energy)
- (c) **Smartphone charging on a wall adapter:** Electrical energy $\rightarrow$ Chemical potential energy stored in the lithium-ion battery
- (d) **Bicycle wheel dynamo powering a headlamp:** Kinetic energy of spinning wheel $\rightarrow$ Electrical energy $\rightarrow$ Radiant light energy

Notice that across all devices, energy was never created from nothing nor destroyed; it simply flowed along a transformation path.

### Example 4: Conversions Between kJ, J, and kcal

(a) Convert $0.5\text{ kJ}$ into joules ($\text{J}$):
- $0.5\text{ kJ} = 0.5 \times 1000\text{ J} = \mathbf{500\text{ J}}$

(b) A nutrition bar contains $180\text{ kcal}$. Convert this to kilojoules ($\text{kJ}$) taking $1\text{ kcal} \approx 4.2\text{ kJ}$:
- $180\text{ kcal} \times 4.2\text{ kJ/kcal} = \mathbf{756\text{ kJ}}$

(c) A lunch portion contains $840\text{ kJ}$. How many kilocalories is this?
- $\frac{840\text{ kJ}}{4.2\text{ kJ/kcal}} = \mathbf{200\text{ kcal}}$

**Check:** (a) $\frac{500\text{ J}}{1000} = 0.5\text{ kJ}$ ✅. (b) $\frac{756}{4.2} = 180\text{ kcal}$ ✅. (c) $200 \times 4.2 = 840\text{ kJ}$ ✅

### Example 5: Energy Consumed by a 60 W Desk Fan in 10 Seconds

A $60\text{ W}$ fan consumes $60\text{ J}$ of electrical energy every second ($60\text{ J/s}$). How much energy does it consume in $10\text{ seconds}$?

$$\text{Energy} = 60\text{ J/s} \times 10\text{ s} = \mathbf{600\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{600\text{ J}}{10\text{ s}} = 60\text{ J/s}$ ✅

### Example 6: Energy Consumed by a 100 W Lamp in 5 Seconds

$$\text{Energy} = 100\text{ J/s} \times 5\text{ s} = \mathbf{500\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{500\text{ J}}{5\text{ s}} = 100\text{ J/s}$ ✅

Returning to Maya's three observations during the power outage:

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing energy is a force or a tangible physical object.**
   Energy is not a force (force is measured in newtons $\text{N}$, energy in joules $\text{J}$). Nor is it matter that takes up space. Energy is a quantitative property measuring the capacity to do work.

2. **Mistake 2: Thinking that "used up" energy has ceased to exist.**
   In everyday talk, people say "the battery is dead" or "the electricity is used up." In physics, energy never vanishes; it merely degrades or disperses into less accessible forms, primarily low-temperature heat.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing thermal energy (heat) with temperature.**
   Heat is the total internal energy transferred; temperature is the measure of the average molecular kinetic energy. A warm bathtub contains much more thermal energy than a boiling cup of tea, even though the tea is at a higher temperature.

4. **Mistake 4: Dismissing light and sound as "not real energy."**
   Both light and sound exert mechanical pressures and displace matter. Sound vibrates your eardrum or can rattle windows, and light drives chemical reactions in photosynthesis and solar panels.

5. **Mistake 5: Stating the unit of energy as the watt ($\text{W}$).**
   The SI unit of energy is strictly the **joule ($\text{J}$)**. The watt ($\text{W}$) is the unit of **power** (the rate of energy transfer: $1\text{ W} = 1\text{ J/s}$, covered in Topic 7).

## Quick Recap

- **Energy** is defined as the capacity or ability to perform work. An object with energy can exert a force to displace another object.
- **SI Unit:** **Joule ($\text{J}$)**, identical to the unit of work ($1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$). $1\text{ kJ} = 1000\text{ J}$, $1\text{ MJ} = 10^6\text{ J}$.
- **Energy is a scalar quantity:** it possesses magnitude only, without direction.
- Major forms of energy: mechanical (kinetic and potential), thermal, radiant (light), acoustic (sound), electrical, chemical, nuclear, and magnetic.
- **Energy transformation:** energy converts seamlessly between forms (e.g., electric fan: electrical $\rightarrow$ kinetic).
- **Energy cannot be created or destroyed;** it only changes forms (Law of Conservation of Energy).
- **Work is the vehicle of energy transfer:** doing work on a system increases its energy; when a system does work, its energy decreases.
- The **Sun** is the primary origin of almost all renewable and non-renewable energy resources on Earth.
- Dietary energy: $1\text{ kcal} \approx 4.2\text{ kJ}$.
