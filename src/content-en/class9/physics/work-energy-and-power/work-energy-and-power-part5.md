# Topic 5: Potential Energy

In the previous topic, we investigated **kinetic energy**: the energy possessed by an object on account of its **motion**. Speeding cars, flying baseballs, rushing river currents, and falling raindrops all carry kinetic energy.

However, a fundamental physical question remains: can an object that is **completely motionless** possess the capacity to perform mechanical work?

The answer is an unequivocal yes. In this topic, we will explore **energy of position and configuration**—the energy an object holds by virtue of where it is placed or how it is strained. This stored capacity is called **potential energy**.

Throughout this topic, we take **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$** for clear, accessible calculations (unless explicitly stated otherwise). Weight and gravity were established in [gravitation-part5.md](../gravitation/gravitation-part5.md), and free-fall kinematics in [gravitation-part4.md](../gravitation/gravitation-part4.md).

## Sophia and the Rooftop Water Reservoir

Late on a Saturday afternoon, Sophia was helping the residential caretaker, **Mr. Henderson**, inspect the rooftop utility systems of her apartment building. At the edge of the terrace stood an elevated **overhead water storage tank**, positioned **$12\text{ metres}$ above the ground-level taps**. A heavy electric pump in the basement had been humming loudly, driving municipal water through vertical pipes into the tank.

"What is that pump doing, Mr. Henderson?" Sophia asked.

"It is doing heavy mechanical work against gravity," Mr. Henderson replied. "Lifting thousands of kilograms of water twelve metres straight up takes substantial electrical energy."

Shortly after, the pump clicked off automatically. The tank was full. Inside, the surface of the water was completely calm and glass-smooth: not a ripple, not a current, zero velocity. Sophia peered through the inspection hatch: "Now the water is sitting entirely motionless. It seems totally passive."

Just then, someone on the ground floor opened a high-volume garden valve. Down below, a fierce jet of water blasted out with a roaring *whoosh*, spraying droplets across the courtyard with intense force.

Sophia looked astonished: "Up here in the tank, the water wasn't moving at all! Where did that tremendous speed, pressure, and kinetic energy come from?"

Mr. Henderson smiled. "While sitting quietly in the tank, the water held a vast reserve of stored energy—a 'hidden capacity to do work' gifted to it by the pump. Let us calculate this hidden energy using physics and mathematics."

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Potential energy ($PE$)** | Energy stored in an object due to its **position**, shape, or internal physical configuration | Water stored in an elevated rooftop reservoir |
| **Gravitational potential energy** | Energy stored in an object as a result of doing work against gravity to raise it to a height | A flowerpot resting on a fourth-floor balcony |
| **Reference level (zero datum)** | The chosen baseline plane from which vertical heights are measured and where $PE$ is defined as zero | Ground level, floor, or tabletop |
| **Vertical height ($h$)** | The straight-line perpendicular elevation above the chosen reference level | A tank $12\text{ m}$ vertically above a ground tap |
| **Elastic potential energy** | Energy stored in a mechanically deformed, stretched, or compressed elastic object | A drawn archery bow or compressed coil spring |
| **Chemical potential energy** | Energy stored within chemical bonds, released during chemical reactions | Food nutrients, automotive petrol, dry-cell batteries |
| **Joule ($\text{J}$)** | The SI unit of energy: $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N} \times 1\text{ m}$ | Lifting a $1\text{ kg}$ object by $0.1\text{ m}$ requires $1\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 0.1\text{ m} = 1\text{ J}$ |

## Part A: What is Potential Energy?

In earlier topics, we defined **energy as the capacity to do work**. Kinetic energy is energy in action, resulting from motion.

Now consider a heavy concrete block resting motionless on the parapet wall of a high roof. Because it is stationary ($v = 0$), its kinetic energy is zero ($KE = 0$). Yet, if the block slips over the edge and falls, it accelerates downward and can crush an obstacle beneath it—performing significant mechanical work. That capacity to do work must have been present within the system all along.

> **Potential energy ($PE$) is defined as the energy possessed by an object by virtue of its position, shape, or internal configuration.**

The word "potential" denotes latent capability: the energy is stored silently, ready to be unleashed as kinetic energy or mechanical work whenever released.

### Three Common Forms of Potential Energy

| Category | Source of Stored Energy | Familiar Real-World Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Gravitational PE** | An object elevated to a height above a reference level against Earth's gravitational pull | Elevated water reservoirs, a raised wrecking ball, a rollercoaster at the peak of a drop |
| **Elastic PE** | An elastic material subjected to mechanical tension, compression, or twisting | A drawn archery bow, a compressed trampoline spring, a stretched rubber slingshot |
| **Chemical PE** | Energy locked in molecular bonds, liberated during exothermic combustion or metabolism | Automobile fuel, LPG cylinders, dietary calories in food, electric battery cells |

In high school physics, elastic and chemical potential energies are recognized conceptually, while **gravitational potential energy** takes center stage for mathematical analysis.

## Part B: Deriving the Formula for Gravitational Potential Energy ($mgh$)

Let us express the rooftop water problem in rigorous physics terms. Suppose an object of mass $m$ is lifted vertically through a height $h$ above a reference plane.

**Step 1:** To lift the body upward at a steady, constant speed without acceleration, the applied lifting force must balance Earth's downward gravitational pull—namely, the object's **weight** (established in Topics 1 and 2):
$$\text{Weight } F = mg$$

**Step 2:** By definition, work is the product of applied force and displacement in the direction of that force:
$$W = F \times s = (mg) \times h$$

**Step 3:** This mechanical work does not vanish, nor does it convert into speed (since the speed remained constant). Instead, it is stored within the gravitational field of the Earth-object system as **gravitational potential energy ($PE$)**:

> $$PE = m \times g \times h = mgh$$

Where:
- $m$ = mass of the body in kilograms ($\text{kg}$)
- $g$ = acceleration due to gravity ($\approx 10\text{ m/s}^2$ on Earth)
- $h$ = vertical height above the chosen reference level in metres ($\text{m}$)
- $PE$ = potential energy in joules ($\text{J}$)

### Unit Verification

$$\text{Unit of } PE = \text{kg} \times \left(\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\right) \times \text{m} = \left(\text{kg}\cdot\frac{\text{m}}{\text{s}^2}\right) \times \text{m} = \text{N} \times \text{m} = \mathbf{Joule (J)}$$

The SI unit of potential energy is the **joule ($\text{J}$)**, matching work and kinetic energy.

### Three Determining Factors

Gravitational potential energy is governed by:
1. **Mass ($m$):** Directly proportional. A heavier mass stores proportionally more $PE$ at the same height.
2. **Vertical height ($h$):** Directly proportional. Raising an object twice as high doubles its stored $PE$.
3. **Local gravity ($g$):** On celestial bodies with weaker gravity (such as the Moon), the same mass at the same height stores less $PE$.

```
         ┌───────┐
         │   m   │   <── Object of mass m
         └───────┘
             :   ▲
             :   :   Upward lifting force F = mg
          h  :   :   Downward gravitational weight = mg
             :   :
     ────────:───────────────────────  Reference Level (PE = 0)
```

> **Figure 5.1** – Lifting an object of mass $m$ at steady speed through vertical height $h$ requires an applied upward force $F = mg$. The work performed ($W = mgh$) is stored completely as gravitational potential energy ($PE = mgh$).

## Part C: The Reference Level: "Measured from Where?"

A vital question arises: from where do we measure height $h$? From the ground? From the floor of the room? From the tabletop?

The choice of zero-height is arbitrary and is called the **reference level (or zero datum)**. We define $PE = 0$ at this chosen boundary.

Crucially:

> **The absolute numerical value of $PE$ depends on the chosen reference level, but the difference in potential energy ($\Delta PE$) between any two points is strictly constant and independent of the reference level.**

Because physical work depends on differences in energy ($\Delta PE$), all physical laws yield identical real-world predictions regardless of where you place the zero line!

```
                  ┌────────┐
                  │  Book  │   <── 1.5 m above floor, 0.7 m above table
                  └────────┘
             ┌──────────────────┐  Tabletop: 0.8 m above floor
             └──┬────────────┬──┘
                │            │
    ======================================  Floor (Ground Level)
```

> **Figure 5.2** – A physics textbook of mass $2\text{ kg}$ rests on a high shelf. Measured from the floor, its height is $1.5\text{ m}$ ($PE = 30\text{ J}$). Measured from the tabletop, its height is $0.7\text{ m}$ ($PE = 14\text{ J}$). The difference between shelf and tabletop is $16\text{ J}$ regardless of reference choice.

*Note on negative potential energy:* If an object is located **below** your designated reference level (for example, a $3\text{ kg}$ weight at the bottom of a $2\text{ m}$ deep trench relative to ground level), its height is negative ($h = -2\text{ m}$), giving $PE = 3 \times 10 \times (-2) = \mathbf{-60\text{ J}}$. A negative sign simply signifies that energy must be supplied to bring the body back up to the zero reference plane.

## Part D: Solved Examples

In all calculations below, take **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$** unless specified otherwise.

### Example 1: Calculating PE Given Mass and Height

A medicine ball of mass $3\text{ kg}$ is placed on a gym storage shelf $5\text{ m}$ above the floor. Taking the floor as the reference level, find its potential energy.

$$PE = mgh = 3\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m} = 30\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{150\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{150\text{ J}}{10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m}} = \frac{150}{50} = 3\text{ kg}$ ✅

### Example 2: Finding Height Given PE and Mass

A carton of mass $4\text{ kg}$ has a potential energy of $600\text{ J}$ relative to the warehouse floor. What is its vertical height?

$$h = \frac{PE}{mg} = \frac{600\text{ J}}{4\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2} = \frac{600\text{ J}}{40\text{ N}} = \mathbf{15\text{ m}}$$

**Check:** $4\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 15\text{ m} = 600\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 3: Finding Mass Given PE and Height

An architectural sculpture mounted $6\text{ m}$ above a gallery floor has a potential energy of $900\text{ J}$. Find the mass of the sculpture.

$$m = \frac{PE}{gh} = \frac{900\text{ J}}{10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 6\text{ m}} = \frac{900}{60} = \mathbf{15\text{ kg}}$$

**Check:** $15\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 6\text{ m} = 900\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 4: The Invariance of Energy Difference Under Reference Change

A textbook of mass $2\text{ kg}$ sits on a shelf $1.5\text{ m}$ above the floor. A study table stands nearby with its tabletop $0.8\text{ m}$ above the floor (so the shelf is $0.7\text{ m}$ above the table). Find:
(a) The book's $PE$ relative to the floor.
(b) The book's $PE$ relative to the tabletop.
(c) The work required to lift the book from the tabletop to the shelf.

- (a) Relative to the floor ($h = 1.5\text{ m}$):
  $$PE_{\text{floor}} = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{30\text{ J}}$$
- (b) Relative to the tabletop ($h = 1.5 - 0.8 = 0.7\text{ m}$):
  $$PE_{\text{table}} = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 0.7\text{ m} = \mathbf{14\text{ J}}$$
- (c) Work to lift the book from table to shelf:
  $$W = mg\Delta h = 20\text{ N} \times 0.7\text{ m} = \mathbf{14\text{ J}}$$
  Alternatively, using the floor reference: $\Delta PE = 30\text{ J} - (20\text{ N} \times 0.8\text{ m}) = 30\text{ J} - 16\text{ J} = \mathbf{14\text{ J}}$.

**Check:** Although absolute values differ ($30\text{ J}$ vs $14\text{ J}$), the difference $\Delta PE = 14\text{ J}$ is identical, matching the lifting work ✅

### Example 5: Work and PE Gain of a Climber

A hiker of mass $60\text{ kg}$ climbs a staircase with a vertical elevation gain of $3\text{ m}$. Calculate the work done against gravity and the potential energy gained relative to the lower landing.

- Body weight: $F = mg = 60\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 600\text{ N}$
- Work done against gravity:
  $$W = F \times h = 600\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{1800\text{ J} = 1.8\text{ kJ}}$$
- The potential energy gained is equal to the work done:
  $$PE = \mathbf{1800\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{1800\text{ J}}{3\text{ m}} = 600\text{ N} = 60\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2$ ✅

### Example 6: Doubling the Height

A $5\text{ kg}$ payload is placed (a) at a height of $100\text{ m}$, and (b) at a height of $200\text{ m}$. Calculate its $PE$ in each state and compare the two values.

- (a) At $h = 100\text{ m}$:
  $$PE_1 = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 100\text{ m} = \mathbf{5000\text{ J} = 5\text{ kJ}}$$
- (b) At $h = 200\text{ m}$:
  $$PE_2 = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 200\text{ m} = \mathbf{10\,000\text{ J} = 10\text{ kJ}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{10\,000}{5000} = 2$, matching $\frac{200}{100} = 2$. Doubling vertical height exactly doubles $PE$ ✅

### Example 7: Stacking 20 Bricks

A construction hoist raises $20$ masonry bricks, each of mass $2\text{ kg}$, to a scaffolding platform $3\text{ m}$ high. What is the total potential energy gained by the bricks?

- Total mass: $M = 20 \times 2\text{ kg} = 40\text{ kg}$
- Total $PE$:
  $$PE = Mgh = 40\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 3\text{ m} = 400\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{1200\text{ J} = 1.2\text{ kJ}}$$

**Check:** Per brick: $2 \times 10 \times 3 = 60\text{ J}$. For $20$ bricks: $20 \times 60\text{ J} = 1200\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 8: Ball Atop a Building

A ball of mass $0.5\text{ kg}$ sits on the roof of a $20\text{ m}$ tall apartment building. Find:
(a) Its $PE$ relative to the ground.
(b) Its $PE$ relative to the ground when it reaches the midpoint of its fall ($10\text{ m}$ high).

- (a) At $h = 20\text{ m}$:
  $$PE = 0.5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 20\text{ m} = 5\text{ N} \times 20\text{ m} = \mathbf{100\text{ J}}$$
- (b) At $h = 10\text{ m}$:
  $$PE = 0.5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 10\text{ m} = 5\text{ N} \times 10\text{ m} = \mathbf{50\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** Midpoint height is half of total height, so $PE$ is halved: $\frac{100}{2} = 50\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 9: Potential Energy Difference Between Building Storeys

In an office tower, each storey has a vertical floor-to-floor height of $3\text{ m}$. An employee of mass $80\text{ kg}$ walks up one flight of stairs from the 4th floor to the 5th floor. By how much does his potential energy increase?

$$\Delta PE = mg\Delta h = 80\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 3\text{ m} = 800\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{2400\text{ J} = 2.4\text{ kJ}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{2400\text{ J}}{800\text{ N}} = 3\text{ m}$ ✅. This elevation change is completely independent of whether height is referenced to the street, sea level, or the 4th floor!

## Part E: Different Locations, Different g: Potential Energy on the Moon

Because $PE = mgh$ depends directly on local gravitational acceleration $g$, the energy stored by raising an object varies across different celestial bodies. On the Moon's surface, gravity is roughly one-sixth of Earth's: $g_{\text{Moon}} \approx 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$.

### Example 10: Earth vs. Moon Comparison

A scientific instrument package of mass $10\text{ kg}$ is lifted through a vertical height of $2\text{ m}$ on Earth ($g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$) and on the Moon ($g = 1.6\text{ m/s}^2$). Compare the potential energy gained in each environment.

- **On Earth:**
  $$PE_{\text{Earth}} = 10\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = 100\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$$
- **On the Moon:**
  $$PE_{\text{Moon}} = 10\text{ kg} \times 1.6\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = 16\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{32\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{32}{200} = 0.16 = \frac{1.6}{10}$ ✅. Because the Moon's gravitational pull is weaker, lifting the mass requires far less mechanical work, storing proportionally less potential energy.

## Part F: Potential Energy is Path-Independent

Suppose a heavy crate of mass $10\text{ kg}$ must be delivered to a storage mezzanine $2\text{ m}$ above the floor. There are three possible routes:
1. Lifted vertically straight up with a crane.
2. Pushed up a smooth, inclined ramp of length $5\text{ m}$.
3. Carried up an irregular zig-zag flight of stairs.

```
                              ┌──┐
                              └──┘  <── Crate (2 m above ground)
                             / │
                   Ramp     /  │
                   (5 m)   /   │  Vertical Height
                          /    │  h = 2 m
    =====================/═════┴==========  Ground Level
```

> **Figure 5.3** – Whether lifted vertically, rolled up a $5\text{ m}$ inclined ramp, or carried via stairs, the crate ends up at the same vertical elevation ($h = 2\text{ m}$). Potential energy depends strictly on vertical height $h$, not on the length or shape of the path taken.

### Example 11: Calculating PE for an Inclined Path

A $10\text{ kg}$ equipment crate is rolled up a frictionless ramp of length $5\text{ m}$ to reach an elevated loading dock $2\text{ m}$ above ground level (take $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$). Find the potential energy gained by the crate.

- For all paths, the vertical displacement is $h = \mathbf{2\text{ m}}$.
- The length of the ramp ($5\text{ m}$) represents the distance along the incline, not the vertical elevation.
- $$PE = mgh = 10\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = 100\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** If an error were made by using the ramp length of $5\text{ m}$, the calculation would yield $100\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = 500\text{ J}$, which is incorrect! Along the ramp, the required pushing force is smaller ($F = 40\text{ N}$), so $W = 40\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = 200\text{ J}$ ✅. Gravitational work and potential energy depend **strictly on vertical height**.

## Part G: Solving Sophia's Rooftop Water Mystery

Let us return to Sophia and Mr. Henderson at the rooftop reservoir.
- Total water mass: $m = 1000\text{ kg}$ ($1000\text{ litres} = 1\text{ cubic metre}$)
- Height above ground taps: $h = 12\text{ m}$
- Local gravity: $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$

$$PE = mgh = 1000\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 12\text{ m} = 10\,000\text{ N} \times 12\text{ m} = \mathbf{120\,000\text{ J} = 120\text{ kJ}}$$

**Check:** $\frac{120\,000\text{ J}}{10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 12\text{ m}} = 1000\text{ kg}$ ✅

**What the electric pump accomplished:**
The pump performed $120\,000\text{ J}$ of mechanical work against gravity to raise the water $12\text{ m}$ into the tank. That entire investment of work was stored silently within the water as **gravitational potential energy ($120\text{ kJ}$)**.

**What happened when the faucet opened:**
When the ground valve opened, gravity pulled the water down through the vertical pipes. As the water descended, its potential energy decreased while its velocity increased. The stored $120\text{ kJ}$ of potential energy converted directly into **kinetic energy of rushing water**, producing the high-speed, roaring spray Sophia observed!

### Example 12: Preview of Energy Conversion During Free Fall

A $2\text{ kg}$ rock is dropped from rest from a height of $5\text{ m}$ (ignore air resistance). Find the potential energy lost and the kinetic energy gained upon striking the ground.

- Initial $PE$ lost during descent:
  $$\Delta PE = mgh = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{100\text{ J}}$$
- Impact velocity from kinematics: $v^2 = 2gh = 2(10)(5) = 100\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2 \implies v = 10\text{ m/s}$.
- Kinetic energy upon impact:
  $$KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}(2\text{ kg})(10\text{ m/s})^2 = 1 \times 100 = \mathbf{100\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $\Delta PE \text{ lost } (100\text{ J}) = KE \text{ gained } (100\text{ J})$ ✅. The lost potential energy transformed precisely into kinetic energy! This preview leads directly to the core principle of **Topic 6: The Law of Conservation of Energy**.

## Part H: Hydroelectric Reservoirs: Potential Energy at Scale

A hydroelectric dam works on the exact same physical principle as Sophia's rooftop tank, but on a colossal municipal scale:
- Millions of tons of river water are trapped behind high concrete dams, holding billions of joules of gravitational potential energy.
- When gates open, water plunges down massive penstocks, converting its gravitational $PE$ into high-velocity $KE$.
- The rushing water slams against giant water turbine blades, converting kinetic energy into rotational mechanical energy, which electrical generators transform into grid electricity.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Forgetting to specify the reference level.**
   Asking "what is the potential energy of the book?" without stating a reference datum is incomplete. A book can have $30\text{ J}$ of $PE$ relative to the floor, yet $14\text{ J}$ relative to a table. Always define the reference plane.

2. **Mistake 2: Using the length of a ramp or slope instead of vertical height.**
   In $PE = mgh$, $h$ is strictly the **vertical elevation**. For a $5\text{ m}$ ramp rising $2\text{ m}$ vertically, $h = 2\text{ m}$, never $5\text{ m}$.

3. **Mistake 3: Omitting $g$ and treating mass as force.**
   Writing $PE = m \times h = 3 \times 5 = 15$ is completely wrong. Mass ($\text{kg}$) is not force. You must include $g$: $PE = 3 \times 10 \times 5 = \mathbf{150\text{ J}}$.

4. **Mistake 4: Believing potential energy depends on the path taken.**
   Whether you use an elevator, stairs, or a winding ramp, the potential energy gain for a given vertical elevation $h$ is identical (ignoring friction). Gravity is a conservative force.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming potential energy must always be positive.**
   If an object lies below the chosen reference plane, its height $h$ is negative, resulting in a valid negative potential energy (e.g., $-60\text{ J}$).

6. **Mistake 6: Mixing different values of $g$ within the same problem.**
   Be consistent: use either $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$ or $g = 9.8\text{ m/s}^2$ throughout a calculation as requested, never mixing both.

## Quick Recap

- **Potential Energy ($PE$):** Energy stored in an object due to its position, shape, or physical state.
- **Gravitational Potential Energy:** $PE = mgh$, where $m$ is mass ($\text{kg}$), $g$ is gravity ($\text{m/s}^2$), and $h$ is vertical height ($\text{m}$). SI unit: **joule ($\text{J}$)**.
- $PE$ represents the work done against gravity to lift an object at steady speed: $W = Fs = (mg)h = mgh$.
- **Reference Level:** $PE$ is measured from an arbitrary zero plane ($PE = 0$). While the numerical value depends on the reference, the **energy difference $\Delta PE$ between two heights is invariant**.
- $PE$ can be negative if the object is located below the reference datum.
- **Path Independence:** $PE$ depends only on the net vertical elevation $h$, not on the ramp length or route taken.
- Forms of potential energy include gravitational (elevated mass), elastic (stretched/compressed springs, bows), and chemical (molecular bonds).
- **Rooftop tank resolution:** The pump imparted $120\text{ kJ}$ of gravitational $PE$ to the water ($1000\text{ kg} \times 10 \times 12$). Opening the tap allowed gravity to convert that stored $PE$ into rushing $KE$.

### Answers

1. $PE = mgh = 8\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 4\text{ m} = 80\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = \mathbf{320\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $\frac{320}{10 \times 4} = 8\text{ kg}$ ✅

2. $m = \frac{PE}{gh} = \frac{500\text{ J}}{10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m}} = \frac{500}{20} = \mathbf{25\text{ kg}}$.
   Check: $25 \times 10 \times 2 = 500\text{ J}$ ✅

3. $h = \frac{PE}{mg} = \frac{240\text{ J}}{2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2} = \frac{240}{20} = \mathbf{12\text{ m}}$.
   Check: $2 \times 10 \times 12 = 240\text{ J}$ ✅

4. Total height $h = 12 \times 0.25\text{ m} = 3\text{ m}$. Weight $F = mg = 45\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 450\text{ N}$.
   $W = 450\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{1350\text{ J} = 1.35\text{ kJ}}$.
   The potential energy gained is also **$1350\text{ J}$**.
   Check: $\frac{1350}{450} = 3\text{ m}$ ✅

5. $PE = mgh = 2000\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 9\text{ m} = 20\,000\text{ N} \times 9\text{ m} = \mathbf{180\,000\text{ J} = 180\text{ kJ}}$.
   The mechanical work done by the pump is also **$180\,000\text{ J}$**.
   Check: $\frac{180\,000}{2000 \times 10} = 9\text{ m}$ ✅

6. (a) On Earth: $PE = 6\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{300\text{ J}}$.
   (b) On the Moon: $PE = 6\text{ kg} \times 1.6\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{48\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $\frac{48}{300} = 0.16 = \frac{1.6}{10}$ ✅

7. (a) Relative to the floor ($h = 2\text{ m}$): $PE = 1.5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{30\text{ J}}$.
   (b) Relative to desk surface ($h = 2 - 0.5 = 1.5\text{ m}$): $PE = 1.5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{22.5\text{ J}}$.
   Work needed to lift from desk to shelf: $W = 15\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{22.5\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $30\text{ J} - (15\text{ N} \times 0.5\text{ m}) = 30 - 7.5 = 22.5\text{ J}$ ✅

8. Vertical height $h = \mathbf{2\text{ m}}$ (the $6\text{ m}$ ramp length is along the incline, not vertical).
   $PE = 30\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{600\text{ J}}$.
   Reason: Gravitational potential energy depends strictly on the vertical elevation change against gravity, independent of the path length.
   Check: Using $6\text{ m}$ would yield $1800\text{ J}$, which is incorrect ✅

9. At $10\text{ m}$: $PE_1 = 4\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 10\text{ m} = \mathbf{400\text{ J}}$.
   At $20\text{ m}$: $PE_2 = 4\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 20\text{ m} = \mathbf{800\text{ J}}$.
   Doubling the height doubles the potential energy ($\frac{800}{400} = 2$).
   Check: $\frac{800}{400} = \frac{20}{10} = 2$ ✅

10. (a) **False** ❌: Potential energy is path-independent; it depends strictly on vertical height $h$.
    (b) **True** ✅: Positions below the chosen zero reference plane yield valid negative potential energy values.
    (c) **False** ❌: The correct formula is $PE = mgh$; omitting $g$ fails both conceptually and dimensionally.
    (d) **True** ✅: Elastic materials store elastic strain energy when mechanically deformed.

11. $PE \text{ lost} = mgh = 1\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 20\text{ m} = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$.
    Velocity squared: $v^2 = 2gh = 2(10)(20) = 400\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2 \implies v = 20\text{ m/s}$.
    $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}(1\text{ kg})(20\text{ m/s})^2 = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$.
    Observation: The potential energy lost during the fall ($200\text{ J}$) equals exactly the kinetic energy gained ($200\text{ J}$).
    Check: $200\text{ J} = 200\text{ J}$ ✅
