# Topic 6: Conservation of Energy

In the previous topic, we discovered that an elevated object holds **potential energy** due to its position: $PE = mgh$. We also saw an intriguing hint: as an object falls freely, its potential energy steadily decreases while its speed increases, converting $PE$ into **kinetic energy ($KE$)**.

This prompts a fundamental physical question: is the amount of kinetic energy gained *exactly equal* to the potential energy lost? Does any energy ever truly vanish into thin air?

In this topic, we will explore one of the most profound and inviolable laws in all of science: **the Law of Conservation of Energy**—the principle that the total energy account of the universe always balances with absolute precision.

Throughout this topic, we use **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$** for clear, straightforward arithmetic. Free-fall kinematics ($v^2 = 2gh$) were developed in [gravitation-part4.md](../gravitation/gravitation-part4.md).

## Maya, Marcus, and the Rollercoaster's First Drop

Maya was standing near the front of a long queue at a theme park, eagerly watching the giant steel rollercoaster. The ride manager, **Marcus**, stood by the master control console. Maya observed the train of passenger cars being towed slowly up the **first towering hill** by a heavy motorized chain lift, the rhythmic *clack-clack-clack* echoing across the plaza.

Watching several cycles, Maya noticed a fascinating engineering detail: **every successive hill along the track was noticeably shorter than the one before it.** The final hill was the lowest of all.

Maya turned to Marcus: "Marcus, with no engine pushing the cars, how does the train manage to climb up the next hill? And why are all the subsequent hills designed shorter than the first one? Why not make them all equally tall?"

Marcus smiled warmly. "A brilliant engineering question, Maya! Here is the answer in a single sentence: **the chain motor on the first hill deposits the entire 'energy bank balance' for the whole journey.** As the coaster hurtles along the rails, it trades potential energy for kinetic energy and back again. But it cannot manufacture new energy on its own, and a small toll is deducted on every hill."

"A bank balance? What deducts from it?" Maya asked.

"Let us analyze the numbers and master the universal law of physics that governs every ride, every fall, and every machine," Marcus replied.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Law of Conservation of Energy** | Energy can neither be created nor destroyed; it can only transform from one form to another. The total energy of an isolated system remains constant | A falling stone converting its $PE$ into $KE$ |
| **Mechanical energy ($ME$)** | The sum of kinetic energy and potential energy: $ME = KE + PE$ | A $2\text{ kg}$ stone at $10\text{ m}$: $PE = 200\text{ J}, KE = 0 \implies ME = 200\text{ J}$ |
| **Isolated system** | A physical system that does not exchange matter or energy with its external surroundings | A falling mass in a complete vacuum |
| **Energy transformation** | The conversion of energy from one physical state or form into another | Gravitational $PE \rightarrow KE \rightarrow$ Thermal heat and sound |
| **Frictional resistance** | A contact force between rubbing surfaces that opposes relative motion, converting mechanical energy into heat | Wheel bearings and brake pads warming up |
| **Air resistance (drag)** | Frictional resistive force exerted by atmospheric gas on a moving body | Aerodynamic drag slowing a skydiver or roller coaster car |
| **Perpetual motion machine** | A hypothetical machine that produces continuous work forever without an external energy supply (physically impossible) | A wheel that supposedly spins indefinitely without stopping |

## Part A: The Law of Conservation of Energy

The **Law of Conservation of Energy** is one of the foundational pillars of physical science:

> **Energy can neither be created from nothing, nor can it be destroyed into nothingness. It can only be transformed from one form into another.**
> **The total energy of an isolated system remains strictly constant over time.**

Two critical caveats to keep in mind:
1. **Total energy** encompasses *all* possible forms: kinetic, gravitational potential, elastic potential, thermal (heat), acoustic (sound), radiant (light), and chemical.
2. While a specific form of energy (such as kinetic energy) may decrease, that exact deficit must reappear in another form. The grand total is eternally conserved.

### Mechanical Energy Conservation

The sum of kinetic energy and potential energy is called **mechanical energy ($ME$)**:

$$ME = KE + PE$$

In an idealized system where dissipative forces like friction and air drag are absent (or negligible):

> $$KE + PE = \text{constant}$$

Whenever potential energy decreases by a certain number of joules, kinetic energy must increase by the exact same amount:

$$\Delta KE = -\Delta PE$$

In the real world, friction and air resistance convert a fraction of mechanical energy into heat and sound, which we will examine in Part D.

## Part B: Conservation of Energy in Free Fall

Consider a rock of mass **$m = 2\text{ kg}$** released from rest from a height of **$h = 20\text{ m}$** above the ground (neglecting air resistance, taking $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$).

Let us compute $PE$, $KE$, speed $v$, and total mechanical energy at various stages of the fall:
- At any elevation $h$, potential energy is $PE = mgh = 2 \times 10 \times h = 20h\text{ J}$.
- The vertical distance fallen is $x = (20 - h)\text{ m}$.
- From free-fall kinematics: $v = \sqrt{2gx} = \sqrt{2(10)(20 - h)}$.
- Kinetic energy: $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$.

### Energy Distribution Table During Free Fall

| Height $h$ ($\text{m}$) | Distance Fallen $x$ ($\text{m}$) | $PE = mgh$ ($\text{J}$) | Speed $v = \sqrt{2gx}$ ($\text{m/s}$) | $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$ ($\text{J}$) | Total Energy $KE + PE$ ($\text{J}$) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **$20$ (Release)** | $0$ | **$400$** | $0$ | **$0$** | **$400$** |
| **$15$** | $5$ | **$300$** | $\sqrt{100} = 10.0$ | $\frac{1}{2}(2)(10)^2 = \mathbf{100}$ | **$400$** |
| **$10$ (Midpoint)** | $10$ | **$200$** | $\sqrt{200} \approx 14.1$ | $\frac{1}{2}(2)(200) = \mathbf{200}$ | **$400$** |
| **$5$** | $15$ | **$100$** | $\sqrt{300} \approx 17.3$ | $\frac{1}{2}(2)(300) = \mathbf{300}$ | **$400$** |
| **$0$ (Impact)** | $20$ | **$0$** | $\sqrt{400} = 20.0$ | $\frac{1}{2}(2)(400) = \mathbf{400}$ | **$400$** |

### Step-by-Step Verification of Intermediate Heights

- **At $h = 15\text{ m}$:** Distance fallen $x = 5\text{ m}$.
  $$v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 5} = \sqrt{100} = 10\text{ m/s}$$
  $$KE = \frac{1}{2}(2\text{ kg})(10\text{ m/s})^2 = 100\text{ J}$$
  $$PE = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 15\text{ m} = 300\text{ J}$$
  $$\text{Total } ME = 100\text{ J} + 300\text{ J} = \mathbf{400\text{ J}}$$ ✅

- **At $h = 10\text{ m}$ (Midpoint):** Distance fallen $x = 10\text{ m}$.
  $$v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 10} = \sqrt{200} \approx 14.14\text{ m/s}$$
  $$KE = \frac{1}{2}(2\text{ kg})(200) = 200\text{ J}$$
  $$PE = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 10\text{ m} = 200\text{ J}$$
  $$\text{Total } ME = 200\text{ J} + 200\text{ J} = \mathbf{400\text{ J}}$$ ✅

Three fundamental observations emerge:
1. **At the highest point ($h = 20\text{ m}$):** All mechanical energy is pure **potential energy ($400\text{ J}$)**, and $KE = 0$.
2. **At ground level ($h = 0\text{ m}$):** All energy has converted completely into **kinetic energy ($400\text{ J}$)**, and $PE = 0$.
3. **At the exact vertical midpoint ($h = 10\text{ m}$):** Potential energy and kinetic energy are **exactly equal** ($200\text{ J}$ each).

At every single instant, **$KE + PE = 400\text{ J}$**.

```
 Height h (m)     Potential Energy (PE)       Kinetic Energy (KE)
     20           [####]                      [    ]
     15           [### ]                      [#   ]
     10           [##  ]                      [##  ]
      5           [#   ]                      [### ]
      0           [    ]                      [####]
                  (PE decreases)              (KE increases)
                  Total bars across any row = 4 (400 J)
```

> **Figure 6.1** – Energy distribution bars during free fall. Each `#` represents $100\text{ J}$. As potential energy diminishes, kinetic energy grows by the identical quantity, keeping the total constant at $400\text{ J}$.

## Part C: General Mathematical Proof of Free-Fall Energy Conservation

The numerical table is a specific demonstration. Now let us prove that $KE + PE = \text{constant}$ holds for **any mass $m$** dropped from **any initial height $h$**.

Suppose a body of mass $m$ is released from rest ($u = 0$) at height $h$ above the ground. Consider an arbitrary instant when it has fallen a vertical distance $x$. Its remaining height above ground is $(h - x)$.

**Step 1 (Speed):** Using $v^2 = u^2 + 2as$ with $u = 0, a = g, s = x$:
$$v^2 = 2gx$$

**Step 2 (Kinetic Energy):**
$$KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}m(2gx) = \mathbf{mgx}$$

**Step 3 (Potential Energy):** The body's current height above the ground is $(h - x)$:
$$PE = mg(h - x) = \mathbf{mgh - mgx}$$

**Step 4 (Total Mechanical Energy):** Adding $KE$ and $PE$:
$$ME = KE + PE = mgx + (mgh - mgx) = \mathbf{mgh}$$

Notice that the position variable $x$ cancels out completely!
At any point during the fall—regardless of how far the body has dropped—its total mechanical energy is always:

$$KE + PE = mgh = \text{Initial Potential Energy} = \text{Constant}$$

Furthermore, from $v^2 = 2gx \implies v = \sqrt{2gx}$, mass $m$ is absent. Therefore, **all freely falling bodies accelerate at the exact same rate and hit the ground with the exact same speed, regardless of their mass** (verified in Example 8).

## Part D: Conservation of Energy in Other Physical Systems

### 1. The Simple Pendulum

A simple pendulum consists of a small mass (bob) suspended from a fixed point by a light string. When displaced to angle $\theta$ and released, it oscillates back and forth.

```
          O   (pivot suspension point)
         /:\
        / : \
       /  :  \
      /   :   \
     A    :    B     <── A and B: extreme turning points (height h)
      ' . : . '
          C          <── C: lowest equilibrium position

    Points A & B: All energy is PE (KE = 0, v = 0)
    Point C     : All energy is KE (PE = 0, speed is maximum)
```

> **Figure 6.2** – In an ideal simple pendulum, mechanical energy shuttles between pure potential energy at the extreme turning points (A and B) and pure kinetic energy at the lowest point (C). In the absence of air drag, the bob swings up to the exact same vertical height $h$ on both sides.

- **At extreme points A and B:** The bob momentarily halts ($v = 0$), so $KE = 0$. Its elevation is highest ($h$), so its energy is purely potential ($PE = mgh$).
- **Descending toward C:** Potential energy decreases as height drops; the bob speeds up as $PE \rightarrow KE$.
- **At the lowest point C:** Height is zero ($PE = 0$), and speed is at its absolute maximum. All energy is kinetic:
  $$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v = \sqrt{2gh}$$

### 2. A Ball Thrown Vertically Upward ($KE \rightarrow PE$)

When a ball is launched straight up with initial speed $u$:
- At ground level, it possesses maximum kinetic energy ($\frac{1}{2}mu^2$) and zero potential energy.
- As it ascends against gravity, gravity performs negative work, decelerating the ball. Kinetic energy smoothly converts into potential energy.
- At maximum height $H$, the vertical velocity is zero ($v = 0$). All mechanical energy is potential: $mgH = \frac{1}{2}mu^2 \implies H = \frac{u^2}{2g}$.
- As it falls back down, $PE$ converts back into $KE$, striking the launch point at the exact original launch speed $u$.

### 3. The Rollercoaster

A rollercoaster is a prime engineering realization of energy conservation:

```
   First Hill
   (Chain lift delivers
   all initial PE)
       /\
      /  \             /\  Second Hill
     /    \           /  \ (Designed shorter)
    /      \         /    \
   /        \_______/      \_____
  Start        Dip            Dip
```

> **Figure 6.3** – Rollercoaster track profile: the first hill is the highest because the lift chain deposits all the mechanical energy. Subsequent hills must be progressively lower because friction and air resistance continuously dissipate mechanical energy into heat and sound.

### 4. Child on a Playground Slide (Frictional Dissipation)

When a child slides down a playground slide of height $h$, pure conservation of mechanical energy would predict an exit speed of $v = \sqrt{2gh}$.

In reality, friction acts between the slide and the child's clothing. Friction does negative work, converting a fraction of the gravitational potential energy into **thermal energy (heating the slide surface)** and **sound**. The final kinetic energy is therefore lower than $mgh$:

$$\text{Initial } PE = \text{Final } KE + \text{Thermal Heat} + \text{Acoustic Sound}$$

Energy is not destroyed; it merely transformed into non-mechanical forms!

### 5. Bouncing Ball

When an elastic ball is dropped from a height $h_1$, it bounces up to a lower height $h_2 < h_1$. With each bounce, its peak height diminishes until it comes to rest on the floor.

Why? During impact, the ball deforms elastically. Internal friction within the rubber molecules and the contact surface generates **thermal energy**, and the collision emits a sharp acoustic **thump**. The lost mechanical energy has dispersed into the environment as ambient heat and sound.

### 6. Hydroelectric Generation and Wind Power

- **Hydroelectric Plant:** Gravitational potential energy stored in elevated reservoir water $\rightarrow$ Kinetic energy of plunging water $\rightarrow$ Rotational mechanical energy of water turbines $\rightarrow$ Electrical energy in generators.
- **Wind Turbine:** Kinetic energy of moving atmospheric air $\rightarrow$ Rotational mechanical energy of giant blades $\rightarrow$ Electrical energy.

In neither facility is energy created; it is gathered and transformed.

### 7. The Impossibility of Perpetual Motion Machines

A **perpetual motion machine of the first kind** is an imagined device that could run continuously without any external energy input, or produce more work output than energy consumed ($W_{\text{out}} > E_{\text{in}}$).

Because friction, air drag, and material deformation inevitably convert mechanical energy into dispersed heat and sound, every physical mechanism experiences energy dissipation. To continue operating, energy must continuously be supplied from an external source. **Perpetual motion machines violate the first law of thermodynamics and are physically impossible.**

### Solving the Rollercoaster Mystery

Now we can return to Maya's questions for Marcus:
1. **How the train climbs without an engine:** The electric lift motor on the first hill performs mechanical work $W = mgh_1$, depositing a massive store of potential energy ($PE_1$). When released, that potential energy converts into kinetic energy on the descent, and that kinetic energy carries the train up the next hill!
2. **Why subsequent hills are progressively shorter:** As the coaster speeds along the steel track, friction in the wheel bearings and aerodynamic drag against the rushing air continuously convert mechanical energy into **heat (warming the rails and wheels) and sound (the roaring rumble of the ride)**.
3. While the *total energy of the universe* is strictly conserved, the *mechanical energy ($KE + PE$)* of the train gradually decreases. If the second hill were as tall as the first, the coaster would lack the energy needed to reach the crest and would stall halfway up.
4. Hence, civil engineers intentionally make every successive hill lower so that the remaining mechanical energy is always sufficient to crest each summit safely.

Marcus pointed at the speeding cars: "You see, Maya? The lift motor made a deposit, friction deducted service fees on every turn, and the train traded its balance between kinetic and potential energy. Physics kept the books balanced to the exact last joule."

## Part E: Solved Examples

In all calculations below, take **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$**.

### Example 1: Finding Impact Speed Using Energy Conservation

A stone is dropped from a height of **$5\text{ m}$**. Find its speed just before striking the ground (neglecting air resistance).

- By conservation of mechanical energy:
  $$\text{Initial } PE = \text{Final } KE$$
  $$mgh = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \implies v^2 = 2gh$$
  $$v = \sqrt{2 \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m}} = \sqrt{100} = \mathbf{10\text{ m/s}}$$

**Check via kinematics:** $v^2 = u^2 + 2gs = 0 + 2(10)(5) = 100 \implies v = 10\text{ m/s}$ ✅

### Example 2: Ball Falling from a High Shelf

A tennis ball rolls off a cupboard shelf **$3.2\text{ m}$** high and falls to the floor. What is its impact speed?

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 3.2\text{ m}} = \sqrt{64} = \mathbf{8\text{ m/s}}$$

**Check:** $(8\text{ m/s})^2 = 64 = 2(10)(3.2)$ ✅

### Example 3: At What Height Does KE Equal PE?

A $2\text{ kg}$ stone is dropped from a height of $20\text{ m}$. At what height above the ground are its kinetic energy and potential energy equal?

- Total mechanical energy:
  $$E_{\text{total}} = mgh = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 20\text{ m} = 400\text{ J}$$
- When $KE = PE$, each must equal half the total energy:
  $$PE = \frac{400\text{ J}}{2} = 200\text{ J}$$
- Finding the corresponding height $h'$:
  $$mgh' = 200\text{ J} \implies 2 \times 10 \times h' = 200 \implies h' = \frac{200}{20} = \mathbf{10\text{ m}}$$

**Check:** At $h' = 10\text{ m}$, the stone has fallen $10\text{ m}$, so $v^2 = 2(10)(10) = 200$. $KE = \frac{1}{2}(2)(200) = 200\text{ J} = PE$ ✅

### Example 4: A Ball Thrown Straight Upward

A ball of mass $0.5\text{ kg}$ is thrown vertically upward with an initial speed of $10\text{ m/s}$. Find its potential energy, kinetic energy, and speed when it is at an elevation of $3\text{ m}$ above the launch point.

- Initial launch energy:
  $$KE_{\text{initial}} = \frac{1}{2}mu^2 = \frac{1}{2}(0.5\text{ kg})(10\text{ m/s})^2 = 25\text{ J}, \quad PE_{\text{initial}} = 0$$
  $$E_{\text{total}} = \mathbf{25\text{ J}}$$
- At $h = 3\text{ m}$:
  $$PE = mgh = 0.5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{15\text{ J}}$$
  $$KE = E_{\text{total}} - PE = 25\text{ J} - 15\text{ J} = \mathbf{10\text{ J}}$$
- Speed at $3\text{ m}$:
  $$\frac{1}{2}mv^2 = 10\text{ J} \implies v^2 = \frac{2 \times 10\text{ J}}{0.5\text{ kg}} = 40\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2 \implies v = \sqrt{40} \approx \mathbf{6.32\text{ m/s}}$$

**Check via kinematics:** $v^2 = u^2 - 2gh = 10^2 - 2(10)(3) = 100 - 60 = 40\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$ ✅

### Example 5: Maximum Height Reached by a Projectile

A stone is projected vertically upward with an initial velocity of $15\text{ m/s}$. Using energy conservation, calculate the maximum height it reaches.

- At the highest point, $v = 0$, so all initial kinetic energy has converted into potential energy:
  $$\frac{1}{2}mu^2 = mgH \implies H = \frac{u^2}{2g}$$
  $$H = \frac{(15\text{ m/s})^2}{2 \times 10\text{ m/s}^2} = \frac{225}{20} = \mathbf{11.25\text{ m}}$$

**Check:** For $m = 0.5\text{ kg}$, $KE_{\text{initial}} = \frac{1}{2}(0.5)(225) = 56.25\text{ J}$. At top, $PE = 0.5 \times 10 \times 11.25 = 56.25\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 6: Speed of a Pendulum Bob

The bob of a long pendulum is pulled aside until it is raised $0.8\text{ m}$ vertically above its lowest point, and then released from rest. What is its speed as it swings through the lowest point?

$$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 0.8\text{ m}} = \sqrt{16} = \mathbf{4\text{ m/s}}$$

**Check:** $v^2 = 16 = 2(10)(0.8)$ ✅

### Example 7: Rollercoaster Analysis (Idealized Frictionless Case)

A $500\text{ kg}$ rollercoaster car is released from rest at the top of a hill $40\text{ m}$ high.
(a) What is its speed at the bottom of the valley?
(b) What is its speed at the crest of the next hill, which is $25\text{ m}$ high?

- Reference level: Bottom of valley ($h = 0$).
  $$E_{\text{total}} = mgh_1 = 500\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 40\text{ m} = \mathbf{200\,000\text{ J} = 200\text{ kJ}}$$
- (a) At the bottom ($h = 0$):
  $$v = \sqrt{2gh_1} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 40} = \sqrt{800} \approx \mathbf{28.28\text{ m/s}}$$
- (b) At the $25\text{ m}$ hill: The vertical drop from the peak is $\Delta h = 40\text{ m} - 25\text{ m} = 15\text{ m}$.
  $$v = \sqrt{2g\Delta h} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 15} = \sqrt{300} \approx \mathbf{17.32\text{ m/s}}$$

**Check via energy:** At $25\text{ m}$, $PE = 500 \times 10 \times 25 = 125\,000\text{ J}$. $KE = \frac{1}{2}(500)(300) = 75\,000\text{ J}$. Total = $125\,000 + 75\,000 = 200\,000\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 8: Independence of Mass in Free Fall

Two metal spheres, one of mass $1\text{ kg}$ and one of mass $10\text{ kg}$, are dropped simultaneously from a height of $20\text{ m}$. Compare their impact speeds and their kinetic energies upon hitting the ground.

- For both spheres:
  $$v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 20} = \sqrt{400} = \mathbf{20\text{ m/s}}$$
- Kinetic energy of the $1\text{ kg}$ sphere:
  $$KE_1 = \frac{1}{2}(1\text{ kg})(20\text{ m/s})^2 = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$$
- Kinetic energy of the $10\text{ kg}$ sphere:
  $$KE_{10} = \frac{1}{2}(10\text{ kg})(20\text{ m/s})^2 = \mathbf{2000\text{ J}}$$

Both strike the ground with the **exact same speed ($20\text{ m/s}$)**. The $10\text{ kg}$ sphere possesses ten times more kinetic energy because it has ten times the mass to accelerate, which required ten times more potential energy ($2000\text{ J}$ vs $200\text{ J}$) to raise originally.

### Example 9: Accounting for Friction on a Slide

A wooden crate of mass $10\text{ kg}$ slides down an inclined ramp from a height of $5\text{ m}$ and reaches the bottom with a speed of $8\text{ m/s}$. How much mechanical energy was transformed into heat and sound? What percentage of the initial energy was dissipated?

- Initial potential energy at the top:
  $$PE_{\text{initial}} = mgh = 10\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{500\text{ J}}$$
- Actual kinetic energy at the bottom:
  $$KE_{\text{bottom}} = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10\text{ kg})(8\text{ m/s})^2 = 5 \times 64 = \mathbf{320\text{ J}}$$
- Energy dissipated as heat and sound:
  $$E_{\text{dissipated}} = 500\text{ J} - 320\text{ J} = \mathbf{180\text{ J}}$$
- Percentage dissipated:
  $$\frac{180\text{ J}}{500\text{ J}} \times 100 = \mathbf{36\%}$$

**Check:** $320\text{ J} + 180\text{ J} = 500\text{ J}$ (Total energy strictly conserved) ✅

### Example 10: Energy Loss in a Bouncing Ball

A rubber ball is dropped from a height of $2\text{ m}$. After striking the hard ground, it rebounds to a peak height of $1.6\text{ m}$ ($80\%$ of its release height). What percentage of its mechanical energy was converted into thermal and acoustic energy?

- Because $PE = mgh$, energy is directly proportional to height.
  $$\text{Fraction of energy retained} = \frac{h_2}{h_1} = \frac{1.6\text{ m}}{2.0\text{ m}} = 0.80 = 80\%$$
- Percentage dissipated as heat and sound:
  $$100\% - 80\% = \mathbf{20\%}$$

**Check:** Let $m = 0.5\text{ kg}$. $PE_1 = 0.5 \times 10 \times 2 = 10\text{ J}$. $PE_2 = 0.5 \times 10 \times 1.6 = 8\text{ J}$. Loss = $2\text{ J} \implies \frac{2}{10} = 20\%$ ✅

### Example 11: Free Fall from a 45 m Tower After 2 Seconds

A stone of mass $2\text{ kg}$ is dropped from a tower $45\text{ m}$ high. Analyze its energy after falling for $t = 2\text{ s}$ and upon striking the ground.

- **Initial state:** $PE = 2 \times 10 \times 45 = \mathbf{900\text{ J}}, KE = 0$.
- **At $t = 2\text{ s}$:**
  Distance fallen: $x = \frac{1}{2}gt^2 = \frac{1}{2}(10)(2)^2 = 20\text{ m}$.
  Speed: $v = gt = 10 \times 2 = 20\text{ m/s}$.
  $$KE = \frac{1}{2}(2\text{ kg})(20\text{ m/s})^2 = \mathbf{400\text{ J}}$$
  Remaining height: $h = 45\text{ m} - 20\text{ m} = 25\text{ m}$.
  $$PE = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 25\text{ m} = \mathbf{500\text{ J}}$$
  $$ME = 400\text{ J} + 500\text{ J} = \mathbf{900\text{ J}}$$ ✅
- **At ground level ($x = 45\text{ m}$):**
  $$v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 45} = \sqrt{900} = 30\text{ m/s}$$
  $$KE = \frac{1}{2}(2)(30)^2 = \mathbf{900\text{ J}}, \quad PE = 0$$
  $$ME = \mathbf{900\text{ J}}$$ ✅

**Check:** Potential energy lost after $2\text{ s}$ ($900 - 500 = 400\text{ J}$) equals kinetic energy gained ($400\text{ J}$) ✅

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Claiming that energy is "destroyed" when friction is present.**
   Friction does not destroy energy; it transforms mechanical energy into non-mechanical forms (heat and acoustic vibrations). The total energy of the universe remains invariant.

2. **Mistake 2: Applying $KE + PE = \text{constant}$ in systems with significant friction.**
   The conservation of *mechanical energy alone* holds only in conservative (frictionless) systems. If friction is present, write: $ME_{\text{initial}} = ME_{\text{final}} + E_{\text{friction}}$.

3. **Mistake 3: Confusing speed with kinetic energy.**
   Doubling the speed quadruples kinetic energy ($KE \propto v^2$). When speed goes from $10\text{ m/s}$ to $20\text{ m/s}$, $KE$ jumps from $100\text{ J}$ to $400\text{ J}$ for a $2\text{ kg}$ object.

4. **Mistake 4: Using the absolute hill height instead of the vertical drop $\Delta h$.**
   When calculating speed on a $25\text{ m}$ hill after descending from a $40\text{ m}$ peak, use $\Delta h = 40 - 25 = 15\text{ m}$, not $25\text{ m}$.

5. **Mistake 5: Assuming heavier objects fall faster because they have more potential energy.**
   In $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, mass cancels completely. A $10\text{ kg}$ bowling ball and a $0.1\text{ kg}$ tennis ball hit the ground at the identical speed (neglecting air resistance).

6. **Mistake 6: Forgetting to take the square root.**
   If $v^2 = 64\text{ m}^2/\text{s}^2$, remember to take $v = \sqrt{64} = 8\text{ m/s}$, not $64\text{ m/s}$.

7. **Mistake 7: Dismissing thermal energy and sound as "unimportant."**
   Thermal dissipation accounts for why real pendulums eventually stop swinging and why perpetual motion machines are impossible.

## Quick Recap

- **Law of Conservation of Energy:** Energy cannot be created or destroyed; it merely changes forms. The total energy of an isolated system is strictly constant.
- **Mechanical Energy:** $ME = KE + PE$. In the absence of friction and air resistance, $ME$ is conserved: $KE + PE = \text{constant}$.
- **Free Fall:** At the peak, all energy is $PE$; at the base, all energy is $KE$; at the exact vertical midpoint, $KE = PE$.
- **Free-Fall Speed:** $v = \sqrt{2gh}$, which is completely independent of the falling object's mass.
- **Simple Pendulum:** Energy cycles between pure $PE$ at the extreme turn points and pure $KE$ at the lowest equilibrium point.
- **Real-World Systems:** Friction and air drag convert mechanical energy into thermal energy (heat) and acoustic energy (sound), which is why coasters need smaller subsequent hills and bouncing balls eventually stop.
- **Perpetual motion machines are impossible** because frictional dissipation cannot be completely eliminated.

## Practice Questions

1. A $4\text{ kg}$ object is dropped from rest from a height of $10\text{ m}$. Using $g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$, calculate:
   (a) Its potential energy at the release point.
   (b) Its speed just before striking the ground.
   (c) Its kinetic energy at impact.
   (d) Its kinetic and potential energy when it is $5\text{ m}$ above the ground.

2. A pendulum bob is released from rest at a height of $0.45\text{ m}$ above its lowest equilibrium point. Find its speed as it passes through the lowest point.

3. A baseball of mass $0.2\text{ kg}$ is thrown vertically upward with an initial speed of $12\text{ m/s}$. Calculate:
   (a) The maximum height reached.
   (b) Its potential energy, kinetic energy, and speed at a height of $4\text{ m}$ above the launch point.

4. A $400\text{ kg}$ rollercoaster car is released from rest at the summit of a $30\text{ m}$ hill. Neglecting friction, find its speed at the bottom of the valley, and its speed at the top of the next $10\text{ m}$ hill.

5. A $25\text{ kg}$ child slides down a playground slide of vertical height $2\text{ m}$ and reaches the bottom with a speed of $5\text{ m/s}$. How much mechanical energy was transformed into heat and sound? What percentage of the initial potential energy does this represent?

6. A rubber ball dropped from a height of $2\text{ m}$ rebounds to a height of only $1.5\text{ m}$ on its first bounce. What percentage of its mechanical energy was lost to the environment? What forms did this lost energy take?

7. Two stones, one of mass $1\text{ kg}$ and one of mass $5\text{ kg}$, are dropped simultaneously from the same height. Compare:
   (a) Their velocities just before striking the ground.
   (b) Their kinetic energies at ground level.

8. A pebble is dropped from a height of $20\text{ m}$, and a second pebble is dropped from $80\text{ m}$. By what factor is the final impact speed of the second pebble greater than that of the first?

9. State whether each statement is **True** or **False**:
   (a) The total energy of an isolated system continuously decreases whenever friction is present.
   (b) At the highest point of a vertically projected ball, its mechanical energy is entirely potential energy.
   (c) In a vacuum, a heavy object dropped from a given height strikes the ground with a greater speed than a lighter object.
   (d) A perpetual motion machine could work indefinitely if friction were made very small.

### Answers

1. $m = 4\text{ kg}, h = 10\text{ m}$.
   (a) $PE = mgh = 4\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 10\text{ m} = \mathbf{400\text{ J}}$.
   (b) $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 10} = \sqrt{200} \approx \mathbf{14.14\text{ m/s}}$.
   (c) $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2 = \frac{1}{2}(4)(200) = \mathbf{400\text{ J}}$ (equals initial $PE$).
   (d) At $h = 5\text{ m}$: $PE = 4 \times 10 \times 5 = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$; $KE = 400\text{ J} - 200\text{ J} = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$.
   Check: $KE + PE = 200 + 200 = 400\text{ J}$ ✅

2. $v = \sqrt{2gh} = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 0.45} = \sqrt{9} = \mathbf{3\text{ m/s}}$.
   Check: $2(10)(0.45) = 9$, and $3^2 = 9$ ✅

3. (a) Total energy = initial $KE = \frac{1}{2}(0.2\text{ kg})(12\text{ m/s})^2 = 0.1 \times 144 = 14.4\text{ J}$.
   At peak, $mgH = 14.4 \implies 0.2 \times 10 \times H = 14.4 \implies 2H = 14.4 \implies H = \mathbf{7.2\text{ m}}$.
   Check: $H = \frac{u^2}{2g} = \frac{144}{20} = 7.2\text{ m}$ ✅
   (b) At $h = 4\text{ m}$:
   $PE = mgh = 0.2 \times 10 \times 4 = \mathbf{8\text{ J}}$.
   $KE = 14.4\text{ J} - 8\text{ J} = \mathbf{6.4\text{ J}}$.
   Speed: $v = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.4}{0.2}} = \sqrt{64} = \mathbf{8\text{ m/s}}$.
   Check: $v^2 = u^2 - 2gh = 144 - 2(10)(4) = 144 - 80 = 64 \implies v = 8\text{ m/s}$ ✅

4. At the bottom: $v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 30} = \sqrt{600} \approx \mathbf{24.49\text{ m/s}}$.
   At crest of $10\text{ m}$ hill: $\Delta h = 30\text{ m} - 10\text{ m} = 20\text{ m}$.
   $v = \sqrt{2 \times 10 \times 20} = \sqrt{400} = \mathbf{20\text{ m/s}}$.
   Check: At $10\text{ m}$, $PE = 400 \times 10 \times 10 = 40\,000\text{ J}$, $KE = \frac{1}{2}(400)(400) = 80\,000\text{ J}$. Total = $120\,000\text{ J} = 400 \times 10 \times 30$ ✅

5. Initial $PE = mgh = 25\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{500\text{ J}}$.
   Final $KE = \frac{1}{2}(25\text{ kg})(5\text{ m/s})^2 = 12.5 \times 25 = \mathbf{312.5\text{ J}}$.
   Thermal and acoustic energy = $500\text{ J} - 312.5\text{ J} = \mathbf{187.5\text{ J}}$.
   Percentage = $\frac{187.5}{500} \times 100 = \mathbf{37.5\%}$.
   Check: $312.5 + 187.5 = 500\text{ J}$ ✅

6. Fraction of energy retained: $\frac{1.5}{2.0} = 0.75 = 75\%$.
   Percentage lost: $100\% - 75\% = \mathbf{25\%}$.
   The lost mechanical energy transformed into **thermal energy (heating the ball and floor)** and **acoustic sound energy** during impact.
   Check: For $m = 1\text{ kg}$, $PE_1 = 20\text{ J}, PE_2 = 15\text{ J}$, loss = $5\text{ J} = 25\%$ ✅

7. (a) Both hit with **identical velocities**: $v = \sqrt{2gh}$ (mass is absent from the velocity equation).
   (b) Kinetic energy: $KE = \frac{1}{2}mv^2$. Since $v$ is identical, $KE \propto m$. The $5\text{ kg}$ stone has **five times the kinetic energy** of the $1\text{ kg}$ stone ($5\times$).
   Check: For $h = 20\text{ m}$, $v^2 = 400$. $KE_1 = 200\text{ J}$, $KE_5 = 1000\text{ J} \implies \frac{1000}{200} = 5$ ✅

8. $v_1 = \sqrt{2(10)(20)} = \sqrt{400} = 20\text{ m/s}$.
   $v_2 = \sqrt{2(10)(80)} = \sqrt{1600} = 40\text{ m/s}$.
   $\text{Ratio} = \frac{40}{20} = \mathbf{2\text{ times}}$.
   Quadrupling the drop height doubles the final impact speed ($v \propto \sqrt{h}$).

9. (a) **False** ❌: The *total* energy of an isolated system is strictly constant; only the *mechanical energy* decreases as it converts into heat and sound.
   (b) **True** ✅: At the top of its trajectory, $v = 0 \implies KE = 0$, so all mechanical energy is potential energy.
   (c) **False** ❌: In a vacuum, acceleration due to gravity is independent of mass, so all bodies land at identical speeds.
   (d) **False** ❌: Even infinitesimal friction drains energy over time; without an external power source, all machines eventually halt.
