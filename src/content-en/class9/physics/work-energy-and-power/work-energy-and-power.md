# Topic 1: What is Work?

In previous chapters, we learned what a force is, how unbalanced forces alter an object's state of motion, how stationary objects begin to move, and how fluids exert pressure. Now, a natural question arises: when a force **actually moves an object**, how much **work** has been done? And how **rapidly** was that work accomplished? In this chapter, we will uncover the precise scientific meanings of **work**, **energy**, and **power**. We begin with the foundational concept: **work**.

## Maya and Marcus at the Community Centre Renovation

A community centre was undergoing major renovations. The site manager, **Marcus**, was busy plastering an exterior wall. Sitting nearby on a clean stack of timber was **Maya**, a high school student. Her midterm physics exam was scheduled for the next morning, so she was studying with total concentration: brow furrowed, eyes glued to her textbook, thoroughly exhausted from hours of mental effort.

Across the courtyard, a construction assistant named **Liam** was carrying stacks of bricks up a wooden ladder to the upper scaffold. He lifted each stack of bricks, climbed the ladder step by step, placed them at the top, and climbed back down for the next batch. His shirt was soaked with sweat and he was breathing heavily.

Closing her textbook with a sigh, Maya said, "Marcus, Liam and I have both worked so hard this afternoon! Liam moved all those heavy bricks, and I spent hours wrestling with complex physics chapters. We both did an immense amount of work, didn't we?"

Marcus smiled warmly. "In everyday conversational English, you are absolutely right, Maya. But **in physics**, only **one of you did work**."

Maya stared in disbelief: "Only one of us? But my brain is completely fried!" Who did work in the physics sense, who did not, and why? Let us solve this mystery using clear scientific principles and numbers.

## Key Vocabulary

| Term | Meaning | Example |

| **Force ($F$)** | A push or pull exerted on an object, measured in newtons ($\text{N}$) | $50\text{ N}$ |

| **Work against gravity** | Work done to lift an object vertically against Earth's gravitational attraction | Lifting bricks up a ladder |

## Part A: Everyday "Work" vs. Physics "Work"

In ordinary daily speech, the word "work" is broad and flexible: studying for hours, sitting at an office desk typing emails, getting mentally exhausted, cleaning a room, or feeling fatigued are all called "working."

In physics, however, **work** has a very precise, non-negotiable definition. In physics, work is done if and only if **both** of the following conditions are satisfied:

> **Condition 1:** A **force** must act on the object.
> **Condition 2:** The object must undergo **displacement in the direction of the force**.

If even one of these conditions is not fulfilled, the work done on that object in physics is strictly **zero ($W = 0$)**.

Therefore, physical effort or fatigue is not the same as scientific work. Fatigue is an internal biological sensation of your muscles and nervous system; physics work is an external mechanical interaction that displaces a body.

Consider the simple diagram below: pushing a heavy box along a smooth floor.

```
        F (applied push) ──────►
      ┌────────────┐
  o   │    box     │ ──────► s (displacement of box)
 /│\──┤            │
 / \  └────────────┘
 ═══════════════════════════════════════  floor
      ◄─────────── s ───────────►
```

> **Figure 1.1** – A box is pushed by an applied force $F$ and slides a distance $s$ in the same direction. There is both a force and a displacement in the direction of the force; therefore, mechanical work is done.

Now consider another scenario: pushing against a solid concrete wall with all your strength.

```
        F ──────►
  o   ╔═════════╗
 /│\──╢  wall   ║    The wall does not budge: s = 0
 / \  ╚═════════╝
 ═══════════════════
```

> **Figure 1.2** – A person pushes forcefully against a rigid wall ($F > 0$), but the wall does not move at all ($s = 0$). Even though the person tires out, in physics, the work done on the wall is strictly $W = 0$.

## Part B: The Formula for Work and the Joule

When the applied force and the displacement occur **in the same direction**, work is defined as:

> **Work = Force $\times$ Displacement (in the direction of the force)**
>
> $$W = F \times s$$

Where:
- $W$ = work done, measured in joules ($\text{J}$)
- $F$ = applied force, measured in newtons ($\text{N}$)
- $s$ = displacement in the direction of the force, measured in metres ($\text{m}$)

We can rearrange this fundamental formula in three handy ways:
- $W = F \times s$
- $F = \frac{W}{s}$
- $s = \frac{W}{F}$

### The Joule (J): The SI Unit of Work

Force is measured in newtons ($\text{N}$), and displacement is measured in metres ($\text{m}$). Consequently, the unit of work is newton-metre ($\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$), which is officially named the **joule ($\text{J}$)** in honour of the English physicist James Prescott Joule.

> **$1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N} \times 1\text{ m} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$**
>
> **One joule ($1\text{ J}$) is defined as the amount of work done when a force of $1\text{ newton}$ displaces an object through a distance of $1\text{ metre}$ in the direction of the force.**

Always remember: **$1\text{ J}$ and $1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ denote the exact same physical unit**, just under different names.

To get an intuitive feel for $1\text{ J}$: an average apple has a mass of approximately $0.1\text{ kg}$, which means Earth's gravity pulls it down with a weight of roughly $1\text{ N}$. Lifting that apple vertically upward through a height of $1\text{ m}$ requires approximately **$1\text{ J}$ of work**.

### Larger Units

When dealing with large amounts of work, we use standard metric prefixes:

| Unit | Meaning | Value in Joules |
|---|---|---|
| **$1\text{ kilojoule (kJ)}$** | One thousand joules | $1000\text{ J} = 10^3\text{ J}$ |
| **$1\text{ megajoule (MJ)}$** | One million joules | $1\,000\,000\text{ J} = 10^6\text{ J}$ |

Just as $1\text{ km} = 1000\text{ m}$, we have $1\text{ kJ} = 1000\text{ J}$, and $1\text{ MJ} = 1000\text{ kJ} = 1\,000\,000\text{ J}$.

### Work is a Scalar Quantity

Work has magnitude (e.g., $100\text{ J}$), but it has **no spatial direction**. We never say "100 joules toward the north." Therefore, **work is a scalar quantity**. Even though force and displacement are both vector quantities, their scalar product yields a scalar result.

### Force, Not Just Mass: Work Against Gravity

To lift an object vertically upward at a constant speed, the upward force applied must balance the downward gravitational pull—namely, the object's **weight**:

> **$\text{Weight } F = m \times g$**

In this chapter, unless specified otherwise, we will use **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$** for straightforward arithmetic (the standard precise value on Earth's surface is approximately $9.8\text{ m/s}^2$). For example, the weight of a $2\text{ kg}$ brick is $2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = \mathbf{20\text{ N}}$.

When an object of mass $m$ is lifted vertically through a vertical height $h$, the upward work done against gravity is:

> $$W = F \times s = (m \times g) \times h = mgh$$

We will explore the deeper significance of this expression in Topic 5 (Gravitational Potential Energy). For now, remember it as: **force equal to weight $\times$ vertical height raised**.

Consider the sketch of a bucket being drawn up from a well:

```
        rope
          │  F = mg (applied upward force)
          ▲
        ┌─┴─┐   ▲
        │bkt│   │ h (vertical displacement)
        └───┘   ▼
    ════ well ═════
```

> **Figure 1.3** – To lift a bucket vertically at constant speed, an upward force equal to its weight ($F = mg$) is applied via the rope, moving the bucket upward through height $h$. Both force and displacement are directed upward, so $W = mgh$.

## Part C: When is Work Done, and When is it Zero?

Examine the 8 everyday scenarios in the table below. In each case, test both fundamental conditions.

| Scenario | Is a force acting? | Does the object move in the direction of the force? | Physics work done? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Pushing firmly against a concrete wall | Yes | No ($s = 0$) | **No** ($W = 0$) |
| 2. Holding a heavy suitcase stationary in your hand | Yes (upward support force equal to weight) | No ($s = 0$) | **No** ($W = 0$) |
| 3. Carrying a backpack while walking horizontally on a level sidewalk | Yes (upward force balancing backpack's weight) | No; displacement is horizontal while the lifting force is vertical ($90^\circ$) | **No work done by this lifting force** (fully explored in Topic 2) |
| 4. Sitting still in a chair studying a book | No net external force moving anything | No ($s = 0$) | **No** ($W = 0$) |
| 5. A builder carrying heavy materials up a flight of stairs | Yes (upward force) | Yes (displaced upward) | **Yes**: positive work done against gravity |
| 6. Pulling a water bucket vertically upward from a well | Yes (tension in rope pulling upward) | Yes (bucket moves upward) | **Yes**: $W = mgh$ |
| 7. Pulling a wagon forward with a horizontal rope | Yes (forward pull) | Yes (wagon moves forward) | **Yes**: $W = F \times s$ |
| 8. A sliding stone slowing down and stopping on a rough surface | Yes (frictional force opposing motion) | Yes, the stone slides, but friction acts in the opposite direction | **Friction does work** (it does negative work, explored in Topic 2) |

Notice three key takeaways:
- In Scenarios 1 and 2: A large force is exerted, but because there is **no displacement ($s = 0$)**, the work done on the object is strictly zero.
- In Scenarios 3 and 8: The force and the motion have **different directions**. If the force is perpendicular to motion, it does no work.
- In Scenario 4: Mental concentration burns calories and exhausts the brain, but because no external object is displaced by a mechanical force, physics work is zero.

## Part D: Solved Examples

In all calculations below, take **$g = 10\text{ m/s}^2$**.

### Example 1: Calculating W (F and s given)

A student pushes a toy cart with a horizontal force of $20\text{ N}$ through a distance of $5\text{ m}$ along the direction of the force. How much work is done?

- $F = 20\text{ N}$, $s = 5\text{ m}$
- $W = F \times s = 20\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{100\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 100\text{ J}}$

**Check:** $\frac{100\text{ J}}{5\text{ m}} = 20\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 2: Calculating F

A force displaces an object through $6\text{ m}$ in its own direction, performing $150\text{ J}$ of work. What was the magnitude of the force?

- $W = 150\text{ J}$, $s = 6\text{ m}$
- $F = \frac{W}{s} = \frac{150\text{ J}}{6\text{ m}} = \frac{150\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}{6\text{ m}} = \mathbf{25\text{ N}}$

**Check:** $25\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = 150\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 3: Calculating s

A constant force of $40\text{ N}$ performs $240\text{ J}$ of work in moving an object along its line of action. What distance did the object travel?

- $W = 240\text{ J}$, $F = 40\text{ N}$
- $s = \frac{W}{F} = \frac{240\text{ J}}{40\text{ N}} = \frac{240\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}}{40\text{ N}} = \mathbf{6\text{ m}}$

**Check:** $40\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = 240\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 4: Lifting a 5 kg Bag Through 2 m

Find the work done in lifting a $5\text{ kg}$ school bag vertically upward through a height of $2\text{ m}$.

- First, find the upward lifting force required:
  $$F = mg = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 50\text{ N}$$
- Next, calculate work done against gravity:
  $$W = F \times h = 50\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{100\text{ J}}$$

**Check:** $W = mgh = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 2\text{ m} = 100\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 5: Pushing a Trolley

A customer exerts a continuous force of $30\text{ N}$ to push a shopping trolley in a straight line through an aisle of length $12\text{ m}$. How much work is done on the trolley?

- $F = 30\text{ N}$, $s = 12\text{ m}$
- $W = F \times s = 30\text{ N} \times 12\text{ m} = \mathbf{360\text{ J}}$

**Check:** $\frac{360\text{ J}}{12\text{ m}} = 30\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 6: A 55 kg Boy Climbing 24 Stairs

A boy of mass $55\text{ kg}$ climbs a staircase consisting of $24$ steps. Each step has a vertical height of $0.25\text{ m}$. Calculate the work done by the boy against gravity.

- Total vertical height climbed:
  $$h = 24 \times 0.25\text{ m} = 6\text{ m}$$
- Upward force needed to lift his own body (his weight):
  $$F = mg = 55\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 550\text{ N}$$
- Work done against gravity:
  $$W = F \times h = 550\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = \mathbf{3300\text{ J} = 3.3\text{ kJ}}$$

**Check:** $W = 55\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 \times 6\text{ m} = 3300\text{ J}$, and $\frac{3300\text{ J}}{1000} = 3.3\text{ kJ}$ ✅

### Example 7: Pulling a Cart and Converting to kJ

A worker pulls a garden cart with a horizontal force of $80\text{ N}$ through a distance of $25\text{ m}$ along the direction of the force.

- $W = F \times s = 80\text{ N} \times 25\text{ m} = \mathbf{2000\text{ J}}$
- Expressing in kilojoules:
  $$\frac{2000\text{ J}}{1000} = \mathbf{2\text{ kJ}}$$

Helpful metric conversions:
- $2.5\text{ kJ} = 2.5 \times 1000 = \mathbf{2500\text{ J}}$
- $4500\text{ J} = \frac{4500}{1000} = \mathbf{4.5\text{ kJ}}$
- $3\text{ MJ} = 3 \times 1\,000\,000 = \mathbf{3\,000\,000\text{ J}}$

**Check:** $2\text{ kJ} \times 1000 = 2000\text{ J}$ ✅, $\frac{2500\text{ J}}{1000} = 2.5\text{ kJ}$ ✅, $4.5\text{ kJ} \times 1000 = 4500\text{ J}$ ✅

### Example 8: Dragging a Box Against Friction

A crate is dragged across a rough warehouse floor through a distance of $5\text{ m}$ at a steady speed. The opposing frictional force between the crate and the floor is $40\text{ N}$. To maintain a steady velocity, the worker must exert a pulling force equal and opposite to friction ($40\text{ N}$). How much work does the worker do?

- $F = 40\text{ N}$, $s = 5\text{ m}$
- $W = F \times s = 40\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{200\text{ J}}$

**Check:** $\frac{200\text{ J}}{5\text{ m}} = 40\text{ N}$ ✅

### Example 9: Comparing Work Done by Two People

Person A lifts a $6\text{ kg}$ carton vertically by $5\text{ m}$. Person B lifts a $15\text{ kg}$ crate vertically by $1.5\text{ m}$. Who performs more work?

- **Person A:**
  $$F_A = 6\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 60\text{ N}$$
  $$W_A = 60\text{ N} \times 5\text{ m} = \mathbf{300\text{ J}}$$
- **Person B:**
  $$F_B = 15\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 150\text{ N}$$
  $$W_B = 150\text{ N} \times 1.5\text{ m} = \mathbf{225\text{ J}}$$
- Since $300\text{ J} > 225\text{ J}$, **Person A performs more work**, even though Person B exerted a much greater force ($150\text{ N} > 60\text{ N}$).

**Check:** For A: $6 \times 10 \times 5 = 300\text{ J}$ ✅. For B: $15 \times 10 \times 1.5 = 225\text{ J}$ ✅

**Key insight:** Work depends on **both** force and displacement. A larger force does not guarantee more work if the displacement is small.

### Example 10: When an Object Does Not Move

A fitness enthusiast pushes against a solid brick pillar with a force of $500\text{ N}$ for two minutes. The pillar does not budge ($s = 0\text{ m}$). How much work is done on the pillar?

- $F = 500\text{ N}$, $s = 0\text{ m}$
- $W = F \times s = 500\text{ N} \times 0\text{ m} = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$

**Check:** Any number $\times 0 = 0$ ✅

## The Mystery Solved: Who Actually Did Work?

Now let us return to Maya and Marcus at the renovation site. Suppose each brick has a mass of $2\text{ kg}$, and the ladder reaches a scaffold platform $3\text{ m}$ high.

**Liam (carrying 1 brick up the ladder):**
- Upward lifting force: $F = mg = 2\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 20\text{ N}$ (upward)
- Displacement: $s = 3\text{ m}$ (upward, in the direction of the force)
- Work done: $W = F \times s = 20\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{60\text{ J}}$

**Check:** $2 \times 10 \times 3 = 60\text{ J}$ ✅ and $\frac{60\text{ J}}{3\text{ m}} = 20\text{ N}$ ✅

If Liam carries $10$ bricks simultaneously:
- Total mass = $10 \times 2\text{ kg} = 20\text{ kg}$
- Lifting force = $20\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 200\text{ N}$
- Work done = $200\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{600\text{ J}}$ (ten times the work, $10 \times 60\text{ J} = 600\text{ J}$ ✅).

**Maya (studying her textbook):**
- Although Maya's brain consumed biochemical energy and her mind felt completely exhausted, neither the textbook nor any external physical object moved through a displacement in the direction of an applied force.
- $W = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$

Marcus concluded: "You see, Maya, Liam applied an upward force on the bricks and displaced them upward—fulfilling both conditions for work. You exerted tremendous mental effort, but in physics, no external displacement occurred under your applied forces. Therefore, your physical work is $0\text{ J}$. That does not diminish the value of your studying, but physics measures mechanical interactions, not mental dedication!" Maya laughed, "So working hard and doing mechanical work are two completely different things!"

## Part E: The Effect of Work: Changing Speed

Whenever net external work is done on an object, the object's physical state changes. If a shopping cart rests motionless on a flat floor and you push it forward with an unbalanced force, it begins to accelerate: **its speed increases**.

In other words, doing positive net work on an object increases its motion—it gives the object kinetic energy. Exactly how work transforms into speed and energy will be developed mathematically in Topic 4 (The Work-Energy Theorem). For now, keep this fundamental link in mind: **doing net work changes an object's kinetic energy**.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

1. **Mistake 1: Believing that holding a heavy weight stationary constitutes work.**
   Holding a $30\text{ kg}$ barbell stationary overhead requires immense muscle tension and causes quick exhaustion, but because the displacement is zero ($s = 0$), the work done on the barbell is $W = F \times 0 = \mathbf{0\text{ J}}$. (Your muscle fibres are rapidly twitching internally, burning metabolic energy, but zero mechanical work is delivered to the barbell.)

2. **Mistake 2: Confusing human effort or exhaustion with physical work.**
   Physical effort and physics work are distinct concepts. Maya studied relentlessly until exhausted, but her physics work was $0\text{ J}$. Work strictly requires mechanical displacement in the direction of force.

3. **Mistake 3: Multiplying mass by distance instead of force by distance.**
   When lifting a $5\text{ kg}$ mass by $2\text{ m}$, writing $W = 5 \times 2 = 10\text{ J}$ is **incorrect**. Mass ($5\text{ kg}$) is not force! You must first calculate weight: $F = mg = 5\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 50\text{ N}$. Then $W = 50\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = \mathbf{100\text{ J}}$.

4. **Mistake 4: Confusing distance travelled with displacement in the direction of force.**
   If an object travels along a winding path and returns to its initial starting point, its net displacement is zero. Always ensure $s$ represents the displacement along the line of action of the force.

5. **Mistake 5: Treating $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$ and $\text{J}$ as different units.**
   $1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m} = 1\text{ J}$. They are two names for the identical unit. Saying "$200\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$ of work" and "$200\text{ J}$ of work" is identical.

6. **Mistake 6: Forgetting the acceleration due to gravity ($g$).**
   The force required to lift an object vertically against gravity is its weight ($mg$), not just $m$. Never omit $g$ when calculating vertical lifting work: $W = mgh$.

## Quick Recap

- In physics, **work is done** when: (1) a force acts on an object, and (2) the object undergoes a displacement in the direction of the force.
- **Formula:** $W = F \times s$, where $F$ is in newtons ($\text{N}$), $s$ is in metres ($\text{m}$), and $W$ is in joules ($\text{J}$).
- **SI Unit:** $1\text{ J} = 1\text{ N} \times 1\text{ m} = 1\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$. Larger units: $1\text{ kJ} = 1000\text{ J}$, $1\text{ MJ} = 1\,000\,000\text{ J}$.
- **Work is a scalar quantity:** it possesses magnitude only, without spatial direction.
- To lift an object of mass $m$ to a vertical height $h$, the work done against gravity is $W = mgh$ (with $g \approx 10\text{ m/s}^2$).
- If there is no displacement ($s = 0$), or if the force is perpendicular to motion, the work done by that force is **zero**.
- Muscular fatigue or mental exertion does not equate to physics work.
- Net work done on an object changes its speed and kinetic energy (Topic 4).
- **Opening story:** Carrying a $2\text{ kg}$ brick up $3\text{ m}$ requires $60\text{ J}$ of work. Studying with a book results in $0\text{ J}$ of mechanical work.

   $W = F \times s = 25\text{ N} \times 6\text{ m} = \mathbf{150\text{ J}}$.
   (The mass of $8\text{ kg}$ is not needed because the pulling force is already known.)
   Check: $\frac{150\text{ J}}{6\text{ m}} = 25\text{ N}$ ✅

8. Person A: $F_A = 3\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 30\text{ N}$; $W_A = 30\text{ N} \times 4\text{ m} = 120\text{ J}$.
   Person B: $F_B = 6\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 60\text{ N}$; $W_B = 60\text{ N} \times 2\text{ m} = 120\text{ J}$.
   Both do **equal work** ($120\text{ J} = 120\text{ J}$). Person B exerts double the force over half the vertical distance.
   Check: $3 \times 10 \times 4 = 120$ ✅, $6 \times 10 \times 2 = 120$ ✅

9. (a) $0.75\text{ kJ} = 0.75 \times 1000 = \mathbf{750\text{ J}}$.
   (b) $5400\text{ J} = \frac{5400}{1000} = \mathbf{5.4\text{ kJ}}$.
   (c) $2\text{ MJ} = 2 \times 1000 = \mathbf{2000\text{ kJ}}$ ($= 2\,000\,000\text{ J}$).
   Check: $\frac{750}{1000} = 0.75$ ✅, $5.4 \times 1000 = 5400$ ✅, $2000\text{ kJ} \times 1000 = 2\,000\,000\text{ J} = 2\text{ MJ}$ ✅

10. Total mass lifted = $12 \times 2\text{ kg} = 24\text{ kg}$.
    Upward force $F = mg = 24\text{ kg} \times 10\text{ m/s}^2 = 240\text{ N}$.
    Work done against gravity $W = F \times h = 240\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m} = \mathbf{720\text{ J}}$.
    The studying student causes zero displacement on the desk or surroundings, so the mechanical work done is strictly $\mathbf{0\text{ J}}$.
    Check: $12 \times (20\text{ N} \times 3\text{ m}) = 12 \times 60\text{ J} = 720\text{ J}$ ✅

Now you can clearly explain the outcome to Maya and Marcus: Liam performed $60\text{ J}$ of mechanical work for each brick carried up the ladder, while Maya performed $0\text{ J}$ of physics work while studying. The next time someone claims they are "doing so much work," ask yourself two questions: was a force exerted, and did the object move in the direction of that force?
